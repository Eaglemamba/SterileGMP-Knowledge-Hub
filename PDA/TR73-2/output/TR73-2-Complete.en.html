1.0 Introduction

Original Text

The European Union regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices, referred to as MDR, introduces new requirements for manufacturers of medical devices. These new requirements, according to MDR Articles 1(8) and 1(9), subparagraphs 2, also apply to pharmaceutical companies developing and manufacturing combinations of medicinal products and medical devices, including prefilled syringes (PFS), both integral and single integral products. The device part of these PFS must comply with the rules outlined in MDR Annex I: General Safety and Performance Requirements (GSPRs). Evidence of compliance, as described in Article 117, must be provided by requesting an opinion from a Notified Body (NB) appropriately accredited for the issuance of such an opinion (1) (See Section 3.0).

PDA Technical Report No. 73-2: Application of Medical Device Regulation Annex I Requirements for Staked Needle Syringes represents the current state of knowledge as to its application. As this is a new process based on a new regulatory requirement, expectations of the NBs and competent authorities may diverge as the process evolves. Ultimately, the responsibility for the entire content of the submission file that is reviewed by the NB lies with the applicant. Accordingly, the intended users of TR-73-2 are applicants, i.e., pharmaceutical companies, their suppliers, and other interested parties.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

MDR (Medical Device Regulation): The new EU medical device regulation, published in 2017 and applied from 26 May 2021, which replaced the former Directive 93/42/EEC. The MDR sets stricter safety and performance requirements for the "device part" of a prefilled syringe (PFS).

GSPRs (General Safety and Performance Requirements): The comprehensive list of requirements in MDR Annex I, covering design, materials, biocompatibility, chemical stability and more. The device part of a PFS must meet each relevant GSPR.

Notified Body (NB): An EU-designated third-party assessment body that reviews whether the device part of a PFS complies with the MDR and issues a formal opinion (NB Opinion).

Analogy

Think of the MDR GSPRs as a "building safety checklist". Just as a building must pass structural, fire and electrical inspections, the device part of a prefilled syringe must satisfy each of the GSPRs for safety and performance. The Notified Body acts as the "building inspector": it reviews your design documents and test reports and grants approval only once everything is confirmed compliant.

Key Notes

TR-73-2 represents the "current state of knowledge". Because the MDR is still a relatively new regulatory framework, the expectations of different NBs and national Competent Authorities may differ. This means:

  • Applicants cannot rely on any single guidance document and need to keep following regulatory developments
  • The final responsibility for the submitted documentation rests with the applicant
  • Pharmaceutical companies, suppliers and other interested parties are all target readers of this report

1.2 Scope

Original Text

While the scope of PDA TR-73 applies only to 1 ml long glass PFS for biotechnology application, TR-73-2 applies to PFS of glass and polymer of different volumes within the definition of MDR Article 1(9), subparagraph 2. The scope of TR-73-2 is not exclusive for biotechnology applications. The device part, including its corresponding components and materials, are in scope as they will be evaluated by the NBs, but the medicinal product (drug) is not.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Staked Needle Syringe: A syringe whose needle is permanently fixed to the barrel and cannot be detached. This is the core subject of this report. By contrast, Luer lock and Luer cone syringes are out of scope.

Device Part vs. Drug: The NB assesses only the device part (barrel, needle, needle shield, plunger rod, etc.). The drug itself is outside the NB's review and is the responsibility of the medicines authority (e.g., EMA).

Fixed Dose: This report covers only fixed-dose PFS and excludes variable-dose designs that require a measuring function.

Key Notes

The scope of TR-73-2 is broader than that of TR-73:

  • Material: Not limited to glass; polymer syringe barrels are also covered
  • Volume: Not limited to 1 mL; applicable to different volumes
  • Application: Not limited to biotechnology products; covers all injectable drug products

Note, however: although packaging is out of scope, packaging-related evidence (such as shipping studies and user labeling) that affects the device part should still be included in the NB submission.

2.1 Glossary

Original Text

Definitions have been provided to help clarify the concepts discussed in this technical report. While some of the definitions vary among companies, the definitions described below are consistent for use within this technical report. Where a definition is based on another published source, the source is cited. Only definitions that are either not mentioned in TR-73 or not taken from the TR-73 glossary are mentioned here.

As there is no agreed-upon EU-wide term for a product combining a medicinal product and a medical device part, such as "prefillable syringes ready for filling," this document uses the term "drug-device combination (DDC)" to refer to the final PFS system.

Applicant
The commercial entity responsible for the marketing authorisation application or post-approval application in the EU (5).
Competent Authorities (National)
A medicines regulatory authority in a European Union Member State (6).
Container Closure Integrity
Container closure integrity (CCI) is the ability of a container closure system to maintain the sterility and product quality of sterile final pharmaceutical, biological, and vaccine products throughout their shelf life.
Device Part
A device that fulfills the definition of a medical device, as defined in the relevant EU legal framework, and is intended to be placed on the EU market or made available on the EU market. This also applies to part of a device that fulfills the definition of Article 2(1). In the context of this document, the device part comprises the syringe subassembly ready for filling plus the plunger rod, needle shield, and finger flange extension.
Direct Contact
Medical device or medical device component that comes into physical contact with body tissue (7).
Drug-Device Combination (DDC) Product
Medicinal products where the medical device and/or device part and the medicinal product form an integral product that is not reusable (hereafter called integral) and where the action of the medicinal product is principal (1). There are two types of DDCs according to MDR Articles 1(8) and 1(9):
  • Any device which, when placed on the market or put into service, incorporates, as an integral part, a substance which, if used separately, would be considered to be a medicinal product... [and] if the action of that substance is principal and not ancillary to that of the device, the integral product will be regulated as a medicinal product (Article 1(8)).
  • If the [medical] device intended to administer a medicinal product and the medicinal product is placed on the market in such a way that they form a single integral product which is intended exclusively for use in the given combination and which is not reusable, that single integral product shall be governed by Directive 2001/83/EC or Regulation (EC) No 726/2004, as applicable (Article 1(9), subparagraph 2) (8).
Indirect Contact
Medical device or medical device component through which a fluid or gas passes, prior to the fluid or gas coming into physical contact with body tissue (in this case the medical device or medical device component itself does not physically contact body tissue) (7).
Market Authorization Holder
The company or other legal entity that has the authorization or aims to receive it, to market a medicine in one, several, or all European Union Member States (adapted from EMA Glossary of Regulatory Terms) (6).
Nanomaterial
A natural, incidental or manufactured material containing particles in an unbound state or as an aggregate or as an agglomerate and where, for 50 % or more of the particles in the number size distribution, one or more external dimensions is in the size range 1-100 nm; Fullerenes, graphene flakes and single-wall carbon nanotubes with one or more external dimensions below 1 nm shall also be deemed to be nanomaterials (Article 2(18)) (1).
Needle Safety Device
A safety feature or mechanism that effectively reduces the risk of an exposure incident (e.g., accidental needlestick) (9); sharps injuries protection system.
Needle Shield, Rigid Needle Shield
Elastomeric syringe closure, which seals the front end of staked needle syringe, designed to protect the needle tip/bevel from damage and allows sterilization of the syringe. The elastomeric syringe closure covered by a rigid housing is called rigid needle shield (10).
Notified Body
Conformity assessment body designated in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (Article 2(42)) (1).
Processing Aids
A substance (or mixture of substances) that is used to facilitate manufacturability, processing, transport, or storage of a material or component. It may assist in, for example, reducing energy/time/resources required to aid the process, increasing production, and cleaning efficiency and machinability. Processing aids do not contribute to the intended attributes of the finished component and do not belong to the intentionally added ingredients of the formulation (e.g., silicone oil, mold release, and anti-tack agents). Processing aids may be removed during the manufacturing process or, depending on the process clearance capability, may remain as low-level residuals in the finished component.
Processing Residues
Residues of processing aids in or on the final drug-device combination.
Quality Certificate
Certificate of Analysis, Certificate of Compliance, or equivalent document providing evidence of compliance with product specifications.
Risk Evaluation
Process of comparing the estimated risk against given risk criteria to determine the acceptability of the risk (11).
Risk Management
Systematic application of management policies, procedures and practices to the tasks of analyzing, evaluating, controlling and monitoring risks (11).
Single-Dose Container
A container that holds a quantity of the preparation intended for total or partial use on one occasion only (Ph. Eur. 3.2) (12).
Staked Needle Syringe
Syringe with a needle integrated into the barrel; the fixation can be done by insert molding, gluing or other bonding methods (13).
State of the Art (SOTA), Generally Acknowledged (GA)
Developed stage of current technical capability and/or accepted clinical practice in regard to products, processes and patient management, based on the relevant consolidated findings of science, technology and experience (14).
Surgically Invasive
Surgically invasive device means (a) an invasive device which penetrates inside the body through the surface of the body, including through mucous membranes of body orifices with the aid or in the context of a surgical operation; and (b) a device which produces penetration other than through a body orifice (Annex VIII, Chapt. 1, 2.2) (1).
Syringe Barrel
Cylindrical glass or polymer body with front end and finger flange (10, 13).
Syringe Components
Can be a glass or polymer syringe barrel with or without lubrication, a needle with lubrication and a needle shield (soft or rigid), plunger (also plunger stopper) with lubrication, coating and/or lamination, plunger rod and, if needed, a backstop.
Syringe Subassembly Ready for Filling (also Prefillable Subassembled Syringe)
Subassembly consisting of a glass or polymer syringe barrel, a needle and a needle shield (soft or rigid) that has been washed and sterilized; considered as starting material for the filling and assembling process of a prefilled syringe (10).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Drug-Device Combination, DDC: This is one of the most central concepts in this report. A DDC is a product in which a drug and a medical device form a single integral product that is not reusable, and in which the action of the drug is the principal one. A PFS is a typical DDC: the syringe is the device part and the liquid is the drug part, combined into one integral product.

Difference between Article 1(8) and Article 1(9):

  • Article 1(8): A device that "incorporates" a substance with medicinal properties; if the action of that substance is principal, the whole product is regulated as a medicinal product
  • Article 1(9), subparagraph 2: A device and a medicinal product placed on the market as a single integral product that is not reusable is governed by Directive 2001/83/EC (i.e., medicinal product law takes precedence)

A PFS mainly falls under the situation described in Article 1(9), subparagraph 2.

Key Term Reference

Direct Contact: The component directly contacts human tissue. Example: the needle penetrating the skin.

Indirect Contact: The component does not contact tissue directly, but a liquid or gas passes through or over it before reaching the body. Example: the inner wall of the barrel (the drug solution passes over it before being injected into the body).

Container Closure Integrity, CCI: The ability of the container closure system to maintain sterility and product quality throughout the shelf life. This is a critical quality attribute of a PFS.

Processing Aids: Substances such as silicone oil and mold release agents that are not part of the formulation but may remain in the finished product at low levels. These Processing Residues must be evaluated and controlled.

Analogy

The DDC concept can be understood through a "ready meal". The lunch box (device) and the food (drug) form one integral product, and the consumer throws it away after eating (not reusable). The function of the food (drug) is the principal one, but the lunch box (device) must still meet food-safe container standards. From a regulatory standpoint, this "ready meal" is mainly governed by food law, but the box still has to meet container safety requirements -- just as a PFS is mainly governed by medicinal product law, while its device part must meet the MDR GSPRs.

Key Notes

Several concepts in the glossary are easily confused and deserve special attention:

  • Syringe Components: All individual parts (barrel, needle, shield, stopper, plunger rod, backstop)
  • Syringe Subassembly Ready for Filling: The cleaned and sterilized barrel + needle + shield assembly, which is the starting material for the filling process
  • Device Part: = Syringe Subassembly + plunger rod + shield + finger flange extension

Distinguishing these three levels is essential for understanding the applicable scope of the later GSPRs.

State of the Art, SOTA -- Special Note

SOTA has a particular legal meaning under the MDR. It does not simply mean the "latest technology"; it refers to "what is accepted as good practice in technology and medicine", i.e., the current level of technology and/or accepted clinical practice reached on the basis of the combined results of science, technology and experience. When preparing GSPR evidence, you must demonstrate that your design and manufacture conform to the SOTA. This means citing relevant standards (harmonised standards), guidance documents and published scientific literature.

3.0 Regulatory Background

Original Text

Moreover, the device part of this single integral (DDC) product (e.g., final prefilled staked needle syringe) must comply with the relevant GSPRs established in MDR Annex I.

In order to evaluate whether GSPR compliance can be self-assessed or if it requires a Notified Body Opinion (NBOp), the device part of the PFS must be classified as a "virtual device." MDR Annex VIII lays out the classification rules that determine the level of information and the involvement of a NB that would be required. Annex VIII, Rule 6 refers to surgically invasive devices intended for transient use. Because the device part of a prefilled syringe falls under this description, it is classified as Class IIa or higher, thus requiring a NB to be involved, as stated in MDR Article 117. That Article amends Directive 2001/83/EC Annex I, Section 3.2, point 12 to state that, if the application dossier to the competent authorities does not already include the results of the conformity assessment of the device, a NBOp must be provided.

The generation of the required data is a joint effort between the manufacturers of the components, device part, and device and the pharmaceutical company. As the pharmaceutical company (the applicant) is filing both for the NBOp and the marketing authorization, it is ultimately responsible for ensuring that all content and data are "attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original and accurate (ALCOA)" (15). The European Medicines Agency (EMA) provides a comprehensive website, updated regularly, with questions and answers on good manufacturing practices (GMP) and data integrity (8).

In summary, applicants who want to place staked needle PFS on the EU market must involve a NB in the assessment of compliance with MDR Annex I GSPRs. The NBOp obtained must be included in the application dossier to the national competent authorities or the EMA.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

DDC (Drug-Device Combination): A prefilled syringe is a "single integral" drug-device combination product. The drug and the device cannot be separated: the needle is already fixed to the syringe barrel (staked needle), and the user receives one complete product. This means it is subject to both medicinal product regulation and medical device regulation.

GSPR (General Safety and Performance Requirements): The core list of requirements set out in MDR Annex I, covering the safety and performance standards a medical device must meet in design, manufacture, labeling and other respects. For the device part of a PFS, each GSPR must be assessed for applicability, and evidence of compliance must be provided.

NBOp (Notified Body Opinion): A formal opinion issued by an EU-designated Notified Body after it assesses the conformity of the device part. It is not a "certificate"; it is a professional assessment report that the competent authority refers to when reviewing the marketing authorisation application.

Virtual Device: The device part of a PFS is not placed on the market as a stand-alone medical device, but it still has to be "virtually classified" under the MDR classification rules to determine the depth of conformity assessment required. This is a unique regulatory concept: there is no separate device marketing authorisation for the product, yet it still goes through the device regulation review process.

Analogy

Imagine you run a restaurant and launch a "set meal" (the drug-device combination product). The set includes food (the drug) and a specially designed utensil (the device part). The health department (the competent authority) requires you not only to ensure the food is safe (drug quality) but also to prove that your special utensil meets utensil safety standards (the GSPRs). Because the utensil is a special knife-and-fork combined design (Class IIa), you cannot simply say "I think it is safe": you must have an independent utensil inspection body (the Notified Body) issue an inspection report (the NBOp), and submit it to the health department together with your food safety documents. In the end, all responsibility for the quality of the entire set meal rests with you (the applicant).

Key Notes

The key logic of the Class IIa classification: MDR Annex VIII Rule 6 addresses "surgically invasive devices intended for transient use". The needle of a prefilled syringe penetrates the skin and enters the body (surgically invasive) and is used for a very short time (transient use), so it is classified as Class IIa or higher. Once a device reaches Class IIa, the manufacturer cannot rely on a self-declaration of conformity alone; a Notified Body must be involved.

The ALCOA principles are non-negotiable: All submitted data must comply with the ALCOA principles: Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate. This is the cornerstone of data integrity and a requirement the EMA repeatedly emphasizes in relation to GMP and data integrity.

Clear allocation of responsibility: Although data generation is a joint effort of the component manufacturer, the device assembler and the pharmaceutical company, the ultimate responsibility lies with the applicant (the pharmaceutical company). This means the pharmaceutical company cannot simply "pass on" the data provided by suppliers; it must ensure the quality and integrity of all data.

Practical Application

Positioning the role of a CDMO: As a CDMO, you may act as both the "device part manufacturer" and the "drug product filler". When your client (the pharmaceutical company) prepares to apply for marketing authorisation in the EU, you will need to:

  • Provide process validation data for the syringe assembly and filling processes
  • Ensure that all test reports comply with the ALCOA principles
  • Support the client in preparing the Notified Body submission
  • Understand which GSPRs are relevant to your manufacturing steps and prepare the corresponding evidence of compliance

Even though the ultimate responsibility lies with the client (the applicant), data quality problems directly affect whether the NBOp is obtained and therefore delay time to market.

Key Concepts

4.3 Device Function

Original Text

This section provides a summary of the device function. The description may be phrased like this:

The staked needle PFS consists of a plunger with a rubber stopper that fits tightly in a cylindrical tube (the syringe barrel) containing the drug. To expel the drug, the user pushes the plunger rod until the rubber stopper hits the end of the barrel. Dosing will be fixed, that is, no dose adjustment with respect to bodyweight and/or body surface area is needed. One PFS equals one dose.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Fixed Dose: "One PFS equals one dose" is one of the core features of a staked needle PFS. Each syringe is pre-filled with a precise single dose, and the user neither needs to nor can adjust the dose. This simplifies use and lowers the risk of dosing errors, but it also means that filling accuracy during manufacture is critical.

Break-loose force and glide force: Although this paragraph does not elaborate on them, the statement that the "rubber stopper fits tightly" implies two important mechanical parameters: break-loose force (the initial force needed to overcome static friction between the stopper and the barrel wall) and glide force (the sustained force needed to keep the stopper moving). These forces directly affect the injection experience.

Analogy

The operating principle of a PFS is like using a large glue stick: the glue in the tube (the drug) is pushed by the rubber stopper at the end of the plunger rod and squeezed out of the opening (the needle). The difference is that a PFS demands very high precision: every unit must deliver a complete and accurate dose.

Key Notes

Although this example wording is short, it contains several points the NB looks for during review: (1) the basic working principle of the device, (2) the method of administration (push-type), and (3) the dosing strategy (fixed dose). When writing, avoid over-simplification, but there is no need to set out all technical details here; details are presented in the later GSPR compliance sections.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

GSPR 10 (General Safety and Performance Requirement 10): GSPR 10 in MDR Annex I addresses "chemical, physical and biological properties". When a device component contains CMR substances, endocrine disruptors (ED) or nanomaterials, it must be specially flagged and a benefit-risk justification provided.

Body Contact classification: ISO 10993-1 classifies materials by contact type:

  • Surgically invasive: Needle: penetrates the skin and enters the body, highest risk
  • Intact skin: Plunger rod, outer surface of the barrel, label
  • Indirect body contact: Stopper, soft part of the needle shield: contact the body indirectly through the drug solution

Drug Product Contact: Components in direct contact with the drug solution require extractables and leachables (E&L) studies.

Material contact classification is like food safety management in a restaurant: utensils that touch food directly (equivalent to drug product contact = Yes) need food-grade certification; the cup rim that touches the lips (analogous to surgically invasive) requires the strictest safety standard; and the cup handle that is merely held in the hand (intact skin) requires a lower standard but must still be safe and harmless.   • The needle is flagged as containing CMR substances: the nickel and chromium in stainless steel (X5CrNi18-9) are CMR substances. Although stainless steel is widely used, the MDR requires explicit disclosure and a benefit-risk justification.

  • Silicone oil appears as a lubricant in several components: it affects glide force and CCI, and may interact with some biologics (for example, causing protein aggregation).

Key Notes - Common Document Patterns in Chapter I

Looking at the Device Part column for GSPR 1-8, several core documents appear repeatedly:

  • Risk Management File: Needed for almost every GSPR; includes the risk management plan, hazard identification, use-related risk analysis and post-market surveillance plan
  • Design Verification Summary Report: Demonstrates that the product meets the design specifications
  • Usability Engineering File: Demonstrates that users can use the device safely and correctly (required for GSPR 1, 5 and 8)
  • Traceability Matrix: Links all requirements to the corresponding verification activities
  • GMP Compliance: Because a prefilled syringe is a combination of drug and device, it must also comply with medicinal product GMP

The pattern in the supplier columns is more uniform: they generally ask for a high-level risk management summary and evidence of QMS implementation (ISO 13485 / ISO 15378).

Analogy

Imagine you are applying for an occupancy permit for a large building:

  • GSPR 1-3 (intended performance and risk management) are like the architectural design review: you need to submit complete design drawings, structural analysis reports and safety assessments
  • GSPR 4-5 (risk control) are like the fire and seismic reviews: you must show specific risk control measures
  • GSPR 6-7 (characteristics, and design/manufacture/packaging) are like material inspection and construction quality control: release specifications, shelf life data and stability data are needed
  • GSPR 8 (risks and side effects) is like the final overall safety assessment: benefit/risk evaluation and disclosure of residual risks

The documents provided by suppliers are like the material certificates issued by the individual subcontractors (the rebar plant, the cement plant).

Key Notes - In-Depth Requirements of Chapter II (GSPR 10.x)

The documentation requirements of Chapter II are far more detailed than those of Chapter I, reflecting the complexity of materials science and biocompatibility:

  • Extractables & Leachables (E&L): GSPR 10.1(a)(b)(d), 10.2, 10.3 and 10.4.1 all require an E&L assessment, including ICH Q3D elemental impurities
  • Biocompatibility: The syringe subassembly refers to ISO 10993-1; the stopper additionally refers to USP <87>
  • REACH / CMR / ED / Phthalates: GSPR 10.4.2 and 10.4.3 specifically address the declaration of carcinogenic/mutagenic/reproductive toxic substances and endocrine-disrupting substances
  • Ph. Eur. (European Pharmacopoeia): The syringe subassembly must comply with Ph. Eur. 3.1.8, 3.2.1, 3.2.2.1, etc.; the stopper must comply with Ph. Eur. 3.1.8 and 3.2.9
  • Container Closure Integrity (CCI): GSPR 10.5 specifically requires CCI and stability testing, and polymer syringes also need permeability studies

  • Syringe subassembly: Refers to ISO 11135 (ethylene oxide sterilization), ISO 11137 (radiation sterilization) and ISO 14937 (general requirements for sterilization)

This reflects the fact that syringe subassemblies and stoppers usually use different sterilization methods: syringes mostly use EtO or radiation, while stoppers often use autoclave (steam) sterilization.

5.2 Chapter I — Risk Management

Original Text

Chapter I of the MDR GSPR list relates primarily to risk management (RM). The pharmaceutical company is required to have an internal RM process that assesses risks associated with their device part in the context of their DDC and its intended use. This may be part of a device RM plan or combined product (i.e., including drug) RM plan. In that case, either the RM standard operating procedure or a description of the process can be shared, for example, as part of an RM file. ICH Quality Guideline Q9: Quality Risk Management provides guidance, as does ISO 14971:2019 Medical Devices — Application of Risk Management to Medical Devices which states:

"The risk management file need not physically contain all the records and other documents. However, it needs to contain at least references or pointers to all required documentation, so that the manufacturer can assemble the information referenced in the risk management file in a timely manner. [...] The risk management file can be in any form or type of medium. [...] See ISO/TR 24971 for guidance on establishing a risk management file for components and devices that were designed without using ISO 14971" (11).

Suppliers of components or device parts can support the pharmaceutical company by providing appropriate documentation, which demonstrates a RM process that communicates risks associated with the respective component(s)/part(s). In the case of an existing RM plan, the supplier may also share this with the pharmaceutical company.

As always, the responsibility of deciding if and how the supportive documents will be used remains with the pharmaceutical company.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Risk Management File: This is not a single document but a "system of document pointers". ISO 14971 expressly allows the risk management file to point to other documents by reference. What matters is that all relevant records can be assembled within a reasonable time.

ICH Q9 vs ISO 14971: Both are risk management standards, but they start from different places. ICH Q9 takes the perspective of drug product quality, while ISO 14971 takes the perspective of medical device safety. For a DDC (drug-device combination product), both need to be referenced.

Analogy

Think of the risk management file as the master index of a safety inspection binder. The index itself does not need to hold the original of every checklist, but it must state clearly that "the fire inspection report is on shelf 3 of cabinet A" and "the electrical safety report is in system B". When an auditor arrives, you should be able to find each report from the index and pull it up quickly.

Key Notes

The last sentence of this section is critical: final responsibility always rests with the drug manufacturer. Even if the component supplier provides complete risk assessment documents, the drug manufacturer must still judge for itself whether they are sufficient and whether they apply to its own product combination. Supplier documents are supporting material and cannot replace the drug manufacturer's own risk management process.

In addition, ISO/TR 24971 gives guidance on building a risk management file for components that were not designed under ISO 14971, which is particularly important for legacy components.

Key Concepts

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Justification of Nonapplicability: In MDR compliance documentation, "not applicable" does not mean "can be ignored". Every GSPR judged not applicable needs a written justification explaining why the requirement does not apply to your product. This is an important part of regulatory review.

TR73-2 proposes three principles for writing justifications:

  • Granularity: If a GSPR contains several sub-requirements, state for each one which do not apply
  • Alignment: Use the same terminology as the GSPR text
  • Precision: Do not write at length; state the logic of nonapplicability directly

Analogy

Imagine applying for a building permit. The form has an item for "fire sprinkler system drawings". If you are building an open-air pavilion, you cannot simply leave it blank; you write, "This building is an open-air structure with no enclosed space, so the indoor fire sprinkler requirements do not apply." When the reviewer reads this, they know you have considered the requirement and have a reasonable basis for it not applying. Writing a justification of nonapplicability under the MDR follows the same logic.

Key Notes

Typical exclusion categories in Table 5.5-1🔍:

  • Passive device: A PFS contains no electronic components, emits no radiation and does not actively supply energy, so the GSPR 16-18, 20.6 and 21 series do not apply
  • Not an Annex XVI product: GSPR 9 applies only to products without a medical purpose (such as aesthetic fillers); a PFS, as a drug delivery device, is outside this scope
  • Labeling regulation takes precedence: The device labeling requirements of GSPR 23 are superseded by EMA/37991/2019 (guideline on labeling of medicinal products). This reflects the dual identity of a DDC: its regulatory requirements as a medicinal product take precedence over device labeling requirements
  • The "one syringe, one dose" logic of GSPR 21.1: A PFS has no dose-setting function because one prefilled syringe is one dose; this is the simplest and most compelling justification of nonapplicability

Practical Application

When preparing MDR compliance documentation, we recommend building a "GSPR applicability matrix":

  • Column 1: GSPR number (1-23, including all sub-clauses)
  • Column 2: Applicable / Not applicable / Partially applicable
  • Column 3: Justification (when not applicable) or index of the corresponding documents (when applicable)
  • Column 4: Responsible party (drug manufacturer / supplier / shared)

This matrix can serve directly as the basis for the GSPR checklist in the Technical Documentation.

Footnote Analysis

Footnote 1 reveals an important regulatory boundary: the immediate labeling of a PFS falls under the medicinal product legislation, Directive 2001/83/EC, not the MDR. However, safety and performance information generated during device design and development should still be reflected in the user-facing labeling content.

Footnote 2 explains that although the PFS device itself has no controls or indicators, the requirement for visual inspection before use is specified in the instructions for use (IFU) and provided in the application dossier submitted to the competent authorities (CAs).

Section Summary

  • Risk management (Section 5.2): MDR Chapter I requires the drug manufacturer to establish an internal risk management process. The risk management file can be a pointer-based document system (it need not physically contain all records), but it must be possible to assemble the records within a reasonable time. Component suppliers can provide supporting documents, but final responsibility rests with the drug manufacturer. Reference standards include ICH Q9 and ISO 14971.
  • Documentation needs for add-on components (Section 5.3): Although the GSPR requirements for the plunger rod, the RNS rigid part and the finger flange extension/backstop are lighter, material safety (biocompatibility, statements on substances of concern) and technical performance (drawings, connectivity statement, physical properties) still need to be evaluated. Special Topic -- TSE/BSE Risk (#23)

Reference #23 mentions the risk of "Animal Spongiform Encephalopathy (ASE)". For prefilled syringes, this means that if any animal-derived material is used in the syringe components (for example, certain lubricants or adhesives), the risk of TSE/BSE transmission must be assessed. This is a requirement specific to EU regulation, reflecting the heightened vigilance toward animal-derived materials that the EU adopted after the BSE crisis of the 1990s.
