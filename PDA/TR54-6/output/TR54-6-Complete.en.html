Foreword

Joint Initiative between IPEC Federation and PDA

In March 2018, the IPEC Federation and PDA announced their first joint initiative. Both associations signed a memorandum of understanding to collaborate on the development of a joint technical report on excipient good manufacturing practices risk assessment in response to input from their respective memberships.

Prior to the collaboration announcement, however, both parties were active on the topic of quality risk management. PDA published the PDA Technical Report No. 54 series. In March 2016, IPEC Europe published the How-To Document – A Guide to Support Manufacturing Authorization Holders (MAH) in their Compliance with the European Commission Guidelines on Risk Assessment for Excipients (2015/C 95/02). Subsequently, in May 2017, IPEC-Americas and IPEC Europe jointly published the Risk Assessment Guide for Excipient Makers, Users, and Distributors.

Both PDA and IPEC Federation believe that presenting a common approach to the legal, regulatory, and related issues concerning excipients is best done as "one voice." Both collaborators see significant potential benefit in leveraging the two organizations' expertise on excipients and drug product manufacture. This technical report will serve as a practical guidance intended for use with existing regulatory and industry standards. The authors expect that the document will enable Manufacturing Authorization Holders of drug product to either set up or benchmark their quality systems, and further establish or continue to collaborate with parties in their excipient supply chain.

This joint PDA-IPEC technical report extends the PDA Technical Report No. 54 series and provides guidance on risk assessments for excipients by presenting a model risk assessment, guidance on key elements, and a collection of actual examples from excipient users in the pharmaceutical industry.

About Parenteral Drug Association (PDA)

The Parenteral Drug Association (PDA) is the leading global provider of science, technology, and regulatory information and education for the pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical community. Founded in 1946 as a nonprofit organization, PDA is committed to developing scientifically sound, practical technical information and resources to advance manufacturing science and regulation. Through the expertise of more than 10,000 members worldwide, PDA promotes the exchange of rapidly evolving information on science, technology, and regulations concerning high-quality pharmaceutical production to better serve patients.

About IPEC Federation

The IPEC Federation (IPEC) is a global organization that promotes quality in pharmaceutical excipients. The IPEC Federation represents five regional International Pharmaceutical Excipient Councils (IPECs) — IPEC-Americas, IPEC Europe, IPEC Japan, IPEC China, and IPEC India — and provides a unified voice to promote the best use of excipients in medicines as a means of improving patient treatment and safety. IPEC's objectives are to contribute to the development and harmonization of international excipient standards, the introduction of useful new excipients to the marketplace, and the development of good manufacturing and good distribution practices for excipients.

What is TR54-6?

PDA Technical Report No. 54-6 (2019) is a technical report jointly published by the Parenteral Drug Association (PDA) and the International Pharmaceutical Excipients Council (IPEC) Federation. It extends the PDA TR54 series and provides practical guidance specifically on quality risk management (QRM) for pharmaceutical excipients.

The project was formally launched in March 2018, when the two organizations signed a memorandum of understanding (MOU) to respond "with one voice" to the pharmaceutical industry's urgent need for guidance on assessing excipient GMP.

Composition of the IPEC Federation

The IPEC Federation consists of five regional excipient councils:

  • IPEC-Americas
  • IPEC Europe
  • IPEC Japan
  • IPEC China
  • IPEC India

Its objectives include promoting international harmonization of excipient standards, introducing new excipients, and establishing GMP and GDP guidelines for excipients.

Regulatory Drivers

This report was published in response to mandatory European regulatory requirements:

  • EU Falsified Medicines Directive (2011/62/EU): requires manufacturing authorization holders to verify that the excipients they use meet appropriate GMP standards.
  • EC Guidelines (2015/C 95/02): European Commission guidelines that require a formalized risk assessment of excipients.
  • PIC/S 2018: incorporates the same requirement into a globally applicable publication, broadening its reach.

TR54-6 responds directly to these regulatory requirements and helps industry implement excipient risk assessment in a systematic way.

Analogy: Sourcing Restaurant Ingredients

Excipient risk assessment is like ingredient management at a fine-dining restaurant. You need to know:

  • Where the ingredients come from (who is the original manufacturer of the excipient?)
  • Who has handled them (which intermediaries are in the supply chain?)
  • Whether they meet food safety standards (does the excipient's GMP level meet the quality requirements for medicinal products?)

In the same way, the manufacturing authorization holder is responsible for ensuring that every excipient meets appropriate quality standards from manufacture through to use, in order to protect patient safety.

Section 1.0 Introduction

1.0 Introduction

Excipients are integrated into the drug product formulation for various reasons: to aid the drug product formulation, to serve as functional or nonfunctional ingredients, or to enable the drug to be released from the drug product in a particular, desired manner. The rationale for their use extends beyond the point of medical use to include maintenance of the safety or function of the drug product, even during storage. While good manufacturing practices (GMP) regulations clearly outline what is required for active pharmaceutical ingredients, only a basic framework exists for ascertaining appropriate GMP for excipients through risk assessment.

In recognition of the criticality of excipients in the production of drug products, the European Commission (EC) Falsified Medicines Directive (2011/62/EU) revised Article 46(f) of Directive 2001/83 to require manufacturing authorization holders to verify that any excipients used in a product are made according to appropriate GMP standards. The Falsified Medicines Directive also committed the EC to publishing Guidelines on the formalised risk assessment for ascertaining the appropriate GMP for excipients of medicinal products for human use (EC Guidelines), which were published on March 19, 2015. In 2018, PIC/S incorporated the same provisions for formalized risk assessment into a publication of the same name, extending the provisions to have global applicability. In the U.S., the 2012 Food and Drug Administration Safety and Innovation Act redefined "current good manufacturing practices" to include "the implementation of oversight and controls over the manufacture of drugs to ensure quality, including managing the risk of and establishing the safety of raw materials [and] materials used in the manufacturing of drugs," in addition to the final drug product. This change also emphasizes the criticality of excipients.

Although the accountability for risk assessment rests entirely with the manufacturing authorization holder, all parties in the supply chain are required to support the process by providing relevant information (e.g., Excipient Information Packages) to the manufacturing authorization holder. The risk management process is applicable throughout the excipient's lifecycle, that is, from selection through termination of use, and remains the responsibility of the manufacturing authorization holder at each stage.

Over the course of an excipient's lifecycle, there will be a broad array of drivers for risk assessment and reassessment. Those drivers may include, among others, product deviations, market recalls, changes in excipient use, supply chain amendments, and adverse trends in quality. The manufacturing authorization holder is responsible for providing evidence that the risk management approach adopted is commensurate with the level of risk to ensure the safety, purity and other quality characteristics of the excipient in use.

To comply with the EC Guidelines and PIC/S publication, each excipient used in a drug product must be assessed for the risks that it poses to the product's quality, safety, and purity. From these risks, the manufacturing authorization holder must determine the quality standards required for the manufacture of the excipient. The manufacturing authorization holder should communicate these quality standards to the excipient supplier and seek the supplier's agreement. Any confirmed gaps in quality standards determined by the assessment must be appropriately mitigated, which may require the manufacturing authorization holder to take action on its own or through collaboration with the supplier. To ensure that risk assessments remain effective during the excipient's lifecycle, the manufacturing authorization holder must incorporate a periodic review into the excipient risk assessment process.

Commentary

Key Concept: Excipient

An excipient is any component of a medicinal product other than the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) and the packaging material. Depending on the source of the definition:

  • EC definition: any constituent of a medicinal product other than the active substance and the packaging material.
  • IPEC definition: a substance, other than the API, that has been appropriately evaluated for safety and is intentionally included in a drug delivery system.

Excipient functions include aiding formulation, acting as a functional or non-functional component, controlling drug release, and maintaining the safety and functionality of the medicinal product during storage.

Key Concept: Manufacturing Authorization Holder (MAH)

The MAH is the legal entity that holds the manufacturing authorization and is responsible for producing the medicinal product. Under the TR54-6 framework, the MAH bears full responsibility for excipient risk assessment, including:

  • Proactively assessing the risk each excipient poses to product quality, safety and purity.
  • Communicating the required quality standards to excipient suppliers.
  • Addressing any GMP gaps identified in the assessment.
  • Periodically reviewing the effectiveness of the risk assessment.

Although the parties in the supply chain must cooperate by providing information, final responsibility remains with the MAH and cannot be transferred.

Important Note: TR54-6 Does Not Replace Regulatory Requirements

The purpose of TR54-6 is to provide a practical guidance framework to help the MAH implement excipient risk assessment. It:

  • Does not replace or modify existing statutory and regulatory requirements.
  • Complements the EC Guidelines, PIC/S publications and the ICH Q9 QRM principles.
  • Provides a model risk assessment and practical examples for reference.

In other words, TR54-6 is an industry best-practice handbook that helps companies meet regulatory requirements, but the regulations themselves (the EC Guidelines, etc.) are the source of legal obligations.

Triggers for Excipient Risk Assessment

An excipient risk assessment should be initiated or repeated in the following situations:

  • Product deviations
  • Market recalls
  • Changes in excipient use
  • Supply chain amendments
  • Adverse trends in quality

The risk management process runs throughout the excipient lifecycle, from initial selection until the excipient is no longer used.

Interpreting Figure 1.0-1: Excipient Suitability Decision Process

Figure 1.0-1 shows an iterative risk management cycle built around the core question, "Is this excipient suitable for use in this medicinal product?" The process has three main stages:

  1. Risk identification: integrates QMS, manufacturing and supply chain information with the route of administration and excipient function to establish the basis for the assessment.
  2. Risk assessment: has two dimensions, the excipient supplier assessment (external) and the internal documentation assessment, and produces a low/medium/high risk score.
  3. Control and review: depending on the risk score, the risk is either accepted or risk reduction measures are implemented, and ongoing monitoring (KPIs, events, audits) keeps the process under dynamic management.
Section 1.1 Purpose

1.1 Purpose

This report is based on the general principles of quality risk management (QRM) as outlined in ICH Q9 Quality Risk Management. It has been developed to provide additional guidance on the excipient risk assessment process required by the EC Guidelines and incorporated into the PIC/S publication. In addition, TR 54-6 presents a model risk assessment and real-world examples from the pharmaceutical industry applicable to excipient use for all dosage forms to illustrate risk assessment as an excipient knowledge management solution. In general, the model presented in this report is based on the risk ranking and filtering method as referenced in ICH Q9. It also addresses pertinent factors such as end-to-end supply chain, control strategy, and benefits and challenges, among others. TR 54-6 will also serve as a practical guidance intended for use in conjunction with existing regulatory and industry standards; however, it does not replace or alter existing statutory and regulatory requirements for assessing excipients for use in medicinal drug products.

Despite the clarity and guidance offered by the EC Guidelines, risk assessors for manufacturing authorization holders still face some challenges in implementing and benchmarking the excipient risk assessment process for two main reasons:

  1. Diversity of quality systems: Quality systems applied during the manufacture of materials used as excipients are diverse and generally based on the material's intended use, which may not be primarily as a pharmaceutical excipient. Therefore, adjusting an excipient manufacturer's or supplier's quality systems to meet the pharmaceutical quality system outlined in the guidelines could prove challenging.
  2. Multiple dosage forms: An excipient may be used in different dosage forms by manufacturing authorization holders. One manufacturing authorization holder may use a single excipient in both tablets and injectables, which may result in different quality requirements applying to a single excipient from the same supplier. This also may lead to conflicting requirements for GMP.

Commentary

Purpose of TR54-6

TR54-6 is built on the general principles of quality risk management (QRM), specifically ICH Q9. Its core purposes are to:

  • Provide additional guidance on the excipient risk assessment process required by the EC Guidelines.
  • Present a model risk assessment that is applicable to all dosage forms.
  • Demonstrate, through real-world examples (Annex I, Sections 10.1-10.6), how risk assessment can be used as a knowledge management tool.
  • Use the ICH Q9 risk ranking and filtering method as the basis for the assessment.

Two Practical Challenges

Challenge 1: Diversity of quality systems

Many excipients are not made specifically for pharmaceutical use but for industries such as food, cosmetics or household products. These manufacturers' quality systems are not designed with pharmaceutical GMP as the first priority, which makes it difficult for the MAH to ask them to upgrade to a pharmaceutical quality system.

Challenge 2: Conflicting GMP requirements from multiple dosage forms

The same excipient may be used in both tablets and injectables. GMP requirements for injectables (parenteral products) are far higher than for oral solid dosage forms, yet both may be purchased from the same supplier. The MAH may therefore hold conflicting internal GMP requirements for the same excipient, which must be managed carefully.

Analogy: A Food Plant Becoming a Pharmaceutical Supplier

Imagine a plant that mainly produces edible starch (under food GMP), and a pharmaceutical company wants to buy that starch as a tablet excipient. Food GMP and pharmaceutical GMP differ significantly, and this is exactly the "diversity of quality systems" challenge described in TR54-6. Through risk assessment, the MAH must judge whether the supplier's existing quality level is sufficient or whether improvements need to be requested.

Section 1.2 Scope

1.2 Scope

The information in TR 54-6 applies to all excipients used in drug products for human use at all stages of the product lifecycle. It assumes the excipient will be used for the drug product in the commercial supply phase; however, the manufacturing authorization holder may choose to apply this practical guidance in the clinical phase as well. That said, this technical report refrains from providing guidance to the manufacturing authorization holder regarding at which clinical phase it should be applied.

Commentary

Understanding the Scope

The scope of TR54-6 has the following characteristics:

  • Who it applies to: all excipients used in human medicinal products (veterinary medicines are not included).
  • Lifecycle: covers all stages of the product lifecycle.
  • Main assumption: use in the commercial supply phase is assumed by default.
  • Clinical phase: the MAH may optionally apply it during clinical trials, but TR54-6 does not specify from which clinical trial phase it should start.

Practical Implications of the Scope

"All excipients" means that, regardless of whether an excipient has a complex pharmacological function, the MAH must perform a risk assessment on it as long as it is included in the product formulation. This covers common excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) and lactose, as well as more complex ones such as polymeric excipients with a controlled-release function.

TR54-6 does not provide direct guidance for veterinary medicinal products, but its principles can be applied by analogy.

2.1 Abbreviations

Commentary

Why Precise Definitions Matter

In a GMP environment, the precision of terminology directly affects the allocation of responsibility and legal obligations. In the excipient supply chain, for example, the following four roles are defined very differently and carry very different GMP responsibilities:

  • Original Manufacturer: the entity that manufactures the material up to the stage at which it can be designated as an excipient; carries the highest GMP responsibility.
  • Distributor: takes legal ownership of the excipient and is responsible for distribution; must comply with GDP requirements.
  • Repackager: repackages the excipient and may change the labeling, introducing additional quality risk.
  • Broker: a pure intermediary that does no physical handling (no storage, transport or repackaging) and merely facilitates the sale and purchase.

Only by distinguishing these roles correctly can the risk level of the supply chain be assessed correctly.

Understanding the Abbreviations: Mapping to Regulatory Bodies

The abbreviations list in Section 2.1 is short, but each abbreviation points to an important regulatory or technical concept:

  • EC (European Commission): the body that issued the EC Guidelines (2015/C 95/02), one of the main regulatory bases for this report.
  • CMO (Contract Manufacturing Organization): plays an important role in complex supply chains (see Section 4.1.6).
  • CQA (Critical Quality Attributes): the core measure in assessing excipient suitability; the source definition is ICH Q8 (R2).
  • GMP (Good Manufacturing Practices): the central subject of the entire TR54-6.
  • QMS (Quality Management System): the maturity of a supplier's QMS needs to be reviewed during supplier assessment.
  • QRM (Quality Risk Management): the overall methodological framework of TR54-6, based on ICH Q9.

What EXCiPACT Certification Means

EXCiPACT is the world's first international certification scheme for pharmaceutical excipient manufacturers and distributors. A supplier holding EXCiPACT certification has passed an independent third-party audit and meets pharmaceutical-grade GMP/GDP requirements.

In the TR54-6 risk assessment framework, whether a supplier holds EXCiPACT certification is an important indicator of its quality level and generally helps lower the risk score.

The Relationship between QTPP and CQAs

The Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP) is a prospective summary of the quality characteristics of the ideal medicinal product and defines "what quality standard the final product should meet." The critical quality attributes (CQAs) are derived from the QTPP and reflect which characteristics are essential to the safety and efficacy of the product.

In excipient risk assessment, CQAs help the MAH judge how strongly an excipient affects the CQAs of the medicinal product, and thereby determine its risk level. For example, an excipient used in an injectable that affects the product's osmolality (a CQA) carries a higher risk level than an excipient used only as a filler in an oral product.

Practical Application: CDMO Scenario Analysis

Context: CDMO Excipient Sourcing

In modern pharmaceutical manufacturing, Contract Development and Manufacturing Organizations (CDMOs) frequently source excipients from multiple global suppliers simultaneously. For example, a CDMO manufacturing both oral solid dosage forms (tablets) and parenteral (injectable) products for multiple innovator clients may use the same excipient — such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) — sourced from three different suppliers in Asia, Europe, and the Americas.

Each supplier has a different supply chain configuration:

  • Supplier A: Direct supply from original manufacturer
  • Supplier B: Via a distributor who repackages into smaller quantities
  • Supplier C: Via a broker, with the original manufacturer unknown

TR 54-6 provides the framework for this CDMO to conduct a formalized risk assessment across all three supply chains, tailored to each dosage form's risk profile.

Commentary

CDMO Scenario: Practical Requirements of TR54-6

When a contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO) manufactures medicinal products in different dosage forms for multiple clients and purchases excipients from many suppliers worldwide, TR54-6 requires the CDMO to consider the following key factors:

1. Assess risk separately by dosage form

The same HPMC excipient has very different CQA requirements when used in an oral tablet (low risk) and in an injectable (high risk). The CDMO must perform an independent assessment for each route of administration and must not generalize.

2. Assess risk by supply chain structure

  • Supplier A (direct supply): shortest supply chain, highest traceability, lowest risk.
  • Supplier B (distributor plus repackager): repackaging introduces contamination and labeling error risks, so the repackager's GMP controls need to be audited.
  • Supplier C (broker, original manufacturer unknown): the least transparent, with the poorest traceability and the highest risk. Under TR54-6, the CDMO should be especially cautious in this case and may need to require the Broker to disclose the original manufacturer.

3. The particular position of the MAH's responsibility

In a CDMO scenario, the legal Manufacturing Authorization Holder is usually the sponsor (the innovator pharmaceutical company), not the CDMO itself. The CDMO must still cooperate by providing the relevant information (such as the excipient information package) and ensure that its supply chain meets the requirements of the client MAH's quality agreement.

4. Consistency of the global risk assessment

TR54-6 recommends a globally consistent risk assessment approach, using the "worst case" score to represent all dosage forms, so that consistent quality requirements are communicated to the excipient manufacturer and different departments within a company do not place conflicting requirements on the same supplier.

3.0 Overall Strategy – A Holistic View

Overall Strategy: A Holistic View

3.0 Overall Strategy – A Holistic View

TR 54-6 provides guidance on quality risk management principles that can be used to assess the risks to the quality, safety, and function of an excipient in a drug product. The proposed generic model allows the user to answer the risk question "Is the excipient fit for use in the drug product?" through the use of a formalized risk assessment.

The model, as described in Section 6.0 and illustrated in Figure 6.0-1, describes the steps involved in identifying whether an excipient is fit for use, considering:

  • An assessment of the excipient's use and function for the excipient user
  • An assessment of the entire Supply Chain

In the model, the potential hazards from the excipient itself, as well as the supply chain, are ranked and evaluated using a risk ranking and filtering approach to evaluate the overall risk of harm to the drug product and, through that, to patient safety.

Based on the results of the risk assessment, a pharmaceutical manufacturer can determine what level of GMP will be required. The guides and standards most widely accepted for excipient GMPs include:

  • EXCiPACT: International Pharmaceutical Excipients Certification Standards for Pharmaceutical Excipient Suppliers
  • NSF/IPEC/ANSI 363: Good Manufacturing Practices for Pharmaceutical Excipients
  • IPEC/PQG: The Joint Good Manufacturing Practices Guide for Pharmaceutical Excipients
  • USP: <1078> GMP for Bulk Pharmaceutical Excipients and <1059> Excipient Performance
  • Japan: Self-imposed Standards of GMP for Pharmaceutical Excipients

Should the risk assessment uncover an issue requiring additional action, a control strategy should be put in place and the affected parties should be notified. Any actions recommended as a result of the risk assessment must be discussed and agreed upon by the excipient manufacturer and the drug product manufacturer or Manufacturing Authorization Holder, and the results documented in a quality agreement or other document.

A global risk assessment of all excipients used by the drug product manufacturer will ensure a standardized assessment across all excipients and drug products and provide a holistic view of the supply chain. This enables the excipient user to provide the excipient manufacturer, with one voice, a single (worst-case) risk assessment — preferable to multiple users within the same company communicating different results to the excipient manufacturer.

3.1 Risk Areas Beyond Quality

While the focus of the excipient risk assessment is quality, a company also should consider other aspects that can impact the excipient and, with this, product availability for the patient. These include:

  • Excipient availability
  • Business continuity risk
  • Environmental, socio-economic, health, safety, and geographic risks
  • General business data

While consideration of these risks is useful as part of an overall strategy, it is out of scope of this document and is not discussed further within TR 54-6.

Section 3 Commentary

Key Concept: The "Holistic View" of Excipient Risk Assessment

The overall TR54-6 strategy requires the MAH (marketing authorization holder) to assess excipient risk with a "holistic view," not just quality issues. This holistic view covers four dimensions:

  • Quality: GMP compliance, manufacturing standards, impurity control
  • Security of supply (Supply Continuity): excipient availability, business continuity risk, alternative suppliers
  • Regulatory compliance: compliance with the EC Guidelines (2015), PIC/S requirements and national pharmacopoeial requirements
  • ESG factors: Environmental, Socio-economic, Geographic and Health & Safety risks

Although the formal scope of TR54-6 covers only quality risk, Section 3.1 explicitly reminds companies not to overlook the other risk dimensions, because these risks equally affect whether patients can obtain treatment in a timely manner.

Key Point: Global Consistent Assessment vs. Decentralized Assessment

TR54-6 recommends a "global consistent risk assessment" that communicates requirements to the excipient manufacturer with "one voice." The benefits of this strategy are:

  • It avoids different departments or sites of the same company reaching contradictory risk assessment conclusions for the same excipient
  • It ensures a consistent assessment basis for all medicinal products that use the same excipient
  • It allows a knowledge repository to be built and shared by sites worldwide

In practice, each local site (local owner) can perform its own assessment, but the results are consolidated in a central repository, with the local owner responsible for updating and reviewing the data.

Basis for Selecting the GMP Standard

The conclusion of the quality risk assessment directly determines the GMP level the excipient manufacturer must meet. TR54-6 lists five industry-recognized standards, of which the most widely used is the EXCiPACT certification scheme, a global certification program for excipient suppliers managed by a non-profit organization and applicable to both manufacturers and distributors. The higher the risk of the excipient (such as excipients for injectables), the stricter the GMP requirements it generally needs to meet.

4.0 Supply Chain — End to End

Supply Chain: The Complete End-to-End Structure

4.0 Supply Chain — End to End

Section 4.0 provides examples of supply chains, helps identify the various actors in the supply chain, and describes their typical roles. Supply chains have become more and more complex over the years. In the past, a manufacturer of pharmaceutical excipients often supplied directly to a drug product manufacturer. Today, the Distributor, Broker, and CMO each play roles in the supply chain, each contributing to the risks to quality in one way or another.

Many materials used as excipients are not manufactured specifically for pharmaceutical use but are intended for other applications such as food, cosmetics, or home care. Intermediaries often become involved, procuring larger quantities from the original manufacturer and repackaging into quantities more suitable for pharmaceutical use. The complexity is magnified for excipients that move internationally.

In both international and domestic supply chains, the point at which legal ownership transfers from one organization to another is a critical juncture. In complex supply chains, legal ownership may transfer several times — for example: original manufacturer → distributor → repackager → end user.

4.1 Types of Supply Chains and Risks

4.1.1 Direct Supply from Manufacturer

Even when an excipient is supplied directly by a manufacturer to an end user, intermediaries may be involved. Logistics operations may be subcontracted, and one or more warehousing companies may be involved. Because all intermediaries are subcontracted by either the original manufacturer or the end user, they should have good information about how to safely transport the excipient. The supply chain illustrated in Figure 4.1.1-1 is one of the lowest-risk arrangements due to the small number of intermediaries and the degree of control that can be applied.

The distributor may have an agreement with the original manufacturer to market the excipient under the distributor's tradename. 4.1.6 Manufacturer and Contract Manufacturing Organizations (CMO)

When one or more CMOs are involved in the supply chain, it is critically important to clearly identify and understand which party gathers information and who owns the risk assessment.

The Manufacturing Authorization Holder is ultimately responsible for the risk assessment. Depending on the quality agreement and relationship between the CMO and manufacturing authorization holder, there are generally three options:

  • The CMO carries out the entire risk assessment, collecting information about the use and function of the excipient in the final drug product — which is then reviewed and approved by the manufacturing authorization holder.
  • The risk assessment is carried out by the manufacturing authorization holder, incorporating information from the CMO.
  • The risk assessment is carried out by the manufacturing authorization holder based on the risk assessment delivered by the CMO.

In any case, the roles and responsibilities for the CMO and manufacturing authorization holder must be clearly defined, including what actions will be taken if the risk assessments completed by the CMO and manufacturing authorization holder result in different outcomes.

Section 4 Commentary

Analogy: Food Delivery Supply Chains

The six types of excipient supply chain can be compared to the different routes food takes from farm to table:

  • 4.1.1 Direct supply: like produce delivered from the farm straight to your home (farm → logistics → consumer). The fewest intermediate steps and the most traceable quality
  • 4.1.2 Via a distributor: farm → large supermarket → consumer. The supermarket takes ownership and then resells; there is a degree of quality control, but you need to confirm that the supermarket (Distributor) has a quality agreement with the farm
  • 4.1.3 Via a repackager: farm → supermarket → repacking company (small packs) → consumer. Every repacking step is a point of contamination or cross-mixing risk, so repacking GMP must be rigorously verified
  • 4.1.4 Via a broker (Broker): the broker brokers between the farm and the supermarket and never touches the goods. If the broker is not transparent, the actual origin of the goods cannot be confirmed and the risk is hard to quantify
  • 4.1.5 International export: from an overseas farm, passing through multiple ports, warehouses and shipping companies. The people at each step may change and the route of each batch may differ, so the risk is the hardest to control
  • 4.1.6 CMO involvement: farm (excipient manufacturer) → contract processing plant (CMO) → pharmaceutical company. It must be clear who is responsible for the excipient risk assessment and who bears final responsibility for the quality of the finished medicinal product (always the MAH)

Key Warning: The Longer the Supply Chain, the Higher the Risk

The core message of TR54-6 is that every additional intermediate step in the supply chain adds the following risks:

  • Adulteration: intermediaries may mix in inferior material
  • Contamination: every repacking or repackaging step is a potential contamination point
  • Documentation gaps: the original manufacturer's CoA and EIP may not be passed on completely to the end user
  • Unclear transfer of ownership: legal responsibility is unclear, making it hard to assign accountability when problems occur

Therefore, the more complex the supply chain, the more resources the MAH needs to invest in audits, in quality agreements, and in establishing stricter incoming inspection controls.

Key Differences between a Broker and a Distributor

Distributor: takes "legal ownership" of the excipient and is responsible for physical storage and transport, and sometimes also for repacking or relabeling.

Broker: "does not touch the goods"; it merely brings buyers and sellers together and does not actually hold or transport the excipient. The risk comes mainly from information transparency: if the Broker is unwilling to disclose the identity of the original manufacturer, the MAH can hardly assess the supply chain risk.

TR54-6 notes that if the Broker is fully transparent and the supply comes from the original manufacturer, its risk can be comparable to direct supply (4.1.1); if it is not transparent, risk assessment is extremely difficult.

Ownership of Risk Assessment in a CMO Scenario

When a CMO is involved, TR54-6 emphasizes that regardless of who performs the assessment, the MAH is always the party ultimately responsible. The three models differ in the division of work, not in the division of responsibility; responsibility always remains with the MAH.

In practice, if the CMO performs the assessment, the MAH must still review and approve it and cannot shift responsibility by saying that "it was handed over to the CMO." The Quality Agreement must clearly specify how to proceed if the CMO's assessment result differs from the MAH's conclusion.

6.0 A Model for Quality Risk Management for Excipients

The TR 54-6 Task Force developed this QRM model by surveying industry practices, analyzing real-world company models shared by manufacturing authorization holders, and combining best practices. The model applies a risk ranking and filtering approach to answer the central risk question:

"Is the excipient fit for use in the drug product?"

Risk ranking and filtering involves breaking this question into as many components as needed to capture all relevant risk factors. The two main factors in the model are:

  • Intrinsic excipient risks — factors inherent to the excipient itself and its use in the drug product
  • Supply chain risks — factors related to the manufacturer, distributor, and transport of the excipient

These two factors are combined into a single relative risk score. Filters in the form of weighting factors or cut-offs support risk acceptance or mitigation decisions.

Key Concepts Explained

How the ICH Q9 QRM Framework Applies to Excipients

ICH Q9 defines the general principles of quality risk management. TR54-6 applies this framework specifically to excipient assessment, resulting in the following cyclical stages:

► Initiate — confirm which excipients need assessment and form the assessment team
↓
► Risk Identification (Risk ID) — identify intrinsic risk factors and supply chain risk factors
↓
► Risk Analysis — calculate the Excipient Risk Priority Number (ERPN) and the Supply Chain Risk Priority Number (SCRPN)
↓
► Risk Evaluation — combine the two scores in a matrix to obtain the overall risk
↓
► Risk Control — if the risk is unacceptable, modify the controls and re-score
↓
► Risk Review — monitor continuously and reassess when a triggering event occurs

This model applies across the whole excipient lifecycle, from selection to discontinuation, and ultimate responsibility rests with the Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH).

Key Point: Why Are Two Risk Pathways Needed?

What makes the TR54-6 model distinctive is that it considers both the excipient itself (intrinsic factors) and the supply chain (external factors). An excipient with high intrinsic risk (for example, polysorbate 80 used in an injectable) may carry an acceptable overall risk if the supply chain is tightly controlled; conversely, even an excipient with low intrinsic risk may carry a high overall risk if the supply chain is opaque.

The scores from the two pathways finally intersect in a risk matrix, which determines whether additional action is required.

6.1

Initiate Risk Management Activities and Identify Assessment Team

Who, when, and what triggers the excipient risk assessment process

6.1 Initiate Risk Management Activities

The risk assessment process begins with the identification of a need to qualify an excipient or to review an existing excipient risk assessment. Triggers include:

  • New product introduction
  • Obtaining a second source for an existing excipient
  • Notification from supplier of major changes
  • Other drivers discussed in Section 7.1 (ongoing review triggers)

The Assessment Team

A successful excipient risk assessment requires a holistic approach encompassing knowledge of the excipient's excipient function in the pharmaceutical dosage form, as well as knowledge of the manufacturer and supply chain. The team should include individuals from various functional areas. The risk assessment process may include participants with defined roles such as:

  • QRM facilitator — knowledgeable about methodology and tools
  • QRM experts — area or function leads for QRM or decision-makers
  • Quality Assurance — focused on excipient use and function
  • Quality — focused on supply chain oversight (excipient manufacturer, supplier, transport)
  • Qualified/responsible person
  • Material sciences, Quality control
  • Regulatory affairs, Technical/operations
  • Sterility assurance (critical for injectables and sterile products)
  • Manufacturer or supplier auditing
  • Procurement
  • R&D, Engineering
  • Nonclinical statistics, Legal, Manufacturing
  • Medical/clinical, Safety and pharmacology/toxicology

Having a multidisciplinary team working collaboratively to gather and review data is critical to ensuring that all risks are considered holistically. The excipient risk management process should be built into the company's existing processes and QTPP-based quality management system (QMS).

Documentation Required

The output of the initiation phase includes:

  • Identification and documentation of the assessment team with defined roles
  • A clear statement of the risk question to be answered
  • Documentation of the trigger that initiated the assessment
  • Scope of the assessment (which drug products or drug product families)
  • Timeline and resource plan for completing the assessment

Commentary

6.1 — The Logic Behind the Assessment Team Composition

The cross-functional assessment team required by TR54-6 reflects the multifaceted nature of excipient risk:

  • Quality Assurance (QA) focuses on the function of the excipient in the drug product and how it is used
  • Supply Chain Quality focuses on oversight of manufacturers, suppliers, and transportation
  • Sterility Assurance is especially critical for injectable or inhalation products
  • Regulatory Affairs ensures the submission documents are consistent with the excipient specifications
  • Procurement provides the commercial relationship and history with the supplier

In practice, not every assessment needs every function to take part; the scope of the assessment and the risk level determine who is actually needed.

Analogy: A Restaurant's Ingredient Safety Review Team

Imagine a high-end restaurant deciding to introduce a new ingredient (for example, imported truffles). It needs to form an assessment team:

  • The head chef (= QA) — evaluates the ingredient's function in the dishes
  • The purchasing manager (= Procurement) — evaluates supplier credentials and supply stability
  • The food safety consultant (= Sterility/Quality) — evaluates the risk of microbial and foreign-matter contamination
  • The legal/regulatory advisor (= Regulatory) — confirms that import standards are met

The logic of an excipient risk assessment is the same; the difference is that the regulatory requirements are stricter and the consequences more serious.

Key Point: Triggers for Initiation

An excipient risk assessment should be initiated or reassessed immediately in the following situations:

  • A new drug product is launched (including a new dosage form or new indication)
  • A second excipient supplier is introduced
  • An existing supplier notifies a major change (process, equipment, site, etc.)
  • Excipient quality problems or adverse event reports appear in the market
  • The supply chain structure changes (for example, a distributor is added)
6.2

Identify Intrinsic Risk Factors

Factors inherent to the excipient — chemical nature, origin, contamination potential, prior knowledge

6.2 Identify Intrinsic Risk Factors

In identifying the excipient's Intrinsic Risk Factors, numerous factors should be considered. The primary source is input from product and process development.

The QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile) of the drug product(s) in which the excipient is to be used provides requirements for the drug product and forms the basis of information about the excipient's use and function. During drug product design and development, scientific studies are conducted and information is collected about how the excipient may interact with other components of the product.

Excipient Attributes to Consider

Examples of parameters that can be considered as excipient attributes include:

  • Characteristics of excipient needed for use in the drug product
  • Manufacturer's excipient specifications
  • Factors learned from the manufacture of the excipient
  • Existence of multiple and/or global manufacturers
  • Lot-to-lot variability

EC Guidelines Risk Factors (March 19, 2015)

The EC Guidelines specify the following as key intrinsic risk factors that must be assessed:

  • TSE/BSE — Transmissible spongiform encephalopathy risk (especially animal-origin excipients)
  • Viral contamination — Potential for viral contamination from biological sources
  • Microbiological / endotoxin / pyrogen contamination — Risk of microbial growth or endotoxin carry-over
  • Process impurities — Residual solvents, catalysts, and other process-generated impurities carried over
  • Raw material impurities — Aflatoxins, pesticides from plant-origin excipients
  • Sterility assurance — For excipients claimed to be sterile
  • Cross-contamination — Impurities from other processes in shared facilities (absence of dedicated equipment)
  • Environmental control and storage/transport conditions — Including cold chain management if appropriate
  • Excipient stability — Degradation risk over time or under storage conditions
  • Packaging integrity — Evidence supporting container closure integrity

Commentary

Intrinsic Risk vs. Supply Chain Risk: The Core Difference

Intrinsic risk is the risk that comes from the excipient itself; these risks exist regardless of who makes it or where it is bought. For example:

  • Water for Injection (WFI) inherently requires strict sterility control
  • Porcine gelatin inherently carries a porcine-source TSE risk (and also matters for patient groups with certain religious or dietary restrictions)

Supply chain risk arises from the supplier's management capability, integrity, and the complexity of supply, and it can be reduced by choosing a better supplier.

Intrinsic risk cannot be eliminated; it can only be managed through CQA control and specification setting.

The Role of the QTPP and CQAs in Identifying Intrinsic Risk

The QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile) defines "what quality the drug product should achieve", while the CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes) describe "which attributes matter most to quality".

These two documents are the starting point for identifying intrinsic risk: the QTPP tells the assessment team what the excipient needs to do (its function), and the CQAs tell them which excipient attributes, if out of control, would affect patient safety.

Analogy: The Intrinsic Risk Logic of Food Allergens

The "intrinsic risk" of an excipient is similar to allergens in food: the risk originates from the characteristics of the substance itself, not from external conditions:

The origin of an excipient (animal, plant, mineral, synthetic) determines its natural level of intrinsic risk. Animal-derived excipients (gelatin, lactose) inherently carry TSE/BSE risk; plant-derived ones may have pesticide or aflatoxin residues; for synthetic ones the main risk is process residues.

6.3.1

6.3.1 Impact of Excipient on Each Type of Drug Product

Correlating excipient attributes to CQAs by route of administration and dosage form

Excipient Impact Assessment — Overview

After identification of intrinsic risk factors (Section 6.2), excipient attributes are assessed for their correlation to the CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes) of the drug product in which the excipient is to be used. The rationale of any impact must be explained. This step is taken for each drug product or drug product family, and results can then be consolidated across products.

Initially, all potential risk factors (hazards) are identified and documented as excipient attributes. Then, CQAs are correlated to the pharmaceutical dosage form using a three-symbol notation:

  • +   Known Impact (documented effect on CQA)
  • –   No Impact (no indication of impact on CQA)
  • ?   Impact cannot be ruled out; more information may be needed

A "Compliance" column is also included near the CQAs so that compliance with pharmacopeial specifications can be ensured. This column allows assessors to force excipient attributes marked for compliance (e.g., a compendial specification the company will claim adherence to) further into the assessment and specification, regardless of actual CQA impact.

Handling of "?" Attributes

Only excipient attributes with a correlation marked "+" are brought forward as needed into the specification for further analysis. Attributes marked "?" should be further investigated to obtain data necessary to determine whether there is potential impact. If further data cannot be gathered, "?" will be treated as "+", taking a worst-case approach.

From Impact Assessment to Specification

Excipient attributes and impact assessments are used to carry out a qualitative failure analysis describing failure options and the effect of failure (consequences). From the failure analysis, the excipient user gains understanding of allowed variation in attributes, and specification limits for each material attribute are proposed to mitigate risk. In this way, the excipient impact assessment is used to justify the excipient specification.

An impact assessment is conducted for each drug product in which the excipient is used:

  • Retrospective: The impact assessment leads to evaluation of the existing specification
  • Prospective: The assessment provides input to one or more new specifications consistent with drug product needs

Commentary

Key Point: Why Are Injectables and Inhalation Products the Highest Risk?

The human body has three main defensive barriers:

  • Skin — topical products meet this barrier, which most substances find hard to penetrate
  • Gastrointestinal mucosa + hepatic first-pass metabolism — harmful substances in oral products are metabolized and degraded by the liver before reaching the systemic circulation
  • Blood-brain barrier — protects the central nervous system

Parenteral (injectable) products bypass both the skin and the gastrointestinal tract, so impurities or endotoxin enter the bloodstream or tissues directly. This is why excipient risk for injectables is rated at the highest level (RFE3 = 5 points).

Inhalation products bypass the gastrointestinal tract, but the enormous surface area of the lungs (about 70 square meters) provides an efficient absorption route, so they are rated as medium risk.

The Three-Symbol Logic of Impact Assessment

TR54-6 uses the three symbols "+", "–", and "?" to express the relationship between an excipient attribute and a CQA:

  • + (known impact): the attribute must be included in the excipient specification controls
  • – (no impact): the attribute does not need to be in the specification and can be removed from the assessment
  • ? (impact cannot be ruled out): further investigation is required; if no data can be obtained, treat it as the worst case (equivalent to "+")

This approach ensures that specifications are set on a scientific basis rather than by convention alone, which is exactly the spirit of ICH Q8 Quality by Design (QbD).

Analogy: The Risk-Level Logic of Airport Security

The logic of impact assessment is similar to airport security adjusting the intensity of inspection according to passenger category:

  • Injectables = VIP diplomatic parcels → strictest scrutiny, no doubt left unresolved
  • Inhalation products = business-class luggage → detailed scrutiny, with focus on particulates
  • Topical/oral products = ordinary luggage → standard scrutiny, protected by barriers

The same excipient (for example, polyethylene glycol, PEG) carries lower risk in an oral tablet, but requires very strict impurity control when used in an injectable.

Impact Assessment → Excipient Specification: The Logic Chain

Figure 6.3.1-1 (page 14 of the original document) shows how the assessment output becomes the specification input:

  1. List all excipient attributes
  2. Mark +/–/? against the CQAs of each dosage form
  3. "+" attributes → enter the process of setting specification limits
  4. Failure analysis → determine the allowable range of attribute variation
  5. Set the specification limits → form the excipient specification

This logic chain ensures that every specification limit has a scientific rationale rather than being set arbitrarily.

6.4 Supply Chain Risk Analysis

A Supply Chain Risk Score (SCRPN) is created using a risk ranking system similar to the Excipient Risk Profile (ERPN) described in Section 6.3.2. The ranking should draw on input from the supplier, including knowledge from questionnaires, previous experience, Audits, site visits, and media coverage.

Minimum Risk Factors for Supply Chain (RFS)

At a minimum, the following six risk factors must be considered when building the SCRPN:

  • RFS1 — Supply Chain Complexity: Supplier–broker–manufacturer relationships; opportunity for fraud; presence of repackaging or manipulation; regional security context.
  • RFS2 — Prior Knowledge of the Manufacturer: Track record, prior experience, delivery reliability, evidence of excipient variability, history of complaints.
  • RFS3 — Organizational Volatility: Stability of management; turnover in key quality and technical positions.
  • RFS4 — Excipient Manufacturer QMS Performance History: Regulatory observations, Audit responses, CAPA timeliness, consent decrees, warning letters.
  • RFS5 — Packaging Suitability: Scientific justification; tamper-evident seals; ability to protect against moisture, heat, fraud.
  • RFS6 — QMS Standards and Certification: Whether the supplier holds relevant certifications (e.g., ISO 9001, EXCiPACT), documentation systems, materials management, supplier qualification programs.
  • RFSx — Additional factors as needed for the specific excipient and its use in the final drug product.

Each RFS selection yields a value of 1 (Low), 3 (Medium), or 5 (High). The SCRPN is calculated as the arithmetic mean across all identified risk factors:

SCRPN Formula

SCRPN = (RF_S1 + RF_S2 + RF_S3 + RF_S4 + RF_S5 + RF_S6) / 6

Result: a number between 1 (Low) and 5 (High)
Note: scale is not linear — do not treat as such.

Users may adjust the calculation to reflect additional risk factors or to weight individual risk factors based on the company's analysis and risk tolerance (footnote 3 in TR54-6).

Supply Chain Risk Analysis — Commentary

Core Concept: What Makes a Supplier "High Risk"?

The design logic of the SCRPN is this: the less transparent the supply chain, the poorer the past performance, and the weaker the QMS, the higher the probability of counterfeiting or loss of quality control. Specifically, the following characteristics indicate high risk:

  • Multiple layers of intermediaries (brokers): Every additional intermediary step is another opportunity for repackaging or adulteration. Buying directly from the manufacturer is the safest option (RFS1 = 1).
  • No audit record: Nothing is known about the manufacturer, or there have been significant complaints that remain unresolved (RFS2 = 5).
  • Unstable organization and staffing: High turnover in key positions means loss of SOP knowledge and a weak quality culture (RFS3 = 5).
  • Active investigation or warning letter: Formal regulatory action (consent decree, warning letter) directly indicates QMS failure (RFS4 = 5).
  • No tamper-evident packaging: Pharmaceutical excipients could be substituted or contaminated in transit without anyone noticing (RFS5 = 5).
  • No certification: Holds no ISO, EXCiPACT, or equivalent certification (RFS6 = 3–5).

Conversely, a supplier that sells directly from a manufacturer in Europe or the US, holds EXCiPACT certification, and completes CAPAs on time will have an SCRPN close to 1 (low risk).

The Relationship Between GDP Compliance and Supply Chain Risk

GDP (Good Distribution Practice) requires that drugs and excipients maintain their quality during transportation. If a supplier complies with GDP, the risk scores for RFS5 (packaging suitability) and RFS1 (supply chain complexity) can both be lowered.

TR54-6 explicitly includes GDP compliance in the considerations for supply chain risk assessment, which is especially important for excipients that require temperature control or moisture protection.

Analogy: Supply Chain Risk Is Like a Food Ingredient's "Origin Record"

Imagine ordering a meal at a restaurant where the chef buys directly from a certified farm (RFS1 = low) and has known the farm owner for 20 years without problems (RFS2 = low). This is like buying excipients directly from the original manufacturer with EXCiPACT certification.

Conversely, if the ingredients pass through five intermediaries, the farm owner changes every year, and the packaging has no tamper-evident label, would you worry about food safety? That is the intuitive meaning of a high SCRPN.

The consequences are more serious for pharmaceutical excipients: once used in an intravenous (IV) solution, any contamination could directly endanger life.

6.5 Risk Evaluation

The excipient risk score (ERPN) and the supply chain risk score (SCRPN) are evaluated together using a Risk Matrix approach. The excipient risk score appears on one axis; the supply chain risk score on the other. The combination creates an overall risk score that answers the central QRM question: "Is the excipient fit for use?"

Both ERPN and SCRPN range between 1 (Low) and 5 (High). The scale is not linear and should not be treated as such. Because risk acceptability varies significantly from company to company, the matrix and its cut-offs should be tailored to the individual company's risk tolerance.

6.5.1 Risk Matrix — Option 1: Calculating the Combined Risk Score (OverallRPN)

In Option 1, the combined risk score is calculated by multiplying ERPN by SCRPN:

OverallRPN Formula (Option 1)

OverallRPN = ERPN × SCRPN

ERPN range:  1 (Low) to 5 (High)
SCRPN range: 1 (Low) to 5 (High)
OverallRPN range: 1 (minimum) to 25 (maximum)

Option 2 allows additional customization. The MAH may weight the ERPN and SCRPN differently based on its internal evaluation. For instance, if excipient-related risk deserves greater emphasis, it can be given double the weight of the supply chain risk score.

The formula uses user-assigned weights (wt) for each score; n is the sum of all weights in the numerator:

Final Risk Score = [wt(ERPN) + wt(SCRPN)] / n

Where n = sum of all weights applied.

Example: wt(ERPN) = 2, wt(SCRPN) = 1 → n = 3
Final Risk Score = [(2 × ERPN) + (1 × SCRPN)] / 3

The MAH may also calculate individual scores for each role in the supply chain — for example, a separate risk score for the original manufacturer and another for each broker — and use these individual mean risk scores instead of a single SCRPN.

Risk Evaluation Methods — Commentary

Comparison of the Two Risk Calculation Methods

Option 1 (OverallRPN):
  OverallRPN = ERPN × SCRPN
  Result range: 1–25
  Decision thresholds:
    > 10  → Unacceptable (mitigation required)
    4–10  → Marginally acceptable (consider mitigation)
    < 4   → Acceptable (no additional action)

Option 2 (Final Risk Score):
  Final Risk Score =
    [wt(ERPN) + wt(SCRPN)] / n
  Result range: 1–5 (weighted average)
  Decision thresholds: defined by the company according to its risk tolerance

When to Use Option 1 and When to Use Option 2

Option 1 (multiplicative/OverallRPN) is the simpler and more conservative method. Multiplication has an "amplifying effect": when both the ERPN and the SCRPN are high, the OverallRPN rises sharply (for example, ERPN = 4 and SCRPN = 4 → OverallRPN = 16, far above the threshold of 10). This lets Option 1 effectively "catch" truly dangerous combinations where high excipient risk and high supply chain risk occur together.

Option 2 (weighted average/Final Risk Score) offers greater flexibility for customization. If a company considers the intrinsic risk of the excipient itself much more important than supply chain complexity (for example, a highly toxic excipient used in an injectable), it can give the ERPN a higher weight so that the score better reflects actual judgment.

Key difference: Option 1 results lie between 1 and 25, while Option 2 results lie between 1 and 5 (still an average after weighting). The thresholds of the two differ and must not be mixed.

Why Does Multiplication "Amplify" Risk?

Mathematically: if ERPN = 3 (medium) and SCRPN = 3 (medium), OverallRPN = 9, which falls in the "marginally acceptable" zone. But if both rise to 4, OverallRPN = 16, which immediately jumps into the "unacceptable" zone.

This non-linear amplification is exactly what the multiplicative formula is designed to do: when two independent sources of risk (the excipient itself plus the supply chain) are both elevated, the overall risk is far more than a simple sum; it is multiplied.

Analogy: OverallRPN Is Like a Traffic Light System

Think of the OverallRPN as the "traffic light" for supplier qualification:

  • Green (Low, <4): Normal monitoring is sufficient. Periodic review of CoAs and an annual review.
  • Yellow (Medium, 4–10): Enhanced monitoring + consider an on-site audit. A written justification for acceptance is required.
  • Red (High, >10): Mandatory mitigation. A full GMP audit + additional testing must be carried out until the OverallRPN is reduced to an acceptable level.

As with a traffic light, red does not mean "can never be used"; it means "cannot be released until mitigation actions are taken".

6.6 Implementation of Controls — Risk Mitigation

When the risk is found to be at an acceptable level, the risk assessment process is concluded. The identified controls should be documented, and the agreement with the manufacturer or supplier should ensure effective maintenance of those controls.

If additional actions are needed, Table 6.6-1🔍 describes factors that should be considered. Specific actions that might lower the residual risk can be found by deconstructing the OverallRPN and investigating the risk scores for both the supply chain and the excipient that are driving the overall risk score.

The level of existing controls may exceed actual requirements. In such cases, a company may consider reducing efforts in lower-risk areas to focus on higher-risk ones — for example, increasing Audit frequency for high-OverallRPN excipients while decreasing it for low-OverallRPN excipients.

Confirmation that additional controls have been agreed upon and implemented should be obtained from both the excipient manufacturer/supplier and the drug product manufacturer, and documented in supply and quality agreements. After controls are implemented, the risk assessment must be reviewed and re-scored to determine the residual risk acceptability. If the OverallRPN remains unacceptable, further mitigation is required. This cycle continues until an acceptable residual risk level is reached.

Risk Mitigation Controls — Commentary

The Four Levels of Risk Mitigation

TR54-6 divides mitigation measures into four levels, each with a different responsible party:

  • Drug Product Manufacturer controls: For example, additional incoming testing, special storage, and reprocessing of the excipient (filtration/irradiation). These are decisions the MAH can make directly, without depending on the supplier's cooperation.
  • Excipient Controls: Require the supplier to raise its GMP standard, change the specification, or add test items (such as identity confirmation on every batch), which directly affects the credibility of the CoA.
  • Supply Chain Controls: Increase the frequency of third-party audits, strengthen change notification mechanisms, and monitor transportation. These are the direct countermeasures for RFS1 to RFS4.
  • Manufacturing Controls: Introduced by the excipient supplier, for example, strengthened cleaning validation, dedicated equipment, and a ban on rework (no rework). These are the countermeasures for the excipient's own ERPN.

The logic of "deconstructing the OverallRPN" is to find out whether the ERPN or the SCRPN is the higher one, and then apply controls first to the higher side, rather than wasting resources on low-risk aspects that are already adequate.

Key Point: Mitigation Is an Iterative Loop, Not a One-Time Decision

TR54-6 explicitly requires that after mitigation measures are implemented, the risk must be re-scored. If the OverallRPN still exceeds the threshold, continue mitigating until an acceptable level is reached. This "implement → re-score → implement again" loop reflects the core spirit of ICH Q9 QRM: risk control is a dynamic management process.

All agreed controls must be documented in the Supply Agreement and the Quality Agreement to ensure they are legally binding.

Application Scenario: How to "Deconstruct" a High OverallRPN to Find Countermeasures

Scenario: An excipient has an OverallRPN = 18 (>10, unacceptable)

Deconstruction: ERPN = 3.6 (medium excipient risk), SCRPN = 5 (high supply chain risk)

Conclusion: The driver is the supply chain, not the excipient itself. Priority actions should target the SCRPN:

  • Conduct a third-party audit (lowers RFS4)
  • Require the supplier to obtain EXCiPACT certification (lowers RFS6)
  • Add a tamper-evident packaging requirement (lowers RFS5)

Assume that after implementation the SCRPN falls to 3.0; the new OverallRPN = 3.6 × 3.0 = 10.8 (still slightly above the threshold) → continue mitigating, for example by adding identity testing on every batch, until the OverallRPN is < 10.

GMP Standards Ladder and Decision Logic — Commentary

EXCiPACT Certification: A GMP Standard Specific to Pharmaceutical Excipients

EXCiPACT™ is a GMP certification scheme specific to pharmaceutical excipients. It is comparable to ISO 9001, but ISO 9001 is a general quality management system, whereas EXCiPACT is an advanced standard designed specifically for pharmaceutical excipient manufacturers.

Key features:

  • EXCiPACT is an "annex" to ISO 9001, not a standalone standard. A manufacturer must hold both ISO 9001 and EXCiPACT certification.
  • EXCiPACT fully incorporates the GMP principles of the EC guidelines, so in a supply chain risk assessment EXCiPACT certification can directly lower the risk score for RFS6 (QMS standard) to Low (1 point).
  • NSF/IPEC/ANSI 363 is substantially equivalent to EXCiPACT, but is an alternative route designed for manufacturers that do not use ISO 9001.
  • The IPEC-PQG GMP Guide is also equivalent to EXCiPACT; these three (EXCiPACT, NSF/IPEC/ANSI 363, IPEC-PQG) represent the same level of risk mitigation capability.

Analogy: EXCiPACT is to excipient manufacturers what ISO 13485 is to medical device manufacturers: in both cases an enhanced version built on top of the general ISO 9001 for specific regulatory requirements.

GMP Standard Decision Logic: Choose the Minimum Requirement by Dosage Form and Risk

The key to understanding the GMP standards ladder is that not every excipient needs the highest standard; instead, based on the OverallRPN and the intended use of the drug product, find the "sufficient" standard. The decision logic is as follows:

  • Low-risk excipient + topical drug product: ISO 9001 or EFfCI GMP is usually sufficient. Pharmaceutical-grade GMP is not needed, and the cost is lower.
  • Medium-risk excipient + oral solid dosage form: EXCiPACT™ or the IPEC-PQG GMP Guide is the standard choice and represents the pharmaceutical industry's "minimum expectation" for excipients.
  • High-risk excipient + parenteral: EC GMP Part II (ICH Q7) or higher. The supplier essentially needs API manufacturing capability.
  • High-risk excipient + sterile dosage form: EC GMP Part I + Annex 1. This is the highest requirement, equivalent to the sterile manufacturing standard of a drug product manufacturing site.

An interesting corollary: if an injectable excipient supplier holds only ISO 9001 without EXCiPACT, then under the TR54-6 logic RFS6 should be rated at least Medium (3 points), which may push up the SCRPN and in turn the OverallRPN, triggering a mitigation requirement.

Analogy: The GMP Standards Ladder Is Like "Driver's License Classes"

ISO 9001 is like an ordinary car driver's license: you can drive, but that does not mean you can drive an ambulance or a truck. EXCiPACT is an added "special vehicle permit" that shows pharmaceutical-grade quality awareness. ICH Q7 is a heavy-vehicle license, suited to complex products such as APIs. EC GMP Annex 1 is a pilot's license: only manufacturers that truly need sterile manufacturing capability must obtain it.

Choosing the wrong license class (an inadequate GMP standard) is not only a compliance issue; it also represents an actual gap in quality assurance, which is reflected directly in the RFS6 score of the SCRPN.

Application Scenario: A CDMO's IV Sorbitol Purchasing Decision

Scenario: A CDMO is using sorbitol in an intravenous (IV) solution formulation. The supplier is a broker with no audit history and no EXCiPACT certification.

TR54-6 requires the MAH to take the following actions before use:

  1. Calculate the OverallRPN: The IV use means a high ERPN (high drug product risk); no audit history + broker + no certification means a high SCRPN → the OverallRPN is very likely > 10 (unacceptable).
  2. Mitigate supply chain risk (lower the SCRPN):
    • Require the broker to provide complete information on the original manufacturer and carry out supply chain traceability (lowers RFS1)
    • Conduct a third-party audit of the original manufacturer or require it to obtain EXCiPACT certification (lowers RFS6)
    • Require tamper-evident packaging and batch-by-batch identity confirmation testing (lowers RFS5 + excipient controls)
  3. Confirm the GMP standard needed: IV use → at least the EC GMP Part I level; for a sterile dosage form, Part I + Annex 1. ISO 9001 alone is far from sufficient.
  4. Re-score: After all mitigation measures are implemented and documented in the Quality Agreement, recalculate the OverallRPN and confirm it has fallen to an acceptable level (<10, preferably <4) before releasing the excipient for use.

Conclusion: TR54-6 does not allow the risk assessment to be "bypassed". No high-risk excipient should be put into production before the mitigation cycle is completed.

7.0 Ongoing Review — Formalized Risk Assessment Lifecycle

Ongoing Review — The Formal Risk Assessment Lifecycle

7.0 The Risk Assessment as a Living Document

Once the excipient risk assessment is formally in place, it will be used as living data and will contribute to a knowledge base through ongoing risk review. Figure 7.0-1 shows the overall generic risk model with respect to risk review only.

Risk Review generally will be conducted in two modes:

  • Periodic (monitoring-driven): Scheduled reassessment at defined intervals based on the excipient's risk tier
  • Event-driven (Trigger-based): Immediate reassessment whenever a qualifying event occurs

The lifecycle model mirrors the generic risk management cycle: Initiate → Risk ID → Risk Analysis → Risk Evaluation → Risk Reduction → Risk Acceptance → Communication → Risk Review → (loop back). This is not a one-time exercise; Lifecycle Management ensures the risk assessment remains current and actionable.

"The risk assessment… will be used as living data and will contribute to a knowledge base through ongoing risk review." — TR54-6, Section 7.0

Core Concept: The Living Document and Lifecycle Management

What Is a "Living Document"?

The core idea of Lifecycle Management is that a risk assessment is not a static document that is "done once and finished" but a living document that is continuously updated. This is consistent with the philosophy of ICH Q10, Pharmaceutical Quality System: quality should be continuously monitored and improved throughout the whole product lifecycle.

Think of it as a car's maintenance record: you do not inspect the car only once at purchase; every time a part is replaced, an accident occurs, or a mileage milestone is reached, the record needs to be updated. The same applies to an excipient risk assessment.

  • The ICH Q9 QRM framework requires ongoing monitoring of the effectiveness of risk controls
  • The ICH Q10 PQS framework requires a knowledge management system so that risk assessments can evolve with new knowledge
  • EU GMP Annex 15 (Qualification and Validation) also emphasizes that periodic review is a regulatory expectation

Analogy: The Annual Review of Car Insurance

Your car insurance does not stay the same forever; every year you review whether the premium and coverage are still appropriate. When you "move house" (new manufacturing site), "buy a new car" (new supplier), or "have an accident" (quality event), you reassess the risk immediately rather than waiting for the annual review.

The logic of TR54-6 is exactly the same: periodic review (like the annual insurance review) + trigger-event review (like an immediate reassessment after a major event).

7.1 Triggers for Risk Assessment Review

Trending of excipient and drug product quality plays a key role in the risk assessment to ensure science- and data-based assessments. Key performance indicators or other metrics should be monitored on an ongoing basis to demonstrate the effectiveness of the risk controls. That monitoring should highlight any Triggers that indicate the need to reassess the risk and, possibly, revise the assessment.

The process, decision, and rationale should all be documented. Updates to the risk assessment may lead to revised control decisions, including CAPA outcomes and revised Supplier Qualification requirements.

Triggers: When Must You Reassess?

The Six Key Triggers You Must Remember

These six categories of triggers are the knowledge points most often tested in examinations and in real work:

  1. Supplier change — a new manufacturer/supplier, or a change in an existing supplier's GMP certification status
  2. Specification change — a revision of the excipient specification or of a pharmacopeial standard (Pharmacopeial changes)
  3. Regulatory action — a recall or warning letter from a competent authority, or updated regulatory requirements
  4. New safety information — new toxicological data or an industry safety event (such as the TSE outbreak)
  5. Audit findings — an internal audit or external inspection finds systemic deficiencies at the supplier
  6. Product formulation change — a new formulation of an existing product, or a completely new product that uses an existing excipient

Any one trigger mandates a reassessment, and the process, decisions, and rationale must be documented.

CDMO Practical Scenario: The FDA Warning Letter Trigger Process

Suppose you are a CDMO and excipient supplier X receives an FDA warning letter citing major deficiencies in its GMP system. The trigger response steps required by TR54-6 are as follows:

  1. Initiate immediately — on the day the warning letter is issued, formally start a Risk Review and record the trigger event
  2. Assess existing inventory — review batches already received and stored, and determine whether there are quality concerns
  3. Enhanced testing — apply strengthened release testing to subsequent incoming batches until the risk is clarified
  4. Qualify an alternative supplier — start the Supplier Qualification process for a backup supplier
  5. Regulatory notification assessment — assess whether the drug regulatory authority needs to be notified (depending on the marketing authorization requirements)
  6. CAPA update — enter the event into the CAPA system and update the supplier assessment record

8.0 Benefits and Value / QRM / Knowledge Management

Benefits and Value / QRM / Knowledge Management

8.0 The Business Case for Formalized Excipient RA

A global excipient risk assessment throughout the drug product lifecycle delivers the following advantages:

  • Documented Knowledge Management: Structured knowledge about the excipient supply chain, building a growing institutional repository
  • Rapid new-product evaluation: Ability to rapidly and effectively evaluate the requirements for new products that use existing or new excipients
  • Preferred supply chain identification: Identification of supply chains that present lower risks and preferred supply chains with which to build long-term relationships
  • Focused oversight: Focus on specific risk mitigation oversight activities for both manufacturer or supplier and user — directing audit resources to where they matter most
  • Deeper product knowledge: More detailed product knowledge and increased understanding of how the risks from excipients may affect product quality
  • Formal trigger database: Formal assessment and database of risk review triggers, such as major changes and critical investigations (CAPA integration)
  • Knowledge-based decision-making: Proactive rather than reactive risk management — moving from crisis response to anticipatory control
  • Common language for stakeholders: Provides a common language and approach for clear communication with stakeholders throughout the supply chain
  • Knowledge-sharing opportunities: Opportunities for comparative assessments between manufacturers, suppliers, and end users
"A global excipient risk assessment throughout the drug product lifecycle delivers… knowledge-based and proactive decision-making." — TR54-6, Section 8.0

Benefits Explained: Why Is the Investment Worthwhile?

The Essence of Knowledge Management

In this context, Knowledge Management means building a structured excipient risk database that accumulates "institutional wisdom" over time, so that each decision is better founded than the last.

This database includes:

  • Supplier audit history
  • CoA trend analysis
  • Complaint records & RCA
  • Change notification log
  • Trends in risk scores across batches

As it accumulates over time, this database lets you complete an excipient risk assessment for a new product in a few hours rather than spending weeks starting from scratch. This is the difference between proactive and reactive risk management.

Analogy: The Credit Score System

Think of a bank's credit score system: every time your supplier completes a transaction (on-time delivery, conforming CoA) it accumulates "credit"; every time a quality event occurs (batch rejection, late delivery) points are deducted. Accumulated over a long period, your "risk score" for each supplier becomes more and more accurate.

The Knowledge Management in TR54-6 builds exactly this kind of supplier "credit history", so that Procurement, QA, and Regulatory Affairs can communicate in a common numerical language rather than each relying on their own intuition.

Regulatory Compliance Value: Three Major Regulatory Expectations

A formalized excipient risk assessment directly responds to the following regulatory requirements:

  • EU GMP Annex 15 (Qualification/Change Control): requires ongoing review and documentation, which corresponds directly to the TR54-6 lifecycle model
  • EU EC Guidelines (2015): explicitly require a "formalized risk assessment" of excipient suppliers to determine the appropriate GMP level; TR54-6 is precisely the implementation guidance for it
  • ICH Q9 QRM: requires risk management to be an ongoing activity rather than a one-time assessment; the trigger mechanism of TR54-6 operationalizes this requirement

In an audit, being able to demonstrate a mature Lifecycle Management system is concrete evidence of regulatory maturity and quality culture.

10.0 Annex I: Real World Examples of Risk Assessments

The generic model proposed in Section 6.0 was developed based on real-world risk assessment processes donated by several marketing authorization holders (MAHs). Many risk assessment approaches used some form of risk ranking and filtering approach — conducting assessments in two separate streams that can then be combined in an overall risk score.

One stream would provide a risk profile for the excipient itself, based on its use in the finished product and any intrinsic risk factors. The second stream would generate a risk profile for the supply chain, including the excipient manufacturer, suppliers, brokers, and any other players, as well as the strength of their quality management systems (QMS). The two streams would then be combined into one risk evaluation to determine whether the overall risk is at an acceptable level or additional controls are needed.

Key Concept: The Two-Stream Model Architecture

Origin of the TR54-6 Generic Model

Section 10 reveals an important fact: the TR54-6 generic model was not designed from scratch but derived from RA processes actually in use at several MAHs (marketing authorization holders). These six examples are those "original models."

The logic of the two-stream model:

  • First stream (Excipient Stream): focuses on the excipient itself, namely what it is, which dosage form it is used in, and its inherent risks (toxicity, likelihood of microbial contamination, impact on critical quality attributes (CQAs))
  • Second stream (Supply Chain Stream): focuses on the supplier, namely the manufacturer's GMP level, supply chain length, intermediaries, and the strength of the quality management system (QMS)
  • Combined decision: the scores from the two streams are merged to judge whether the overall risk is acceptable or whether an additional control strategy is needed

Why Do the Six Companies' Models Differ?

Each company has a different excipient portfolio, dosage forms, supplier base and resource constraints. TR54-6 provides a framework, not a single answer. The six examples are there precisely to demonstrate this flexibility.

10.1 Example 1: Company U

Company U is a global manufacturer of various pharmaceutical dosage forms. The company established a Microsoft Excel tool to be used as a corporate standard.

When multiple manufacturing sites use the same excipient provided by the same excipient manufacturing site, the risk assessments can be shared across all sites (with appropriate site-specific modifications to accommodate excipient usage, supply chain complexity, and other factors).

First, basic information about the excipient and the manufacturer is collected. Then, the collected information is consolidated to produce an overview. For each section of the model, the parameters to evaluate are defined, each parameter is described in detail, and possible scenarios are developed. The severity of each parameter is assigned a standard value. In conducting the evaluation, Company U determines the actual probability of each parameter.

The analysis is conducted as follows:

  • Excipient and excipient usage risk score — Each identified risk factor is evaluated based on the actual origin or usage of the excipient and the actual supply chain to determine the probability of risk (P). The predefined weight, or severity (S), is used to calculate the risk score for each parameter (P × S). The overall excipient risk score is calculated as the average across the parameters.
  • Combined material risk score — A combined material risk score is calculated by adding the excipient risk score and the excipient usage risk score.
  • Manufacturer risk score — The list of risk factors is evaluated based on the actual performance and QMS of the manufacturer. The predefined weight (S) is used to calculate each parameter's risk score (P × S). The overall manufacturer risk score is the average across all parameters.
  • Gap analysis and actions — The combined material risk score and the manufacturer risk score are evaluated together using a risk matrix to identify the "gap level." Where a gap exists between the manufacturer risk score and the combined material risk score, the gap must be evaluated to determine the appropriate remediation.

Study Guide: Company U, the Gap Analysis Matrix Method

Key Concept: What Is a "Gap"?

Company U's innovation is that it does not only ask "how high is the risk?" but asks "how large is the difference between the supplier's GMP capability and the GMP level required for this excipient?" That difference is the Gap.

Three possible outcomes of the gap analysis:

  • Gap = Low: the supplier's capability is sufficient to manage the excipient risk → accept and maintain existing controls
  • Gap = Medium: the supplier's capability is somewhat insufficient → coach the supplier to improve, or increase audit/testing frequency
  • Gap = High: the supplier is seriously inadequate to control a high-risk excipient → immediate action is required: (a) request a CAPA, (b) mandate development of a backup supplier, or (c) replace the supplier

Analogy: Gap Analysis Is Like a "Loan Review"

Imagine a bank deciding whether to approve a loan:

  • Loan amount (excipient risk) = the more you want to borrow, the stricter the requirements
  • Applicant's credit (manufacturer's GMP level) = whether your finances are sufficient to repay
  • Gap = the extent to which your finances fall short of supporting the loan amount

A high-risk excipient (a large loan) paired with a supplier with weak GMP (poor credit) = the largest gap, and the bank will decline the loan or require collateral.

Feature of Company U: Shared RA Mechanism

When several manufacturing sites use the same excipient from the same supplier, the RA results can be shared across sites (with site-specific modifications added). This greatly saves assessment resources for multi-site groups and is a best practice for corporate standardization.

10.2 Example 2: Company V

Company V is a global manufacturer of pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical solid oral dosage forms and lyophilized injectables. To a large extent, its products are manufactured through contract manufacturing organizations (CMOs).

The risk model was created to meet the European Commission Guidelines on the Formalised Risk Assessment for Ascertaining the Appropriate GMP for Excipients of Medicinal Products for Human Use (EC Guidelines), and the risk factors to be considered reflect those in the guidelines. The model was implemented in a global SOP that applies for all sites manufacturing commercial products and, going forward, for new products, including biologics.

This model was extended for products during their transfer into commercial production. The quality risk assessment tool is applied based on risk ranking and filtering, evaluating the risk factors in two groups:

  • Multiple criteria analysis
    • Function and use of excipient (→ Score Excipient)
    • Manufacturer (→ Score Manufacturer)
    • Suppliers (→ Score Supplier)
  • Risk score for each excipient (low, medium, or high)
    • Independent risk factors
    • Risk ranking and weighing
    • Overall weighing

The three single scores — Function and Use, Manufacturer, and Suppliers — were combined by multiplication into a weighted RPN score. Acceptability was evaluated based on the RPN value, and mitigation actions were considered according to the overall risk score (low, medium, or high).

Figure 10.2-1🔍: Risk Assessment Model for Company V

[Figure 10.2-1🔍 — Three parallel scoring streams: (1) Function & Use of Excipient → Score Excipient; (2) Manufacturer assessment → Score Manufacturer; (3) Supplier assessment → Score Supplier. Three scores combined by multiplication → Weighted RPN → Risk level (Low / Medium / High) → Mitigation actions]

Key outcomes from implementation:

  • No major issues were found for existing products, though some minor changes in audit strategy and other controls were instituted as a result.
  • One benefit of performing the risk assessment was obtaining the collected knowledge and an overview of the supply chains.
  • Company V found that responsibilities for information-gathering need to be changed in the global SOP. Currently, the global support function gathers needed information from different stakeholders. Using one point of contact for each product is under discussion as an alternative.
  • While the model has been well accepted, it has not yet been used as a living risk assessment as much as would be liked.
  • The risk assessment is stored in the GMP documentation system. Software automatically triggers formal review and approval cycles globally. More flexibility to revise and update the risk assessment would be appreciated, especially in cases where the RA is completed before the drug product is commercially available. The company plans to introduce a new electronic tool to serve this purpose.

Study Guide: Company V, the Multiplicative Weighted RPN Model

Analogy: This Is Like a "Credit Score (FICO Score)"

The US FICO credit score model weights several factors (payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, new credit applications, credit mix) and combines them into a single score (300-850) used to predict default risk.

Company V's RPN model does the same thing:

  • Score Excipient = the risk weighting of the excipient itself
  • Score Manufacturer = the score for the manufacturer's GMP capability
  • Score Supplier = the score for the supply chain (intermediaries/agents)
  • Weighted RPN = Score Excipient × Score Manufacturer × Score Supplier

The benefit of multiplication (rather than addition): if any one score is very high, the final RPN is pulled up, so "good scores" elsewhere cannot mask a single serious weakness.

Real Challenges Company V Faced After Implementation

The original text honestly records several difficulties, which are very valuable for learners:

  • Unclear responsibility for information gathering: who collects which data? The split between global support functions and site product owners needs to be redefined.
  • A living RA is hard to sustain: once the RA is completed and filed in the document system it is "frozen" and is not kept updated to reflect changes in the supply chain.
  • CMO complexity: most products are made through CMOs, so the RA must cover the excipient sources used by the CMO, and the information is harder to obtain.

These challenges may well exist in your company too. Recommendation: establish RA triggers (supplier changes, major quality events) to keep the RA current.

Link to the EC Guidelines

Company V's model maps explicitly to the 2015 European Commission guidelines (EC Guidelines on Formalised Risk Assessment for Excipients GMP). This means that if a medicinal product marketed in Europe uses the Company V model for its excipient RA, it maps directly to the regulatory requirements.

10.3 Example 3: Company W

Company W is a global manufacturer of pharmaceuticals and devices, primarily parenteral products.

As part of the material assessment, the company examines the correlation of impact between parameters (attributes) describing the excipient and the CQAs of the product in which it is an ingredient. An excipient assessment provides input to material specifications, as illustrated in Figure 10.3-1. In this way, the company evaluates the impact of the excipient on the finished drug products.

Attributes are derived by ideation from a defined list of potential risk sources. Attributes with impact are included on the specification, and limits or other controls are defined after analyzing the possible impacts of variation in the attribute. This method uses a qualitative assessment with a simple +/− score for impact combined with text rationales and references describing the underlying science. The impact is evaluated considering the amount of material to be used and its function in the formulation.

The result of the assessment is used to define the excipient's specification based on the specific use in a specific drug product. An assessment is made for each use in a drug product.

Figure 10.3-1🔍: Risk Assessment Model for Company W

[Figure 10.3-1🔍 — Excipient attribute list → Ideation of potential risk sources → Attribute-to-CQA impact assessment (+/−) → Attributes with impact → Specification setting & limits; In parallel: Supply chain assessment (qualitative: impact / likelihood / controls) → Additional controls if risk identified]

An assessment of the supply chain is carried out in parallel with the material assessment. The supply chain — including manufacturer, retailers, brokers, and others — is evaluated using a predefined list of risk elements, brainstorming possible relevant issues in the specific supply chain for the particular excipient. The identified risks are then evaluated qualitatively for impact, likelihood, and controls in place. If a risk becomes evident, the company identifies any additional controls that would reduce the risk to an acceptable level.

Study Guide: Company W, the Simplified Qualitative +/- Matrix Method

What Makes Company W Unique

Compared with the other five companies, which use numerical scoring (such as P×S or RPN), Company W chose a purely qualitative method, using only +/- to judge whether each attribute has an impact on the CQAs.

Why can Company W do this?

  • Its main products are parenteral products, and the scientific understanding of each attribute is already deep, so the qualitative judgments are backed by solid scientific literature
  • A binary "impact / no impact" judgment is actually clearer and avoids the subjective error of numerical scoring
  • The assessment output feeds directly into the material specification; the aim is to define specification limits, not merely to judge the supplier's risk level

Simplified Matrix: When Does It Apply, and When Is It Not Enough?

Situations suited to the simplified +/- method:

  • A simple product portfolio (for example, Company W mainly makes injectables, so the dosage form is uniform)
  • Mature scientific knowledge and clear attribute impacts
  • The purpose of the RA is to set specifications rather than to compare suppliers

Unsuitable situations (a more complex model is needed):

  • A large excipient portfolio spanning several dosage forms (oral solids, injectables, topicals)
  • Many suppliers that need to be ranked to set audit priorities
  • Regulators require quantitative risk scores as a record

Analogy: +/- Is Like an "Aviation Safety Checklist"

A pilot's pre-flight checklist uses Yes/No, not a 1-10 score. For items with clearly known risks, a binary confirmation is often faster and less prone to "score inflation" than numerical scoring. Company W's method reflects the same logic.

10.4 Example 4: Company X

Company X expects its suppliers to comply with the IPEC-PQG Joint GMP Guide, EXCiPACT Standard, or NSF/IPEC/ANSI 363. The excipient risk management process considered input from the manufacturer qualification process, audits, relationships, testing, and outside intelligence. The company also worked with its CMOs to align expectations between them and the MAH and rationalized scores.

The actual process is owned by the company's organizational product quality management unit (PQM) and follows the model illustrated in Figure 10.4-1.

The product quality manager (PQM) is responsible for pulling together information and subject matter experts from across Company X to provide the data needed to complete the spreadsheet. Much of the information comes from the company's audit team. The model allows for rationalization across materials and suppliers to ensure that scoring is consistent and can be readily justified.

Company X maintains an SOP that describes the overall process, including triggers for revisiting a risk assessment. Because the PQMs also are involved in the change control process for company materials, they have firsthand knowledge of any changes that would require a review of the excipient risk assessment. As part of this process, Company X has already made several revisions to some excipient risk assessments.

Initially, there was some resistance to the concept and process. Now that the system is in place, however, staff have noticed that it is easier to find information without going into several systems or speaking to several departments.

Study Guide: Company X, Hybrid Scoring + Cross-Site Rationalization

Structure of Company X's Scoring Formula

Company X's model integrates several sources, and the scoring structure can be described as follows:

Inherent Excipient Risk Score
  = Excipient property risk (Intrinsic Risk)
  + Usage context risk (Usage Risk: dosage form/route of administration)

Supply Chain Risk Score
  = Manufacturer audit results
  + Manufacturer qualification
  + Test data
  + External intelligence (industry developments, warning letters, etc.)
  + Relationship quality (years of cooperation, transparency)

Combined Risk Score = Inherent Risk Score + Supply Chain Risk Score
→ maps to the control strategy (low/medium/high)

Difference from the generic OverallRPN: Company X does not use a simple P×S×D multiplication but an additive model, and introduces "external intelligence" and "relationship quality," two factors that are less common in the generic model. This reflects its CMO-based operating model, in which the supplier relationship itself is a risk signal.

What Is "Cross-Material Rationalization"?

Imagine a company with 200 excipients supplied by 50 suppliers. If each excipient is scored independently, different assessors may give different scores for "similar situations," creating inconsistency.

Company X's PQM is responsible for ensuring that the same supplier and the same scoring dimension receive the same score, or an explainable difference, across the RAs of different products. This is "rationalization": giving the scoring system internal consistency so that it can withstand regulatory inspection.

Change Control as an RA Trigger

Company X embeds RA review directly in the change control process: whenever there is a change affecting an excipient (supplier, specification, process), the PQM is automatically informed and triggers an RA review. This solves the difficulty of maintaining a "living RA" and is an institutional design worth learning from.

10.5 Example 5: Company Y

The model illustrated in Figure 10.5-1🔍 applies to finished products for human use that Company Y manufactures for European markets.

Company Y manufacturing sites completed risk ranking of each excipient from each manufacturer used at their sites.

A risk ranking tool is used to evaluate each excipient from each manufacturer. A weight of 1 (low), 3 (medium), or 10 (high) is assigned for each of six criteria and, from that, an overall risk ranking of the excipient (low, medium, or high) is calculated. Where the GMPs used to manufacture excipients are found to be inappropriate according to risk ranking, mitigation measures are implemented.

A risk analysis tool for manufacturer audit frequency is then used to assess the risk and determine audit frequency. Based on the outcome, a second risk assessment could be performed to determine if:

  1. Additional mitigation measures should be implemented, or
  2. Use of the excipient from that specific manufacturer should be discontinued.

If Company Y decides to continue to use the excipient after implementing additional mitigation measures to reduce the remaining risk to an acceptable level, the company documents this decision in a "statement to maintain business."

Company Y periodically reevaluates risk profiles during the review of a manufacturer's performance. A risk assessment is reevaluated in cases of a significant quality event, e.g., significant change by manufacturer, serious GMP deficiencies, or loss of GMP certification.

[Figure 10.5-1🔍 — Risk ranking tool (6 criteria, weights 1/3/10) → Overall excipient risk ranking (Low/Medium/High); If GMP inappropriate → Mitigation measures; Risk analysis for audit frequency → Audit schedule; Second RA: continue with additional mitigation (document "statement to maintain business") OR discontinue use; Periodic review + quality event trigger → RA reevaluation]

  • The result of a risk assessment sometimes varies between sites. However, after implementing a control strategy, the sites could reach the same decision to continue use of the excipient from that manufacturer.

Study Guide: Company Y, the Tiered Audit Frequency Model

Application Scenario: A CDMO Applying Company Y's Tiered Supplier Qualification Model

Suppose you are a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization) making injectable products for several pharmaceutical company clients. Your excipient suppliers can be managed using Company Y's tiering logic:

  • Tier 1 (highest risk): excipients for injectables (water for injection, buffers, preservatives) + single-source supplier + not yet EXCiPACT certified
    • Audit frequency: on-site audit every 12-18 months
    • Controls: CoA review for every batch + periodic incoming testing
  • Tier 2 (medium risk): oral solid excipients + several backup suppliers + compliant with pharmacopoeial standards
    • Audit frequency: every 2-3 years (may be replaced by a questionnaire)
    • Controls: CoA review + sampling tests
  • Tier 3 (low risk): topical/local-use excipients + well-known multinational supplier + ISO certification in place
    • Audit frequency: every 4-5 years, or questionnaire only
    • Controls: CoA review is sufficient

Company Y's 1/3/10 weighting system is the quantitative basis supporting this tiered decision.

What Is the "Statement to Maintain Business"?

This is a document specific to Company Y, used to record the formal decision that "we know this supplier has deficiencies, but for the following reasons we continue to use it after implementing additional controls."

Why this document matters:

  • It provides evidence during regulatory inspection that the decision was informed and justified, not a disregard of risk
  • It records all additional control measures (CAPA requirements, increased testing, etc.)
  • It sets a review date, so that the risk decision does not become permanent

A similar concept in quality management is called "risk acceptance with documented rationale."

10.6 Example 6: Company Z

Company Z's model was designed as a centralized process focused on highlighting the inherent risks and control strategies associated with an excipient, regardless of the manufacturer. The process is also designed to provide the user with the minimum quality standard expected for the manufacture of the excipient and its associated key risks. These risk and control strategies provide input for the gap analysis and, subsequently, to the quality risk assessment performed on supplier sites.

Company Z established a corporate procedure that outlines the requirements for the excipient risk assessment process and its linkages to the overarching supplier quality management process.

Figure 10.6-1🔍: Risk Assessment Model for Company Z

[Figure 10.6-1🔍 — Centralized excipient RA team performs inherent risk assessment (excipient-level, manufacturer-independent) → Output: Quality standard + Control strategy; Each supplier quality manager performs gap analysis (excipient standard vs. actual supplier capability) → Gaps identified → Validated gaps + other supplier intelligence → Supplier site quality risk assessment → Supplier risk ranking (Low/Medium/High) → Quality oversight level; Vendor assurance database shares results to formulation sites; Change control → Update supplier risk ranking; Periodic review & quality events → Ad hoc RA review]

The first part of the "end-to-end process excipient risk assessment" determines the control strategy and standards that are established by the centralized excipient risk assessment team. In the second part, the quality management group of the global supplier uses the output of the risk assessment to conduct individual supplier site quality risk assessments. The information from the individual supplier site quality risk assessments is shared with the formulation sites through a vendor assurance database system, highlighting any changes in supplier risk ranking via change control.

After the need for an excipient RA is identified, the centralized, cross-functional team performs the assessment, which focuses entirely on inherent risk. Supplier risks are not considered at this time. An electronic tool is used, and the output is used to determine the appropriate quality level for the excipient and any relevant excipient control strategy.

Each supplier quality manager then performs a gap analysis of the excipient supplier quality standard against an assessment of defined standards and excipient control strategies for supplied excipients. This identifies any gaps for the supplier. All gaps are challenged to ensure their validity and valid gaps are used as input, along with other supplier quality management intelligence, in performing the supplier site quality risk assessment. This final assessment generates a supplier risk ranking (low, medium, or high) which, in turn, determines the quality oversight to be applied.

The maintenance of the excipient RA is managed under the periodic review process. Quality events that impact excipient quality trigger an ad hoc excipient RA review.

Company Z implemented this process in March 2016 in line with the EC Guidelines.

Key benefits observed:

  • Consistency in managing excipients and suppliers. The centralized approach provided a concise framework for consistent input into the initial steps of the process. Implementation formalized the process and improved the documentation of information, supporting existing supplier risk assessment processes.
  • Grouping excipient grades into categories aligned with the route of administration, origin, and function has reduced the number of assessments required.
  • Conducting a formalized assessment process provides clarity to sourcing partners, both internal and external.
  • The risk assessment acts as an effective standard for comparing and assessing supply chain risks.

Study Guide: Company Z, Centralized + End-to-End Multi-Tier Supply Chain RA

Key Concept: Risk Management in a Complex Multi-Tier Supply Chain

Company Z's model is the most complex of the six because it deals with the real problem of "multi-tier supply chain risk": not only "my excipient supplier," but the "supplier's supplier" and even risks further upstream.

Company Z's two-stage architecture:

  • Stage 1 (centralized inherent risk assessment): a cross-functional central team assesses the inherent risk of the excipient itself, independent of any supplier. The output is "the minimum quality standard and control strategy this excipient should meet," which is the baseline for the whole supplier network.
  • Stage 2 (gap analysis for each supplier): each supplier quality manager uses the Stage 1 baseline as the standard to perform a gap analysis on the supplier sites they are responsible for, arriving at a risk level for each site.

Why two stages? This avoids "supplier bias": the people assessing the inherent risk of an excipient should not also be the people assessing a specific supplier, otherwise a close relationship may lead them to underestimate supplier risk.

Analogy: A Food Safety Inspection System

Imagine a food and drug authority (FDA) inspecting a large food supply chain:

  • Step 1: the FDA sets a "minimum safety standard for lettuce" (the inherent risk baseline), which is uniform nationwide and independent of any farm
  • Step 2: local inspectors visit individual farms and perform a gap analysis against the standard
  • Result: each farm receives its own risk level, which determines its inspection frequency

Company Z's "centralized RA + individual supplier gap analysis" follows exactly the same logic.

The Role of the "Vendor Assurance Database"

This database is the key to getting information to flow across the organization: centralized RA results → supplier risk level → automatic notification to each drug product site. When a supplier's risk level is updated through change control, all relevant sites know immediately, keeping supply chain management consistent throughout.

In large multinational pharmaceutical companies, digital supplier management systems of this kind (such as Veeva Vault QMS and TrackWise) are the technical foundation for implementing a "living RA."

Summary: Comparing the Six Company Models

Combined Analysis of the Six Companies: Commonalities and Differences

What the Six Companies Have in Common (the ICH Q9 Core Is Unchanged)

Whichever model is used, all six companies follow the same underlying logic:

  1. Hazard Identification: what are the inherent risks of the excipient? Where are the vulnerabilities in the supply chain?
  2. Risk Assessment: a combined assessment of likelihood (P) × severity (S)
  3. Risk Control: accept / strengthen controls / require a CAPA / replace the supplier
  4. Review & Communication: periodic review + triggers ensure the RA remains valid
  5. Injectables are always the most strictly reviewed: whichever model is used, excipients for parenteral use receive the highest scores and the strictest controls

Differences: Each Company's Individual Choices

  • Scoring method: numerical scoring vs. qualitative +/-; additive vs. multiplicative

  • Degree of digitalization: Excel (U) vs. enterprise database (Z)
  • Supply chain complexity: direct supplier (Y) vs. multi-tier supply chain (Z)

Which model to choose should depend on your company's excipient portfolio complexity, the size of the supplier base, the resources available, and the requirements of the regulatory markets.
