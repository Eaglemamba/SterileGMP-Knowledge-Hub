1.0 Introduction

1.0 Introduction

Microbial data deviations are microbial test results that fall outside the product specification or acceptance criterion established in a drug application, drug master file, official compendia, good manufacturing practice regulations, or internally by the manufacturer. This includes, but is not limited to, results generated during environmental and water monitoring as well as pharmaceutical ingredient, in-process, and finished product testing. The validity of the test results is supported by a documented laboratory and manufacturing investigation, where applicable.

The information presented in this technical report was compiled by a team of experts representing both industry microbiologists and regulators and reflects current industry best practices. The membership of personnel from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on the task force does not imply Agency endorsement. Feedback from industry and regulatory professionals was evaluated and incorporated into the technical report to ensure that the suggested methods, terminology, and practices for conducting microbial data deviation investigations reflect sound science and current technological approaches. This technical report is not intended to establish any mandatory or implied standards. Published references supporting the recommendations presented are cited in the text and listed in Section 7.0 References.

Definitions for the terms used throughout this report are provided in the Glossary. When available, the same or similar technical definitions have been used as those included in the FDA or International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) guidelines. To avoid ambiguity, the task force members believe that use and definitions of these terms should also be consistent and standardized when incorporated into company-specific standard operating procedures, validation protocols, and regulatory submissions.

Guided Commentary

Core Concept: What Is a Microbial Data Deviation?

A microbial test result that falls outside any of the following benchmarks constitutes a deviation:

  • Specifications in the drug application or Drug Master File (DMF)
  • Official compendia (USP, EP, etc.)
  • GMP regulatory requirements
  • Standards set internally by the manufacturer

Scope: environmental monitoring (EM), water systems, raw material components, intermediates and finished product testing. All microbial data fall within its remit.

Analogy: A Deviation Is Like a Warning Light on a Car Dashboard

A warning light does not mean the car is broken, but you must investigate the cause: is it a real engine fault (a true process problem), or a false sensor alarm (laboratory error)? TR88 is the "diagnostic manual" that gives you the diagnostic flow for each kind of warning light.

Regulatory Focus: FDA Participation Is Not Endorsement

FDA staff took part in the TR88 task force, but this report is not a mandatory standard and does not represent formal FDA endorsement. It is industry best-practice guidance, not a regulatory mandate.

CDMO practical implication: you can cite TR88 as the scientific basis for internal SOPs, but if a client contract contains stricter requirements, the contract prevails.

1.1 Purpose

1.1 Purpose

The FDA issued a Guidance for Industry: Investigating Out of Specification (OOS) Test Results for Pharmaceutical Production in 2006 in response to the U.S. versus Barr Laboratories decision, which is applicable to laboratory testing results for products regulated by the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) (1, 2). The FDA guidance does not extend to microbiological failure investigations, however. Consequently, PDA developed this technical report to provide approaches, specific to the pharmaceutical industry, for investigating various types of microbial data deviations based on current scientific knowledge and modern testing technologies.

The term "microbial data deviation" is preferred to "out-of-specification" or "out-of-limit result" in this technical report, as most microbiological tests, especially in a sterile product manufacturing facility, are in-process tests, not finished-product tests that must meet a regulatory-approved specification. For simplicity and consistency, the term "deviation" will be used throughout this document.

Technical Report No. 88 presents a holistic approach for performing a microbiological investigation. It provides a framework to assist with focusing on the investigational areas that may contain or contribute to the root cause of data deviations, such as sampling, test methodology, and suitability of the test, as opposed to providing methods for analysis of batch acceptability. (Batch acceptability determinations and release decisions are made by the quality control unit as required by FDA good manufacturing practice (GMP) regulations, not by deviation investigation teams.)

Guided Commentary

Why Call It a "Deviation" Rather Than OOS?

FDA's 2006 OOS guidance originates from the U.S. v. Barr Laboratories ruling and applies only to finished product testing regulated by CDER; it expressly does not apply to microbiological failure investigations.

The particular nature of microbiology: most microbial tests (especially in aseptic filling environments) are in-process tests, not finished product tests with regulatory specifications. Using the term "OOS" would create regulatory confusion, so TR88 uses "deviation" throughout.

Analogy: OOS Is the Export Customs Gate, a Deviation Is the Production-Line QC Station

OOS is like customs: it guards only the final exit (finished product), where a clear regulatory specification line exists. A microbial deviation is more like a QC station on the production line: every step (EM, water systems, intermediates) may need intervention, but there is no single "pass/release line". TR88 is the diagnostic framework for these intermediate stations.

Important Boundary: Investigation Is Not the Batch Disposition Decision

TR88 draws a clear line: the deviation investigation team is responsible for finding the root cause, but the batch disposition decision belongs to the QCU, in accordance with FDA GMP regulations. The two must not be confused.

CDMO red line: even if the investigation concludes "laboratory error, result invalid", whether the batch is released still requires formal QCU approval; the investigation team cannot decide this on its own.

1.2 Scope

1.2 Scope

Each of the three sections of this document focuses on distinct areas where microbial data is collected and inspected for deviation investigations and with the following designated responsibilities:

Roles and Responsibilities—Focuses on personnel, especially microbiologists, in the laboratory investigations and cross-functional teams responsible for manufacturing investigations.

Conducting Laboratory Investigations (Phase I)—Provides additional details on how an investigation may be conducted to determine that the microbial test was valid and what is the root cause of a failure when the result is deemed invalid. Subsections focus on different microbiological tests conducted, with points for consideration in the final determination of the investigation.

Conducting Manufacturing Investigations (Phase II)—Illustrates where a microbiologist, acting as a subject matter expert (SME), can contribute to the evaluation of personnel activities, environmental changes, utilities, materials, and process-related events and can assist in determining the probable cause of the deviation and the most appropriate corrective and preventive actions (CAPA).

If needed and when appropriate, Phase I and Phase II investigation activities can be conducted in parallel.

The roles of other contributors to deviation investigations required for effective root cause analysis, such as representatives from manufacturing, process engineering, and quality assurance, will also be briefly described. The composition of the investigation team will depend on the nature of the investigation.

This technical report promotes a lifecycle approach, but greater emphasis is placed on laboratory and manufacturing investigations of marketed drug products. The approaches described may be suitable during research and product development and the manufacture of clinical supplies, as well as the manufacture and testing of regulatory-approved products. The microbial tests discussed in this technical report include microbial attribute testing of nonsterile products, components, sterility testing, bacterial endotoxin assay, mycoplasma testing, environmental and personnel monitoring, and microbial monitoring of utilities including pharmaceutical-grade water and compressed gases.

Microbial assays of antibiotics and vitamins and the detection and enumeration of protozoans, viruses, and prions are not included in the scope of this report. Additionally, batch disposition is separate from the outcome of a microbial investigation report. This document is directed specifically to pharmaceutical manufacturing; however, the industry practices may be suitable for deviation investigations in the biological, consumer health, medical devices, and cosmetic industries. In addition, these can be extended to contract manufacturing and testing organizations supporting pharmaceutical companies.

While this document provides a framework illustrating how deviations and the resulting investigations might be conducted, each company is responsible for developing their internal policies and procedures suitable for its specific needs that also meets current GMPs.

Guided Commentary

Core Framework: Two-Phase Investigation, Phase I + Phase II

Phase I: Laboratory Investigation

  • Core question: is this test result valid?
  • Who investigates: the microbiology laboratory (analyst + management)
  • Goal: confirm the validity of the result; if invalid, determine the root cause of the laboratory error

Phase II: Manufacturing Investigation

  • Core question: if the test is valid, what went wrong on the manufacturing side?
  • Who investigates: a cross-functional team (manufacturing, engineering, QA + microbiology SME)
  • Goal: determine the most probable cause (probable cause) and propose CAPA

Key point: the two phases can run in parallel; Phase II does not have to wait until Phase I is fully complete. Time sensitivity demands this.

Analogy: Dual-Track Diagnosis in the Emergency Room

When a patient arrives in the emergency room, the doctors work on two tracks at once: (1) confirm whether the test data are correct (Phase I); (2) investigate the possible cause of illness (Phase II: source of infection, medication history, environmental exposure). Only by running both tracks in parallel can the truth be found quickly.

CDMO Key Insight: The Quality Agreement Must Divide Phase I vs. Phase II Responsibilities

TR88 explicitly states that the framework extends to "contract manufacturing and testing organizations". For an aseptic filling CDMO:

  • Phase I (laboratory): usually led by the CDMO's microbiology laboratory
  • Phase II (manufacturing): led by the CDMO, but the client may ask to participate or to receive the report
  • The contractual Quality Agreement must clearly specify who is responsible for which phase, timeline requirements, report format and approval mechanism

Consequences of leaving this unspecified: complaint disputes, inspection findings, and batches the client refuses to release.

2.0 Glossary of Terms

2.0 Glossary of Terms

Terminology Analysis

Three-Tier Warning Structure: Alert Level → Action Limit → Adverse Trend

Understanding how these three terms relate to each other is the foundation of EM management:

  • Alert Level: an early warning. An excursion does not necessarily require corrective action, but monitoring and follow-up must be intensified.
  • Action Limit: an excursion means the process has departed from its normal operating range; a written investigation must be initiated and corrective action taken.
  • Adverse Trend: not a single excursion event but a pattern of rising excursion frequency or of consecutive low-level microbial recoveries, indicating that process control is slipping and a systematic evaluation is needed.

Analogy: A Three-Colour Traffic Light

Alert Level = yellow light (slow down, observe); Action Limit = red light (stop, take action); Adverse Trend = the pattern warning after repeated yellow lights (this intersection is badly designed and needs a fundamental fix).

Invalid Result vs. Invalid Test: A Subtle but Important Distinction

  • Invalid Result: the test was completed, but the Phase I investigation confirmed that the result is invalid.
  • Invalid Test: the test itself failed to meet method requirements; this includes cases where the test was aborted (for example, an isolator failure during a sterility test).

This distinction affects how the investigation is recorded and what triggers follow-up actions; the two must be clearly distinguished in the Phase I report.

The Central Role of CAPA: More Than a Form

TR88 defines CAPA as a subsystem for "collecting and analyzing information, identifying and investigating product and quality problems, and taking appropriate and effective corrective and/or preventive action to prevent their recurrence". It is a complete accountability mechanism, not something that ends once a form is filled in.

CAPA effectiveness is the ultimate test of any deviation investigation. If the same deviation recurs after CAPA implementation, regulators will ask why the root cause was wrong.

Correction vs. Corrective Action: Treating Symptoms vs. Treating the Cause

  • Correction: deals with the deviation that has already occurred (repair, rework); it treats the symptom.
  • Corrective Action: eliminates the cause of the deviation and prevents recurrence; it treats the root.

Common inspection finding: the company performs only a Correction (for example, a retest) with no real Corrective Action (eliminating the root cause), so the deviation keeps recurring.

Term Identification Exercise

Classify which term each of the following situations belongs to:

  1. Over three consecutive months, the Alert Level excursion rate in the aseptic room rises from 2% to 8%.
  2. The RABS fails during a sterility test and the test is aborted.
  3. A water system microbial count exceeds the Action Limit and a written investigation is initiated.
  4. It is found that the tester used the wrong culture medium, so the result is not reliable.

Answer: 1. Adverse Trend; 2. Invalid Test; 3. Environmental Monitoring Excursion (+ triggers an OOL investigation); 4. Invalid Result (involving Laboratory Error)

Abbreviation Quick Reference

TR88 Core Abbreviation Groups

These abbreviations appear densely in later sections, so building muscle memory is very important:

  • EM (environmental monitoring): the most central routine microbial monitoring activity at an aseptic filling site.

3.0 Microbiologist Roles and Responsibilities — 3.1 Laboratory Microbiologist Responsibilities

3.1 Laboratory Microbiologist Responsibilities

The principal responsibility for achieving accurate laboratory testing results resides with the analyst performing the microbiological test. Recognition of potential practices that could create invalid results should be an integral part of the laboratory analyst's training. The analyst is responsible for performing all testing according to standard operating procedures (SOP) and in-house documentation practices. The analyst should only use qualified, calibrated, and well-maintained laboratory instruments and equipment and approved growth media, diluents, and reagents.

The analyst should check the product test results for compliance with their specifications before discarding test preparations, whenever practical. If any potential or suspected laboratory errors occurred during testing, the analyst must discontinue testing immediately and notify their laboratory management. The analyst, with guidance and instruction from the laboratory management, must collect and retain all test samples, reagents, consumables, paperwork, and any other relevant materials related to the affected assay, even if the test is deemed invalid, to aid the laboratory investigation.

If errors are clear and indisputable, such as atypical appearance of a test sample (e.g., unexpected particulate matter present), damaged test container, test sample spillage, use of wrong pipetting volumes, missing dilution steps, aseptic handling missteps, or use of the wrong testing medium, the analyst should immediately document the event. When in doubt, the analyst may consult with the laboratory management to confirm that the test should be discontinued.

The analyst should not knowingly continue a test that they expect to invalidate at a later time for an assignable cause (i.e., analyses should not be completed for the sole purpose of seeing that a passing result can be obtained when obvious errors are known). In addition, the analyst is responsible for assisting management with the laboratory investigation.

Guided Commentary

Core Analyst Responsibilities: The First Line of Defense for Data Quality

The responsibilities of the laboratory analyst include:

  • Perform all tests strictly according to SOPs
  • Use only qualified, calibrated instruments and approved media / diluents / reagents
  • Confirm that results meet specification before discarding test preparations
  • Stop the test immediately and notify management when a potential error is noticed
  • Retain all relevant samples, reagents, consumables and records to support the investigation
  • Assist management with the laboratory investigation

Analogy: The Analyst Is the Laboratory's "Honest Witness"

When a surgeon finds an instrument contaminated during an operation, the first duty is not to carry on and hope nothing goes wrong, but to pause immediately, report and preserve the evidence. The laboratory analyst's role is exactly the same. Stopping when a problem is found is professionalism, not dereliction of duty.

Regulatory Red Line: Do Not Continue a Problematic Test Just to Obtain a Passing Result

TR88 states explicitly: "analyses should not be completed for the sole purpose of seeing that a passing result can be obtained when obvious errors are known."

This is not only a quality requirement but also the regulatory bottom line of FDA data integrity. Violating this principle can lead to a Warning Letter, Form 483 observations, and even criminal charges.

CDMO management implication: analysts need psychological safety, so that when they find a problem they can stop and report immediately rather than push on for fear of affecting the production schedule. This is the responsibility of management and organizational culture.

3.2 Microbiologist Role in a Manufacturing Investigation

3.2 Microbiologist Role in a Manufacturing Investigation

The key to successful manufacturing investigations of microbial testing failures with pharmaceutical ingredients and finished drug products is the inclusion of an experienced microbiologist as an SME on the manufacturing investigation team. This microbiologist may work in the quality control (QC) microbiology testing laboratory at the manufacturing site, the quality control unit (QCU), product development, or a corporate microbiology support group. Ideally, the microbiologist should have a comprehensive knowledge of product formulation and specific manufacturing operations, obtained from frequent shop-floor visits or surveillance of the overall process. For smaller organizations, without internal microbiological expertise, the microbiologist may be an outside consultant. To be truly effective, the SME must be a trained microbiologist with broad experience in microbiological investigations. Furthermore, it would be advantageous if the SME is familiar with the actual product implicated in the failure. Without this expertise on the investigation team, it is less likely that the root cause of the failure can be determined, and the investigation may be hampered or severely limited.

The microbiologist should be engaged in the investigation as soon as is practical, as microbiological investigations are always time-sensitive.

The microbiologist should be part of the first step in an investigation, such as performing a walk-through of the manufacturing area, to become oriented and identify situations and events that are nonroutine and may be directly linked to the deviation. To be successful, the microbiologist must have access to all related testing results, have the cooperation of manufacturing managers, supervisors, operators, and support personnel, and have the authority to interview staff, inspect processing equipment, take investigatory microbial samples, and review change control and batch records. In some instances, access to customer complaint records may be necessary. For failures related to purchased pharmaceutical ingredients, it is highly desirable that a microbiologist join any audit team visiting the ingredient supplier to conduct an investigational review.

Systematic, proactive risk assessments for assessing the overall aseptic manufacturing process and controls have been proven helpful in identifying potential weaknesses in advance, and may mitigate the occurrence of a deviation, for example, sterility failures and endotoxin or environmental monitoring (EM) excursions (12).

Guided Commentary

The Microbiologist in a Manufacturing Investigation: A Technical Bridge, Indispensable

Characteristics of the ideal SME microbiologist emphasized by TR88:

  • Formal training in microbiology
  • Broad hands-on experience with microbial investigations
  • Familiarity with the product formulation and the specific manufacturing operations (ideally knowledge built up through walkthroughs on the floor)
  • Ideally, familiarity with the specific product involved in the failure

TR88 states directly that if the investigation team has no such person, "the investigation may be hampered or severely limited".

Analogy: The Microbiologist Is the Forensic Examiner at the Crime Scene

Police (manufacturing/engineering staff) can describe the scene, but to judge "was this contamination source left within the last 24 hours?" you need a forensic examiner (the microbiology SME). The examiner must be involved at the scene early, because microbial evidence (strains, environmental conditions) disappears over time. "Time-sensitive" is not a metaphor; it is the physical reality of microbiology.

Practical Key: The Microbiologist Must Have Formal Investigation Authority

TR88 lists the authority the microbiology SME must have during an investigation:

  • Access to all relevant test results
  • Interview manufacturing staff (operators, supervisors, support personnel)
  • Inspect processing equipment
  • Collect investigatory microbial samples
  • Review change control records and batch records
  • (When necessary) review complaint records

If the microbiologist is "stonewalled" by manufacturing during an investigation, or lacks formal authority to collect additional samples, the quality of the investigation will be severely compromised. This is a problem management must solve at the system level.

CDMO Application: Proactive Prevention Beats Reactive Investigation

TR88 points out that systematic, proactive risk assessment helps identify process weaknesses early and can reduce the incidence of deviations (including sterility test failures and endotoxin / EM excursions).

Takeaway for the Operations Director: do not bring in a microbiology SME only after a deviation occurs. Having microbiologists take part regularly in process risk assessments (FMEA, PHA) is what real proactive quality management looks like.

3.3 Microbiology Laboratory Management Responsibilities

3.3 Microbiology Laboratory Management Responsibilities

Upon notification by a laboratory analyst of a suspected laboratory error, the laboratory management must conduct an objective assessment to determine if an error has occurred. Data should be reviewed promptly to ensure the contemporaneous memory of events and to determine if the results might be attributed to an isolated or systematic laboratory error. In the absence of laboratory error, a confirmed valid test could indicate problems in the manufacturing operations; however, all facts need to be evaluated before predetermining a cause. The correct compliance assumption is that the results are valid until proven otherwise.

Laboratory management should ensure that the probable cause is supported by evidence, using root cause analysis tools when appropriate (e.g., 5 Whys, Ishikawa/fishbone diagram). Laboratory management should promptly notify the QCU and any impacted departments of the suspected deviation and inform them that a laboratory investigation has been initiated and when it might be completed.

If testing is conducted by a contract testing laboratory, the quality agreement between the contract testing laboratory and the manufacturer should clearly assign responsibilities, include a timeline, and specify the requirements of the laboratory investigation.

Guided Commentary

Action Framework for Laboratory Management

After receiving the analyst's report, the standard management response flow is:

  1. Objective assessment: did an error really occur? Avoid preconceptions.
  2. Classify the error: isolated vs. systematic laboratory error
  3. Working premise: "the result is valid until proven invalid"; you cannot assume a test problem just because the result looks bad.
  4. Use root cause tools: 5 Whys, fishbone diagram (Ishikawa / Fishbone Diagram)
  5. Proactively notify the QCU and affected departments: promptly, with an estimated investigation completion time

Analogy: The Judge's "Presumption of Innocence"

"A result is valid until proven invalid" is the microbiology version of the "presumption of innocence". If every unwelcome result is by default blamed on "laboratory error", the whole quality system loses its meaning. This is also one of the core principles FDA cares about most in data integrity.

The "Probable Cause" Principle: Detection Needs Evidence, Not Certainty

TR88 uses probable cause, not a definitive root cause. In reality, microbial investigations rarely reach 100% certainty: strains may not be traceable and environmental conditions may have changed.

What regulators require is: conclusions supported by evidence, root cause tools used appropriately, and CAPA designed against the probable cause. "No definitive root cause could be found" is not an acceptable conclusion, but "the most probable cause is X, based on Y and Z, and the CAPA is A" is acceptable.

Special Provisions for CDMO Contract Laboratories

If testing is performed by a contract testing laboratory, the Quality Agreement must:

  • Clearly assign investigation responsibilities (who is responsible for which part)
  • Include the investigation timeline
  • Specify the specific requirements for the laboratory investigation

Dual implication for a CDMO: you may be the "contract acceptor" (accountable to the client) and also the "contract giver" (setting requirements for a subcontracted laboratory). The Quality Agreement in both directions must cover the allocation of deviation investigation responsibilities.

3.4 Subject Matter Expert Responsibilities

3.4 Subject Matter Expert Responsibilities

SMEs can support the investigation by providing technical assistance related to the assay methods and technologies utilized, designing any investigational testing, and providing general scientific guidance, as needed. This expert could have greater experience and scientific background related to the assay or manufacturing process from within the company. Hiring a qualified outside consultant may also be an option.

Guided Commentary

The SME's Role: Technical Adviser, Not Investigation Lead

The SME (Subject Matter Expert) is positioned in TR88 as a supporting role:

  • Provide specialist help on test methods and techniques
  • Design investigational testing
  • Provide general scientific guidance

Note: the definition of SME is broader than that of the "manufacturing investigation microbiologist (section 3.2)": an SME can be an expert in any field (equipment engineer, statistician, etc.) and can come from outside the company.

Analogy: The SME Is the Investigation's "Outside Brain" or "Consulting Physician"

For major medical cases, outside experts are invited for a consultation (second opinion). A deviation investigation invites an SME to bring in depth of expertise beyond day-to-day operational knowledge. This is especially useful at a CDMO, because uncommon test methods or equipment problems may exceed internal capability.

When Is an External SME Needed?

  • Unusual organisms are involved (for example, a sterility test failure with a spore-forming organism)
  • Special identification techniques are required (for example, whole genome sequencing, WGS)
  • A major complaint or regulatory investigation requires an independent third-party opinion
  • The internal investigation has stalled and the root cause remains undetermined

Best practice: build a list of external SMEs, including contact details and qualification information, and have it ready before a deviation occurs.

3.5 Quality Control Unit Responsibilities

3.5 Quality Control Unit Responsibilities

The QCU oversees all aspects of the microbial deviation investigation. A QCU representative or other approved personnel would be responsible for coordinating with the cross-functional team involved in supporting the investigation, including personnel from manufacturing, facilities, calibration, validation; approving any laboratory retesting plans; and approving the laboratory and manufacturing investigations. The QCU also would be responsible for overseeing quality agreements made with contract testing laboratories and for ensuring that microbial deviation investigations are adequately addressed.

Guided Commentary

QCU: The Ultimate Overseer and Approving Authority of the Investigation

In a deviation investigation the QCU plays the role of overseeing and approving, rather than executing directly:

  • Coordinate the cross-functional investigation team (manufacturing, facilities, calibration, validation, etc.)
  • Approve laboratory retesting plans
  • Approve the Phase I (laboratory) and Phase II (manufacturing) investigation reports and conclusions
  • Oversee implementation of Quality Agreements with contract testing laboratories
  • Ensure that microbial deviation investigations are handled thoroughly and appropriately

Analogy: The QCU Is the Judge in Court, Not the Detective

The detectives (laboratory management, microbiology SME, cross-functional team) gather evidence and propose conclusions. The judge (QCU) reviews whether the process was compliant and whether the verdict is supported, and formally approves the final conclusion. Without the judge's approval, no conclusion has any force.

Checklist of Systems for the CDMO Operations Director to Put in Place

Make sure the following systems are in place so that you can operate effectively when a deviation occurs:

  • Analyst level: the training program includes criteria for judging "when to stop a test"; build a psychologically safe culture in which people dare to report problems rather than hide them.
  • Laboratory management: written procedures define the standard response timeline after a report is received (for example, initial assessment within 1 hour); training in the use of root cause analysis tools.
  • Microbiology SME: the SOP explicitly lists the SME's investigation access authority; build an external SME list (with emergency contacts).
  • QCU: the approval process has clear SLAs; the Quality Agreement with the client already covers the Phase I vs. Phase II division of responsibilities.

Key Takeaways of This Section

  1. Deviation definition: a microbial data deviation covers all microbial testing of EM, water systems, raw materials, intermediates and finished product; exceeding any established benchmark (regulation, compendium, internal standard) constitutes a deviation. The validity of a test result must be supported by a documented investigation.
  2. Terminology precision: use "deviation" rather than "OOS" because most microbial tests are in-process tests; the three tiers of Alert Level, Action Limit and Adverse Trend must be clearly distinguished; Invalid Result and Invalid Test have different investigative implications.
  3. Two-phase framework: Phase I (laboratory, confirming test validity) and Phase II (manufacturing, confirming the process root cause) can run in parallel; the batch disposition decision always belongs to the QCU, not to the investigation team.
  4. 4.0 Phase I: Conducting the Microbiology Laboratory Investigation

Original Text (English)

The first phase of a microbial data deviation (MDD) investigation is an initial assessment of the results reported by the microbiology laboratory. The purpose of the laboratory investigation is to establish the validity of test results, thereby confirming that a deviation has occurred. If a laboratory error has occurred, which would invalidate the test, the QCU (Quality Control Unit) would approve retesting and/or resampling of the pharmaceutical ingredient or drug product/drug substance. Some compendial microbiology testing chapters have limited information to address how to handle sample deviations and investigations. This technical report will discuss additional factors for microbiological evaluation of sterility, bacterial endotoxin, and other microbiological testing conditions for invalidating test results or allowing an initial test to be repeated.

Pharmaceutical QC microbiology laboratories vary in size and complexity. These factors may impact the level of experience with dealing with deviations in terms of the training, experience, and academic background of the analysts and laboratory management.

Contrary to the belief that microbial failure investigations are usually inconclusive, experience has demonstrated that the causes of the microbial contamination are determinable through robust investigations conducted by teams that include an experienced microbiologist. In the absence of a definitive cause, the most probable cause(s) can be determined based on the available information and follow-up observations, additional testing, and batch-record review. To avoid a confirmation bias, the team must be willing to reject their original hypothesis about the probable cause in light of the evidence subsequently collected. If possible, as many questions should be asked as to why the probable cause is not correct as are asked about why it is correct. Hindsight, where the successes of past microbial failure investigations often are exaggerated and may incorrectly influence the direction of the current investigation, is another bias that must be recognized and avoided. However, a history of conducting investigations will enhance the success with current investigations.

When no obvious laboratory errors are noted, the laboratory management must initiate a laboratory investigation promptly and must complete it as soon as is practically possible in accordance with regulatory expectations and company policies and procedures. Accurately recording the sequence of activities may prove helpful in determining root cause during an investigation. Some tests, such as microbial identification, may require more time and may delay completion of the laboratory investigation. Core Concept: The Fundamental Purpose of Phase I

First ask "Can this test result be trusted?", then ask "Is there a problem with the product?"

The starting logic of Phase I is very clear: before a deviation is declared, you must first confirm that the test itself was valid. If the laboratory made an error, that "positive result" may not represent product contamination at all.

This logic has a direct regulatory basis: USP <71> allows a sterility test to be "invalidated" under specific conditions, followed by a retest. The prerequisite, however, is that you can present strong evidence of laboratory error.

Analogy: Medical Diagnosis

Imagine a patient's blood report shows an abnormal value. Before telling the patient "you are ill", the doctor first asks: Was this tube contaminated? Was the centrifuge calibrated? Have the reagents expired?

Phase I is this "first confirm the equipment is fine" step. If the tube was contaminated, the value does not indicate any disease.

In an aseptic filling context: turbidity in the culture medium does not mean the product is non-sterile. It is only a signal, and an investigation is needed to confirm where the signal comes from.

Important Warning: Confirmation Bias

The source document specifically mentions two dangerous mindsets:

  • Confirmation Bias: Once you have decided "it is a product problem", you may collect only evidence that supports that hypothesis and ignore evidence pointing the other way.
  • Hindsight Bias: Applying the success of past investigations to the current case can send you in the wrong direction.

For a CDMO, both biases are especially dangerous, because the client is often anxious to know "whether there is a problem with their product", and your laboratory, under that pressure, may lean toward a conclusion before the investigation is complete.

4.1 Microbial Identification

Original Text (English)

Identifying the microorganism(s) implicated in product failure is a critical step in a laboratory investigation and, subsequently, in the manufacturing investigation. Knowledge of the source of the microorganism may lead to a possible route to determine which microorganism contaminated the product.

Microbiological identification systems are based on different analytical methodologies, with their inherent limitations, and the databases related to the system. Identification is accomplished by matching characteristics (genotypic, phenotypic, or proteotypic) to an established standard (reference) microorganism, such as a strain-type in the database. If a microorganism is not included in the library or database of the system, it will not be identified. Therefore, pharmaceutical manufacturers should review the breadth of the database of the identification system they plan to use and its applicability to their use requirements.

As stated in USP <1113> Microbial Characterization, Identification and Strain Typing, the hierarchy of microbial identification errors of impact, in descending order, is (1) misidentification to genera, (2) misidentification to species, and (3) no identification. Incorrect identifications could lead to a faulty investigation, inappropriate CAPAs, and ill-advised product disposition. For example, a misidentification may result in a nonsterile drug product being released when it contains an objectionable microorganism. If the in-house identification system cannot identify the isolate or if there is doubt regarding the identification, it should be sent to a contract testing laboratory for confirmation; otherwise, a misidentified microorganism will always be carried in the records.

The need for microbial identification is specifically cited in USP <62> Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Tests for Specified Microorganisms. This chapter presents a requirement for confirmatory identification tests for organisms that grow on selective or diagnostic media and demonstrate defined morphological characteristics. As a related aspect, USP <71> Sterility Tests allows for invalidation of the test if, after identification of the microorganism(s) isolated from the test, the contamination may be unequivocally ascribed to faults with respect to the material and/or the technique used in conducting the sterility test procedure.

Taking a polyphasic identification approach is recommended, that is, one that assembles and assimilates many levels of information from molecular, physiological, morphological, serological, or ecological sources to classify a microorganism. Some vendors of identification instruments encourage microbiologists to forgo the preliminary characterization steps, such as colony description, Gram reaction, cellular morphology description, and/or diagnostic screening. Forgoing these steps is not recommended as it is often necessary to assess the microorganism identification against all available information to determine if the identification makes sense. For example, Salmonella typhi is unlikely to be isolated from a cleanroom, but this pathogen could be present in an animal-derived ingredient.

Another consideration is that many phenotypic identification systems require the preliminary characterization of the isolate for the selection of the appropriate identification kit. An incorrect Gram stain may result in a misidentification and flawed investigation. Criticality of this routine test should not be overlooked.

Core Concept: Three Tiers of Microbial Identification

TR88 recommends a polyphasic identification approach, moving from coarse to fine in three tiers:

  1. Morphological tier: Colony appearance, Gram stain, and cell morphology. This is the most basic and fastest step and must not be skipped.
  2. Phenotypic/biochemical tier: API test strips and automated biochemical identification (such as Vitek 2). These depend on a database and carry a risk of false positives.
  3. Genotypic/proteotypic tier: MALDI-TOF MS (matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry), 16S rRNA sequencing, and ribo-printing. These offer the highest precision but require time and equipment.

When an identification result is used to decide whether to invalidate a sterility test, it must reach a sufficient level of confirmation: usually the molecular level, together with strain typing, in order to attribute the source.

Analogy: The Logic of a DNA Paternity Test

Saying "this person looks like so-and-so" based on appearance alone is not enough to establish paternity legally. You need a DNA comparison.

Likewise, in a sterility test investigation, saying only "this organism has also appeared in EM" is not enough to justify invalidation. You need strain typing, such as 16S rRNA sequencing or rep-PCR, to confirm that the two isolates are "the same individual" and not merely "the same species".

The same species ≠ the same strain. This distinction is critical in regulatory investigations.

The Cost of Identification Errors: Three Levels of Failure

USP <1113> ranks the severity of identification errors (from highest to lowest):

  1. Misidentification at genus level: The most serious. It can mislead the whole investigation and lead to the wrong CAPA.
  2. Misidentification at species level: Serious. It could cause a pathogen to be mistaken for a harmless organism and the product to be released.
  3. No identification: Relatively manageable; at least you know that you do not know, and the isolate can be sent to an external laboratory for confirmation.

The most dangerous scenario: identifying an objectionable microorganism as a "harmless environmental organism" and releasing the product.

CDMO Application: Never Skip the Gram Stain

The source document specifically warns that some instrument vendors encourage skipping the preliminary morphology steps and going straight to automated identification. TR88 clearly does not recommend this.

In a CDMO aseptic fill-finish context, the Gram stain matters because it:

  • Confirms culture purity (ruling out mixed-organism contamination)
  • Guides selection of the correct follow-on identification kit (Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms use different kits)
  • Provides an independent morphological record that can support any later dispute

A wrong Gram stain result puts the entire identification chain in error from the very start.

4.2.1 Sterility Testing Investigations

Original Text (English)

If a laboratory sterility test (USP <71>) result used for release testing of a pharmaceutical or biological drug product or a medical device indicates microbial growth in the microbiological growth medium (i.e., turbidity, pellicle formation, precipitation, or flocculent growth) during the incubation period and is subsequently confirmed via subculture and/or Gram reaction, it raises the question: Was the observed growth a result of a sampling error, laboratory error, or a true product contamination? This scenario with a finished product is commonly referred to as an 'out of specification (OOS) test result.' Due to the unique nature of viable microorganisms, unlike chemical analytes, microbiologists also describe these aberrant results as a 'microbiological data deviation (MDD).' The response to this presumptive failure to meet USP <71> requirements is to investigate the source of the microbial contamination in order to confirm the validity of the test results. The purpose of the investigation is to confirm that the test results were obtained while utilizing good laboratory practices, including proper aseptic technique, appropriate equipment and reagents, and sterile assay media/components within an appropriate Grade A/ISO 5 environment.

Deviations in equipment, procedures, or personnel performance that require corrective action should be discovered during the investigation. If the positive sterility test is determined to be valid, an investigation will be conducted to determine the root cause of the manufacturing practice that caused the tested product lot to become contaminated. If no definitive root cause is determined, then the manufacturing corrective actions might be based on the probable causes identified and the measures defined to prevent recurrence of such an event. The decision on the disposition of the batch needs to consider that, as no analytical cause was identified, the batch might be nonsterile. If a laboratory investigation concludes that errors or events caused the test contamination to come from the laboratory environment or met other compendial invalidation criteria, the sterility test result would be invalidated, and the sterility test would be repeated with the same number of units and quantity of product.

USP <71> requires that the sterility test media be visually examined for evidence of microbial growth at intervals during the incubation and at its conclusion. This evidence may present itself as turbidity throughout the media, a pellicle growing on the surface of the soybean-casein digest medium (SCDM), growth at the interface between the aerobic and anaerobic portions of the fluid thioglycollate medium (FTM), as floccular growth within the media, or as precipitation at the bottom of the media. The mode of growth should be documented within the sterility test report.

Although not specifically recommended in USP <71>, microscopic examination of suspected microbial growth and subculturing the contents of test media container/enclosed membrane canisters are common industry practice. The sampling of the media must be done aseptically to avoid additional microbial contamination. Incubation of the original container must complete the full 14-day period described in USP <71> in order to ensure discovery of slower growing microorganisms. Aliquot removal from the canisters (or sterility test vessel used for direct inoculation) can be challenging when using a sterile Pasteur pipette (or sterile syringe) as it is difficult to assure the same subsequent culture growth conditions that may be needed in the event of an investigation. For example, the proximity of the growth within the media may be diagnostic as to the type of microorganism(s) that had been recovered. Secondly, it is not advisable to disperse the macroscopic growth within the media, especially for FTM because the aeration of the media may be fatal for the presence of strict anaerobes.

Subculturing to isolate the suspected microbial contaminant may include:

  • Transferring up to 10 mL of the test media into fresh SCDM and FTM and incubating the media for at least 14 days at 20–25 °C and 30–35 °C, respectively. The subculture time of incubation should mimic the time period that was necessary for the original time of incubation in order to optimize microbial recovery required by the targeted species (for example, some environmental fungi require the lower temperature range and are inhibited by the higher temperature range).
  • Streaking out from the test media onto soybean-casein digest agar (SCDA) and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) and incubating aerobically at 30–35 °C for SCDA and 20–25 °C for SDA, until microbial growth is obtained and/or,
  • Alternative enrichment medium (i.e., Columbia blood agar) may also be used for subculturing from the primary SCDM and FTM to support slow growing fastidious microorganism.

The medium and mode of growth within the medium will provide insight as to whether the microorganism is a strict anaerobe, facultative anaerobe, or strict aerobe and what subculturing method would be most appropriate. Any isolates should be identified at least to species and, if available, strain-typed to aid the follow-up investigations with isolates recovered from the manufacturing or material bioburden. If subculturing fails to recover the microorganism, then a direct Gram stain on an aliquot of the suspect microbial growth should be performed. If no microbial presence is detected with the direct Gram stain, the turbidity may be a result of product-media precipitation or an artifact of the media after prolonged incubation (for example, the low percent of agar in the FTM can precipitate out of solution and causes medium turbidity).

Careful review and documentation of the microbial appearance within the SCDM and the FTM test tubes or canisters before any inadvertent jostling or shaking. This is especially true when the product may potentially make the test media turbid or cause a precipitation (e.g., suspensions or liposome-based drug products). These observations should be recorded in the sterility test report or, if available, photomicrographs can be taken with a camera mounted on the microscope.

In the absence of the isolation of a microorganism on subculture of the test media and/or no microscopic evidence of the presence of microorganisms, the deviation sterility test failure is not confirmed and, therefore, the product passes the test.

USP <71>, which has been harmonized with the European Pharmacopoeia and Japanese Pharmacopoeia, is frequently used as the method of choice for finished product release and is cited in many regulatory applications. The section entitled "Observation and Interpretation of Results" describes the four acceptable conditions that may be used to invalidate the initial test result and allow for retesting:

  1. The data of the microbiological monitoring of the sterility testing facility show a fault (Table 4.2.1-1🔍)
  2. A review of the testing procedure used during the test in question reveals a fault (Table 4.2.1-2🔍)
  3. Microbial growth is found in the negative controls (Section 4.2.1.1)
  4. After determination of the identity of the microorganisms isolated from the test, the growth of this species (or these species) may be ascribed unequivocally to faults with respect to the material and/or the technique used in conducting the sterility test procedure (Section 4.2.1.2)

If evidence of microbial growth is found, the product to be examined does not comply with the test for sterility unless it can be clearly demonstrated that the test was invalid for causes unrelated to the product to be examined. If the test is declared to be invalid, it is repeated with the same number of units as in the original test. If no evidence of microbial growth is found in the repeat test, the product examined complies with the test for sterility. If microbial growth is found in the repeat test, the product examined does not comply with the test for sterility.

Commentary and Guided Reflection

Core Concept: The Four Invalidation Conditions

USP <71> defines four conditions under which a sterility test can be "invalidated" and retested:

  1. Environmental monitoring (EM) data show a deficiency in the laboratory facility (Table 4.2.1-1🔍)
  2. Review of the operating procedure reveals a lapse (Table 4.2.1-2🔍)
  3. Microbial growth in the negative control (Section 4.2.1.1)
  4. Identification results show that the contamination clearly came from the laboratory environment or the operator's technique (Section 4.2.1.2)

These four conditions do not mean "pick any one and you may retest"; each one must be supported by sufficient objective evidence.

Important Clarification: Invalid ≠ Pass

This is the most common misconception. Invalidation means:

  • The original test result, because a laboratory error has been confirmed, cannot represent the true sterility status of the product
  • A repeat test is allowed, using the same number of units and the same quantity
  • The repeat test result is what is formally used as the basis for batch release

It does not mean the product has passed the sterility test. If growth is still seen in the repeat test, the batch does not meet the sterility requirement.

Regulatory inspectors are very sensitive to this logic: if a firm releases a batch directly on the grounds that "the original test was invalidated", without complete retest records, it will face serious observations.

Analogy: A Referee Disallowing a Goal

In a football match, when the referee disallows a goal (for example for offside), it does not mean the score is 0-0 and the result is settled. It only means that goal does not count and the match goes on.

Sterility test invalidation works the same way: that test "does not count", but the sterility status of the product is still unconfirmed. You must take the shot again (the retest) before you can decide the outcome.

Special Considerations for FTM

Fluid Thioglycollate Medium (FTM) is used to culture anaerobes and has several special handling points:

  • Do not shake or break up visible growth, because this can oxygenate the medium and kill strict anaerobes
  • The low-concentration agar in FTM can precipitate after prolonged incubation and cause pseudo-turbidity, which is a common source of false positives
  • The location of growth is itself diagnostic: near the surface = aerobes; at the bottom = anaerobes; uniform turbidity = facultative anaerobes

Original Text (English)

If the appearance of microbial growth is found in the negative or manipulation control (i.e., system control), they should be carefully reviewed for the presence of contamination. Commentary and Guided Reflection

Core Concept: The Purpose of the Negative Control

The negative control, also called the manipulation control or system control in sterility testing, is a culture medium container to which no test sample is added; it is handled and incubated under the same environmental conditions as the test samples.

Its purpose is to monitor whether the manipulation itself introduces contamination.

If growth appears in the negative control, it shows a problem with the environment or the manipulation itself, and the results of the entire test are called into question regardless of the test samples. This directly satisfies the third invalidation condition of USP <71>.

Analogy: The Laboratory's "Blank Exam Paper"

Imagine an exam in which the invigilator also submits a blank paper, and answers appear on it. This indicates a problem with the printing of the exam paper itself, not that a particular student cheated.

The negative control is that blank paper. It should not show any growth. If it does, the problem lies in the system itself.

Key Point: Contamination Does Not Always Look Like Uniform Turbidity

The source document specifically reminds us that not all contamination causes uniform turbidity of the medium. In practice, the following may appear:

  • Surface film: Strictly aerobic organisms form a film at the liquid surface that looks like an oil slick, not turbidity
  • Budding fungal growth on the membrane filter or glass wall: This may be tiny white spots that are easily overlooked
  • Sediment at the bottom: This must be distinguished from the false positive caused by FTM agar precipitate

Therefore, review of the negative control requires careful observation under good lighting, not just a visual check of overall turbidity.

Core Concept: Escalating Identification from "Same Species" to "Same Strain"

This section describes the operating logic of the most stringent invalidation condition:

  1. Initial comparison: The species name of the test isolate is the same as that of the EM isolate
  2. Strain typing confirmation: If the species name is the same, perform strain typing to confirm whether it is the same strain
  3. Attribution decision: If strain typing confirms that the two are the same strain, and the EM isolate came from the sterility testing room, the contamination can be "unequivocally ascribed" to the laboratory environment

"Unequivocally ascribe" is a very high standard in regulatory language: not "possible" but "certain". This requires molecular evidence at the strain level.

Cross-Contamination Risk from QC Challenge Organisms

A risk that is easily overlooked: if the positive organism in the test is of the same species as a QC challenge organism the laboratory uses for the growth promotion test or the method suitability test, genotypic comparison must be performed to rule out the possibility that the QC challenge organism escaped and contaminated the test.

This means the strain characteristics of QC challenge organisms need complete genotype records so that comparison can be made during an investigation.

Analogy: The Forensic DNA Comparison Process

Blood stains are found at a crime scene and a suspect has the same blood type, but the same blood type is not enough to convict. A full DNA comparison is needed.

A test isolate and an EM isolate with the same species name are like the same blood type. You still need a "DNA comparison", that is, strain typing, to confirm that they really are the same strain from the same contamination source.

CDMO Application: The Strategic Value of an Isolate Library

Effective strain comparison requires a complete facility isolate library that includes:

  • Historical EM isolates (with genotypic fingerprints)
  • Genotype records of all QC challenge organisms
  • Isolates from process water, equipment surfaces, and personnel gloves

This library is useful not only during investigations but is also an important tool for trend analysis and for improving the EM program. For a CDMO, where different clients' products may introduce different microorganisms, building an isolate library is especially important.

Core Concept: CAPA Must Correspond to the Invalidation Condition

The TR88 structure is very clear: four invalidation conditions, each matched to a set of CAPAs. This is no accident. When regulatory inspectors review a CAPA, they check:

  • Does the CAPA correspond to the confirmed root cause?
  • Is the CAPA specific and executable, or is it generic?
  • How will the effectiveness of the CAPA be verified?

A good CAPA must be "case-specific"; the source document stresses this term in particular.

Analogy: Treating the Cause, Not Just the Symptom

If you find that the cause of a fever is a bacterial infection, the CAPA is to use antibiotics. If your CAPA is "tell the patient to drink more water", that only treats the symptom, not the cause.

Similarly, if invalidation condition 1 (facility deficiency) is due to an overdue HEPA filter certification, the CAPA must include "establish a HEPA certification schedule management system", not just "strengthen cleaning".

Key CAPA for Condition 2: The Two-Operator Requirement

TR88 recommends a two-operator requirement for sterility testing. The reasons for this practice:

  • Reduced fatigue: Sterility test manipulations take a long time, and a single operator tires easily, leading to operating errors
  • Action verification: A second person confirms each critical step, reducing the risk of missed steps
  • Real-time recording: One person operates and one records, improving the timeliness and completeness of raw data

In a CDMO environment with limited staff, how to implement this requirement needs to be clearly defined in shift scheduling and SOPs.

CDMO Application: Strategy for Periodic Use of Sporicides

The CAPA for condition 1 specifically mentions the periodic use of a sporicidal agent. In a CDMO sterility testing facility:

  • VHP (vaporized hydrogen peroxide) is the most common sporicidal method for isolators and must be performed regularly
  • For laminar airflow (LAF) hoods, the use of a sporicidal agent needs to be integrated with the rotation plan for routine cleaning agents
  • The validation of sporicidal agents must be periodically reconfirmed, especially after facility changes

If a sporicide is introduced after an invalidation, there must be a record showing that this was a decision made within the CAPA framework, not a change to routine practice.

CAPA for Condition 4: The Regulatory Expectation for Consistent Identification Techniques

This CAPA looks simple but has deeper implications:

If you use MALDI-TOF to identify the test isolate, you must also reconfirm historical EM isolates by MALDI-TOF (or an equivalent technique); you cannot compare against Vitek 2 results.

The logic behind this requirement: the comparison of two identification results is scientifically meaningful only when "equivalent techniques and equivalent databases" are used. If the techniques are inconsistent, even identical results may lead a regulatory reviewer to question the validity of the comparison.

4.2.2 Bacterial Endotoxins Testing (BET) Investigations

4.2.2 Bacterial Endotoxins Testing Investigations

Compendial bacterial endotoxins testing (BET) detect and/or quantify lipopolysaccharide cell membrane components derived from the outer-cell membrane of Gram-negative bacteria that are most commonly responsible for human pyrogenic responses. This method is more comparable to chemical assay than to the traditional methods used for the detection and enumeration of microorganisms in pharmaceuticals, biopharmaceuticals, and medical devices. For this reason, the enzyme cascade-based Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) BET has unique analytical requirements that may affect the reliability of the test results. USP <85> Bacterial Endotoxin Tests describes the procedures for performing both the gel clot and spectrophotometric methods for detecting and quantifying bacterial endotoxins. Because the methods are based on enzymatic components and principles, incubation conditions (e.g., temperature and time) are important, and laboratory analysts should have a technical understanding of the potential inhibition and enhancement of the product matrix on the product/lysate mixture. Another critical factor is the measurement and, if necessary, the adjustment of the product–lysate mixture to fall within the required pH range as stated in the lysate instructions for use. If the pH is not within the specified range, the endotoxin test results can be significantly altered from the true results (usually lower). Therefore, it is important that analysts understand the details of the compendial test and are trained to recognize how changes in the procedures may cause erroneous results is imperative (14). Section 4.2.2 describes general approaches for investigating BET deviations and provides guidance and references that contain detailed protocols on how to conduct investigations.

A deviation may be due to one of the following from a partial list of reasons:

  • Error during product dilution (excessive volume transfer during serial dilution preparation), incorrect volume (too little or too much) during reagent rehydration, misplacement of handled test tubes causing a control standard endotoxin (CSE) serial dilution for positive control preparation and inhibition and enhancement results to be incorrect, mislabeling of test tubes, or a test tube mix-up between product and CSE serial dilutions
  • CSE cross-contamination caused by pipette tips (or pipets) used repeatedly
  • Automated pipettors or bulbs contaminated with aspirated endotoxin solution because of improper use or not being cleaned routinely
  • Working on a lab bench with a nearby splash sink that creates water aerosols (tap water commonly contaminated with Gram-negative bacteria)
  • Analyst not performing the test according to the prescribed method
  • Reagent(s) or equipment not adequately depyrogenated or not handled aseptically with pyrogen-free water
  • Unexpected occurrence during the analysis that interrupted the completion of the gel clot (e.g., table vibration from draw closure or fan vibration in air-circulating stand-up incubators)
  • Glucans, at high enough concentrations, present in the test material may cause false positives
  • Contaminated water baths used for incubation of gel clot test tubes

Section 4.2.2.1 compiles a number of factors important to BET that, if not performed correctly, may culminate in a faulty analysis.

Commentary

Core Concept: BET Is a Chemical Assay, Not a Microbial Growth Test

BET detects endotoxin, a chemical substance (lipopolysaccharide, LPS), not viable bacteria. The LAL reaction is an enzyme cascade: endotoxin activates Factor C, which activates Factor B, which finally cleaves coagulogen to form a gel or change light absorbance.

This means the failure modes of the test are completely different from those of culture methods. The problem is not "the organisms did not grow" but "the enzyme reaction was interfered with".

  • pH sensitivity: The LAL reaction requires pH 6.0–8.0; being too acidic or too alkaline lowers the result (false negative)
  • Inhibition: Proteins, surfactants, and chelating agents (EDTA) in the product matrix can inhibit the LAL enzymes
  • Enhancement: Some components instead amplify the signal and cause false positives
  • Glucan interference: Glucans (a fungal cell wall component) can activate the Factor G pathway and cause false positives

Everyday Analogy: Pregnancy Test vs. Blood Culture

Think of BET as a pregnancy test: it detects the presence of one specific chemical substance (hCG / endotoxin) and does not culture whether "bacteria grow". A pregnancy test is also affected by pH deviation and by expired reagents, and the principle is exactly the same.

This is why the first step in investigating a BET OOS is "are the reagents and operating conditions correct?" and not "was aseptic technique compromised?".

Key Point: The Most Common Causes of False Failures in the LAL Test

  • Errors in serial dilution (pipetting error, swapped tubes)
  • Reuse of pipette tips causing CSE cross-contamination
  • Splashing from a nearby sink: tap water itself contains Gram-negative bacteria
  • pH not checked or adjusted
  • Vibration or disturbance during incubation (gel-clot method)

4.2.2.1 Compendial Bacterial Endotoxins Test

4.2.2.1 Compendial Bacterial Endotoxins Test

When faced with an endotoxin LAL OOS result it would be best to start with a review of the mandatory requirements and controls in the harmonized compendial chapter, USP <85>. As with any testing used to release products, performing these key analytical steps and documenting them accurately and contemporaneously as required by company SOPs and the lysate manufacturer's instructions for use is essential. These records will prove critical in the event the laboratory management or auditors review or question the validity of the results. The harmonized compendial chapters include specific considerations for addressing BET analytical anomalies:

  • In the section for INTERPRETATION of the harmonized gel-clot method, when a positive result is found for only one duplicate of Solution A (product only), the test should be repeated. In the repeat test, the preparation under test complies with the test if a negative result is found for both replicates of Solution A. The preparation does not comply with the test if a positive result is found for one or both replicates of Solution A. However, if the preparation does not comply with the test at a dilution less than the maximum valid dilution (MVD), the test may be repeated using a greater product dilution, not exceeding the MVD.
  • Along with each gel-clot Limit and Quantitative Test, a 2λ positive product control, a 2λ positive control in BET water, and a negative control with just BET water should be included.
  • For the Photometric Quantitative Techniques, the absolute value of the correlation coefficient, r, must be 0.980 for the range of endotoxin concentrations used.
  • For the Photometric Quantitative Techniques, to be considered free of factors that interfere with the assay under the conditions of the test, the measured concentration of the endotoxin added to the sample solution must be within 50%–200% of the known added endotoxin concentration after subtraction of any endotoxin detected in the solution without added endotoxin. Note: Knowing how to interpret the value of the positive product control (PPC) is very important when reading either under or over the allowed range. Even an assay within the allowable PPC range may still give false test results, which may be caused by the natural endotoxin in the test sample and the spiked CSE combining to give an acceptable value for this control.
  • Additional suggestions on how to investigate bacterial endotoxin deviation can be found in USP <1085> Guidelines on the Endotoxins Test, in the section entitled “Out-of-Specification Results and Retesting Considerations” (21).
  • For those laboratories responsible for performing BET on sterile implantable devices or combination products (one that includes a medical device), specific guidance is provided in USP <161> Medical Devices–Bacterial Endotoxin and Pyrogen Tests (22). For medical device testing, careful attention to the compendial requirements should be given to performing the bacterial endotoxin extraction step from the device products.

Commentary

Core Concept: Key Compliance Check Points in USP <85>

When regulatory inspectors review a BET OOS, USP <85> is the first document they open. The following control points are the ones where deficiencies are most easily found:

  • Replicate tube reading rule: If only one of the two tubes of Solution A is positive, a repeat test is allowed, but both tubes must be negative to pass
  • MVD limit: Dilution cannot be extended indefinitely; it must stay within the Maximum Valid Dilution
  • PPC recovery 50–200%: This is the core window for assessing inhibition/enhancement; being within the range does not necessarily mean there is no problem
  • Correlation coefficient r ≥ 0.980: The quality requirement for the standard curve in photometric methods

Analogy: The PPC Is Like Calibrating the Taste of a Dish

The Positive Product Control (PPC) is like a chef calibrating his palate before serving by tasting a "standard dish" with a known amount of salt: if the saltiness you perceive is within the expected range, your senses (= the test system) can be trusted. But if the product itself already has natural salt, then even if it tastes "just right" after the addition, that does not mean the test system is free of problems. This is the trap in interpreting the PPC.

Key Point: The Three-Layer USP Document Structure

  • USP <85>: The mandatory compendial methods (gel-clot + photometric methods)
  • USP <1085>: A guidance chapter (non-mandatory) that offers recommendations for OOS investigations
  • USP <161>: Specific to medical devices and combination products (pay special attention to the extraction step)

4.2.2.2 FDA Guidance

4.2.2.2 FDA Guidance

When performing a deviation investigation for results associated with BET, the 2012 FDA Guidance for Industry: Pyrogen and Endotoxin Testing: Questions and Answers may offer valuable insights as to some regulatory expectations (23). In general, the guidance suggests that the sampling plan used by firms should consider the potential presence of bacterial endotoxin contamination in raw materials, in-process materials, and the finished product. Specifically, they should consider aspects of the manufacturing design, including consistency of a manufacturing process, impact of in-process hold-times, endotoxin-removal steps, and finished-product endotoxin specifications. Sections of this FDA guidance document may be particularly helpful during deviation investigations, for example:

When is retesting appropriate? When conflicting results occur within a test run, firms should consult USP Chapter <85>, Gel Clot Limits Test, Interpretation, for guidance on repeat testing. As specified in Chapter <85>, if the test failure occurred at less than the maximum valid dilution (MVD), the test should be repeated using a greater (product) dilution not exceeding the MVD… All testing procedures, including those for retesting within the above limits, should be specified in advance in the standard operating procedures approved by the firm’s quality control unit.

Because of the unique nature of endotoxins to clump or bind to container surfaces, the issue of sample storage and handling becomes important. The FDA guidance recommends firms “establish procedures for storing and handling (which includes product-mixing) samples for bacterial endotoxin analysis…”

FDA recommends that pooled samples be a composite of aseptically removed aliquots (after at least 30 seconds of vigorous mixing) from each of the product containers (24). In this way, the original, individual containers will be available for possible retesting in the event the pooled sample displays an OOS result.

…For purposes of evaluating the relative risk of product contamination, quantitative testing may be preferable to limit testing to facilitate product quality trending and to identify and correct excursions before they exceed the specification and cause product failure.

Therefore, when an endotoxin is detected, quantitating the actual amount present is preferred to depending on the guesswork of using a limit test approach.

Two other documents that may be helpful during the investigation of either a laboratory deviation or the manufacturing areas are: FDA's Inspection Technical Guides: No. 40 Bacterial Endotoxins/Pyrogens and standards document ANSI/AAMI ST72:2019 Bacterial Endotoxins—Test Methodologies, Routine Monitoring, and Alternatives to Batch Testing (25, 26). The AAMI standard is acknowledged as an acceptable standard for performing BET and has been referenced in the 2012 FDA Guidance document excerpted above. Both of these documents may be used by FDA and other international government investigators during their inspectional preparation of both parenteral and medical device manufacturers.

Commentary

Core Concept: The Special Physical Properties of Endotoxin Affect Sampling

The endotoxin molecule (LPS) is amphiphilic; it aggregates spontaneously in aqueous solution and also adsorbs readily to container surfaces. These two properties make the sampling step critical:

  • Aggregation effect: If a sample is not shaken sufficiently, LPS may settle at the bottom or on the tube wall, giving an uneven concentration at sampling
  • Container adsorption: Containers that are not adequately depyrogenated may "release" residual endotoxin and cause false positives
  • 30 seconds of vigorous shaking: An operating requirement explicitly set by the FDA, intended to break up aggregates and ensure representative sampling

Analogy: Quantitative vs. Limit Test = Blood Glucose Meter vs. Urine Glucose Strip

A limit test tells you only "above or not above the specification", like a urine glucose strip with just two colors, positive and negative. A quantitative test gives you the actual value, like a blood glucose meter that tells you whether it is 6.5 mmol/L or 12 mmol/L. The FDA recommends quantitative testing first, because trend management needs numerical values, not just a pass/fail judgment.

Key Point: Preconditions for Retesting

The FDA clearly states that the retest procedure must be pre-specified in the SOP. You cannot decide whether to retest, or how many times, after an OOS has occurred. This is the gray area most likely to be challenged in an audit.

In addition, retaining the original individual containers rather than combining them into a single pooled sample gives you a "fallback" to retest them individually when an OOS occurs.

Practical Action

Check your BET SOP: Does it clearly specify the shaking time for pooled samples (at least 30 seconds)? Are the retest conditions (when allowed, and the maximum number of times) defined in advance? If not, this should be a priority improvement item at the next SOP review.

4.2.3 Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing (AET) Investigations

4.2.3 Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing Investigations

The test methods and acceptance criteria for different dosage forms are defined in USP <51> Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing. In general, the test is only applicable to aqueous, multiple-use sterile and nonsterile drug products that may be subject to microbial contamination during use. USP <51> defines aqueous as a water activity (Aw) of more than 0.6. Products with Aw < 0.6 will not support the growth of microorganisms and may be demonstrably self-preserving. The test confirms the effectiveness of the antimicrobial preservative system and/or other supporting physicochemical attributes to meet product category criteria. Although a drug product would be required to meet the test criteria during development and to be demonstrated as effective in a long-term stability study, the test is a shelf-life specification, but not a release requirement. In contrast, the preservative concentration required within the formulation to meet the product category criteria at release and at expiry is a product specification included in the regulatory submission. Typically, USP <51> is looked upon as a stability-indicating pass/fail test, where the results in terms of log reduction of the challenge organisms at specified time intervals are evaluated against the log reduction requirement for the product category. Given the inherent variability of the test method, marginally preserved products have a potential to fail at one or more time intervals in long-term stability studies supporting marketed products (27).

Core Concept: The Investigation Logic Tree for AET

AET (antimicrobial effectiveness testing) is a test specific to multi-dose products and confirms that the product will not become microbially contaminated during use through repeated opening of the container. USP <51> divides products into Category A (injectables, ophthalmics), Category B (topicals, orals), and Category C (special dosage forms), each with different log reduction requirements.

  2. Is the inoculum quality correct? → Excessive strain passaging and reduced viability can cause false failures

AET is a stability-indicating test (shelf-life specification), not a batch release requirement. An OOS does not mean the batch must be recalled immediately; it means the long-term stability strategy needs to be evaluated. 4.2.4 Mycoplasma Testing Investigations

4.2.4 Mycoplasma Testing Investigations

Mycoplasma contamination is a serious concern when using mammalian, plant, and insect cell cultures in the development and production of cell-derived biopharmaceutical products for human use. These products may include viral vaccines, monoclonal antibodies, growth factors, replacement therapies, and gene and cell therapies. Contamination of cell culture ingredients and cell lines represent a potential patient safety risk as well as an economical loss in terms of reduced product yields, rejections, and recalls. To minimize these risks, master and working cell lines or banks, cell culture ingredients, inoculum expansions, process materials, and finished products are routinely screened for mycoplasma.

The most common contaminating mycoplasma species (95%), in descending order, are Mycoplasma orale (human cell lines), M. hyorhinis (porcine cell lines), M. arginini (bovine cell lines), M. fermentans (human cell lines), M. hominis (human cell lines), and Acholeplasma laidlawii (bovine cell lines).

Core Concept: Why Is Mycoplasma Especially Dangerous?

4.2.5 Biological Indicators Investigations

Biological indicators (BI) are used during the validation of moist heat, dry heat, and gaseous sterilization processes and during vapor-phase hydrogen peroxide decontamination cycles. BIs may also be used for monitoring routine cycles to support the release of products that are used by the pharmaceutical industry. Additionally, a BI can be used as a load monitor to support parametric release of moist-heat sterilized products in the U.S. Typically, they consist of high numbers of resistant bacterial spores with characterized calibrated D-values that are included in sterilization loads, retrieved, and cultured to demonstrate the lethality of their destruction during the sterilization cycle.

It is an expectation that a company will conduct confirmatory testing of the population and purity stated in the certificate of analysis of the incoming BI lot and growth of the cultured spore strip. Low counts may reflect the incomplete recovery from the matrix supporting the bacterial spores, whereas the growth of a heat-resistant spore-forming bacteria like Geobacillus stearothermophilus from a BI is most likely caused by contamination of the laminar flow hood and/or test supplies from positive controls in the testing environment (30).

Probable causes of failure would include gross mishandling, insufficient exposure to the sterilization parameters, and higher BI D-values that result in a lower probability of destruction in the sterilization cycle. Other points to consider include:

  • Were the BIs stored and handled prior to use, as recommended by the manufacturer?
  • Were the BIs incubated in accordance with regulatory expectations and manufacturer instructions?
  • Were the BIs used in the manner prescribed by the manufacturer?
  • Was the appropriate BI used for the product being tested (e.g., ampoule BI used for liquid products)?
  • In cases where the BI is directly inoculated into a solution or onto a substrate, what is the D-value as used in that form?

PDA Technical Report 51 Biological Indicators for Gas and Vapor-Phase Decontamination Processes: Specification, Manufacture, Control and Use provides further guidance on this topic (31).

Commentary

Core Concept: D-Value and Z-Value, the Language of Sterility Assurance

D-value (Decimal Reduction Time): The time required at a specified temperature to kill 90% (1 log) of the target organisms. For example, the D-value of G. stearothermophilus at 121°C is about 1.5 minutes.

Z-value (Temperature Coefficient): The temperature change required to change the D-value by 1 log (10-fold). For typical moist heat sterilization, Z = 10°C.

A BI failure ≠ a non-sterile product. The first directions for investigating a BI failure are:

  1. Were the sterilization cycle parameters really met (temperature, time, pressure records)?
  2. Is the D-value of the BI itself high (CoA recheck)?
  3. Was the BI placed at the coldest point (worst-case location)?
  4. Was there contamination of the G. stearothermophilus positive control during BI incubation?

Analogy: A BI Is Like the "Doneness Indicator" of an Egg

A BI is like putting a "standard egg" in the pot to judge whether the water has really boiled long enough. If that egg is not cooked, the first thing to confirm is not "whether the other eggs have a problem" but "whether this egg was placed in the spot hardest to heat" and "whether the thermometer made an error". A positive BI is a warning, not a verdict that directly negates the sterility of the whole load.

Key Point: The Role of the BI in Parametric Release

In the United States, sterile materials can use parametric release in place of the traditional sterility test, but only if BI data used as a load monitor support the cycle performance. Once a BI fails, the basis for parametric release is shaken, which makes the investigation more rigorous.

4.2.6 Aseptic Process Simulation Failures (Media Fills)

4.2.6 Aseptic Process Simulation Failures (Media Fills)

Proper design and execution of aseptic process simulations can be found in PDA Technical Report 22: Process Simulation for Aseptically Filled Products, FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing—Current Good Manufacturing Practice, PIC/S Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products, Guide to GMP for Medicinal Products, and ISO 13408-5 (3, 5, 32, 33). Any media-filled containers that, upon inspection, score as “turbid” during the incubation period should be examined by a microbiologist and transferred to the microbiology laboratory for investigation. The growth of microorganisms in culture medium may include any slight haze, pellicle formation, floccular amassment, or precipitation. The first task, prior to opening the container to subculture the media, is to establish if the container is flawed in any manner that would compromise the sterility of the medium. Damage may take the form of inadequate crimps to a stoppered vial, leakage around the container closure, or chips or cracks in the container that could threaten integrity. Any damage should be adequately recorded and, if possible, supplemented with photographs. Involving packaging experts in the laboratory investigation who can evaluate the extent of damage may be helpful, for instance, in performing physicochemical integrity testing to confirm that the damage would result in microbial ingress. The media fill may be inspected for damaged/nonintegral units, and those units should be discarded. The rationale for excluding these units from the final run tally should be fully documented and the deviation should be explained in the media fill report. (3, 34).

In the absence of obvious damage, the contents of a turbid container would be aseptically sampled and subcultured with appropriate solid media and incubated at 20–25 °C and 30–35 °C for three to seven days. The contents of the media fill containers may be examined microscopically for the presence of microorganisms. Any isolates should be identified at least to species and, preferably, strain-typed to aid the follow-up investigations. Guidance may be found in USP <1113> (16).

Commentary

Core Concept: A Media Fill Failure Is the Highest Alert Line in the Aseptic Process

APS (Aseptic Process Simulation, i.e., media fill) is a full simulation of the aseptic filling process. Unlike other microbiological tests, the media fill criterion is the most stringent:

  • A single contaminated unit constitutes a failure and requires a full investigation
  • Regulations require zero contamination (0/5000+) to meet the standard when the batch size is ≥ 5,000 units
  • After a failure, commercial production usually has to be suspended, and revalidation can occur only after the investigation is complete and CAPA has been implemented

The core question of the investigation: where did this contaminant come from?

  • The filling line (equipment surfaces, liquid contact paths)
  • Personnel (skin flora, timing of interventions)
  • Environment (airborne particles, EM data)
  • Container integrity defects (poor closure crimping, breakage)

Analogy: A Media Fill Is Like a "Scored Rehearsal" for Sterile Surgery

A media fill is like a surgical team simulating the complete operation without a patient and finally counting "which culture dish was contaminated". Once a dish (= a filled container) is turbid, you must ask whether the scalpel (equipment), the surgical gloves (personnel interventions), or the operating room air (environment) was the problem. Strain typing is like DNA forensics, helping you find the source at the crime scene.

Key Point: Container Integrity Is a Priority Item to Check

When a turbid container is found, assess container integrity before opening the vial. If the closure is poorly crimped (under-crimp) or the container is cracked, the container should be excluded from the count and the reason documented. However, the exclusion decision must be supported by a physical/chemical integrity test (CCI test) and cannot rest on visual judgment alone.

The EM for the same run shows one manual intervention during the filling operation, at a time close to the fill position of the turbid container. Core Concept: The Management Significance of Alert Level vs. Action Limit

EM systems use two tiers of limits:

  • Alert Level: An early warning signal that a trend may be worsening and that monitoring and investigation should be strengthened. It does not require an immediate production stop, but it must be recorded and followed up.
  • Action Limit: Exceeding this value requires immediate action: start a deviation investigation, assess product risk, and increase disinfection frequency.

The logical order of an EM deviation investigation:

  1. First rule out sampling technique problems (the most common source of false positives)
  2. Confirm the suitability of media and equipment
  3. Identify the isolate to species level
  4. Compare against historical EM trend data (has this organism appeared before? is there an upward trend?)
  5. Trace the contamination source (personnel / water system / raw materials / environment)

Analogy: EM Is the Cleanroom's "Thermometer + Pulse Monitor"

An alert level is a body temperature of 37.5°C: worth noticing but possibly just a mild cold; an action limit is 39°C: you must see a doctor. But even at 39°C, the doctor's first step is to "first rule out an error in how the temperature was taken" (underarm not held tight? faulty thermometer?), not to prescribe a fever reducer straight away. The EM investigation principle of "first confirm the sampling technique" follows the same logic.

Key Point: Identifying the Isolate to Species Level Is Critical

Different species point to different contamination sources:

  • Staphylococcus epidermidis (Gram-positive cocci) → personnel skin
  • Bacillus spp. (Gram-positive spore-forming rods) → environment / raw materials
  • Pseudomonas spp. (Gram-negative rods) → water system / biofilm
  • Cladosporium spp. (mold) → HVAC / filters

The identification result directly determines the direction of the CAPA; you must never stop at recording only "Gram-negative rods".

4.2.8 Utilities Monitoring

4.2.8 Utilities Monitoring

4.2.8.1 Pharmaceutical-Grade Water Systems

The purpose of the laboratory investigation is to determine whether a definitive source of sampling and/or laboratory error contributed to the root cause of the deviation. This section addresses the initial and extended laboratory investigations associated with samples collected from pharmaceutical-grade water systems, such as purified water and water for injection (WFI).

Microbiological testing of pharmaceutical water systems, including the source water (i.e., potable water) and purification steps, typically consists of total viable microbial count, BET, and/or a test for the absence of coliforms, depending on the grade of water and any local regulatory requirements. After a pharmaceutical water system is successfully validated, both the system and the source are subject to periodic microbial monitoring using standard methods. Action limits and alert levels should be established to recognize adverse trends in the water system. These alert levels or action limits should be based on historical data, the intended purpose of the sampling plan, as well as the recommended compendial or regulatory limits. In general, specifications are not set for water systems.

In a well-designed, well-operated pharmaceutical water system, sanitized by maintaining a minimum turbulence level and elevated temperature (> 65 °C) or periodic heat or chemical sanitization, test results above the action limit should be very rare.

Several events can signal that a laboratory investigation should be conducted:

  • A single test result that exceeds an action limit
  • Multiple test results that exceed an alert level, thus indicating an adverse trend
  • Isolation of microorganisms of concern based on the dosage forms manufactured in the facility

The cross-functional team conducting the laboratory investigation should review the collection and microbiological testing of the subject water sample to determine any potential sampling and laboratory errors. The data collected should include the sampling location, sample containers, sampling technique, and any alert level or action limit excursions associated with the sampling location, loop, or water system, and the operators collecting the water samples.

If this investigation does not identify a definitive source of laboratory error to explain the deviation result, the laboratory should immediately notify the owner of the utility system, operational users, and the QCU.

4.2.8.1.1 Evaluation of Sample Handling

The methods used for preparing, storing, and attaching hoses and sampling apparatus to the sample ports are critical. Regulatory guidance recommends that each point of use should be flushed using the same flushing procedure used in the manufacturing process (35).

The potential for changes in the microbial population in samples should be limited by restricting the holding time before testing and controlling the storage temperature. If the samples cannot be tested within two hours, they should be transported and stored at refrigeration temperature for a maximum of 24 hours. In situations where even 24 hours is not possible (such as when using off-site contract laboratories), it is particularly important to qualify the microbiological sample hold times and storage conditions. (See APHA/AWWA/WEF Standard Methods, USP <1231>, EMA 496873, and Japan: G8 for guidance (28, 36-38). Prior to removing the test aliquot, all samples should be adequately mixed to ensure a homogeneous mixture is achieved. Any parameters for storage and transportation of water samples that are different from standard methods should be qualified to ensure the samples are not negatively affected, resulting in potentially invalid results.

4.2.8.1.2 Evaluation of Test Methods

Review of all materials associated with the testing and their appropriateness for the grade of water tested is required in a laboratory investigation. Sterility of the membrane filters, sample containers, pipettes, and utensils used to perform testing must be suitable for their intended use and must conform within sterilization dating. Also, all diluents and reagents used must be suitable (such as sterile and/or endotoxin-free) and within their expiry dating.

Any deviation from these requirements must be evaluated and investigated. Confluent microbial growth, in contrast to discrete colonies on the membranes or pour plates, may be indicative of equipment contamination. The position of the analyst’s head, hands, and arms in the path of the airflow (preferably in a laminar flow hood with horizontal air flow) over the test materials may also be a source of microbial contamination (personnel-related considerations are discussed further in Section 5.2.1).

The identity of the microorganism(s) associated with the investigation may also reveal information about the source of contamination. The most common water isolates are Gram-negative, nonfermenting, rod-shaped bacteria historically described as pseudomonads, which may be indicators for a biofilm formation within the water system. The recovery of organisms that do not thrive or survive under the low ionic strength/low carbon conditions found in a pharmaceutical water system may suggest contamination of the sample or insufficient control or flushing of the sampling apparatus and/or hoses attached at the port. Gram-positive cocci commonly found on the human skin, such as Staphylococcus epidermidis, can be indicative of potential sampling contamination, while Gram-positive spore-formers, such as Bacillus species or the fungus Cladosporium species, can be indicative of potential environmental contamination.

4.2.8.1.3 Evaluation of Trending Data

Reviewing the historical test data will help determine if an adverse trend is associated with individual points-of-use, loops, and water systems. This information will be also useful for the manufacturing investigation.

Commentary

Core Concept: Microbial Control Mechanisms of the WFI System

Microbial control in pharmaceutical water systems (especially WFI) relies on two main mechanisms:

  • Heat maintenance: The WFI loop circulates at >65°C, a temperature at which most non-thermophilic bacteria cannot multiply
  • Turbulence: A minimum flow velocity ensures that biofilm cannot establish itself stably on the pipe wall

The most dangerous failure mode is biofilm: after Gram-negative rods such as Pseudomonas form a biofilm at low-flow or dead-leg locations, it sheds periodically into the main piping and causes repeated excursions. This is one of the most common root causes in Phase II investigations.

Order of the sampling investigation: first rule out sampling errors → then evaluate the test method → finally analyze trend data.

Analogy: Pipe Biofilm Is Like Limescale, but More Dangerous

Biofilm is like a city of microorganisms growing on the inner wall of a stainless steel pipe: the outer layer is protected by slime (polysaccharide) that disinfectants struggle to penetrate, and even if the upstream water quality is acceptable, the biofilm keeps "replenishing" the bacterial counts at downstream sampling points. When Pseudomonas is repeatedly isolated from WFI, you can almost directly suspect a biofilm problem at a specific pipe location, requiring stronger pasteurization or chemical sanitization.

Key Time Windows for Sample Handling

  • Testing within 2 hours at room temperature is acceptable (ideal)
  • Beyond 2 hours: the sample must be refrigerated, for a maximum of 24 hours
  • Shipment to an outsourced laboratory (>24 hours): validation data are needed to support the extended hold conditions
  • Before sampling, the sample point must be flushed according to the manufacturing flow
  • Mix the sample thoroughly before testing to ensure homogeneity

4.3 Phase I: Conclusion and Next Steps

4.3 Phase I: Conclusion and Next Steps

Evaluation and questions related to the Phase I deviation investigation must use strong, evidence-based data. The investigation may not be definitive but may suggest probable cause(s). The Phase I investigation commonly concludes with one of three possible outcomes:

  1. Events uncovered during the investigation may be responsible for the deviation and present a clear root cause that is directly responsible for the deviation. Examples could show contamination due to failure to follow SOPs, use of poor aseptic techniques during either sampling or testing, a recognized laboratory error, or the use of nonsterile testing materials. There may be a direct link between isolate identification and the same contamination from the testing environment. In these cases, the test may be considered invalid.

    In the case of invalid tests, the Phase I investigation would conclude without advancing to the Phase II manufacturing investigation, or where Phase I and Phase II investigations were undertaken concurrently, the cancellation of the Phase II investigation would be warranted. Depending on the sample type and source of contamination, resampling or retesting may also be considered as long as the QCU agrees. CAPAs must be implemented (preferably prior to any retesting or resampling), however, in order to remediate the original cause of the invalidation.

  2. While the investigation might have revealed issues, there may be insufficient evidence to directly correlate the investigative findings to the deviation. For example, if the EM conducted during the testing showed microorganisms present but the organisms were a different genus and species than the contamination within the sample, the environmental results may cast doubt on the sample, but they would not invalidate the original result. Thus, the Phase I laboratory investigation would be found inconclusive, not providing sufficient evidence to invalidate the result, and the result is deemed to be valid. The investigation would then proceed to a Phase II manufacturing investigation.

  3. The results of the investigation did not show any evidence of testing failure. In other words, it can be clearly demonstrated that all procedures were followed, analysts were properly qualified to perform the testing, and all materials, equipment, and testing environment were considered appropriate for the test. The calculations for reporting data and all final results were correct and verified. Therefore, the deviation is confirmed and deemed valid, so the investigation must proceed to Phase II in order to determine the source of contamination from the processes used during manufacturing.

Commentary

Analogy: Three Verdicts in a Court Trial

Conclusion 1 (invalid test) = "acquitted": there is concrete counter-evidence (Lab Error), the facts of the offense cannot be established, and the case is closed. But CAPA is the "bail condition" to prevent reoffending.

Conclusion 2 (inconclusive) = "doubts exist but the evidence is insufficient to convict": the case is passed to a higher court (Phase II) for continued review.

Conclusion 3 (confirmed deviation) = "direct indictment": the investigation is clear-cut, there is no Lab Error, the deviation is established, and the manufacturing side must be held accountable (Phase II).

Key Point: CAPA Must Be Implemented Before the Retest

This is the point regulators care about most: you cannot retest first, find that the new result passes, and then go back and add a CAPA. The correct order is:

  1. Identify the root cause of the Lab Error
  2. Implement the CAPA (procedure correction, retraining, equipment calibration, etc.)
  3. Only after the QCU has approved the CAPA may the retest/resample be performed

If this order is skipped, FDA/EMA inspectors will question the credibility of the retest result.

  • BET (4.2.2): LAL is an enzyme reaction, not a microbial culture. Failure modes arise from pH deviation (6.0–8.0 required), inhibition/enhancement effects, and handling contamination (tap water splashes, CSE cross-contamination). The USP <85> PPC recovery of 50–200% is the core compliance indicator; the retest procedure must be pre-specified in the SOP.
  • AET (4.2.3): USP <51> applies to multi-dose aqueous products and is a stability-indicating test, not a release specification.   • Mycoplasma (4.2.4): A risk specific to biologics (MAbs, gene/cell therapy).   • BI (4.2.5): D-value (time for a log reduction) and Z-value (temperature coefficient) are the core parameters of sterility assurance.   • Media Fill (4.2.6): The most stringent threshold in the aseptic process: a single turbid container triggers a full investigation. Investigation order: first confirm container integrity → then isolate and culture → identify the strain to species level, ideally with strain typing, to link the filling line, personnel, or environment as the contamination source.
  • EM (4.2.7): Distinguish Alert Level (early warning) from Action Limit (immediate action). The investigation first rules out sampling technique problems, then identifies the isolate to judge the contamination source (skin flora vs. water system flora vs. environmental flora), and compares against historical trends.
  • Water Systems (4.2.8): The typical root cause for WFI is dead-leg biofilm (Pseudomonas). Before sampling, flush according to the process; test within 2 hours (or refrigerate for ≤ 24 hours). Confluent growth suggests equipment contamination rather than a water quality problem.
  • The three Phase I conclusions (4.3): (1) Lab Error found → test invalid → CAPA first → retest → no move to Phase II; (2) doubts but insufficient evidence → inconclusive → move to Phase II; (3) no sign of Lab Error → deviation confirmed → Phase II is mandatory.

5.0 Phase II: Manufacturing Investigation - Overview

5.0 Phase II: Manufacturing Investigation

Once laboratory error has been eliminated or found inconclusive through the Phase I investigative process, the investigation focuses on the manufacturing facility, personnel, processes, and materials. A manufacturing investigation includes, but is not limited to, several principal elements:

  • The cross-functional investigation team must represent all departments responsible for handling the deviated sample whether it is a pharmaceutical excipient, in-process intermediate, drug substance, or drug product. This component is essential to conducting an effective investigation. The QCU or another suitable individual may lead the team, which should include representatives from manufacturing, plant engineering, process validation, maintenance, and quality control. In a microbiological-related investigation, the contribution of a microbiologist on the team will be critical.
  • The QCU should have overall responsibility for managing or approving the outcome of the manufacturing investigation and for notifying upper management of the investigation findings and batch disposition.
  • Each investigation should be thoroughly documented and include a time for completion, as specified by company policies and procedures. Microbiological-related investigations are complex and may not be able to be completed within the specified time period; in these cases, an SOP-governed mechanism should be in place for extending the completion date. This extension must be justified and supported by the QCU.

While additional assessments can be performed if required by company policy or procedure, every manufacturing investigation should include the following actions:

  • Determine all batches in scope. Does the initial scope of the investigation need to be expanded based on laboratory findings? Batches under investigation should not be released.
  • Determine the definitive or probable cause (root cause analysis). The investigation must be comprehensive, especially if the probable cause may not be identified. When conducting the root cause analysis, all aspects must be considered, and a thoroughly objective approach must be taken to evaluate the cause of the deviation. Identifying the probable cause is critical. It determines the type and degree of CAPAs implemented, which affects the likelihood of the deviation recurring.
  • Determine if this is a recurrence of a previous deviation or a first-time incident. Is there a pattern of microbial contamination? Were previous CAPAs effective?
  • Determine the potential impact. What impact might this incident have on other batches manufactured in the facility? This impact assessment may include pharmaceutical ingredients, manufacturing equipment, or other processing lines in the same facility, other sites within the company that are manufacturing the same or related products, and distributed product.
  • Determine the level of risk. What risk might be posed to a person taking the product? What effect could there be on the stability, safety, and efficacy of the product over the shelf life of the product?
  • Identify CAPAs required and develop a plan for implementation. Create a list of all the possible solutions that would prevent the deviation from recurring and select the most effective changes for implementation.
  • Clearly identify the deviation. State what the deviation is and how it exceeded the limits. Be as specific, simple, and concise as possible.
  • Decide what, if any, manufacturing should cease during the investigation. Depending on the nature of the deviation (e.g., high microbial counts, damage to facility, microorganisms identified), the team should consider recommending that manufacturing in the affected area should cease until remediation (e.g., extra cleaning and sanitization, more environmental sampling) has been completed and before the investigation is closed. All actions taken to demonstrate containment of the deviation should be documented.
  • Maintain clear communications. Interview personnel involved and revise the investigation as needed. Provide routine updates to management and ensure that members of the team are well informed and kept up to date on the progress of the investigation.
  • Inspect the area where the deviation occurred. Locate the deviation on a plan of the facility and look at surrounding areas that might contribute to the microbial contamination. Consider personnel and material flows, and nonroutine events, as well as temperature, humidity, and space pressurization excursions.
  • Look for trends. Review prior related investigations to assess. Is there an adverse trend—same location, common personnel involvement, recurring microorganisms, root cause? If an adverse trend is identified, act promptly to remediate the problem.
  • Evaluate data. A thorough review of production or batch records should be conducted to determine if falsification or incorrect recording of data from either intentional or unintentional human error during the time of recording results occurred.

While SOPs are crucial to effectively manage an investigation, other tools can provide valuable assistance in identifying possible causes of the deviation, such as brainstorming, fishbone diagram, FMEA/HACCP, cause-and-effect analysis, and the 5-why technique.

Commentary - 5.0 Phase II Overview

Core concept: what triggers Phase II

Once Phase I (the laboratory investigation) is complete, two situations trigger Phase II:

  • Laboratory error ruled out: Phase I confirms that the deviation is real, so the investigation must move to the manufacturing side.
  • Phase I is inconclusive: laboratory error can be neither excluded nor confirmed, and the conservative principle requires the investigation to continue.

This logic matters - regulators do not accept closing a case just because Phase I did not find a problem.

Analogy: a cross-functional investigation is like reconstructing a crime scene

Imagine a sophisticated burglary: you need forensic examiners (QC Microbiology), building experts (Engineering), security records (manufacturing records) and maintenance logs (Maintenance) to piece it together. If any one department is missing, key clues may be overlooked. The QCU acts as the lead investigator, responsible for the final decision and for notifying management.

Key point: deciding when to widen the scope of the investigation

"Identify all batches involved" is the first action item, for a very practical reason: if the batches before and after on the same production line are also affected but have already been released, the risk spills over to the market. The challenge for a CDMO is that the same facility may run products for several clients at the same time, so once shared equipment is identified, the scope can quickly expand to batches across clients.

Investigation toolbox

TR88 lists a number of RCA tools; these are the ones most commonly used in sterile manufacturing:

  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram: visualizes cause-and-effect relationships using the 6M framework; described in detail in Section 5.1.
  • 5-Why technique: suits deviations with a clear causal chain; ask "why" five levels deep.
  • FMEA: when a deviation keeps recurring, evaluates systemic failure modes.
  • Brainstorming: cross-functional brainstorming to make sure rare scenarios are not missed.

5.1 Root Cause Analysis

5.1 Root Cause Analysis

When writing a manufacturing investigation, use of root cause analysis tools are recommended. One common approach is the use of a fishbone diagram (Figure 5.1-1); it can be used to ensure that all inputs that may have contributed to the deviation are adequately considered and assessed as a potential root cause.

Thinking through the entire scope of factors that may have influenced the deviation is very important, as is recording each potential root cause identified in the investigation, even if it is determined not likely to have been a contributing factor. For example, if pharmaceutical ingredients and packaging components were assessed and were determined to not have caused the deviation, the investigation should reflect that information. This demonstrates that the investigator performed due diligence in examining the potential impact of these materials as a source of contamination and subsequently ruled them out as a potential root cause.

The categories of contributing factors to be considered in a root cause analysis may vary based on the process involved. They can also be modified depending on the situation, for example, the dosage form, facility design, or nature of the deviation itself, location within the facility, sample type, classified area level, or involvement of personnel. The investigating team is responsible for ensuring that all contributing factors have been assessed so that no potential root cause of the deviation is overlooked.

Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram

The fishbone diagram, also called the Ishikawa diagram, is shaped like a fish:

  • Head = the problem (the deviation itself, for example an EM excursion)
  • Main spine = the six categories (6M)
  • Small bones = the specific factors under each category

The 6M categories map to sterile manufacturing as follows:

  • Man (people): training, gowning, behavior
  • Machine (equipment): HVAC, filling machine, sterilizer
  • Material: raw materials, packaging components, cleaning agents
  • Method: SOPs, process steps
  • Measurement: EM methods, culture media, sampling technique
  • Environment: differential pressure, temperature, cleanroom design

Analogy: "ruled-out items" must be documented too

Newcomers often overlook this point. Imagine you are a detective: you check every suspect and finally settle on one. But if you did not record why you excluded the others, the judge will question whether your investigation was complete. Regulators think the same way - during an FDA or EMA inspection they will ask, "Did you evaluate XYZ?" If the investigation report does not mention it, it is as if it was never done.

Key point: adjust the depth of the investigation to the situation

TR88 states clearly that the scope of the 6M categories can be adjusted according to the following factors:

  • Dosage form (injectable vs. oral solid)
  • Facility design (isolator vs. open cleanroom)
  • Nature of the deviation itself (single event vs. trend)
  • Sampling location (ISO 5 vs. ISO 8)

This gives investigators reasonable flexibility, but it also means you must explain in the report "why a category does not apply"; you cannot simply leave it out.

In practice: fishbone challenges at a CDMO

At a CDMO, the processes of several clients may be running in the same facility at the same time. When a deviation occurs, the "Material" bone of the fishbone may need to cover all of the following: materials you purchased yourself, API supplied by the client, and shared cleaning agents - each supply chain is an independent contamination pathway.

Commentary - Impact Assessment and Conclusion Levels

The four investigation conclusion levels

TR88 clearly distinguishes four levels, which is very important in communication with regulators:

  • Root Cause: high certainty, supported by direct evidence. This is the ideal conclusion, but it is hard to reach in microbial deviations.
  • Most Probable Cause: the most likely cause, but one that cannot be confirmed 100%. This is the conclusion level regulators accept most often in practice.
  • Possible Cause: plausible, but the evidence is not sufficient to confirm it and more data are needed.
  • Unknown Cause: the data are not sufficient to draw any conclusion. This is the hardest conclusion to justify to regulators and must be accompanied by a strong CAPA.

Analogy: "most probable cause" is like a legal standard of reasonable doubt

"Most Probable Cause" is like "beyond reasonable doubt" in a criminal trial - you do not need to be 100% certain, but your argument must be convincing enough for the reviewer to accept the explanation as reasonable. The key is that your CAPA must target this most probable cause directly, rather than a generic "strengthen training".

Key point: the layers of patient risk assessment

The impact assessment requires patient risk to be evaluated across several dimensions:

  • Route of administration: injectables (intravenous, intramuscular) carry far greater risk than oral or topical products.
  • Target population: immunocompromised, pediatric and post-operative patients are high-risk groups.
  • Microbial virulence: even if it is not directly pathogenic, a microorganism may produce toxins, degrade the API, change viscosity or cause discoloration.
  • Antimicrobial resistance: if the product itself contains an antibiotic, is the isolated microorganism resistant? This directly affects the assessment of efficacy.

For a CDMO, the final risk assessment usually needs the involvement of the client (MAH), because they hold the clinical data on the target population.

In practice: how do you decide the conclusion level?

The next time you write an investigation report, state clearly in the "Conclusion" section which level was reached (Root Cause / Most Probable Cause / Possible / Unknown), and list the evidence that supports that level. This lets FDA/EMA reviewers see the depth of your investigation at a glance, and helps you target the CAPA design that follows.

Commentary - CAPA Plan Elements

Three core elements of CAPA

An effective CAPA plan must be:

  • Targeted: the CAPA must directly eliminate the confirmed root cause or most probable cause, rather than a generic "raise employee awareness".
  • Trackable: every action item must have a clear due date and owner, and be tracked in an electronic deviation management system.
  • Verifiable: after the CAPA is closed, there must be a defined point in time for an effectiveness check to confirm that the deviation has not recurred.

Analogy: CAPA is like a follow-up clinic visit after surgery

Once the surgery (the immediate corrective action) is done, the patient needs a follow-up visit to confirm that the wound has healed and function has recovered. The effectiveness check is that follow-up visit - you cannot close the case right after the operation; you must confirm the patient has really recovered. If the deviation recurs six months later, the surgery may have treated the symptom but not the cause, and the root cause needs to be re-evaluated.

Key point: process owner buy-in

TR88 specifically stresses that CAPA needs "agreement with the process owner". This is not bureaucracy but a practical necessity: if the manufacturing department does not accept a CAPA, then even if it is marked complete in the system, the actual execution may be watered down. The role of the quality unit is to build consensus, not to assign actions unilaterally. In a CDMO setting, some CAPAs may also need client approval (such as process changes), which affects timeline planning.

Key point: re-evaluating alert levels and action limits

Table 5.1-3🔍 mentions confirming whether the current alert level and action limit are still appropriate. This is an easily overlooked CAPA direction - sometimes the root cause of a deviation is not the contamination event itself, but limits that are set too loosely, so that no early intervention occurs when a trend worsens. Periodically re-evaluating monitoring limits is the mark of a mature EM program.

In practice: what can your electronic system do?

TR88 clearly recommends using an electronic investigation database to track CAPAs. Evaluate your current system:

  • Can the system automatically remind users of CAPA due dates?
  • Can it track the reasons for delayed execution, together with the authorization records?
  • Can it set automatic due-date reminders for effectiveness checks?
  • Can it perform trend analysis across batches and products to identify CAPA failure modes?

If the current system lacks these functions, that in itself is a systems improvement worth raising as a CAPA.

5.2.1 Sterility-Positive Investigations

5.2.1 Sterility-Positive Investigations

Should the laboratory confirm that a positive sterility result is valid, and no fault was attributed to the laboratory and actual testing of samples submitted for analysis, the lot of product or material should be considered nonsterile, and a comprehensive investigation into the source of contamination is required.

Commonly, this is a collaborative effort between the QCU, laboratory, and production along with any additional SMEs relevant to the manufacturing facilities and ancillary equipment. In order to guide this step of the investigation, both scientific and regulatory considerations are provided to incorporate into your investigative strategy.

All sterility test isolates should be identified to the species level, and the data should be reviewed for any potential linkages to organisms isolated from the production environments, personnel who participate in the manufacturing process directly or indirectly by handling of the sampling containers throughout the process, and any in-process bioburden testing. If the same species of microorganism was recovered from EM or supporting activities during batch manufacturing or testing, these colonies should be identified to the strain-type level. Using the same microbial identification platform for both the sterility test isolate(s) and any isolates recovered from the production areas is advisable to eliminate potential differences in software databases if using two different microbial identification platforms.

Useful regulatory guidance for conducting a sterility test failure investigation for an aseptically filled product are the 2004 FDA guidance on sterile drug products and some insightful chapters in the PDA book Microbial Risk and Investigations. Experience has shown that the investigational approach for aseptically filled sterile products will be significantly more complex than investigating a sterility test failure associated with terminal modalities.

Personnel conducting manual aseptic operations in open ISO 5/Grade A environments (e.g. BSCs, LFHs, etc.) are monitored prior to each exit from the Grade A area via touch plates/finger dab sampling, sleeve sampling and other qualified gown locations using TSA contact and standard plates. Commentary: 5.2.1 Sterility-Positive Investigations

Core concept: sterility positive = the most serious deviation result

A sterility positive is the most serious deviation result in sterile manufacturing. Once the laboratory confirms that the result is valid (laboratory error ruled out), the batch is deemed nonsterile and a full Phase II investigation must be started.

The investigation has one core question: when, where and how did the contamination get in? Answering it requires the QCU, the laboratory and production to work together, bringing in an SME (subject matter expert) where necessary.

Analogy: from species to strain is like going from "nationality" to "fingerprint"

Think of microbial identification as identifying a person: knowing only "Asian" (genus level) is not enough, "Taiwanese" (species level) is better, but what is really useful is the "ID number" (strain level). TR88 requires that if the same species is found in environmental monitoring (EM) or during the batch process, strain-type identification must be performed to decide whether it is "the same person" as the isolate from the positive sterility test sample.

Comparing results from the same identification platform is more reliable - databases from different vendors differ, and mixing them may cause one strain to be misidentified as two.

Investigation focus: Table 5.2.1-1🔍 seven areas to check

  • Equipment sterilization: biological indicator storage, introduction of new equipment, pre-sterilization bioburden
  • In-process bioburden: trends for WFI and product in-process controls
  • Batch monitoring data: viable particles, non-viable particles, grade A excursions
  • Personnel interventions and glove monitoring: non-standard interventions, aseptic technique during in-process sampling
  • Batch manufacturing records: hold times, filter integrity, sterilization cycle parameters
  • Historical review: common factors in past failures, adequacy of EM sampling location coverage
  • Change management and APS: HEPA repairs, flooding/leak events, whether the APS was completed within 6 months

Commentary: 5.2.2 Investigating Sources of Bacterial Endotoxin

Core concept: endotoxin = the "remains" of gram-negative bacteria

Bacterial endotoxin is the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the cell wall of gram-negative bacteria. LPS persists after the bacteria die and is extremely heat resistant - ordinary sterilization conditions cannot remove it; it can only be destroyed by dry heat at 250°C or above (depyrogenation).

The direction of the investigation therefore differs from a sterility positive: the question is not "where did the viable organisms come from" but "at which step did gram-negative bacteria proliferate and leave LPS contamination behind".

Analogy: endotoxin is like "DNA at a crime scene"

Even if the culprit (the bacteria) has been killed, DNA (LPS) is still left at the scene. You have to trace where that DNA was left: the water system? Raw materials? Inadequate equipment cleaning? Or purified water mistakenly used in place of WFI?

This is why Table 5.2.2-1🔍 specifically stresses "whether equipment that should have been rinsed with WFI was rinsed with purified water" - the limit for purified water (≤100 CFU/mL) is far looser than that for WFI (<0.25 EU/mL), so mixing them up can introduce LPS.

Key points to check

  • Does the water system have a history of gram-negative organisms or excursions?
  • Have excipients of natural origin (plant/animal) been screened for endotoxin?
  • Is the validation status of the depyrogenation oven/tunnel still valid?
  • Do the compressed air lines contain water (standing water is a breeding ground for gram-negative bacteria)?
  • Has the CMO issued a notification of process changes?
5.2.3 Pharmaceutical Ingredient-Related Investigations

5.2.3 Pharmaceutical Ingredient-Related Investigations

Pharmaceutical ingredients contaminated by Gram-negative bacteria may result in endotoxin contamination. Mold contamination could potentially lead to mycotoxin contamination. GMP requirements, including those identified in 21 CFR 210, 21 CFR 211, and ICH Quality Guideline Q7, include specific definitions as well as requirements for procedures for sampling and testing.

Representative samples for microbiological testing of a pharmaceutical ingredient are taken from individual drums of the incoming shipment but the ingredient lots may not be well mixed. Creating composite samples from the top, middle, and bottom of individual drums, or taking samples from each individual drum may mask pockets of microbial contamination. Unless the warehouse sampling occurs in a sampling booth, under controlled conditions, using trained personnel with the appropriate PPE and sample containers, there is a potential for sample contamination.

With respect to the manufacturing investigation, a pharmaceutical company will probably not be able to gain full access to details of the ingredient supplier's manufacturing process or visit the manufacturing site to conduct an investigation, unless it is a requirement in the quality agreement. Many pharmaceutical excipients are viewed as commodities that are supplied to other industries without GMP compliance. Many excipient suppliers set their specifications based solely on the relevant USP monograph requirements, which may not include microbial requirements.

Core concept: excipients are not necessarily made under GMP

Many pharmaceutical excipients are chemical or agricultural commodities, and the supplier's specification only matches the USP compendial monograph - and USP monographs often contain no microbial limit requirements. Compositing top, middle and bottom samples from the same drum into one sample is standard practice, but it has a dilution effect: if contamination is concentrated in one hot spot, the mixed result may fall just below the limit and let the lot pass release. TR88 points out this risk explicitly - especially for powder raw materials in large bulk drums.

Practical points for CDMOs

  • Excipients of natural origin (plant/animal/biological) carry a higher contamination risk than synthetic ones and need stronger monitoring
  • If a skip-lot program is in place, suspend it immediately when a deviation occurs and revert to testing every lot
  • Annual CoA verification: confirm that the specifications issued by the supplier are still consistent with the current pharmacopoeia
  • During the investigation, make sure reserve samples are retained for additional microbiological testing

5.2.4 Evaluation of Confirmed Mycoplasma Contamination

5.2.4 Evaluation of Confirmed Mycoplasma Contamination

Investigation should be conducted in a timely manner as mycoplasma contamination can interrupt production and prove costly to the firm.

Common sources of mycoplasma contamination are master and secondary cell lines, raw materials, and growth medium that has not been rendered sterile using a 0.1 µm filtration step. Attention should be given to investigating these materials. The authentication, acquisition, banking, passage, and cultivation history of the master cell bank and working cell lines should be reviewed, with particular emphasis on certificates of analysis, certificates of origin, and the reported mycoplasma test results. Testing additional samples from the working cell bank may be advisable. Human contact or contaminated cell culture media and reagents may contaminate cell banks, and a new working cell bank would need to be established in order to continue production.

Cell culture media is usually made mycoplasma-free by heat treatment, irradiation, antibiotic use, or 0.1 µm retention filtration. That these procedures are fully validated and documented should be verified. The media, equipment sterilization, and handling of inoculum production must be as well controlled as large-scale cell culture. The manufacturing batch records should be reviewed thoroughly, especially noting manufacturing deviations and staffing changes. If available, retained production materials could be tested using a rapid method to trace the potential source of the mycoplasma contamination.

When the mycoplasma are of human origin (Mycoplasma orale, M. fermentans, and M. hominis), human contamination may likely play a role in the failure. Additional consideration should be given to species of mycoplasma that are of animal or nonanimal origin (Acholeplasma laidlawii, M. hyorhinis, M. synoviae, and Spiroplasma) as they may be indicative of contamination of specific growth media or additive growth components.

Commentary: 5.2.4 Mycoplasma Contamination Investigation

Core concept: mycoplasma is the "invisible killer" specific to biologics

Mycoplasma are the smallest bacteria and have no cell wall, which means:

  • A standard 0.22 µm filter cannot retain them - a 0.1 µm filter is needed
  • They do not grow easily on general microbiological media and are not detected by routine EM
  • After infecting a cell culture, the cells may look normal but their function is impaired

This makes it the hardest to detect and most threatening contamination source in biopharmaceutical manufacturing (vaccines, antibodies, cell therapies).

Analogy: mycoplasma is like a "stowaway without a passport"

A general microbial screening system (passport control) cannot catch it - it has no cell wall (passport), does not show up on routine media, and is not stopped by a 0.22 µm membrane. It can only be found with PCR or a designated mycoplasma test.

Once confirmed, action must be fast: a contaminated cell bank usually cannot be "cleaned"; production can only be re-established from a backup cell bank.

Species and source correspondence

  • M. orale / M. fermentans / M. hominis: human origin → suspect contamination from personnel handling
  • M. hyorhinis / M. synoviae: animal origin (pig/chicken) → suspect media components (such as fetal bovine serum)
  • Acholeplasma laidlawii: widespread → suspect contamination of media or additives

Species identification points you directly to the step where the investigation should trace the source, saving a great deal of investigation time.

5.2.5 Component (Container Closure) Investigations

5.2.5 Component (Container Closure) Investigations

Some of the major causal factors of microbial contamination in primary packaging components that threaten the sterility assurance of sterile drug products include the container (e.g., vial, cartridge, flexible container, or syringe) and closure (e.g., syringe cap and plunger, stopper, seal).

Specific microbial tests have the potential to fail as a result of a defective container or closure system, such as the bioburden test, sterility test, process simulation media fill (turbid vial), container closure/seal integrity test, and BET. Test failures can occur during initial component validation or revalidation testing, component release testing, in-process monitoring, finished-product release testing, or stability testing.

In every case, a thorough review of the components, filling, and closure operations should be performed. Involving the supplier early in the investigation is the best plan, as they can help provide data from the manufacturer. A complete history will be needed that covers manufacture supply, storage, preparation, sterilization, use, and final finished presentation in order to successfully complete the investigation.

5.2.5.1 Pharmaceutical Containers

Vials, cartridges, and syringes used for sterile products are regarded as primary containers. Upon container receipt, a 100% inspection is not possible, both because of the destructive nature of functional testing, the size of the incoming shipments, and the general reluctance to return quality samples to inventory. Thus, acceptable quality-level attribute inspection plans are widely used for packaging components.

After the initial testing of packaging components for bioburden, endotoxin, and physical characteristics, a defect inspection and certificate of analysis review is performed. Dimensional tests are important because the necks of containers and closures form the barrier or seal that prevents microbiological and other contamination ingress. Containers that are out of round, have seam or form defects, or have cracks can present paths of ingress. Molded glass can be traced to a specific mold at the factory to identify recurring defects.

Shipping cartons that are received infested, wet, crushed, or torn should be rejected. Containers used during production of a product with a failed microbial test should be reviewed.

5.2.5.2 Pharmaceutical Closures

The most common closure for a sterile injectable product filled in a glass vial is an elastomeric stopper and crimped aluminum seal. Special consideration should be given to the semipermeable nature of elastomers. A review of the sterilization process validation also may be useful. Containers and closures can be purchased raw and unprocessed, ready-to-sterilize, and ready-to-use. These different preparations should be considered during an investigation.

5.2.5.3 Component Contamination

Post-manufacture microbiological and environmental contamination can include nonviable particles, microorganisms, and bacterial endotoxins. Viruses are generally not a concern with nonanimal raw material used to manufacture packaging components. In most cases, the high-heat extrusion process associated with glass and elastomer manufacture is microbiologically unfavorable; however, environmental controls in the manufacturing plant are often inadequate to maintain these conditions during sorting and packaging for shipment. Downstream processing is required and must include rigorous processing controls.

5.2.5.4 Container–Closure Integrity

Physical deformation, damage, and out-of-tolerance dimensional specifications of both containers and closures can result in sterile products becoming nonsterile through microbial ingress. Emphasis should be given to manufacturing set-up, in-process controls, and container–closure testing at release and in stability programs. Failure of the container–closure system and the inability to maintain microbial integrity should be fully investigated.

Commentary: 5.2.5 Container Closure System Investigations

Core concept: the packaging system is itself a sterile barrier

From a QC perspective, the safety of a sterile drug depends on two layers of protection: sterility assurance in the process (APS/sterilization) and the physical barrier of the container closure (CCI, container closure integrity). The latter is often underestimated - but an out-of-round bottle neck or a rubber stopper with a pinhole is enough to cause a sterility failure partway through a stability study.

Analogy: the glass vial is like a safe, and the rubber stopper is like the combination dial

The safe itself (the glass vial) must be free of cracks and correctly dimensioned; the combination dial (rubber stopper + aluminum seal) must fit precisely. If any one "mechanical tolerance is exceeded", microorganisms can "pick the lock" and get in. That is why the investigation must review all three parts - vial, stopper and seal - as well as the filling/stoppering machine settings.

Special note: rubber is a semi-permeable material - even with no macroscopic cracks, trace gases and moisture can still permeate during long-term storage and carry in contaminants.

Four-layer investigation structure: 5.2.5.1-5.2.5.4

  • 5.2.5.1 Container: bottle shape deviations (out-of-round), glass mold traceability
  • 5.2.5.2 Closure: semi-permeability of rubber stoppers, validation of aluminum crimp force
  • 5.2.5.3 Component contamination: environmental control after high-temperature extrusion, downstream packaging controls
  • 5.2.5.4 Seal integrity: CCI testing, CCI monitoring in the stability program

5.2.6 Environmental Monitoring

5.2.6 Environmental Monitoring

The pharmaceutical industry has many controlled and/or classified environments in which products are manufactured, and the key to the success of an EM investigation is understanding these requirements and the possible sources of contamination. Out-of-level monitoring results can come from the following types of environments:

  • Classified cleanrooms for sterile product manufacturing
  • Controlled or classified rooms for nonsterile product manufacturing
  • Controlled or classified rooms for low bioburden product manufacturing (e.g., biologics)
  • Laminar flow hoods, restrictive access barrier systems (RABS), and isolator systems used for product manufacturing or finished-product release testing

Measures for control can be implemented based on the following key concepts:

  • Minimize the entry of contaminants by incorporating good facility design, proper layout, efficient material and personnel flow, and sound supplier controls
  • Reduce contamination during the manufacturing process using well-developed process and equipment controls
  • Establish and maintain proper hygiene and PPE practices
  • Institute appropriate aseptic techniques and cleanroom behaviors
  • Assure a state of control through routine EM and analysis

  • Mold or Gram-negative or Gram-positive spore-forming organisms recovered in the critical locations associated with a specific manufacturing process

5.2.6.2 Manufacturing Process Evaluation

The purpose of an EM program is to determine the adequacy of the environmental controls and the aseptic technique of personnel in the manufacturing area. A well-maintained and well-operated facility requires good facility design, operation, and maintenance; functional HVAC systems; temperature and humidity control; supply of HEPA-filtered, laminar flow air; proper movement of materials, equipment, and personnel; and a sound facility cleaning and sanitization program.

Loss of environmental control in the cleanroom should be considered a potential source of microbial contamination. Excursions in temperature, differential pressure gradients, humidity, nonviable particulate levels, and appropriate cleaning and disinfection activities can contribute to microbial contamination in the cleanroom environment.

Microbial contamination can enter the manufacturing facility, including cleanrooms, in a variety of ways; in fact, the most common cause of fungal spore and bacterial endospore contamination in cleanrooms are items that enter the cleanroom. Cardboard and other cellulose-based materials known to shed particles should never enter GMP-compliant manufacturing areas. Items entering critical aseptic processing areas should first be double-wrapped, unwrapped, and wiped down with a sporicidal agent.

The age and maintenance level of facilities can affect contamination rates in the cleanroom. Ceiling tiles, HEPA-filter units, cleanroom flooring, materials of construction, drainage systems, and duct work are areas of concern in aging facilities that may need to be kept on a routine schedule for repairs. Drains in manufacturing facilities require routine disinfection with a sporicidal agent to prevent microbial proliferation.

5.2.6.3 Resampling or Investigational Monitoring

The need for additional monitoring to determine root cause should be considered early in the investigation to avoid losing time and to ensure that microbiological results will be representative of the conditions.

Based on the type of manufacturing facility, the frequency for routine environmental monitoring should be considered and a decision should be made as to whether resampling or investigational monitoring is warranted. Scenarios in which resampling may be useful include low frequency monitoring locations and locations found as important during facility walk-throughs. If the isolate identified as part of an EM excursion or routine sample is considered a unique microorganism (e.g., first time recovered in the facility), some investigational monitoring may be warranted.

At the discretion of the QC microbiologist and in conjunction with approval from the QCU, additional locations may be sampled, or specialized microbiological media may be utilized. For example, recovery of mold from the wall, but not the floor, may suggest that sampling be conducted of nearby ceiling locations, adjacent walls, and additional portions of the same wall. Specific microbiological medium that may improve recovery and/or detection of the unique microorganism(s) may be used (e.g., Sabouraud dextrose agar may be useful in response to mold recoveries). SOPs should allow discretionary sampling that may aid the investigation.

Commentary: 5.2.6 Environmental Monitoring Investigations

Core concept: an EM investigation = detecting contamination sources, not just managing numbers

At many facilities, an EM investigation stops at "excursion, resample, close if normal". The TR88 framework requires far more: you must understand where the contamination came from and why it appeared at this point in time, and take real root cause corrective action.

Three levels of investigation logic:

  1. Historical trend evaluation (5.2.6.1): look at the data first and find patterns in time, location and species
  2. Process evaluation (5.2.6.2): walk the facility, check the HVAC, review cleaning records, and look at items entering and leaving the cleanroom
  3. Resampling/investigational monitoring (5.2.6.3): targeted supplementary sampling to confirm or rule out hypotheses

Analogy: environmental contamination is like a "leaking pipe" - find the source, not just wipe the floor

If you find mold on a wall but only wipe the surface and resample, it is like wiping the floor when a pipe is leaking. TR88 points out: mold on the wall → trace to the ceiling, adjacent walls and drains → it may be HVAC duct condensation or aging building materials. Fixing the cause is the real investigation.

Common blind spots in EM investigations

  • Cardboard is an important carrier of mold spores and bacterial endospores and should never enter a GMP area
  • Drains are high-risk locations and need regular disinfection with a sporicidal agent
  • Ceiling tiles, HEPA frames and floor joints in older facilities are chronic sources of contamination
  • Seasonal trends (such as increased mold in the rainy season) are meaningful signals and must not be ignored
  • Deviations in differential pressure, temperature and humidity can themselves be early indicators of microbial excursions

Commentary: 5.2.7 Media Fill Positive Investigations

Core concept: one turbid vial = an investigation must be started

A media fill (APS) is the "mock exam" of the sterile process. If even one media vial is turbid, it counts as a failure and a full investigation must be started. There is no "acceptable failure rate".

Analogy: the position of the turbid vial is like a "crime timeline"

When a detective works a case, "when it happened" matters as much as "what happened". The positions of turbid vials in a media fill form that timeline:

  • Turbid vials at the start → contamination during assembly/setup (filling pistons, needles)
  • Turbid vials in the middle → introduced by an intervention (clearing vials from the conveyor, replenishing stoppers)
  • Turbid vials at the end → operator fatigue or lax aseptic technique later in the run

Video review is the most direct tool - if there are sequential numbers or timestamps, you can pinpoint exactly which action contaminated which vial.

Quick reference: species and contamination source

  • Staphylococcus / Cutibacterium → human skin flora
  • Bacillus / Paenibacillus → environmental spore formers (equipment, walls, material entry)
  • Burkholderia / Pseudomonas → incorrectly prepared or expired disinfectant (gram-negative bacteria proliferate)
  • Aspergillus / Penicillium → mold spores (packaging materials, air, facility structure)

5.2.8 Utilities Monitoring

5.2.8 Utilities Monitoring

If the initial laboratory investigation (Section 4.0) does not find a conclusive source of a laboratory error that explains the deviation, deviations related to pharmaceutical water systems should be examined. Water is used in many ways in pharmaceutical manufacturing, and the required grade of water varies depending upon how it is used.

Pharmaceutical water systems consist of several components, including a water supply, a robust purification system that should consistently produce water of the intended quality under a range of conditions, and the storage and distribution systems that assure the water continues to meet all required quality attributes until it is delivered to the process.

The cross-functional team charged with investigating any deviation related to a water system should be familiar with the potential risks associated with its design, installation, operation, use, and maintenance. This team also needs to understand both the value and the limitations of data derived from system qualification and monitoring.

A monitoring plan should define the locations and frequency of sampling and the attributes for testing. The plan should encompass the entire system, from the water supply to the points of use, and relevant points in between on the generation, storage, and distribution systems. The sampling plan should reflect the results of intensive sampling during qualification, recent historical data, risk factors, and mitigations.

Routine monitoring of the water generation, storage, and distribution systems verifies that they continue to operate in a state of control, while monitoring at the points-of-use evaluates the quality of water delivered to the process.

Microbiological monitoring typically involves manual sample collection from a storage tank, a site on the loop, or at a point of use. Each step should be carefully designed to minimize potential errors and to ensure the reported result accurately reflects the condition of the material sampled.

The approach to investigating the root cause of a deviation on a pharmaceutical water system should be guided by the location and timing of the sample and by the test involved. If multiple deviations of the same type and level are observed, a cause that affects the entire system is more likely. If the deviation is not consistent with results from other contemporaneous samples, causes related to the sample point or to sampling or testing may be suspected.

In the case of a deviation for bioburden, the identity of the organism recovered is essential information. Recovery of organisms that thrive in water and form biofilms may suggest one set of causes, whereas recovery of organisms that are not compatible with the water system may suggest another set of causes, possibly related to environmental contamination.

Water Quality Standards:

  • Potable water: free of coliforms; ≤ 500 CFU/mL (per USP <1231>)
  • Purified water: ≤ 100 CFU/mL; meets conductivity and TOC limits
  • WFI: < 10 CFU/100 mL; bacterial endotoxin < 0.25 EU/mL

Potable water quality may be affected by seasonal variations due to runoff of melting snow or periods of low precipitation. Agricultural or industrial runoff may enter surface water sources or aquifers. An aging infrastructure may increase the chance of contamination entering the water supply via a damaged water main.

Risks related to the design and installation of the water system should be identified and remediated prior to qualification testing. Dead legs, as well as poor welds and other irregular surfaces, increase the risk of biofilm development. Recent design standards specify that pipes branching off the main recirculation loop should not exceed 1.5 times the pipe diameter, and the use of valves with no dead legs is now common.

Operational controls for storage and distribution systems typically include parameters such as temperature, flow rate, and pressure. The rate of water usage affects the stagnant time of water in the system and may be related to the root cause.

The investigation of a deviation may include a review of routine and corrective maintenance records. Invasive maintenance adds contamination risks, so measures to mitigate these risks and return the system to service should be prepared in advance. Measures should include periodic replacement of key parts, such as elastomers and vent filters on holding tanks.

Practices for operation of the sample or use port should be considered, especially if the deviation is not consistent with other results. Flushing prior to sampling (and use) is important to physically remove the opportunistic organisms found on hoses, sampling apparatus, or the sample port. Hoses should be removed when not in use and should be stored in a free-draining position.

5.2.8.1 Compressed Gases

Compressed gases used during the manufacturing and testing of pharmaceutical products are air, nitrogen, oxygen, and carbon dioxide. Inappropriate design and piping for distribution of these gaseous materials can introduce unwanted microorganisms. Pharmaceutical-grade compressed air is treated to remove moisture, and chemical residue like lubricants and point-of-use sterilizing-grade filters are employed.

The introduction of uncontrolled water moisture or inadequately filtered compressed gas, coupled with poorly designed pretreatment and sanitization processes of the gas delivery systems, can adversely affect the system and lead to product contamination.

Even though these utility systems have been qualified to perform as designed, the materials used during their construction may contain unexpected component defects (i.e., poor welding or incomplete passivation), as well as presenting potential ingress of grease and oils used for equipment lubrication. In addition, the metals and plastics may deteriorate over time, causing breaches or leaks in the plumbing materials or in components connected to the pipes (e.g., gauges, shut-off valves).

Commentary: 5.2.8 Utilities Investigations

Core concept: a water system investigation = working through the flow diagram from top to bottom

Deviation investigations for pharmaceutical water systems need systematic thinking, because any step (source water → pretreatment → generation → storage → distribution loop → point of use) could be the root cause.

Key decision logic:

  • Multiple points out of limits at the same time → systemic problem (possibly biofilm along the whole loop or an equipment failure)
  • One point out of limits, others normal → sampling point issue, localized piping problem or operator error
  • Organisms typical of water (waterborne bacteria) → biofilm formation, dead legs, low-flow areas
  • Organisms not typical of water → contamination entering from the external environment

Analogy: a water system is like a city water network - a dead leg is the most dangerous stagnant zone

Imagine a city water network: the main line circulates continuously (the WFI loop), but if the shut-off valve on some branch (dead leg) is closed, the water stagnates there and goes dead. Microorganisms multiply rapidly in still water and form a biofilm - that biofilm is like a "permanent contamination source" on the inner pipe wall, periodically shedding a few organisms downstream.

This is why the design standard requires that branch length not exceed 1.5 times the main pipe diameter. It is the most direct and effective structural preventive measure.

5.2.8.1 Compressed gases: the invisible contamination vector

Compressed air, nitrogen, oxygen and CO₂ can all become carriers of microbial contamination, especially when:

  • The system contains moisture that has not been removed (condensate)
  • The filter is not sterilizing grade (0.22 µm) or has failed
  • Lubricating oils/greases or welding defects contaminate the inside of the piping
  • Aging pipe material causes leaks

Qualification of the compressed gas system is necessary, but remember: a qualified system does not stay qualified forever in use - material aging, leaks and piping reconfiguration can all change the state of the system.

CDMO operations summary

The core logic of a Phase II specific investigation is: species = clue, location = corroboration, trend = context. Once a deviation is confirmed as valid (not laboratory error), start a cross-functional investigation team immediately and choose the matching 5.2.x framework according to the deviation type.

For a sterile manufacturing CDMO, the following four types of deviation are the highest alert level and call for the fullest investigation resources:

  • Sterility test positive (5.2.1)
  • Media fill positive (5.2.7)
  • Confirmed mycoplasma (5.2.4)
  • Consecutive WFI excursions (5.2.8)

An investigation is not about closing the case - it is about making sure the next batch is safe. If the root cause is not found, the CAPA is meaningless.

Overall analogy: Phase II is a "systematic diagnosis of the underlying disease"

Phase I is like emergency triage (ruling out false positives and confirming a real deviation); Phase II is a "full-body systematic diagnosis" - working back from the contamination "symptoms" (species, location, time) to the "cause" (water system, raw materials, equipment, personnel, packaging materials). These eight 5.2.x subsections are that systematic checklist of questions.

6.0 Conclusions - Overview

6.0 Conclusions

The investigation of microbial monitoring and testing deviations is a critical activity in the pharmaceutical industry that is not adequately covered by the FDA Guidance for Industry: Investigating Out of Specification (OOS) Test Results for Pharmaceutical Production (1). This technical report provides industry best practices related to microbial investigations to eliminate that gap in guidance.

These investigations are operationally divided into two phases—Phase I laboratory investigations and Phase II manufacturing investigations—with different teams responsible for each activity. Because of the importance of timeliness in microbial investigations, the manufacturing investigation may be initiated prior to completion of the laboratory investigation.

The objective of the laboratory investigation is to confirm that the sampling and testing was conducted correctly, and the reported result was valid. The investigational team is usually limited to the microbiologist conducting the test, the laboratory management, an SME with knowledge and experience with the test, and a representative of the QCU.

The major points to consider in a laboratory investigation are:

  • Integrity of the test sample used in the microbial test
  • Educational background, skill, and experience of the microbiologist conducting the test
  • Testing and environmental and personnel monitoring history associated with the microbiologist
  • Suitability of the equipment, supplies, media, diluents, and reagents used for the test
  • Environmental controls, including microbial testing facilities, laminar flow hoods, isolators, and PPE associated with the test
  • Reading of plates, scoring of microbiological broth, recording of data, accuracy of calculations, and review and reporting of the results
  • Microbial identification of the isolate implicated in the deviation

Once the validity of the microbial test is confirmed, a multifunctional team is assembled to conduct the manufacturing investigation related to the failure. Representatives of the microbiology laboratory, manufacturing, engineering, maintenance, and process validation may be members of the manufacturing investigation team usually led by the QCU. To increase the chances of a successful investigation, an experienced pharmaceutical microbiologist familiar with manufacturing operations is usually added as an SME.

The major points to consider in a manufacturing investigation are:

  • Unit operation and location within the facility directly related to the failure
  • Possible contribution of surrounding support areas
  • Possible contribution to the failure of deviations in environmental controls, i.e., space pressurization, airflow patterns, temperature, humidity, power outages, material flow, facility cleaning, and sanitization
  • Possible contribution of pharmaceutical ingredients, ingredient water, in-process materials, packaging components, and utilities
  • Deviation in critical manufacturing steps, as documented in the batch record
  • Recent change controls
  • Related in-process bioburden, environmental, and personnel monitoring deviations
  • Interviews with operators, laboratory management, and production managers as to the possible cause of the failure
  • Follow-up microbial monitoring and testing

The investigation reports must adequately document the team findings. If the root cause of the out-of-limit or out-of-specification microbial test result cannot be definitively established, the most probable cause or causes must be presented. Investigating out-of-trend results may help identify laboratory and/or manufacturing issues before they become out-of-specification results. To prevent the recurrence of these microbial failures, a CAPA plan must be implemented, with responsible individuals reporting their progress on the plan to the QCU and their management.

Commentary and a CDMO Practice Perspective

The core contribution of TR88: filling a regulatory gap

The FDA OOS guidance (Ref. 1) is the starting point for deviation investigations at every pharmaceutical company, but it was designed for chemical testing. The nature of microbiological testing - living organisms, environmental contamination, human factors - makes it far more complex than a chemical OOS. TR88 exists to fill this gap and to provide industry best practice for investigating microbial deviations.

For a CDMO, this report is more than a reference document; it is the basis for your defense during client audits. When a QP asks, "What is the basis for your microbiological OOS investigation procedure?", TR88 is the answer.

Two-phase framework: one integrated system, not two independent processes

The most important architectural contribution of TR88 is dividing the investigation into two sequential phases:

  • Phase I (laboratory investigation): confirms whether the test itself is valid. Carried out by a small team, focusing on the analyst, equipment, media and operating procedures.
  • Phase II (manufacturing investigation): once the laboratory result is confirmed as valid, moves to the manufacturing environment. A cross-functional team, led by the QCU.

Key logic: if Phase I finds a problem with the test itself (for example, the analyst contaminated the media), there is no need to proceed to Phase II - this is a resource-efficiency design. But the two phases can be "started in parallel", because the time sensitivity of microbial investigations (pressure for batch release) does not allow waiting.

"Most probable cause": an important regulatory concept

TR88 explicitly allows this: when a definitive root cause cannot be determined, "most probable cause" is an acceptable investigation conclusion.

This matters a great deal in day-to-day work. The reality of microbiology is that contamination events often cannot be reproduced or traced back with 100% certainty. If your SOP requires every OOS report to have a "definitive root cause", you end up in a dilemma - either you write a root cause that cannot be defended, or you leave the batch on hold indefinitely.

The right approach is to support the "most probable cause" with sufficient scientific reasoning and propose a reasonable CAPA to prevent recurrence. Regulators accept this logic.

The CDMO-specific layer: dividing responsibilities in the contract quality agreement

In a CDMO environment, the allocation of Phase I and Phase II responsibilities is more complicated than at a brand-name manufacturer. Typical questions:

  • A client's raw material fails testing at the CDMO site: who performs Phase I? The CDMO's microbiology laboratory, or the client's QC?
  • The root cause found in Phase II involves the specification design of the client's raw material: who is responsible for the CAPA?
  • The investigation report has different levels of disclosure for external parties (the client) and for regulators

Recommended action: in the contract quality agreement (CQA), state explicitly that "for a microbiological OOS investigation, Phase I is led by the CDMO laboratory, Phase II involves both parties, and the final report is approved by the CDMO QU before being sent to the client". A vague agreement leads to a broken relationship when a real deviation occurs.

CAPA effectiveness checks: the real end point of an investigation

TR88 stresses that a CAPA must be "implemented" and that the owner must report progress to the QCU. But the deeper implication is: an investigation is not truly complete until the effectiveness of the CAPA has been confirmed.

A common shortfall: the CAPA is closed, but there is no follow-up environmental monitoring trend analysis or recurrence detection mechanism. At the next audit, the auditor will ask, "How did you confirm that the CAPA for that earlier Pseudomonas event was effective?" If the answer is "we trained the analysts", that is not enough - you need data to support it.

The single most important success factor

TR88 mentions the SME (subject matter expert) twice in its conclusions, and both times stresses "a senior pharmaceutical microbiologist with manufacturing operations experience".

This is not an accident. Among all the factors that raise the success rate of investigations, an SME who truly knows how to connect microbiological science with manufacturing reality is the single most critical variable. This person is not a QC technician who only reads numbers, but a hybrid expert who can walk into the aseptic filling area, understand the airflow design and talk with engineers.

Implication for the CDMO Operations Director: developing this kind of talent in-house, or building a network of external SME consultants, should be a priority in building organizational capability.

A Reading Guide to Key References for CDMO Operations Leaders

Of the 50 references, the following core documents have the greatest impact on day-to-day CDMO aseptic filling operations, and it is recommended to become familiar with them first:

Ref. 1 - FDA OOS Guidance (2006)

The regulatory basis for deviation investigations at every pharmaceutical company. TR88 exists because this guidance is not sufficient to cover the particular features of microbiological testing, but the OOS guidance's own two-phase logic (Phase I laboratory / Phase II manufacturing) is still the backbone of TR88. During an FDA inspection, your microbiological OOS SOP must be able to align with this guidance.

Ref. 3 - FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance (2004)

The core regulatory document for aseptic filling facilities, defining cleanroom classification, environmental monitoring requirements, media fill acceptance criteria and more. All TR88 microbiological OOS investigations must be conducted within this framework. For a CDMO, it is the first document reviewed when clients audit a site for selection.

Ref. 4 - PDA TR13 Environmental Monitoring (2014)

The industry standard for designing an environmental monitoring program (EMP), including selection of sampling locations, setting of frequencies, calculation of alert and action levels, and trend analysis methods. In a Phase II manufacturing investigation, analysis of historical EM data is one of the core tasks, and TR13 is your reference book. It is recommended to read it together with TR88.

Ref. 13 - USP <71> Sterility Tests

The standard method for sterility testing of finished products. A sterility test failure is the most serious deviation scenario in TR88. USP <71> defines negative controls, positive controls, media selection, incubation conditions, and the circumstances in which a retest may be performed - directly linked to the TR88 Phase I investigation.

Ref. 14 - USP <85> Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET)

The core USP chapter for endotoxin control of injectable products. The investigation logic for an OOS BET result (test interference vs. true contamination) is a typical Phase I analysis case. For a CDMO with injectable products, endotoxin investigation capability is a basic skill.

Ref. 32 - PDA TR22 Process Simulation for Aseptically Filled Products (Media Fill) (2011)

The industry standard for aseptic filling process validation. A media fill (APS/LVS) failure is the most serious scenario in a TR88 Phase II investigation and usually triggers a batch recall assessment directly. TR22 defines the design, execution, acceptance criteria and failure investigation steps for an APS, and is required reading for a CDMO's head of process validation.

Ref. 39 - PDA TR80 Data Integrity (2018)

Data integrity management in the microbiology laboratory. In recent FDA inspections, data integrity deficiencies have been among the leading items in Form 483 observations and warning letters. A Phase I laboratory investigation inevitably involves a review of the integrity of raw data, and TR80 provides a systematic framework for laboratory DI management.

Ref. 33 — PIC/S GMP Annex 1 (2018)

The EU/PIC/S core technical annex for sterile medicinal product GMP (note: the 2022 version has been updated; this document cites the 2018 version). For a global CDMO serving European clients, Annex 1 requirements are often stricter than the FDA's, especially for documentation of the contamination control strategy (CCS). It is recommended to confirm that your site has been updated to the 2022 requirements.

Exercise: set your own reference priority order

As a CDMO Operations Director, if your aseptic filling site needs to review updated regulatory documents each year, the suggested priority order is:

  1. FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance (Ref. 3) - read it whenever the FDA updates it
  2. PIC/S Annex 1 (Ref. 33) - essential for European clients, now updated to the 2022 version
  3. USP <71>, <85> (Ref. 13, 14) - check with each USP update
  4. PDA TR13, TR22, TR88 (Ref. 4, 32) - industry best practice, updated on a 5-year cycle

Building this list and adding it as an annual agenda item in the Quality Management Review is a low-cost but high-value regulatory compliance management action.

TR88 Master Synthesis

The overall value of TR88 from the perspective of a CDMO Operations Director

  • The core contribution of TR88 is to provide a complete framework for microbial deviation investigation, filling the gaps the FDA OOS guidance cannot cover. It is not a regulatory requirement, but in an inspection it is your best basis for defense.
  • The two-phase framework is the operational backbone: Phase I (laboratory validity) is confirmed first, and Phase II (manufacturing investigation) follows - the two can run in parallel, but there is a logical order. In a CDMO environment, this framework has a third dimension: the division of responsibilities between the client and the CDMO must be clearly specified in the contract quality agreement.
  • "Most probable cause" is regulatory wisdom: do not let an investigation extend endlessly in pursuit of a "perfect root cause". Sufficient scientific reasoning + a reasonable CAPA = an acceptable investigation report. This is the realism that regulators recognize.
  • CAPA effectiveness is the real end point: in many organizations CAPA management stops at "closure" rather than "verified effective". Establishing a CAPA effectiveness check based on trend data is a key step toward proactive quality management.
  • SMEs are the highest-return investment: a senior microbiologist with manufacturing floor experience is the success factor TR88 stresses repeatedly. In talent strategy, such a hybrid SME has more impact than any software tool.
  • Knowledge links: TR88, together with PDA TR13 (environmental monitoring), TR22 (media fill) and TR80 (data integrity), forms a mutually supporting body of knowledge. A CDMO leader who has read all of these reports will rank in the top 20% of the industry in command of sterile pharmaceutical quality management.
