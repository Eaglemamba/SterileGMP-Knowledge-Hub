1.0 Introduction

Original Text

Drug manufacturers have employed contamination control measures for decades as a core element of good manufacturing practices. Commonly, these are a collection of practices that were developed separately and applied without clear consideration for their interdependence.

The ongoing evolution of contamination control principles that this document addresses is a shift to a holistic approach, where the practices are designed to work together to achieve proactive contamination control and are evaluated for their collective effectiveness. The holistic approach also demands that contamination control measures be tailored to the specific risks around each individual process.

Many GMP practices are part of a company's contamination control strategy (CCS) including, for example, how processes and facilities are designed (including cleaning and disinfection), how raw materials and consumables are selected and managed, and how personnel are trained and developed. The CCS is also intended to drive continuous improvement and/or remediation. The success of any one CCS element is intrinsically linked to the others, and the success of the CCS depends upon how well the individual elements work together to reduce the contamination hazards for a specific process (see Figure 3.0-1🔍).

All drug manufacturers have a CCS that includes a multitude of GMP practices documented across numerous operational procedures and programs. The rationales for those practices are often captured in risk assessments, validations, and technical documents. A CCS record creates an umbrella document that brings the relevant information together so it can be understood and evaluated holistically. All CCS documents should summarize the contamination control practices, along with the underlying rationales, and reference the supportive procedures and reports. This technical report outlines how to implement an effective CCS using a holistic approach, highlights a selection of best practices, and identifies outdated practices and mindsets. The document opens with a conceptual discussion of the various CCS elements (Section 3.0) with the understanding that all drug manufacturing firms will have these elements in various forms and levels of maturity. The many existing guidances and standards for specific CCS elements, for example, critical utility control, gowning practices, and process hold times, are referenced in Section 14.0. This technical report does not replace or reiterate those detailed documents; rather it acts as a roadmap. Appendices are also included that provide valuable case studies and practical examples as well as a template for creating a CCS document (see Section 18.0).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Contamination Control Strategy, CCS: A CCS is a planned set of processes and measures used to identify, assess, control and monitor contamination risks. It is not a single SOP but an "umbrella document" that brings contamination control measures scattered across the site together into one strategy that can be evaluated as a whole.

Holistic Approach: In the past, contamination control measures in the pharmaceutical industry were handled in silos; for example, cleaning validation belonged to cleaning validation and environmental monitoring belonged to environmental monitoring. The shift TR90 emphasizes is that these measures must be designed as an integrated whole that works together, and must be tailored to the risks of each specific process.

Analogy

Imagine a hospital infection control plan. A hospital has many measures: operating room disinfection, hand hygiene rules for staff, air filtration systems, visitor management, waste handling and more. If these measures work in silos (excellent operating room disinfection but lax waste handling), the overall infection risk is still high. A CCS is like the hospital's "overall infection control strategy": it links all the measures together, makes sure every link is in place and supports the others, and adjusts the intensity to the risk level of each ward (for example, ICU vs. general ward).

Key Notes

Positioning of TR90: This report is not meant to replace or repeat existing technical guidance (such as cleaning validation or aseptic processing). It serves as a "roadmap" that helps you link all the relevant GMP practices into one logical, assessable, holistic strategy.

Regulatory driver: EU GMP Annex 1 (2022 revision) explicitly requires manufacturers to establish a formal CCS document. This is no longer a "recommendation" but a "requirement", which makes TR90 an important industry reference for building a CCS.

1.1 Purpose

Original Text

PDA Technical Report No. 90: Contamination Control Strategy Development in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Technical Report provides guidance on how to establish an effective CCS for either a new or existing drug manufacturing facility or process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

New vs. Existing: TR90 applies to two situations:

  • New facilities/new processes: The CCS mindset can be built in from the design phase, establishing a holistic contamination control strategy from the very beginning. This is the ideal case.
  • Existing facilities/existing processes: The contamination control measures already in place need to be "collected, organized and linked" into a CCS document, and potential gaps identified. For most companies, this is the more common real-world challenge.

Analogy

This is like organizing your household insurance. If you have just bought a new house, you can plan a complete insurance package from the start (fire, earthquake, theft and so on). But if you have lived there for ten years, the various policies may have been bought at different times from different insurers, and now someone needs to compile all the policies into one overview to check for overlaps or omissions. TR90 is that "organizing guide".

Key Notes

For a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization), building a CCS is even more challenging: your facility may manufacture products for several different clients at the same time, each with different risk characteristics. The CCS needs enough flexibility to cover these differences while still providing a unified framework.

1.2 Scope

Original Text

This document focuses on contamination control practices against microbial and other adventitious agents, pyrogens such as endotoxin, and foreign particulate matter in the manufacture of sterile drugs, low bioburden drug substances, and nonsterile drugs that are vulnerable to contamination. Yet, the principles presented in this technical report can be applied more widely to any drug manufacturing or compounding process.

Secondary and tertiary packaging considerations, chemical contaminants including product cross-contamination, and inherent particulate matter are out of the scope of this technical report.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three types of contamination covered by TR90:

  • Microbial contamination: Bacteria, molds, yeasts, and other adventitious agents such as viruses and mycoplasma
  • Pyrogens/Endotoxin: Components of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria; even after the microorganisms are killed, endotoxin can remain and cause a fever response
  • Foreign particulate matter: Extraneous particles that should not be present in the product, such as fibers, metal fragments and glass shards

The three types of product covered by TR90:

  • Sterile drugs: Injectables, ophthalmic preparations and so on, which require the most stringent contamination control
  • Low bioburden drug substances: Drug substances for which a low microbial level must be maintained during processing
  • Nonsterile drugs vulnerable to contamination: Such as oral liquids and certain topical preparations

Key Notes

What is excluded is just as important! TR90 explicitly excludes the following:

  • Chemical contaminants: Including cross-contamination, which is addressed by other guidance (such as EU GMP Chapters 3 and 5)
  • Inherent particulate matter: Particles arising from the nature of the product itself (such as protein aggregates), which are not extraneous contamination
  • Secondary/tertiary packaging: Outer cartons, labels and other packaging that does not directly contact the product

Understanding the exclusions helps you stay focused: a CCS addresses "microorganisms, pyrogens and foreign particulate matter that should not be in the product".

Analogy

It is like a restaurant's food safety plan: TR90 is concerned with "things that should not be in the ingredients" (such as bacteria and foreign objects), not with "the nutritional proportions of the ingredients themselves" (that is a recipe question). Likewise, the print quality of the restaurant's takeaway paper bags (secondary packaging) is outside the scope of the food safety plan.

  3. Why does TR90 exclude inherent particulate matter (such as protein aggregates) from its scope? What is the practical implication for writing a CCS?
2.0 Glossary and Abbreviations

Original Text

Glossary

Action Limit
An established value that, when exceeded, indicates a process is outside of its normal operating conditions. A response to such an excursion requires a documented investigation, product impact assessment, and corrective actions based on the results of that investigation.

Alert Level
An established value that, when exceeded, provides an early warning of a potential drift from the normal operating conditions and validated state. This type of warning does require an appropriate documented investigation (e.g., trend analysis) and may require corrective actions depending on the results of the investigation.

Aseptic Filling
Part of aseptic processing in which a presterilized or aseptically manufactured product is filled and sealed into sterile containers in a critical area, RABS, or isolator.

Aseptic Processing
Handling of product, packaging materials, and/or processing equipment in a controlled environment in which the air supply, materials, equipment, and personnel are controlled to maintain product sterility.

Bioburden
The type and number of microorganisms per unit of material.

Cleanroom
A room designed, maintained, and controlled to prevent particle and microbiological contamination of a drug or medical device. Such a room is assigned and reproducibly meets an appropriate air cleanliness classification.

Closed System
A system in which the sterile product is not exposed to the surrounding environment.

Contamination
The undesired introduction of impurities of a chemical or microbiological nature or of foreign matter, into or onto a material or product during production, sampling, packaging or repackaging, storage, or transport.

Contamination Control Strategy
A planned set of processes and measures for the identification, assessment, control, and monitoring of contamination risks that include microorganisms, pyrogens/endotoxins, and foreign particles, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality.

Control Measure
A step taken to minimize the risk of contamination.

Critical Area/Critical Zone
An area/zone designed to maintain the sterility of materials where sterilized product, containers, closures, and equipment may be exposed to the manufacturing environment.

Decontamination
The overall process of removal or reduction of any contaminants (chemical residue or microorganisms) from an area, processing equipment, or person. The method of decontamination used (e.g., cleaning, disinfection, sterilization) should be chosen and validated to achieve a level of cleanliness appropriate to the intended use of the item decontaminated.

Disinfection
The process by which the reduction of the number of microorganisms is achieved by the irreversible action of a product on their structure or metabolism, to a level deemed to be appropriate for a defined purpose.

Grade A Air Supply
Air which is passed through a filter qualified as capable of producing grade A total particle quality air, but where there is no requirement to perform continuous total particle monitoring or meet grade A viable monitoring limits.

Isolator
An enclosure capable of being subject to reproducible interior bio-decontamination, with an internal work zone meeting grade A conditions that provides uncompromised, continuous isolation of its interior from the external environment (e.g. surrounding cleanroom air and personnel).
Closed Isolator systems exclude external contamination from the isolator's interior by transfer of material via aseptic connection to auxiliary equipment, rather than using openings to the surrounding environment. Closed systems remain sealed throughout operations.
Open Isolator systems are designed to allow for the continuous or semicontinuous ingress and/or egress of materials during operations through one or more openings. Openings are engineered (e.g., using continuous overpressure) to exclude the entry of external contamination into the isolator.

Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS)
System that provides an enclosed, but not fully sealed, environment meeting defined air quality conditions (for aseptic processing grade A), and using a rigid-wall enclosure and integrated gloves to separate its interior from the surrounding cleanroom environment. The inner surfaces of the RABS are disinfected and decontaminated with a sporicidal agent. Operators use gloves, half suits, RTPs and other integrated transfer ports to perform manipulations or convey materials to the interior of the RABS. Depending on the design, doors are rarely opened, and only under strictly pre-defined conditions.

Sanitization
Process of reducing the number of all forms of microbial contamination including fungi, viruses, and bacteria.

Sterilization
A process used to render a system free of viable microorganisms with a specified probability.

2.1 Abbreviations

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

The difference between Alert Level and Action Limit is critical:

  • Alert Level: Like the "low fuel" warning light on a car dashboard. You notice it, record it and start watching the trend, but you do not need to stop the car immediately.
  • Action Limit: Like the "engine overheating" warning light. You must act immediately: stop the car, investigate the cause, fix the problem, and document everything.

In environmental monitoring (EM), exceeding an Alert Level calls for trend analysis; exceeding an Action Limit calls for a formal investigation, a product impact assessment and CAPA.

Analogy

The difference between Isolator and RABS:

Think of an Isolator as a space station: it is completely separated from the external environment, all materials enter and leave through special docking ports (airlocks), and the internal environment is maintained entirely on its own.

Think of a RABS (Restricted Access Barrier System) as a glove box inside a cleanroom: it has a physical barrier and gloves, but it is not completely sealed. You reach into the box through the gloves, but the space above the box is still open to the cleanroom. RABS doors are "rarely opened", but can be opened under strictly defined conditions.

Decontamination Hierarchy

The glossary contains several concepts that look alike. They are listed below from the lowest to the highest killing power:

Sterilization
  • Sanitization: Reduces the number of microorganisms (including fungi, viruses and bacteria)
  • Disinfection: Destroys microbial structure/metabolism through an irreversible action, reducing the count to an "appropriate level"
  • Sterilization: Achieves a state free of viable microorganisms at a defined probability (such as SAL 10-6); this is the highest level

Decontamination is an overarching concept that covers all of the methods above.

3.0 Elements of a Contamination Control Strategy

Original Text

The CCS is a combination of interwoven and successively linked elements, which includes many elements of a pharmaceutical quality system and GMP measures. Figure 3.0-1🔍 illustrates this concept, the elements of which are described in this section.

The foundational elements—scientific knowledge, quality risk management, and personnel awareness/quality culture (dark blue)—inform and influence every other aspect of the CCS. These elements are described in more detail in Section 3.1.1. These foundations of CCS align well with the enablers described in ICH Quality Guideline Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System.

The individual control elements (red) are designed using the foundational elements, validated to show they can reasonably achieve the appropriate level of control (green), and then monitored to verify they achieve ongoing control (purple). The individual controls in the red row do not comprise an exhaustive list.

The quality systems, such as investigation, change control, and corrective and preventive actions (CAPA)—feedback loops—provide the mechanisms to continuously refine and improve controls and respond to unexpected events.

The governance (light blue) assesses the output of each CCS element (e.g., monitoring data, validation results, investigations, change controls) to ensure the overall CCS remains holistic and effective. A weakness in any of these elements or a mismatch between elements can lead to an ineffective CCS and therefore, contamination.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Contamination Control Strategy (CCS): This is not a single document but a systematic, integrated framework. The CCS links together all contamination-control-related elements of the Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS) and GMP measures, forming a multi-layered, interlocking safety net.

Link to ICH Q10: TR90 states explicitly that the foundational elements of a CCS are fully consistent with the "Enablers" in ICH Q10, namely knowledge management and quality risk management. This means the CCS is not a new system separate from the existing quality system; it focuses the existing framework on the core goal of contamination control.

Figure 3.0-1🔍 Analysis

Figure 3.0-1🔍 is the key figure for understanding TR90 as a whole. It uses color-coded layers to show the five levels of the CCS:

  • Dark blue (bottom layer) = foundational elements: scientific knowledge, QRM, personnel awareness/quality culture. This is the base for everything else.
  • Red = individual control elements: specific measures such as process design, facility design, equipment, cleaning and disinfection, and environmental monitoring
  • Green = validation: demonstrates that each control element actually achieves the intended level of control
  • Purple = monitoring: continually verifies that control measures remain effective in routine operation
  • Light blue (top layer) = governance: reviews the overall effectiveness of all CCS elements from a holistic perspective

Note in particular the feedback loops in the figure, which include deviation investigation, change control, and CAPA. These mechanisms ensure the CCS keeps improving rather than staying static.

Analogy

Think of the CCS as a hospital infection control program. A hospital does not prevent hospital-acquired infections with a single measure. It needs all of the following at once: medical knowledge (foundation), handwashing and disinfection procedures (individual controls), regular audits to confirm the procedures work (validation), ongoing surveillance of infection rates (monitoring), and periodic review meetings at the hospital director level (governance). If any one link is weak, gaps appear in the whole infection prevention system.

Key Notes

The source text stresses: "A weakness in any of these elements or a mismatch between elements can lead to an ineffective CCS."

This sentence carries two meanings:

  • A weakness in a single element is enough to make the whole strategy fail (the barrel principle: the shortest stave sets the water level)
  • A mismatch between elements is equally dangerous. For example, an advanced facility design with insufficiently trained personnel, or a thorough monitoring system with a lax governance review mechanism, are both typical "mismatches"

3.1 Foundations of a Contamination Control Strategy

Original Text

Understanding the foundational elements of scientific knowledge, quality risk management, and personnel awareness/quality culture is crucial to the development of an effective CCS.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Section 3.1 states explicitly the three foundational pillars of a CCS. These are not "optional extras" but prerequisites for developing an effective CCS. Without scientific knowledge you cannot identify risks; without QRM you cannot assess and prioritize them; without a quality culture you cannot ensure that the measures are actually carried out.

3.1.1 Scientific Knowledge Foundational Element

Original Text

Both scientific knowledge of the process and technical knowledge of preventing contamination are the foundations of the CCS. They are used to inform the quality risk management (QRM) foundational element of the CCS, including the elements outlined below. These elements should be summarized and/or referenced in the CCS document. When establishing the CCS, knowledge of how to prevent contamination for each step of a manufacturing process should also be considered (references to helpful technical knowledge guidances are included here as well as in Section 14.0):

Process Knowledge

  • Potential ingress points for contaminants to enter the process stream including particulates, microorganisms, viruses/bacteriophages, spores, endotoxins, and other microbial by-products (e.g., exotoxins, proteases, and other metabolites)
  • Potential proliferation points for microorganisms and viruses, including bacteriophages, to grow in the raw materials, solutions, process equipment, and process stream, thereby, allowing formation of undesirable microbial by-products
  • Microbial growth potential identified for each process step by assessing attributes such as pH, temperature, nutrients, water activity, and duration of exposure and/or by performing a profiling study of process matrices such as the antimicrobial effectiveness test
  • Viral proliferation potential identified for each process step considering the presence of viral/bacteriophage cell hosts
  • Process removal or reduction capability for potential contaminants (e.g., filtration, heating, and chromatography)
  • History of contamination events and trends (from microbial, viral, or foreign particulate matter), microbiological and endotoxin concentration, and microbial flora profile for existing processes

Note: The viral aspects noted above are only applicable for processes that use living cell systems.

Technical Knowledge

  • Microbial attributes, behavior, and biofilm development (PDA Technical Report No. 69: Bioburden and Biofilm Management in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Operations)
  • Aseptic processing (PDA Points to Consider for Aseptic Processing, Parts 1 and 2)
  • Viral attributes, behavior, and host infection (PDA Technical Report No. 83: Virus Contamination in Biomanufacturing: Risk Mitigation, Preparedness and Response)
  • Process design principles (PDA Technical Report No. 41 (rev. 2008): Virus Filtration; Technical Report No. 42: Process Validation of Protein Manufacturing; Technical Report No. 44: Quality Risk Management for Aseptic Processes; Technical Report No. 45: Filtration of Liquids Using Cellulose-Based Depth Filters; Technical Report No. 60: Process Validation: A Lifecycle Approach; Technical Report No. 81: Cell-Based Therapy Control Strategy; PDA Points to Consider for Aseptic Processing, Parts 1 and 2)
  • Facility and utility design principles (ISPE Baseline Guide: Volume 3 – Sterile Product Manufacturing Facilities; PDA Points to Consider for Aging Facilities)
  • Equipment design principles (PDA Technical Report No. 34: Design and Validation of Isolator Systems for the Manufacturing and Testing of Health Care Products; PDA Points to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Pharmaceutical Products in Isolators)
  • Cleaning, disinfection, decontamination, sanitization, sterilization principles (PDA Technical Report No. 1 (Rev. 2007): Validation of Moist Heat Sterilization Processes: Cycle Design, Development, Qualification and Ongoing Control; Technical Report No. 3 (Rev. 2013): Validation of Dry Heat Processes Used for Depyrogenation and Sterilization; Technical Report No. 26 (Rev. 2008): Sterilizing Filtration of Liquids; Technical Report No. 29 (Rev. 2012): Points to Consider for Cleaning Validation; Technical Report No. 48: Moist Heat Sterilizer Systems: Design, Commissioning, Operation, Qualification and Maintenance; Technical Report No. 49: Points to Consider for Biotechnology Cleaning Validation; Technical Report No. 61: Steam In Place; Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities; U.S. Pharmacopeia (USP) General Chapter <1072> Disinfectants and Antiseptics)
  • Raw material quality (ICH Quality Guideline Q7A: Good Manufacturing Practice Guidance for Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients)
  • Quality Risk Management principles (PDA Technical Report No. 54: Implementation of Quality Risk Management for Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology Manufacturing Operations; ICH Quality Guideline Q9: Quality Risk Management)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Scientific Knowledge: TR90 divides scientific knowledge into two categories:

  • Process Knowledge: understanding where and how your specific process can become contaminated
  • Technical Knowledge: understanding the general principles and best practices for preventing contamination

You need both. With process knowledge but no knowledge of contamination prevention techniques, you know where the problem is but not how to solve it; with technical knowledge but no understanding of the process, you have the tools but do not know where to apply them.

Key Notes

The six aspects in the Process Knowledge list are essentially answering three key questions:

  1. Where does contamination come in? (Ingress points)
  2. Can contamination proliferate during the process? (Proliferation points; microbial growth potential)
  3. How much contamination can the process remove? (Removal/reduction capability)

    4. Adding historical data (contamination events and trends) completes the risk picture. Note: the virus-related aspects apply only to processes that use live cell systems (such as biologics).

Analogy

Imagine you are a city flood control engineer. You need two kinds of knowledge:

  • Process knowledge = knowing your city: Which areas are low-lying and prone to waterlogging (ingress points)? Which areas drain poorly so that water keeps rising (proliferation)? How much rainfall can the existing levees and drainage systems handle (removal capability)? Where has flooding occurred in the past (history)?
  • Technical knowledge = knowing flood control engineering principles: how to design levees, how to build drainage systems, how to select pump station specifications. This is general engineering knowledge that applies in any city.

In its Technical Knowledge section, TR90 cites many PDA technical reports as support, which reflects that the CCS is a cross-disciplinary, comprehensive strategy. The following is a map of the references by topic:

  • Microbiology fundamentals: TR69 (bioburden and biofilm management)
  • Aseptic processing: PDA Points to Consider (Aseptic Fill/Finish, Parts 1 & 2)
  • Virus control: TR83 (viral contamination), TR41 (virus filtration)
  • Process design and validation: TR42, TR44, TR60, TR81
  • Facility/equipment design: ISPE Baseline Guide Vol. 3, TR34
  • Cleaning and sterilization: TR1, TR3, TR26, TR29, TR48, TR49, TR61, TR70, USP <1072>
  • Raw material quality: ICH Q7A

For a CDMO, this means the CCS team needs experts from multiple fields, including microbiology, engineering, quality assurance, and process development.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, you may serve multiple clients with different products at the same time. The Process Knowledge for each product differs: some are small-molecule aseptic fills, some are cell therapy products, and some are biologics. But a large part of the Technical Knowledge is common to all.

Practical recommendation: Build a cross-product "technical knowledge database" (citing all the references listed in TR90), then develop a dedicated Process Knowledge assessment for each product/client. This ensures the knowledge is complete while improving efficiency.

3.1.2 Quality Risk Management Foundational Element

Original Text

To achieve contamination control, the design of new and existing manufacturing processes and facilities should employ QRM principles to identify and assess risk with the intent to prevent and control contamination.

The CCS should be designed with redundant individual control elements so that no single failure in one element will result in contamination. If any contamination risks are not well-controlled through process design or engineered into the facility design, this will result in other CCS elements (e.g., procedural controls) being built up to compensate, thereby increasing the overall cost and complexity of manufacturing throughout the life of the product.

For example, a process step that is designed to be manual and open (exposed directly to the environment) requires all the surrounding CCS elements to be more robust than if the process were closed, including additional technical and procedural measures (e.g., local unidirectional flow protection, personnel and material movement controls, environmental controls such as cleaning, disinfection, environmental monitoring, in-process testing of that step, and validation).

For new processes, existing contamination control technologies and procedures that have proven effective in processes with similar risk profiles can be used as a model (e.g., connections, sanitization or sterilization practices, sampling methods). New contamination control technologies and procedures that are not yet proven effective in a relevant CCS, should be designed and assessed using QRM principles to reduce the risk of contamination.

New construction or renovation of production areas are ideal opportunities for innovation. This should involve an evaluation of the existing CCS for any ways to improve contamination control through innovative technologies, such as closed or barrier systems, automation, and advanced monitoring and detection methods.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Risk Management (QRM): The role of QRM in the CCS is that of a "decision engine". The design of all control measures should be based on the results of risk assessment: where to invest more resources and where simplification is acceptable.

Redundant Control Elements: This is a key principle. The CCS must be designed with no single point of failure. When one control element fails, other elements can still prevent contamination from occurring. It is like an aircraft having multiple independent hydraulic systems.

Analogy

Think of the control needs of "open vs. closed processes" as protecting a plate of sushi in different environments:

  • Closed process = sushi in a sealed container: basic temperature control is enough to keep it safe
  • Open process = sushi at an open-air stall: you need insect netting, dust covers, temperature monitoring, scheduled replacement, staff hygiene management, cleaning of the surroundings, and so on. Every additional measure is a cost.

The message of TR90 is clear: choosing a higher level of engineering control (such as a closed system) at the design stage can greatly reduce operational complexity and cost over the whole product lifecycle.

Key Notes

The source text contains a very important piece of economic logic:

"If any contamination risks are not well-controlled through process design or engineered into the facility design, this will result in other CCS elements (e.g., procedural controls) being built up to compensate, thereby increasing the overall cost and complexity of manufacturing throughout the life of the product."

The implication is: money saved on design up front is paid back later several times over in operating costs. A poorly designed facility will need more personnel, more complex procedures, and more frequent monitoring throughout its life to compensate for design shortcomings. This has major implications for CDMO facility investment decisions.

Key Notes: Innovation Opportunity

TR90 specifically points out that a new build or renovation is the best time to introduce innovation. This specifically includes:

  • Closed or barrier systems: such as isolators and RABS
  • Automation: reduces personnel intervention, and so reduces the largest source of contamination, people
  • Advanced monitoring and detection: such as rapid microbial detection and continuous particle monitoring

If your site is planning an expansion or renovation, this is a golden opportunity to revisit the CCS and upgrade it.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Suppose a client asks you to manufacture a product that requires a manual aseptic filling step in an open environment. Under the QRM principles of TR90, you would need to:

  1. Assess the additional risk that the open step introduces
  2. Design redundant control measures (unidirectional airflow protection, personnel flow control, stricter environmental monitoring)
  3. Calculate the long-term cost of these additional measures
  4. Propose to the client whether a closed-system design could be used instead to reduce overall cost

This is an opportunity for the CDMO to demonstrate technical leadership and create value for the client.

Key Concepts

Quality Culture: Quality culture is not a slogan on the wall. 4.0 Process Design, Microbial Control, and Monitoring

Original Text

Manufacturing processes should be designed, commissioned, qualified, and monitored by personnel with appropriate process, engineering, and microbiological knowledge. The manufacturing process should be designed and validated to prevent potential contamination from microorganisms, microbial byproducts (e.g., endotoxins), and foreign particulate matter.

CQAs can include a broad range of characteristics that must be controlled during the process. In this TR only microbiological and particulate attributes are addressed (see TR 60 for further discussion on establishing CQAs). The following sections address the location for testing and setting appropriate limits for assessing critical control points (CCPs).

Controlling the potential ingress of microbial contaminants throughout the drug substance and sterile drug product manufacturing processes is a key element to any contamination control program because contaminants can alter the process performance and adulterate the final product (e.g., clogging of process filters, degradation of process solutions or product molecule, unexpected impurities). For nonsterile drug products, elevated levels of microorganisms can lead to unwanted degradation and impurities; also, the presence of specific objectionable organisms can directly impact patient safety.

Risk-based process and monitoring plans for microbial control should be in place to address the specific product, process, and facility design. These plans may vary based on the type of product produced (e.g., sterile, nonsterile, biologic, small molecule), the stage of manufacturing (e.g., drug product, intermediate, drug substance), and the design of the facility (e.g., isolator, RABS, single-use, stainless steel, multiuse). The three main microbial contamination considerations for the risk assessment within the manufacturing facility, equipment, and processes are:

  • Microbial Ingress: What are the sources of contamination and how are they gaining access to the manufacturing environment?
  • Proliferation: Are there environmental factors or processing conditions that may increase the risk or extent of a contamination?
  • Persistence: Are the cleaning, sanitization/disinfection/sterilization, and monitoring programs appropriate for all the product, contact equipment, systems, and facilities to ensure bioburden is being eliminated or kept in check?

PDA Technical Report No. 69: Bioburden and Biofilm Management in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Operations provides further guidance on these three considerations.

These risk-based process and monitoring plans should consider that microorganisms may enter the manufacturing process from multiple sources—from incoming raw materials, facility design and inputs, and drug substance manufacturing, culminating in the final packaging of drug product. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Critical Control Points, CCPs: This term is borrowed from the HACCP concept in food safety. In pharmaceutical manufacturing, a CCP is a process step that, if it goes out of control, directly affects product quality. Each CCP needs a defined monitoring method and defined limits.

CQAs (critical quality attributes): This TR focuses on two categories of CQAs: microbiological attributes and particulate attributes. For other types of CQAs (such as chemical purity and potency), see PDA TR 60.

The Three Microbial Risk Dimensions

TR90 groups microbial contamination risk into three core dimensions, which form the basic framework for a CCS risk assessment:

  • Microbial Ingress: Where do contaminants come from, and how do they enter the process? This covers every possible entry point, including raw materials, personnel, equipment, and the environment.
  • Proliferation: Once microorganisms are in the system, do conditions allow them to multiply? Factors include temperature, nutrients, and hold time.
  • Persistence: Are the existing cleaning, disinfection, sterilization, and monitoring procedures sufficient to eliminate or control the microorganisms? Biofilm formation is a classic example of persistence.

Analogy

Think of microbial contamination control as a hospital infection control plan:

  • Ingress = visitors and outside items bring in pathogens → access control and disinfection are needed
  • Proliferation = a warm, humid hospital environment lets bacteria multiply → air-conditioning control and dryness are needed
  • Persistence = some stubborn superbugs cannot be cleaned away → stronger disinfection regimens and regular monitoring are needed

A pharmaceutical CCS works like hospital infection control: it must block contamination on three fronts at once, namely what comes in, what grows, and what refuses to leave.

Key Notes

This section stresses that process and monitoring plans must be risk-based, not one-size-fits-all. A plan should be tailored to the following factors:

  • Product type (sterile vs. non-sterile, biologic vs. small molecule)
  • Manufacturing stage (drug substance vs. intermediate vs. drug product)
  • Facility design (isolator vs. RABS vs. single-use vs. stainless steel vs. multi-purpose)

This means there is no universal CCS template: every product/process/facility combination needs its own assessment.

4.1 Low-Bioburden Drug Substance Process Control

Original Text

Drug substances (DS) are active ingredients with pharmacological activity for the prevention and treatment of diseases. DS undergo additional manufacturing steps that may include formulation, sterilization, and filling; those that undergo sterile filtration are generally not monitored for the presence of foreign particulate matter. Maintaining microbial control while processing the DS is critical because, although potential bioburden may be filtered out in subsequent manufacturing steps, microbial contamination during the initial processing can introduce product variability. Inconsistency of the DS could impact (a) safety, due to the release of toxic by-products, including bacterial endotoxins; (b) potency, due to degradation or modification of the product by microbial enzymes; and (c) purity, due to changes in the impurity profiles from microbial by-products.

DS and other process intermediates should be handled and stored under conditions that consider microbial ingress and proliferation and minimize the risk of microbial contamination. The probability of microbial ingress and its impact on product quality should be assessed case by case. The risk of impact on products stored frozen is low because organisms will not proliferate during storage. Microbial ingress in refrigerated products, however, may result in unacceptable bioburden levels during storage, so appropriate procedural controls should be in place to minimize it. For example, steps where a process stream is directly exposed to the environment may be performed under unidirectional Grade A air supply and a Grade C background may be sufficient based on the overall CCS.

Bottles containing the DS should be closed with a predefined torque that ensures the integrity of the seal and, at the same time, prevents cracking or damaging the bottle; in addition, sampling that introduces a pipette into the bottle should be avoided to prevent microbial ingress. Single-use systems (SUS) should be certified sterile by the vendor; single-use bags should be handled in a manner that prevents leaks, and the bags containing the product should be inspected for leaks before use. Bags that are frozen during storage may warrant additional integrity checks. Mobile stainless-steel vessels should be sterilized; vessel integrity may be validated using a hold study with a growth-promoting solution. The length of the hold may be included in the risk assessment, as it may be impractical to conduct the study for the complete length of the proposed storage time.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Drug Substance, DS: The pharmacologically active ingredient. For biologics, this usually means the purified but not yet formulated protein or antibody. DS is generally not manufactured aseptically; instead it is held under low-bioburden control.

Why is microbial control at the DS stage so important? Even if later steps (such as sterile filtration) can remove microbial cells, the damage microorganisms cause during the DS process is irreversible:

  • Safety: Once toxic by-products such as bacterial endotoxin are released, filtration cannot fully remove them
  • Potency: Microbial enzymes may degrade or modify the product molecule
  • Purity: Microbial by-products can change the impurity profile

Analogy

Imagine you are preparing a pot of premium soup stock. Even if you will strain out all the solids through a fine sieve at the end, if the stock becomes contaminated during simmering (for example, something unclean falls in), the dissolved harmful substances cannot be strained out.

In the same way, even if microbial contamination in a DS process is later removed by filtration, the changes in product quality caused by the endotoxins and enzymes the microorganisms have already released cannot be reversed by downstream filtration. This is why upstream microbial control is so important.

Key Notes

Storage conditions and microbial risk:

  • Frozen storage: Microorganisms do not proliferate, so the risk is low
  • Refrigerated storage: Microbial growth is slow, but prolonged storage can still lead to excursions above limits, so procedural controls are needed
  • Room-temperature storage: This carries the highest risk and needs the strictest controls (but has one advantage: turbidity from microbial growth can be seen by eye)

Container integrity is key: Whether bottles, single-use bags, or stainless steel vessels, seal integrity directly determines the risk of microbial ingress. Pay particular attention to the fact that freeze/thaw cycles can compromise container integrity.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A CDMO manufactures biologic DS under contract and uses single-use bag systems for storage and shipping. The key control points in practice are:

  • Confirm that the SUS supplier provides a sterility certificate, and that incoming inspection includes an integrity check
  • Visually inspect bag integrity both before and after freezing
  • Establish a DS storage hold time validation study, using growth-promoting media to challenge the microbial control capability
  • Avoid sampling by inserting a pipette directly into the DS bottle; use a closed sampling system instead

4.1.1 Viral and Mycoplasma Considerations

Original Text

Viral and mycoplasma contaminations in cell cultures (where applicable) are much less common than bacterial or fungal contaminations, but they can significantly interrupt manufacturing operations that result in a facility shutdown and product supply shortage.

Virus contamination of biological products can originate from contaminated cells with endogenous retroviruses or a latent infection or from cells contaminated during manipulation. Adventitious viruses can also be introduced from extraneous sources, including personnel, poorly designed processes or facilities, contaminated raw materials, and cross-contamination. For example, contamination with minute mouse virus has been traced to the presence of rodents in the facility. Since virus particles are not retained by 0.1-µm or 0.2-µm filters, filtration of the media or culture is not effective in removing them. Absence of adventitious viruses contaminating the final product is achieved by a combination of preventive measures, for instance, the use of appropriate sources of raw materials, heat inactivation or irradiation of the media, virus detection measures such as adventitious agent testing, and viral clearance measures in the process. PDA Technical Report 41 (Revised 2022): Virus Retentive Filtration provides further guidance on this topic.

Most of the sources of mycoplasma contamination are the same as viruses, so contamination control measures against viral contamination also reduce the challenges of mycoplasma contamination. Indiscriminate use of antibiotics during cell culture has also been reported to increase the probability of mycoplasma contamination because it may lead to less rigorous aseptic technique. Mycoplasmas are resistant to commonly used antibiotics, and they cannot be detected by turbidity or standard microscopic techniques. Of note, 0.1-µm filters are effective at reducing mycoplasma and have become commonplace for media filtration.

One advantage to testing for virus and mycoplasma early in the process is the ability to respond promptly to a confirmed contamination event. Having a strong remediation plan in place in the event of a contamination and all critical personnel well trained on the plan to ensure prompt execution is very important. Additional information on virus contamination is available in PDA Technical Report No. 83: Virus Contamination in Biomanufacturing: Risk Mitigation, Preparedness, and Response.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Adventitious virus: A virus unintentionally introduced into a cell culture system. Sources can be raw materials (for example, animal-derived media components), operators, the facility environment, or even pests in the plant (as in the minute mouse virus case).

Endogenous retrovirus: Viral sequences integrated into the host cell genome. One example is the retrovirus-like particles naturally present in CHO cell lines. These are not "contamination" but an inherent property of the cell line, yet they still require viral clearance in the downstream process.

Mycoplasma: A tiny prokaryote with no cell wall. Because of their very small size and lack of a cell wall, they pose the following particular challenges:

  • They are resistant to common antibiotics (such as penicillins) because they have no cell wall
  • They cannot be detected by standard turbidity methods or ordinary microscopy
  • They can pass through 0.2 µm filters (although 0.1 µm filters can reduce them effectively)

Analogy

Virus and mycoplasma contamination is like termites and hidden plumbing leaks in a building:

  • They occur far less often than ordinary bacterial contamination (like dust and dirt), but when they do occur the consequences are extremely serious
  • Viruses are not retained by conventional filters (just as termites can enter through the tiniest gaps), so special detection and removal measures are needed
  • Once contamination is confirmed, the whole facility often has to stop production for cleanup (just as a serious termite infestation requires fumigating the whole building)

This is why prevention and early detection are so critical: by the time the problem is found, the losses are already astronomical.

Key Notes

Virus vs. mycoplasma vs. bacteria: differences in filtration effectiveness:

  • Bacteria: Effectively retained by 0.2 µm filters
  • Mycoplasma: May pass through 0.2 µm; 0.1 µm can reduce them effectively → this is why 0.1 µm filtration is now widely used for culture media
  • Viruses: Not retained by either 0.1 µm or 0.2 µm → a dedicated virus removal filter (about 20 nm) or a virus inactivation step is needed

The antibiotic trap: The document specifically warns against overuse of antibiotics. On the surface, antibiotics can "mask" bacterial contamination, but in practice they lead operators to relax the rigor of aseptic technique, and they are ineffective against mycoplasma, so the result is a higher risk of mycoplasma contamination.

Regulatory Context

Cross-references to related PDA technical reports:

  • PDA TR 41 (2022 revision): Virus Retentive Filtration: design and validation of downstream virus clearance steps
  • PDA TR 83: Virus Contamination in Biomanufacturing: Risk Mitigation, Preparedness, and Response: establishing a contamination response plan

For a CDMO, the impact of a virus contamination event goes beyond the loss of a single batch. It can also lead to a facility shutdown lasting months, full cleaning validation, regulatory investigation, and supply interruption for customers.

  • Sterilization step = the security checkpoint: strict screening through X-ray and metal detectors
  • Post-sterilization = the restricted area/departure lounge: once inside, there is no more contact with the outside, and any risk brought in from outside is met with zero tolerance

Key Notes

Easily overlooked critical control points:

  • Final filtration location: EU GMP Annex 1 requires that the final filtration point for sterile filtration be as close as possible to the filling needle. This shortens the path along which the post-filtration product is exposed to contamination risk.
  • Microbial control of chromatography resins: Resins have a porous structure, which limits contact between cleaning agents and microorganisms, and resins are more prone to biofilm growth near the end of their lifetime, so it is essential to validate the effectiveness of the sanitizing solution
  • The hidden benefit of room-temperature media storage: Holding sterile media at room temperature before use allows turbidity from microbial growth to be seen by eye, which is a simple but effective means of detecting contamination
  • PUPSIT: Pre-use post-sterilization integrity testing confirms filter integrity before filtration starts, like checking the gas before leaving the house

Special Challenges of Nonsterilizable Products

Cell therapy, some gene therapy, and vaccine products cannot undergo terminal sterilization or sterile filtration, because these steps would destroy the active component (living cells or viral vectors). This means:

  • The entire process must be performed under aseptic conditions (end-to-end aseptic processing)
  • CCS requirements are even more stringent, because there is no downstream "safety net" (such as a filtration or sterilization step)
  • Control of extraneous particulates is also extremely important, and must cover all solutions and product contact surfaces

This is one of the biggest challenges a CDMO faces when entering the ATMP field.

4.3 Nonsterile Drug Product Process Control

Original Text

Effective CCS is also important for nonsterile finished products that are susceptible to contamination. Microbial contamination can lead to degradation of the product, loss of potency, and potentially unsafe levels of microorganisms and by-products being administered to the patient. Consider that many nonsterile products are available without prescription to a broad population of people with a range of underlying ailments or immune-compromising conditions. Nonsterile products and their intermediates should be handled and stored under conditions that limit the risk of microbial contamination considering the specific hazards of microbial ingress and proliferation.

Liquid and semisolid nonsterile products, such as syrups, creams, and ointments, are inherently prone to contamination due to the water activity levels that support microbial growth. For these products, preservatives are often added to avoid fouling during the shelf life. Preservatives are rarely effective against all potentially contaminating species or high levels of bioburden and may lose effectiveness over the product's shelf life, so microbial control is also important to prevent and detect microorganisms during the process.

Solid dosage products, such as tablets and dry powders, are generally less prone to contamination due to lower water activity; however, they are not contamination-proof. Specific contamination hazards should be identified in the manufacturing process and in the handling and distribution of nonsterile products. Some process operations can introduce unacceptable levels of contamination, such as tablet-coating, and there is potential for contamination during handling and dispensing activities, for example, blister packs protecting the product until administration.

Foreign matter, such as particulates, should also be controlled in nonsterile products because foreign matter can include toxic materials that leach into the product, can introduce microbial contamination that degrades the product, and can lead to product rejection for cosmetic reasons.

Raw material and equipment controls are particularly important for nonsterile products that are often made on a large scale. Raw materials can be a source of very high levels of microorganisms and their byproducts into the process and may overchallenge the process controls, resulting in final product contamination. Process water is one of the most abundant raw materials and is well known for introducing contaminants, including those that may be resistant to preservatives and difficult to detect with traditional microbiological testing, such as Burkholderia cepacia. Equipment often includes large vessels that are potential sources of high levels of microorganisms. Equipment must be cleaned and drained when not in use to prevent fouling with biofilms. For equipment designs that may prevent the cleanability or drainability, additional actions should be undertaken periodically to reduce the occurrence of biofilm (see PDA Technical Report 69 for further details). In addition, the process considerations in Tables 4.2-1 and 4.2-2 can be useful for nonsterile products to extrapolate from and customize to the organization's operations.

Process monitoring for nonsterile products often includes methods to detect total microbial numbers as well as specific microorganisms that are considered objectionable for that product, and potentially the patient population, based on its route of administration. In-process monitoring of nonsterile product manufacture, which is generally less frequent for low-bioburden drug substance processes, should be conducted where specific hazards exist in the nonsterile production process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Nonsterile Product CCS: Many people wrongly assume that only sterile injectables need a rigorous contamination control strategy. In fact, nonsterile products (such as syrups, creams, ointments and tablets) also need an effective CCS. Microbial contamination can cause product degradation and loss of potency, and can even expose patients to unsafe levels of microorganisms and their by-products.

Water Activity: This is the key parameter that determines whether microorganisms can grow in a product. Liquid and semisolid dosage forms have higher water activity and are therefore inherently more susceptible to microbial contamination. Solid dosage forms (tablets, dry powders) have lower water activity but are not completely immune.

Objectionable Organisms: Monitoring of nonsterile products looks not only at total microbial counts but also at specific "objectionable organisms", which depend on the route of administration and the target patient population.

Analogy

Think of food safety management in a restaurant. Even dry goods that need no refrigeration (such as crackers and nuts) must be protected from pests and mold -- this is like the control of solid dosage forms. Soups and sauces (liquid/semisolid products), with their high water content, are more prone to bacterial growth and need stricter temperature control and preservatives. But even with preservatives, if the raw ingredients are heavily contaminated (for example, unclean water), the best preservative cannot rescue the product -- which is why raw material control is so important.

Key Notes

The Burkholderia cepacia threat: TR90 specifically names microorganisms such as Burkholderia cepacia. They can survive in pharmaceutical water, may be resistant to preservatives, and are difficult to detect with conventional microbiological test methods. In recent years the FDA has paid close attention to recalls of nonsterile products caused by such organisms.

Biofilm risk: Dead legs and poorly draining areas in large equipment are prone to biofilm formation. Once a biofilm has formed, routine cleaning can hardly eradicate it, and it becomes a persistent source of contamination. TR90 cites PDA TR69 for further reference.

Limitations of preservatives: Preservatives are not a cure-all -- they rarely act against all potential contaminating species, and their efficacy may decline over the product shelf life. In-process microbial control therefore remains the indispensable first line of defense.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Suppose your site manufactures both oral liquids and topical creams. When developing the CCS, each needs to be assessed separately:

  • Oral liquids: high water activity, so attention is needed on the microbial quality of process water, cleaning validation of containers, and the Preservative Effectiveness Test (PET)
  • Topical creams: besides microorganisms, foreign matter control also needs attention, because appearance quality equally affects product acceptability
  • Shared equipment must have cleaning validation, with particular attention to drainability in the design to prevent biofilm formation

4.4 In-Process Monitoring

Original Text

In-process monitoring provides information about the state of control of the process, facility, and product. A holistic control plan includes monitoring for microbial control of the process, facility, and product, including intermediates. Mycoplasma and viruses are also included in the monitoring plan of biological products with cell systems that can propagate those contaminants. Particle monitoring is included for the environment, and visual inspection of product-contacting equipment, consumables, and solutions is conducted where a foreign particle risk for the product exists.

A strategic sampling plan should take into consideration the criticality of the process steps, the susceptibility to microbial ingress, the growth-promoting ability of the process intermediates and solutions, and the downstream particle-removal steps. The collection and handling of in-process samples should ensure the chain of custody from collection to final review of the test results. Table 4.4.1-1🔍 provides more detail on monitoring plans.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

In-Process Monitoring: This is the window into whether the process, the facility and the product are in a state of control. It is more than "sampling and testing"; it is a component of a holistic control plan covering microbial control, Mycoplasma, viruses and particulate monitoring.

Strategic Sampling Plan: More sampling is not necessarily better; sampling must be planned "strategically". Factors to consider include the criticality of the process step, its susceptibility to microbial ingress, the growth-promoting capacity of the intermediate, and downstream particulate removal steps.

Chain of Custody: The ability to trace everything from sampling through to the review of the final result, ensuring sample integrity and the reliability of results.

Analogy

In-process monitoring is like a hospital infection control program -- rather than waiting until patients show symptoms of infection, monitoring points are placed along every possible route of infection. The operating room has airborne particle monitoring (environmental monitoring), surgical instruments have sterilization verification (equipment monitoring), and healthcare staff undergo regular hand hygiene audits (personnel monitoring). Likewise, monitoring in pharmaceutical processes needs to be deployed strategically at each potential risk point.

Key Notes

Additional requirements for biological products: If a biological product is manufactured (using cell systems), the monitoring plan must also include testing for Mycoplasma and viruses, because these contaminants can proliferate in cell culture systems.

The importance of visual inspection: TR90 specifically mentions "visual inspection" of product-contact equipment, consumables and solutions to detect the risk of foreign particulates, a control measure that is often underestimated but extremely important.

4.4.1 Bioburden/Endotoxin Monitoring

Original Text

The control and monitoring of bioburden and endotoxin levels throughout the manufacture of DS and DP are important elements of the overall microbial control program. Process monitoring provides valuable insight into the microbial status of the manufacturing process, which in turn leads to the microbial quality of the final product. A risk-based assessment involving the process mapping of potential ingress sites and operational steps that are susceptible to bioburden growth should be used to develop the monitoring plan. The assessment should include an evaluation of the contamination risks and the capability of the process at each process step to mitigate each risk. Recurring sporadic contaminations should be investigated as they may be indicative of biofilm present in product-contact surfaces of the equipment or of pervasive environmental contamination in the process areas. PDA Technical Report 69 contains additional information on biofilm.

Several aspects should be considered when developing a bioburden and endotoxin monitoring plan. What, when, and how to monitor should be well planned, based on the risks to the process and product, while at the same time ensuring the sampling process does not contaminate the process or produce false-positive results. A sensible approach includes mapping of the process based on the steps with the likelihood of microorganism ingress; susceptible locations where residual risks cannot be mitigated should then be monitored based on that mapping. In general, performing bioburden monitoring should be considered before bioburden reduction steps are conducted. Inputs range widely from sterile raw materials to highly growth-promoting media used for production. The manufacturing facility and processes may include a multitude of manufacturing risk levels, including the design and classification of the area and its EM history. That history should include contaminations that spread or are persistent and difficult to eliminate, growth-promotion properties of the in-process intermediates, and the type of equipment—from open tank processes and single-use bag systems to bioreactors with intricate piping and valve configurations (16).

Dealing with the data produced can also be challenging. As in-process bioburden counts are typically low and not normally distributed, classical statistical approaches (e.g., parametric or ±3 standard deviations) cannot be used for establishing meaningful limits. Therefore, a scientific, risk-based approach should be employed to set limits that can be further evaluated upon gathering sufficient historical data from longer-term manufacturing. Also, a robust metric control plan should be developed that covers the facility and the overall network to ensure there is appropriate senior management visibility to the microbial/endotoxin state of control.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Bioburden: The number of viable microorganisms present in a process intermediate or the final product. It is the core indicator for assessing the microbial control status of a process.

Endotoxin: A component of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria. Even after the microorganisms have been removed by filtration, endotoxin may remain. For injectable products, endotoxin can cause pyrogenic reactions and is an important safety indicator.

Risk-Based Assessment: The monitoring plan should be developed on the basis of process mapping, identifying potential points of microbial ingress and the operating steps prone to microbial proliferation, rather than simply sampling at every step.

EM History: Past environmental monitoring data are an important reference for designing the monitoring plan, especially for contamination events that are widespread, persistent and difficult to eliminate.

Analogy

Designing a bioburden and endotoxin monitoring plan is like designing the security system for a building. You would not install identical cameras in every room; instead you first draw the floor plan (the process map), identify the places most vulnerable to intrusion (such as the front entrance, the underground parking entrance and the rooftop skylight), and then deploy the densest monitoring in these high-risk areas. Similarly, the point before a bioburden reduction step (such as filtration) is the key sampling point -- like checking for suspicious persons before the security checkpoint.

Key Notes

Recurring Sporadic Contaminations: TR90 particularly stresses that such seemingly random contamination events may indicate a biofilm on the product-contact surfaces of equipment or widespread environmental contamination in the process area. A thorough investigation must be carried out; do not dismiss them as merely sporadic events.

The risk of sampling itself: The sampling process may introduce contamination or produce false-positive results. The design of sampling methods and timing must therefore balance the need to obtain information with the need to protect process integrity.

Statistical challenges with the data: In-process bioburden counts are usually low and not normally distributed, so traditional parametric statistical methods (such as ±3 standard deviations) cannot be used to establish meaningful control limits. Limits should be set using a scientific, risk-based approach and continuously evaluated and adjusted once sufficient historical data have accumulated.

Limit-Setting Logic

Because of the particular nature of bioburden data, the limit-setting logic recommended by TR90 is as follows:

Traditional approach (not applicable):
  Alert Limit = Mean + 2SD
  Action Limit = Mean + 3SD
  Problem: data are low and not normally distributed, so SD is meaningless

Recommended approach:
  1. Risk assessment → initial limit setting
  2. Long-term manufacturing → accumulate historical data
  3. Data trend analysis → limit evaluation and update
  4. Network-level metric control plan → high-level visibility

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: You are establishing a bioburden/endotoxin monitoring plan for a new biologic drug substance (Biologic DS) process. Key steps:

  • Step 1 - Process mapping: From cell culture, harvest and purification through to the final drug substance, annotate the microbial ingress risk at each step
  • Step 2 - Risk ranking: open operations > semi-closed operations > fully closed operations; growth-promoting media > buffers > cleaning water
  • Step 3 - Sampling point selection: Give priority to sampling before the bioburden reduction step (such as filtration); sample after high-risk open operations
  • Step 4 - Initial limits: Set initial limits based on the risk assessment and revise them as data accumulate over manufacturing batches
  • Step 5 - Trend monitoring: Establish a cross-site metric control plan to ensure management has visibility of the microbial/endotoxin control status

  2. What underlying problems might recurring sporadic contamination events indicate? How should they be investigated?

5.1 Facility Design

Original Text

Key elements of the CCS should be decided during the design phase of a production facility to minimize the risk of contamination based on the specific process design and hazards. Facility designs provide environmental control through air pressure cascades, area classifications, cleanability, physical segregation, and flows based upon Good Engineering Practices (GEPs). These design features establish the structure-based barriers that reduce the airborne movement of contaminants into the manufacturing areas and enable the removal of contaminants that do enter.

QRM is critical for the design and management of new and existing facilities, equipment, processes, and manufacturing activities. Appropriate use of QRM tools should identify the contamination risks to the product and environment, and then mitigate those risks using appropriate contamination control measures. Risk mitigation for contamination ingress should be incorporated during equipment design (3).

Special consideration should be given to prevent adventitious agent contamination from other products, for instance, when multiple viral products are manufactured in the same facility. In this case, the facility design should consider the biosafety level(s) of the viral products to identify such segregation and containment measures, personnel and material flow, personal protective equipment, disinfection strategy, physical separation of rooms including the corridor and testing labs, and segregation and dedication of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC). These elements should be included in a risk assessment that supports existing facilities and the facility design phase where possible. The ISPE Baseline Guide: Volume 7 – Risk-Based Manufacture of Pharmaceutical Products (Second Edition) provides information on a risk-based approach to manage the risk of cross-contamination.

Closed or functionally closed processes are the most effective contamination control measures to create barriers between the product and the environment. These principles apply to single or multiuse equipment. PDA Technical Report No. 54-5: Quality Risk Management for the Design, Qualification, and Operation of Manufacturing Systems provides a detailed and thorough guideline for manufacturing systems design. PDA Technical Report 69 also details how facility design and environmental control contribute to contamination control.

The key difference between Holistic and Non-Holistic:

5.2 Personnel Flow

Original Text

During the design phase of any facility, the movement or "flow" of personnel, material, and wastes should be established using QRM principles. People are the primary source of microorganisms in cleanrooms, so personnel flow is critically important to contamination control.

Where the process is exposed and inherently at risk of contamination from the environment, requiring unidirectional movement of personnel and limiting areas where people congregate can minimize potential contamination. For an existing facility where unidirectional flow is not achievable, additional procedural and design controls are often needed to prevent people with different levels of gowning from comingling.

Gowning and behavior are key controls to limit contamination from personnel; they are discussed in Section 8.0.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Personnel Flow: The design of the routes people take through the facility. TR90 opens by stating that "people are the primary source of microorganisms in the cleanroom", which is the basic premise of all sterile manufacturing.

Unidirectional Movement: Personnel move in a single direction only, from the cleaner area to the less clean area, and do not turn back. This prevents people who have been in a lower-cleanliness area from carrying contamination back into a higher-cleanliness area.

Comingling: Personnel in different gowning levels mixing together. For example, a Grade B operator in full sterile gowning and a Grade D operator in plant uniform passing each other in the same corridor is a serious contamination risk.

Analogy

Think of an airport terminal: passengers who have passed security (cleanroom personnel) and those who have not (personnel from unclassified areas) are strictly separated. Once you have cleared security and entered the controlled area, you cannot freely walk back out and return, because that would defeat the purpose of the whole screening. Unidirectional personnel flow in a pharmaceutical plant follows the same logic.

Key Notes

The challenge for existing facilities: TR90 pragmatically acknowledges that not every plant can achieve perfect unidirectional flow. For existing facilities, additional "procedural controls" and "design remediation" are needed to prevent comingling, for example time management (staggered entry and exit by shift), visual signage systems and electronic access control.

It is worth noting that TR90 leaves the details of gowning and behavioral rules to Section 8.0. What is emphasized here is "route design", that is, control at the level of physical space.

5.3 Material and Waste Controls

Original Text

Flow paths of material and waste are important to control because they can carry contaminants into and around the facility, and the movement of waste out of the facility may cause contamination as it exits. Facility design should minimize the movement of material and waste and separate them from the flow of personnel between areas of different classifications. These separate and dedicated pathways will also help prevent cross-contamination between products. Receiving and storage areas must be of sufficient size and managed to account for materials and wastes in transit to prevent particulate and microbial contamination from personnel as they navigate the clean zones. Additionally, a defined spill response is important to prevent contamination.

Proper material transfer is essential to limiting the ingress of contaminants to classified environments. Consider that external packaging (e.g., cardboard, plastic) are heavily laden with microorganisms from the outside environment, especially mold and bacterial spores. Some of these microbes are attracted by the electrostatic charge of the packaging materials or are inherent to the packaging material and should not be allowed to enter classified areas.

While there are several approaches to contamination control for material transfer, the three most common are chemical disinfection, shedding layers of packaging, and direct transfer through a sterilization system (e.g., pass through autoclave, e-beam system) or through a sterile-designed connection (e.g., rapid transfer port). Chemical disinfection is the process of using a validated chemical agent to treat material or equipment before transfer into a classified area; it should include physical application (i.e., wiping) to achieve mechanical removal as well. This process should be qualified and include a validated minimum contact time with the disinfection agent. Purchasing or preparing items with multiple layers of sterile packaging or bags is another strategy, whereby a layer is removed at the transition to a higher-grade area.

An equipment flow should be clearly defined for the facility, including a map showing entry and exit and from storage to the area of use, with protection from the areas of greatest risk where contamination can occur (e.g., unidirectional material air locks). The surfaces of portable and nondedicated equipment should be maintained, cleaned, and disinfected and staged appropriately.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Material Transfer: The procedure for safely bringing materials from an unclassified area into the clean manufacturing area. This is a high-risk step because outer packaging (cartons, plastic film) carries large numbers of microorganisms, especially molds and bacterial spores.

Three material transfer control strategies:

  • Chemical Disinfection: Wiping the material surface with a validated disinfectant, which must include physical wiping (mechanical removal) and a minimum contact time.
  • Shedding Layers: Materials are prepared in multiple layers of sterile packaging, and one layer is removed on entry to each higher cleanliness area, much like the multiple checkpoints at an airport.
  • Direct Sterilization Transfer: Direct transfer through a pass-through autoclave, an electron beam system or a Rapid Transfer Port (RTP).

Spill Response: The emergency procedure for when materials or waste spill in a cleanroom. It is often overlooked but extremely important.

Analogy

Imagine bringing ingredients bought at an outside market into an operating-room-grade kitchen: you would never carry in muddy vegetables or blood-stained plastic bags directly. You would first remove the outer packaging outside the kitchen (shedding layers), then wipe with disinfectant (chemical disinfection), or pass the ingredients through a UV disinfection box (sterilizing transfer system). Each "checkpoint" lowers the risk of contaminants entering along with the ingredients.

Key Notes

Easily overlooked details:

  • The electrostatic charge on outer packaging attracts microorganisms. This is not an intuitive contamination mechanism, but TR90 specifically raises it.
  • Chemical disinfection is more than "just spraying": it requires physical wiping (mechanical removal) plus a validated minimum contact time.
  • The direction of waste flow also matters: waste moving "out" can equally spread contamination, so attention must not be limited to materials coming "in".

Practical Application

For a CDMO handling several clients' products at once, designing dedicated routes for materials and waste is especially important. Consider the following scenarios:

  • Does the route taking Product A's culture medium from the warehouse to the preparation area cross Product B's waste exit route?
  • Are separate material airlocks needed for different clients' raw materials?
  • Has the Rapid Transfer Port (RTP) been validated as a sterile transfer method in isolator operations?

  2. How does your facility ensure that the "minimum contact time" for chemical disinfection is followed consistently? What are the common deviations?
  3. Design a material transfer process from the warehouse to a Grade B area, listing the contamination control measures at each step.

5.4 Cleaning and Disinfection for Facilities

Original Text

The cleaning and disinfection program is essential for an effective CCS. Refer to PDA Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities for further information and details on developing an effective program.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Cleaning and Disinfection Program: TR90 mentions this only briefly here and refers readers to PDA TR70 for full guidance. This indicates that cleaning and disinfection is a separate and substantial subject that deserves dedicated study.

Cleaning and disinfection are two different steps: cleaning removes visible and invisible residues (organic and inorganic), while disinfection kills or reduces the remaining microorganisms. The order matters: clean first, then disinfect, because organic residues reduce the efficacy of the disinfectant.

Analogy

It is like washing dishes at home: you would not drop dirty plates straight into disinfectant. You first remove grease and food residue with dish soap (cleaning), and only then use disinfectant or heat to kill germs (disinfection). If a film of grease remains on the dish, the disinfectant cannot reach the bacteria and its effect is greatly reduced.

Key Notes

Although this section of TR90 is short, the importance of the cleaning and disinfection program in the CCS should not be underestimated. A complete program should include:

  • Disinfectant selection and rotation strategy (to prevent microbial resistance)
  • Disinfectant efficacy validation
  • Cleaning and disinfection frequency and schedule
  • Residue control (to prevent disinfectant residues from affecting the product)
  • Personnel training and verification of consistent execution

It is recommended to read PDA TR70 alongside to build a complete body of knowledge.

This may also apply to noncritical "support" utilities that are not in product contact, such as cooling and vacuum systems and pretreated feed water.

Key Concepts

Critical Utility Systems: Systems that come into direct contact with the product stream: water, steam, HVAC and gases. They are the "lifeblood" of the process. You hardly notice them when they run normally, but when they fail, the consequences can be severe enough to cause production shutdown or product contamination.

Analogy

Utilities are like the plumbing and wiring of a building: when the pipes work normally, you open the tap and get clean water without thinking about the pipework.   2. From "passing test results = no problem" to "physical parameter trends are the early warning": Microbial testing has inherent limitations (small sample volume, long incubation time), whereas physical parameters (temperature, pressure, flow rate, TOC) reflect the system state in real time.
  3. From "investigate only when limits are exceeded" to "any abnormal trend deserves attention": This includes sub-alert level results, because they may be an early sign of system deterioration.

5.6 Water and Steam

Original Text

Several detailed guidelines exist for contamination control of water systems because water is one of the main raw materials used in drug manufacturing. Valuable information is available in PDA Technical Report 69, USP <1231> Water for Pharmaceutical Purposes, ISPE Baseline Guide Vol. 4: Water & Steam Systems, 3rd Edition, and EMA Guideline on the Quality of Water for Pharmaceutical Use. Steam is derived from process water and used for sterilization activities, which are critical to the CCS. Qualification and management strategies should be formed by an interdepartmental team that includes a microbiologist experienced in monitoring and contamination control. Water systems undergo an extensive qualification to ensure seasonal variation is monitored. During the entire qualification period, all results, including sub-alert level results and species identification, should be reviewed for adverse trends by an appropriate SME. This interdisciplinary approach has proven successful at catching and preventing major problems later in routine operations which, in turn, can impact the contamination control program.

Viewing new water systems as "inherently risky" is useful. Unknown flaws in the design, assembly, or management of the system can allow microbial biofilm to develop, which may present as sporadic, sub-alert results. PDA Technical Report 69 contains further details on preventing and reducing biofilm.

The routine water-monitoring program should be based on QRM principles and the learnings from the qualification period. The critical points of use that deliver water to the process should be monitored more frequently than noncritical points of use that are used for facility cleaning or not used at all. Also, water ports that represent the worst-case location in the distribution loop should be monitored frequently.

On-line technologies, such as total organic carbon and conductivity, have increased the ability to detect water system problems in real time, potentially avoiding the use of compromised water for production. Similar on-line technologies for real-time bioburden and endotoxins are currently being investigated; they offer a huge advantage over lengthy lab-based assays in that atypical results are identified immediately and can be corrected, thus addressing contamination issue in a timelier manner.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Water Systems: Water is one of the most important raw materials in pharmaceutical manufacturing. Pharmaceutical water grades include Purified Water (PW) and Water for Injection (WFI), and their microbial and endotoxin control directly affects product quality.

Microbial Biofilm: A film-like community of microorganisms formed on the walls of a water system. Once established, biofilm is extremely difficult to remove completely and continuously sheds microorganisms into the water, leading to intermittent microbial results. TR90 specifically stresses that a new system should be regarded as "inherently risky".

Seasonal Variation: The microbial quality of a water system changes with the seasons (temperature, variation in source water quality), so qualification must cover a full seasonal cycle.

Critical Points of Use: Outlets that deliver water directly into the process. They should be monitored more frequently than outlets used for cleaning or those not in use.

Analogy

Think of a water system as the tap water pipework in your home: newly installed pipes look clean, but tiny scratches made during installation and leftover weld debris can become a "breeding ground" where bacteria attach. Over time these bacteria form a "scale-like" layer of biofilm. On the surface, test results are occasionally slightly high and occasionally normal (sporadic, sub-alert results), yet the biofilm is quietly growing on the pipe wall. By the time you actually exceed a limit, the problem is already serious. This is why TR90 says a new system should be treated as "inherently risky".

Key Notes

The importance of an interdepartmental team: TR90 explicitly requires that the qualification and management strategy for water systems be developed by an interdepartmental team that must include a microbiologist with experience in monitoring and contamination control. This is not "an engineering department matter"; it is a core CCS issue.

Advances in real-time monitoring technology: TOC (total organic carbon) and conductivity can already be monitored online in real time, but real-time detection of microorganisms and endotoxin is still under development. Once these technologies mature, they will greatly shorten the time from "discovering a problem" to "taking action".

Worst-case Location: The position in the water distribution loop where flow is slowest, temperature is lowest, or dead legs are most likely to form. These are the places where microorganisms proliferate most easily.

Practical Application

Key considerations for a CDMO building or expanding a WFI system:

  • Qualification must cover the seasonal variation of at least one full year
  • Even for results below the alert level, perform species identification, because the appearance of certain species may point to a system design flaw
  • Set up a trend monitoring system that also tracks sub-alert level results
  • Work with suppliers to confirm that the piping material, weld quality and slope design promote drainage and prevent dead legs

  1. Why does TR90 recommend treating a new water system as "inherently risky"? Give three possible sources of risk.
  2. Explain why "sporadic, sub-alert results" may be more worrying than a single "action level excursion".
  3. If your WFI system shows a sub-alert level trend of microbial growth during summer in the qualification period, how would you handle it?

5.7 Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning (HVAC)

Original Text

The heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) system is a critical element of the contamination control strategy as it is the primary system that attains the appropriate environmental quality. In addition, isolators or other barrier technology should be used to protect sterile products.

Correct operation of the HVAC system ensures appropriate air quality by:

  • Introducing preconditioned air of the right quality
  • Distributing clean air while removing contamination via strategically placed HEPA filters and air returns
  • Establishing the air pressure cascade to prevent ingress of contaminants from less-controlled areas
  • Maintaining the temperature and relative humidity to prevent microbial growth and provide optimal working conditions for operators (e.g., to avoid sweating, shivering, and distracting discomfort)

To achieve this state, the HVAC environmental controls should be optimized and qualified to maintain the appropriate level of environmental quality. (Section 7.0 further discusses environmental qualification and monitoring.) To prevent process contamination, the other elements of the CCS, including the traffic of personnel and materials, cleaning and disinfection program, and specific hazards in each area, should be considered. For example, an area with heavy personnel traffic will require more air changes than a lightly used area, and an area that is routinely exposed to steaming operations may require active temperature and humidity control and a robust exhaust system.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

HVAC System: TR90 positions HVAC as the "primary system for achieving appropriate environmental quality". It has four main functions:

  1. Introducing pretreated air: Ensuring that the air supplied to the cleanroom meets requirements
  2. HEPA filtration and exhaust: Purifying the air through strategically placed HEPA filters and removing contamination through return air grilles
  3. Establishing the air pressure cascade: Higher-grade areas are kept at positive pressure to prevent air from lower-grade areas flowing back in
  4. Temperature and humidity control: Suppressing microbial growth while keeping operators comfortable. Discomfort leads to abnormal behavior (sweating increases particle shedding, shivering affects precise operations)

Barrier Technology: Including isolators and RABS, which provide an additional layer of product protection on top of HVAC.

Analogy

HVAC is the cleanroom's "respiratory system": it continuously draws in filtered fresh air (inhaling) while expelling contaminated old air (exhaling). The pressure cascade acts like an "invisible door": air always flows from the high-pressure (clean) area to the low-pressure (dirtier) area, so even when you open a door, no dirty air flows "backward" into the clean area. If the "respiratory system" fails, for example through a damaged HEPA filter or pressure imbalance, the whole cleanroom is like a person who "cannot breathe properly" and is systematically exposed to contamination risk.

Key Notes

HVAC design must consider actual use: TR90 gives two examples:

  • Areas with heavy personnel traffic need more air changes, because people are the main source of particles and microorganisms
  • Areas where steam operations are frequent need active temperature and humidity control and a strong exhaust system; otherwise a hot, humid environment will actually promote microbial growth

This again confirms TR90's holistic thinking: HVAC cannot be designed in isolation but must be considered together with other CCS elements such as personnel flow, material flow, and cleaning and disinfection.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 mention operators' "sweating, shivering, and distracting discomfort"? How is this related to contamination control?
  2. When designing an HVAC system, how do you determine the number of air changes for each area? What factors must be considered?
  3. If the HVAC pressure cascade unexpectedly reverses at some point (for example, on restart after a power outage), what response procedure should your CCS include?

5.8 Gases

Original Text

Compressed gases are often introduced into the manufacturing process stream and into production equipment after point-of-use filtration. Noncritical support gases that are not product-contacting may also warrant strong control because they can impact other critical systems.

Procedures for the qualification, maintenance, and monitoring of gas systems should be created using QRM principles. Once qualified, a routine monitoring program should be put in place that includes physical and microbiological parameters. Moisture is a critical parameter because moisture can lead to microbial proliferation, especially for molds, which require less moisture than bacteria and yeasts.

GMP regulations from the EU and U.S. mention that compressed gases should have similar or better physical and microbiological quality than the cleanroom air where the gases are used. However, gases that enter a closed process stream should meet the air quality demanded by the process. For example, a low-bioburden process that is only exposed to a Grade A air supply when open processing occurs should be fed gas that meets Grade A air-supply quality.

More information about gases is provided in ISO 8573-1: 2010 Compressed Air — Part 1: Contaminants and Purity Classes, ISPE’s Good Practice Guide: Process Gases, and the major pharmacopeias. Information about gas filtration can be found in PDA Technical Report No. 40: Sterilizing Filtration of Gases.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Compressed Gases: Gases commonly used in pharmaceutical manufacturing include nitrogen (N2), compressed air and carbon dioxide (CO2). They are used for bottle blowing, blanketing, product transfer, sterile connections and similar purposes.

Point-of-Use Filtration: Final filtration of the gas at the location closest to where it is used, before it enters the process or equipment (usually with a 0.2 micron or finer filter).

Moisture: TR90 specifically identifies moisture as a key parameter for gas systems, because molds need very little moisture to grow (less than bacteria and yeasts). If trace moisture appears in a dry gas system, it may indicate a leak or a failed dryer.

Analogy

A gas system is like the natural gas pipeline in your home: you expect pure natural gas when you turn on the stove, not "dirty gas" mixed with moisture or dust. If moisture condenses in the line, it not only reduces combustion efficiency but also corrodes the pipe. In a pharmaceutical plant, that moisture becomes a breeding ground for microorganisms. So the "dryness" of a gas system is not just an engineering quality issue but a contamination control issue.

Key Notes

The logic behind gas quality standards:

  • Gases used in open processes: The quality should be equal to or better than the cleanroom air quality of that area (for example, gas used in a Grade A area should meet Grade A air quality).
  • Gases used in closed processes: The quality should meet the requirements of the process itself. It does not necessarily have to reach the ambient air grade, but it must satisfy the process needs.

This is risk-based, graded control: not every gas needs to meet the highest standard, and the quality requirement is set according to "how the gas contacts the product".

Noncritical support gases also need control: Even if they do not contact the product directly, poor-quality support gases can affect other critical systems (for example, a valve driven by compressed air that contains moisture and particles may malfunction or lose its seal).

Practical Application

Key points for managing gas systems in a CDMO environment:

  • Establish a qualification plan for the gas system covering parameters such as particles, moisture, oil and microorganisms
  • Define the replacement frequency and integrity testing plan for point-of-use filters
  • Pay particular attention to cleaning of gas lines at product changeover, to avoid residues from the previous batch cross-contaminating through the gas system
  • Include gas system monitoring data in the CCS's overall trend analysis

Practice Questions

  1. Why are molds more sensitive than bacteria and yeasts to moisture in gas systems? What does this imply for the dryness requirements of gas systems?
  2. If the moisture content of nitrogen blanketing used in a closed process is found to be high, what are the possible root causes? What investigation steps would you take?
  3. EU GMP and U.S. GMP require compressed gas quality to be "equal to or better than" the cleanroom air quality of the area of use. Explain the rationale for this requirement.

Section Summary

  • The design stage determines the foundation of the CCS: The structural barriers of the facility (air pressure cascades, area classifications, physical segregation) are the first line of defense against contamination and must be considered with QRM from the design stage.
  • Closed processes are the most effective control measure: Closed or functionally closed processes isolate the product from the environment most effectively and reduce reliance on personnel behavior and environmental cleanliness.
  • People are the main source of contamination in the cleanroom: Personnel flow design (unidirectional movement, preventing comingling of personnel in different gowning levels) is the key to controlling human-borne contamination.
  • Three material transfer strategies: Chemical disinfection (with physical wiping), shedding of packaging layers, and direct transfer through a sterilization system/RTP. Each method must be validated.
  • Utilities are a core part of the CCS, not standalone equipment: Water, steam, HVAC and gas systems directly affect product quality and must be managed with a holistic approach.
  • Physical parameter trends take priority over microbial test results: Microbial testing has inherent limitations, while physical parameters such as TOC, conductivity, differential pressure and moisture give more immediate early warning.
  • Sub-alert level trends also deserve attention: Do not wait until an action level is exceeded before acting; any abnormal trend may be an early sign of system deterioration.
  • A new water system should be regarded as "inherently risky": Biofilm may quietly develop even during qualification, so continuous monitoring by an interdepartmental team is needed.

6.0 Raw Materials

Original Text

Raw materials can introduce contaminants to the process stream and final drug products, so ensuring that raw materials are of appropriate quality is crucial to the CCS.

Raw materials are any substances, active or inactive, that are used during the manufacturing process of a drug. Pharmaceutical excipients are the raw materials, aside from active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), that are used to formulate final drug products. Water is considered a significant raw material and excipient (see Section 5.4.1). Consumables and other product-contact materials also require strict control. Primary packaging materials are discussed in Section 10.0.

The contamination risk profile of a raw material is a direct outcome of the origin and subsequent processing of the source material. A quality risk assessment should be performed to identify the risks from each raw material, identify which materials are critical, and establish the control measures needed to support the CCS, such as testing and processing. Development of a strong collaborative relationship with the raw material supplier can ensure transparency, knowledge-sharing, and risk mitigation on an ongoing basis for contamination control (see Section 6.7).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Raw Materials: All substances used in drug manufacturing, including the API (active pharmaceutical ingredient), excipients, water, consumables, and materials that contact the product. Every raw material can be an entry point for contamination.

Contamination Risk Profile: The contamination risk of a raw material depends on two main factors: (1) origin - animal, plant, mineral, or synthetic? (2) subsequent processing - what treatment steps has it undergone? Together these determine the material's inherent bioburden and the likelihood of extraneous contamination.

Analogy

Imagine you are the head chef of a high-end restaurant. Hygiene in your kitchen is not enough; you must also make sure the ingredients you buy are clean to begin with. If the seafood supplier's cold chain is broken, even the best kitchen hygiene cannot save ingredients that have already spoiled. Raw material control in a CCS works like ingredient traceability in a restaurant: every step from the source to the kitchen must be traceable and controlled.

Key Notes

TR90 places particular emphasis on building a "partnership" with suppliers rather than a mere buyer-seller relationship. This reflects the spirit of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022): the contamination control strategy must extend upstream into the supply chain, rather than starting only when the material arrives at the site.

Note the cross-references: water control is in Section 5.4.1, packaging materials in Section 10.0, and supplier partnership in Section 6.7.

  2. If you were the head of quality at a CDMO, how would you perform a risk assessment of a newly introduced raw material? What factors would you consider?
6.1 Specifications

Original Text

Raw material specifications are often developed by the material manufacturer and relate to the amount of control that is understood by the manufacturer, from the material's origin through its processing. The largest end-user of materials is often not the pharmaceutical manufacturer, thus supplier-derived specifications may not be entirely relevant. Pharmaceutical manufacturers should develop relevant specifications that consider the risk profile of the material based on its origin, the material's manufacturing process, and the level of quality needed for the drug manufacturing process. For excipients, it is also critical that the specifications be aligned with the intended final product specifications and dosage use (4-5).

Pharmacopeias are a good source to obtain benchmark starting-specifications because they contain monographs and criteria for many common materials. If no monograph exists for a material, developing a decision tree to determine microbiological criteria could prove useful; Decision Tree #6, in ICH Quality Guideline Q6A: Specifications: Test Procedures and Acceptance Criteria for New Drug Substances and New Drug Products: Chemical Substances, provides a model approach.

Excipients that are used to formulate sterile products should be tested for bioburden and bacterial endotoxin levels. Acceptable bioburden levels and types should be based on their presence in the final product formulation and how the product will be sterilized, for example, terminal sterilization or aseptic filtration. Both bioburden level and identification of spore-producing bacteria are considerations for terminally sterilized products; bioburden levels alone are considered for aseptically filtered products. Minimally, acceptable bacterial endotoxin levels should be based on the USP <85> calculation, which relates to product maximum dose, along with final product formulation and administration considerations (6).

Some raw materials used in low bioburden processes may also warrant microbiological testing based on risks to the process and the material risk profile.

Animal-sourced materials have a higher risk of introducing adventitious agents and should be avoided, unless necessary, for the pharmaceutical manufacturing process. If used, animal-sourced excipients should be certified by the supplier to be free of contaminants relevant to the excipient origin, for example, bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) (7).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Specifications: A raw material specification is a quantitative statement of quality requirements. TR90 stresses a commonly overlooked fact: supplier specifications are usually designed for the supplier's largest customers, and a pharmaceutical manufacturer is often not the biggest buyer. The manufacturer must therefore set tighter or more targeted specifications of its own, based on its process needs.

Pharmacopeia: National pharmacopeias (such as USP, EP, and JP) contain monographs for many commonly used materials and are a good starting point for setting specifications.

ICH Q6A Decision Tree #6: When no pharmacopeial monograph exists, ICH Q6A provides a decision tree approach for deciding whether microbial limits are needed.

Key Notes

Terminal sterilization vs. aseptic filtration - different specification requirements:

  • Terminal Sterilization: Both the bioburden level and the identification of spore-forming organisms must be evaluated (because spores are the most resistant to sterilization).
  • Aseptic Filtration: Only the bioburden level needs to be considered (because filtration removes all microorganisms, but does not remove endotoxin).

Both must meet the bacterial endotoxin limit calculation in USP <85>.

Formula

Endotoxin limit calculation per USP <85>:

Endotoxin Limit = K / M

K = threshold pyrogenic dose
  = 5 EU/kg (intravenous drug products)
M = maximum dose (maximum dose/kg/hour)

Example: an injectable with a maximum dose = 10 mL/kg/hr
    Endotoxin Limit = 5 / 10 = 0.5 EU/mL

Analogy

A raw material specification is like the "incoming inspection standard" for food ingredients. If you run a top-tier sushi restaurant, your freshness standard for tuna will certainly be stricter than that of a conveyor-belt sushi shop. Likewise, a manufacturer of sterile injectables will set stricter microbial specifications for excipients than a manufacturer of oral dosage forms. A supplier's "generic specification" is like the market's basic grading, and for the sushi restaurant it is far from enough.

Animal-Sourced Material Risk

Raw materials of animal origin carry the highest risk of adventitious agents, including:

  • BSE/TSE: Bovine spongiform encephalopathy / transmissible spongiform encephalopathies, caused by prions, which cannot be removed by conventional sterilization methods.
  • Viruses: For example, porcine circovirus once contaminated a rotavirus vaccine.

Principle: avoid wherever possible (should be avoided, unless necessary). If use is unavoidable, the supplier must provide certification that the material is free of contaminants such as BSE.

6.2 Biological Contamination Risk

Original Text

A formal risk assessment is needed to establish the appropriate control measures for raw materials, considering the material properties, origin, supplier history, intended process use, handling, storage, sampling, and testing. The output of this risk assessment is a test plan that establishes the type and frequency of microbiological testing needed to monitor the supplier controls and the possible need for additional treatment and controls by the end user.

The origin of a raw material is the first factor considered to determine the microbiological risk. The origin can predict the scope of possible inherent bioburden and other adventitious agents. Geographical and climate-type information on the origin can indicate a higher risk of inherent bioburden. Biological origin (plant, animal, or mineral-derived) can indicate that an excipient carries bioburden, viruses, or prions as compared to chemical or physical origin (synthetic or semisynthetic processing) (8-9).

Choosing raw materials that are inherently not growth-promoting for microorganisms reduces the contamination risk and should result in less material testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Risk Assessment: The risk assessment here is not an abstract concept; it must have a defined output - a test plan that defines the types and frequency of testing. The assessment must consider eight elements:

  1. Material properties
  2. Origin
  3. Supplier history
  4. Intended process use
  5. Handling
  6. Storage
  7. Sampling
  8. Testing

Analogy

This is like choosing where your ingredients come from: vegetables bought directly from an organic farm and vegetables from a wholesale market of unknown origin carry completely different levels of risk. The first step in assessing raw material risk is also to "look at the pedigree": synthetic materials are usually the cleanest, and animal-derived materials carry the highest risk.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A client asks you to use a plant-derived excipient in its sterile injectable formulation. As the CDMO, you should:

  • Evaluate the excipient's place of origin and climate (for example, Southeast Asia vs. Europe)
  • Confirm whether the supplier has bioburden control measures in place
  • Define the incoming testing frequency and test items based on the risk assessment results
  • Consider whether it can be replaced with an equivalent excipient of synthetic origin to reduce risk

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 suggest that choosing raw materials that "do not promote microbial growth" can reduce testing needs? How does this correspond to QRM principles?
  2. Suppose a raw material has a good supplier history, but its place of origin changes from Europe to India. How should your risk assessment conclusion be adjusted?

Origin determines the risk level:

Geography and climate also matter: raw materials from hot, humid tropical environments generally have a higher inherent bioburden.

6.3 Sources of Extraneous Bioburden

Original Text

Contamination control is most effective when preventive measures are in place; this principle applies across the CCS elements and especially to raw materials. Harvesting, handling, processing, and storage of raw materials can lead to microbiological contamination that becomes part of the inherent bioburden of incoming raw materials.

Therefore, contamination control should begin with the supplier, not waiting for incoming inspection and testing by the pharmaceutical manufacturer. Suppliers can take many steps to reduce the microbiological risk using contamination control principles, for example:

  • Maintaining good housekeeping and using clean equipment to prepare materials
  • Treating materials to reduce bioburden (e.g., heat, radiation), however, such treatment should be demonstrated to not lead to irreversible degradation of the material's quality attributes.
  • Packaging materials in clean, single-use containers with wipeable surfaces
  • When controls are put in place by the supplier, receiving higher quality raw material more consistently is possible prior to use in pharmaceutical products.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Extraneous Bioburden: Foreign microbial contamination introduced during harvesting, handling, processing, and storage of the raw material. Once introduced, it becomes part of the "inherent" bioburden of the incoming material, and it is difficult afterward to tell whether it occurred naturally or was introduced.

Preventive Measures: The core philosophy of TR90 - prevention is better than detection. Rather than sampling and testing heavily at receipt, it is better to have the supplier establish controls at the source.

Analogy

This is like the "farm to table" concept in food safety: if vegetables pick up soil and insect eggs at harvest and get wet in the rain during transport, it is hard to wash them completely clean in the kitchen. The best approach is to maintain hygiene controls at every step of harvesting, packaging, and transport. The same applies to contamination control of pharmaceutical raw materials: "don't wait until incoming inspection to start controlling."

Key Notes

Four measures suppliers can take to reduce microbial risk:

  • Good Housekeeping: Keep equipment and the environment clean.
  • Bioburden Reduction Treatment: For example, heat or irradiation, but it must be shown that the treatment does not cause irreversible degradation of the material's quality attributes.
  • Clean Packaging: Use single-use containers with wipeable surfaces.
  • Continuous Improvement: When suppliers implement controls, the manufacturer can steadily receive higher-quality raw materials.

Special note: when a bioburden reduction treatment (such as irradiation) is applied to a raw material, it must be verified that the treatment does not affect the material's critical quality attributes. This is a commonly overlooked requirement.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 say "contamination control should begin with the supplier"? What does this imply for supplier management practice at a CDMO?
  2. If a supplier applies gamma irradiation to an excipient to reduce bioburden, what must the manufacturer confirm?
6.4 Other Contaminants

Original Text

Material inspections should be performed by the supplier and, upon receipt, by the drug manufacturer to identify such nonmicrobiological contaminants as insects, pest residue, machinery residue, and other foreign particulate contaminants. Inspection of the finished product should also be performed to identify particulate contaminants after the product and packaging components are combined.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Nonmicrobiological Contaminants: Besides microorganisms, raw materials may contain physical contaminants, including:

  • Insects: Insects that may enter during storage.
  • Pest residue: Rodent droppings, insect eggs, and the like.
  • Machinery residue: Metal fragments, lubricating oil, and the like shed from processing equipment.
  • Foreign particulate: Glass, fibers, plastic fragments, and the like.

Analogy

Just as you inspect the appearance of ingredients when they arrive at a restaurant - checking for insects, foreign matter, and damaged packaging - a pharmaceutical manufacturer must also perform a "visual inspection" when receiving raw materials. And this is not only the manufacturer's responsibility: the supplier must also carry out a round of inspection before shipment. Dual checks minimize the risk as far as possible.

Key Notes

Although this section is short, it makes an important point: material inspection is a two-layer defense - inspection by the supplier before shipment plus inspection by the manufacturer at receipt. In addition, the finished product should be inspected again to confirm that combining the product with its packaging has not introduced new particulate contamination.

For a CDMO, the SOP for visual inspection of incoming materials should include clear acceptance/rejection criteria and photographic references.

Practice Questions

6.5 Sampling of Raw Materials and Excipients for Testing

Original Text

Key aspects to consider when sampling raw materials for microbiological testing are technique, location, tools, method, and storage. Microbiological contamination, if present, is commonly found to be heterogeneously distributed in materials as well as in the environment. Considering this, the frequency, quantity, and location to sample should be chosen to obtain the most representative samples.

Sampling from each received container (e.g., drum) in a batch is a good practice to perform representative sampling for use in sterile product manufacture. After supplier testing, any handling and sampling performed by a pharmaceutical manufacturer in their own facility should be controlled to prevent extraneous contamination. Specifically, handling should limit the exposure of raw material and excipients to the same environmental classification to which the product will be exposed during the manufacturing step where that material will be used. For sterile raw materials and excipients, sampling should be closed. When closed sampling is not possible, sampling should be conducted in the same environmental classification as production. Otherwise, the sampled unit should be excluded from production use.

Transfer of packaged materials into a cleanroom area should follow the cleanroom material transfer procedure of shedding any additional wrappings (double-/triple-bags or containers) or performing external surface disinfection as the material container moves from an outer room to an inner room. There should also be an appropriate procedure for disposal of used wrappings and containers for movement out of cleanroom areas to prevent cross-contamination by residual material.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Heterogeneously Distributed: Microbial contamination is usually not evenly distributed in a material. In one drum of raw material, one corner may hold large numbers of microorganisms while the rest has almost none. This means that a sample taken at random does not necessarily represent the whole drum.

Closed Sampling: For sterile raw materials and excipients, sampling must be performed in a closed manner so that the act of sampling does not itself introduce extraneous contamination.

Representative Sampling: Sampling frequency, sample size, and sampling location must all be considered to ensure that the samples truly reflect the quality of the whole batch of material.

Key Notes

Strict requirements for the sampling environment classification:

  • Sterile material sampling → must use closed sampling
  • Closed sampling not possible → must be done in the same environmental grade as production
  • Neither condition can be met → the sampled unit must be excluded from use in production

This is a step-by-step logic: closed > same-grade environment > exclusion from use. There is no fourth option.

Analogy

Imagine opening a drum of powder in a cleanroom to take a sample - it is like opening a pack of gauze in an operating room. If you open the gauze in the corridor, that gauze can no longer be used in surgery. In the same way, if you sample a raw material in an environment that does not meet the required grade, that drum of material should no longer be used to manufacture sterile products.

Cleanroom Transfer

The procedure for transferring materials into the cleanroom includes:

  • Removing the outer packaging layer by layer (double bag, triple bag, or container)
  • Disinfecting the outer surface of the container as it moves from the outer room to the inner room
  • Used packaging and containers leaving the cleanroom also need a corresponding disposal procedure, to prevent residual material from causing cross-contamination

  2. A CDMO's sampling room is only Grade C, but its production area is Grade B/A. In this situation, how should microbial sampling of excipients be handled?
  3. Why do used packaging materials leaving the cleanroom also need a dedicated procedure? What is the risk?
6.6 Importance of Supplier Quality Systems

Original Text

The significance of a supplier's quality system cannot be overstated. Raw materials can be key sources of contamination and are primarily controlled through the supplier's quality system.

Before purchasing raw materials for GMP manufacturing, a quality agreement with the supplier should be in place. For raw materials, an evaluation of the supplier's quality system should be conducted. For critical raw materials, special attention should be paid to the supplier's process flow and controls (e.g., hazard analysis of critical control points (HACCP) approach), representative sampling and appropriate testing (including sample size, location, and frequency), trends of all specification test results, and good packaging and distribution practices.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Agreement: Before purchasing GMP raw materials, a quality agreement must be signed with the supplier. This document defines each party's quality responsibilities and obligations and is the legal basis for supplier management.

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points): A systematic risk management method originating in the food industry, which TR90 recommends for evaluating the process controls of suppliers of critical raw materials. The core of HACCP is to identify the critical steps that could lead to contamination and to set monitoring measures at those steps.

Key Notes

Five key focus areas when evaluating suppliers of critical raw materials:

  1. Process Flow & Controls: The supplier's process flow and control measures (such as HACCP).
  2. Representative Sampling: Representativeness of sampling (sample size, sampling location, frequency).
  3. Appropriate Testing: Appropriate test items and methods.
  4. Specification Test Trends: Trend analysis of all specification test results.
  5. Packaging & Distribution: Good packaging and distribution practices.

Note: TR90 uses the phrase "cannot be overstated" to stress the importance of supplier quality systems. This is unusually strong wording for the report and shows that the regulatory community recognizes raw material suppliers as one of the weakest links in the contamination control chain.

Analogy

A supplier quality system is like the central kitchen management system of a restaurant chain. McDonald's maintains consistent food safety worldwide not because every outlet has a top chef, but because the central kitchen has a rigorous HACCP system. Likewise, a pharmaceutical manufacturer cannot rely on its own incoming inspection alone to assure raw material quality; it must make sure the supplier itself has a sound quality system.

  2. If a supplier's specification test results show a trend gradually approaching the limit (while still within specification), what action should you take?
6.7 Starting Materials Unique to Biopharmaceutical Manufacturing

Original Text

The use and control of biological starting materials (i.e., cell banks or virus banks) are unique to biopharmaceutical and vaccine manufacturing. The contamination of biological starting material can significantly impact the performance of a biopharmaceutical drug substance and thus, negatively influence patient safety and the therapeutic effect.

Biological starting material should be procured from an approved supplier to ensure adequate contamination control. Contamination control for biological starting material is similar to the controls required for sterile product manufacture in terms of the use, cleaning, and monitoring of manufacturing and storage facilities as well as employing adequate, trained personnel and adhering to GMPs.

Master and working banks are sterile or axenic, and they should be manipulated in a dedicated, well-controlled and contained environment, such as a Grade A setting, where aseptic technique is critical. Biological starting material should have adequate source documentation to ensure that animal-derived materials, if used, were controlled for transmissible spongiform encephalopathy (TSE) and BSE. Testing is performed for sterility (or purity for microbial materials) and to ensure that mycoplasma, bacterial endotoxins, and viral contamination are not present.

Biological starting material should always be handled as sterile materials. ICH Quality Guideline Q5A: Quality of Biotechnological Products: Viral Safety Evaluation of Biotechnology Products Derived from Cell Lines of Human or Animal Origin describes some of the expectations related to test requirements of cell banks.

All equipment in the manufacturing area should be adequately cleaned and decontaminated to maintain the state of sterility of the materials and to prevent cross-contamination. This usually means that equipment can be either sterilized or steam-sanitized. All supplies that come in contact with the biological starting material should either be purchased sterile or sterilized prior to use.

Other materials in biopharmaceutical manufacturing, such as buffers and culture media, should be assessed for contamination risk. Evaluation of the supplier's manufacturing process, specifications, and analytical testing trends can provide substantial input for the risk assessment. The potential for extrinsic contamination should be considered during the contamination risk assessment along with the impurity removal capability of the process. The potential risk for microbiological growth and development of bacterial endotoxins in media or buffers that are held for extended periods of time can be high. Assessment of these materials should be included in the risk assessment of the bulk drug substance. Section 9.0 provides further detail on process controls for drug substances and drug product.

Appropriate, integrity-assured (by testing) packaging for these types of biopharmaceutical starting materials is important in preventing contamination. If sterilization is possible prior to the packaging of the starting materials before being stored or transported, it should be considered. If single-use packaging is used, additional protective-barrier packaging should be considered for transport. PDA Technical Report No. 66: Application of Single-Use Systems in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing can provide more information.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Biological Starting Materials: Starting materials unique to biopharmaceutical manufacturing, mainly Cell Banks and Virus Banks. What makes these materials special is that they are living organisms themselves; once contaminated, the whole process may fail or product quality may be seriously compromised.

Master and Working Banks: The cell bank system uses a two-tier structure: the Master Cell Bank (MCB) is the source, and the Working Cell Bank (WCB) is expanded from the MCB for routine production. Both must be sterile or axenic.

Axenic: The material contains no foreign microorganisms, although it may itself be a microorganism (such as the working strain used in some microbial fermentation processes).

Key Notes

Testing requirements for biological starting materials:

  • Sterility: Or purity - used for microbial materials.
  • Mycoplasma: One of the most common latent contaminants in cell culture; it does not change the appearance of the culture but does affect cell behavior.
  • Bacterial Endotoxins: A component of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria.
  • Viral Contamination: Assessed per ICH Q5A.
  • TSE/BSE: If animal-derived materials are used, traceability documentation must be available.

Operating environment requirement: Cell bank operations must be performed in a Grade A environment using aseptic technique. This is equivalent to the environmental requirements for aseptic filling.

Buffer & Media Hold Risk

TR90 specifically warns that media or buffers held for long periods carry a high risk of microbial growth and endotoxin generation. This is because:

  • Culture media are nutrient-rich environments that readily support microbial growth
  • Buffers are lower in nutrients, but still carry risk when stored at room temperature for long periods
  • Once microorganisms grow and lyse, the endotoxin released cannot be completely removed even if the microorganisms are subsequently filtered out

The hold time of these materials must therefore be validated.

Analogy

A cell bank is like a seed bank: the Master Cell Bank is the original seed kept in the vault, and the Working Cell Bank is seed grown from the original for each season's planting. If the seeds are contaminated by disease or pests, the whole crop will be affected. So the seeds must be kept in the strictest environment (Grade A = vault level), and every tool that touches them must be sterile.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A CDMO manufactures monoclonal antibody products for several clients at the same time. In cell bank management, special attention is needed on the following:

  • Cross-contamination control: Cell banks of different clients must have dedicated storage areas and dedicated handling times.
  • Supply Chain Integrity: Temperature monitoring and packaging integrity during transport of client-supplied cell banks.
  • Media management: Hold time validation for shared media, ensuring they remain within the validated period under the scheduling of different product processes.
  • Equipment cleaning validation: Equipment cleaning between different products must be validated to prevent cross-contamination from residues.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is mycoplasma specifically listed as a test item? What is special about the harm caused by mycoplasma contamination?
  2. TR90 recommends that biological starting materials "should always be handled as sterile materials". What specific impact does this have on a CDMO's material management SOPs?
  3. How does the risk differ between media stored for 48 hours and for 7 days after preparation? How would you set the validation strategy for hold time?
6.8 Starting Materials for Advanced Therapy Medicinal Product Manufacturing

Original Text

Strong contamination control, based on quality risk assessment, is needed for the biological starting materials used in advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMP), also known as cell and gene therapy products (CGTP), to prevent adventitious agents from being introduced from the biologic origin and to prevent cross-contamination. Contamination of these materials can significantly influence product safety and the therapeutic effect, and thus affect the patient's health. ATMP starting materials can include human donor cells or tissues, banked human cell lines, viral banks, viral vectors and their supporting cell lines, and naked nucleic acids. The level of contamination control needed for ATMP starting materials should be based on the level of risk to the finished drug product.

Human donor cells and tissues are collected and initially tested according to good clinical practices and good clinical laboratory practices, rather than GMPs. Traceability of the human donor cells and the health assessment of the donor are the keys to contamination control at the point of collection. When donor material undergoes manipulation or selection for the ATMP, it becomes starting material for manufacture and requires GMP controls as appropriate by phase.

Some of the starting materials may not be GMP or compendial-grade, so the risk assessment should consider the hazards and controls related to material origin, supply chain, manufacturing controls, and analytical testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

ATMP / CGTP (Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products / Cell and Gene Therapy Products): These are the most cutting-edge type of pharmaceutical product, including CAR-T cell therapies and gene therapy vectors. What is unique about their starting materials:

  • Human Donor Cells/Tissues: For example, the patient's white blood cells in CAR-T therapy.
  • Banked Human Cell Lines: For example, HEK293, used for viral vector production.
  • Viral Banks: Viral seed banks used for gene therapy.
  • Viral Vectors: For example, AAV and lentivirus.
  • Naked Nucleic Acids: For example, mRNA and plasmid DNA.

Key Notes

Special challenges of ATMP starting materials:

  • Non-GMP sources: Human donor material is first collected in a clinical setting, following GCP (Good Clinical Practice) and GCLP (Good Clinical Laboratory Practice) rather than GMP. This means the manufacturer cannot fully control quality at the most upstream stage.
  • The transition point from clinical to GMP: When donor material begins to be manipulated or screened for use in ATMP manufacture, it "becomes" a starting material, and GMP controls must begin to apply at that point.
  • Phase-Appropriate GMP: The stringency of GMP controls should match the stage of product development (for example, clinical Phase I vs. commercial stage).
  • Non-pharmacopeial-grade materials: Some starting materials may not be available in GMP or pharmacopeial grade, so the risk assessment must be especially thorough.

Analogy

Managing ATMP starting materials is like managing donors in organ transplantation: you cannot "manufacture" a perfect donor; you can only screen and evaluate as well as possible. The patient's blood is drawn, sent to a CDMO for genetic modification, and then infused back into the patient. The most critical first step in the whole process is to confirm the health status and infection risk of the donor (the patient). This cannot be controlled inside a GMP facility; it happens at the collection stage in the hospital. Traceability and donor health assessment therefore become the first line of defense in contamination control.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: An emerging CDMO is building CAR-T cell therapy manufacturing capability. In starting material control, it needs to consider:

  • Supply chain design: Establish standardized collection and transport procedures with hospitals (including temperature control and transport time limits).
  • Risk assessment framework: Establish additional risk assessment procedures for materials from non-GMP sources.
  • Traceability system: A complete chain of identity and chain of custody from the patient's blood draw to release of the finished product.
  • Viral vector management: If several viral vectors are produced in the same facility, strict cross-contamination control measures are required.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is managing ATMP starting materials more complex than for conventional biologics? Analyze from the perspective of "controllability of the source".
  2. Where is the transition point at which human donor cells change from a "clinical sample" to a "GMP starting material"? Why is this transition point important?
  3. If a reagent used in ATMP manufacture can only be obtained as "research grade", how should you carry out risk assessment and quality control?

Section Summary

  • Raw materials are an entry point for contamination: All raw materials (API, excipients, water, consumables) can introduce contaminants into the process and must be risk-assessed according to their origin and processing.
  • Specifications must be tailored: A supplier's generic specification is not necessarily suitable for pharmaceutical use. The manufacturer should set tighter specifications of its own based on process needs, pharmacopeial standards, and ICH guidelines.
  • Origin determines the risk level: Animal origin > plant origin > mineral origin > synthetic origin. Geographic and climate factors also affect risk.
  • Prevention is better than detection: Contamination control should start at the supplier, not at incoming inspection. Building a partnership with suppliers is key.
  • Sampling must be representative: Microbial contamination is heterogeneously distributed, so the sampling strategy must consider frequency, sample size, and location. Sterile materials must be sampled in a closed manner.
  • Supplier quality systems are critical: Sign a quality agreement before purchasing, evaluate suppliers of critical raw materials in a HACCP-style manner, and monitor specification trends.
  • Biological starting materials need special controls: Cell banks and virus banks must be handled in a Grade A environment and tested for sterility, mycoplasma, endotoxin, and viral contamination.
  • ATMPs face unique challenges: Starting materials may come from non-GMP settings (such as patient cells collected in a hospital), and traceability and donor health assessment are the first line of defense.

7.0 Environmental Control, Validation, and Monitoring

Original Text

Environmental control is a fundamental part of the CCS because contaminants from the environment can enter the utilities, raw materials, equipment, and the process stream from the air and through surface transfer. A spike in environmental contaminants (e.g., seasonal variations) can challenge the other elements of the CCS. For instance, molds and spore-forming bacteria are in high concentrations outdoors, and a spike in these organisms in the cleanroom can challenge the facility cleaning/disinfection and equipment sanitization/sterilization programs (spore-forming bacteria are still the most common bioreactor contaminants). Increased skin bacteria from the operators in the environment can challenge the aseptic processing controls; skin bacteria are still the most common contaminants found in products intended to be sterile.

Environmental monitoring (EM) programs do not prevent or control contamination, but they provide an indicator of environmental quality through multiple discrete snapshots in time. The main purpose of the EM program is to measure the performance of the contamination controls related to the environment and to highlight the potential loss of control due to a variety of causes, such as personnel behavior, disinfection practices, HVAC equipment malfunction, and facility age. An ongoing generation of EM trends can be used for remediation to ensure that the facility continues to operate in a state of control and to prevent extrinsic contamination of the product. The effectiveness of an EM program is dependent on the proper identification of critical control points (CCPs) as part of a risk assessment (e.g., HACCP).

"An EM program with acceptable trends should not be used to support poor aseptic or disinfection practices, for example, to defend a decrease in disinfection frequencies or the elimination of sporicidal agents."

Section 15.0, Table 9 and Table 10 provide additional points to consider regarding EM and testing controls. PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2022): Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program also provides extensive guidance on establishing an effective EM program for facilities, critical utilities, and personnel.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Environmental Control: One of the foundations of the CCS. Contaminants in the environment can enter utilities, raw materials, equipment, and processes through airborne spread or surface transfer. Seasonal changes (such as the spring and summer mold peaks) directly challenge the other CCS elements.

Environmental Monitoring (EM): EM itself does not prevent or control contamination; it only provides discrete snapshots of environmental quality. Its core value lies in measuring the performance of contamination control measures and giving early warning before control is lost.

Critical Control Points (CCPs): The effectiveness of an EM program depends on correctly identifying CCPs. CCPs are usually determined through a HACCP risk assessment.

Analogy

EM is like an infection control round in a hospital. The round itself does not kill any germs, but it tells you where disinfection was not thorough enough and which ward's air cleanliness has declined. Consistently passing results do not mean you can clean less often or stop using bleach. In the same way, a good EM trend must never be used as an excuse to reduce disinfection frequency or drop the sporicidal agent.

Key Notes

Two common sources of contamination:

  • Spore-forming bacteria: Remain the most common contaminants in bioreactors. High outdoor concentrations of mold and spore-formers during seasonal peaks can penetrate the cleanroom and challenge the cleaning/disinfection and equipment sterilization programs.
  • Skin bacteria: Remain the most common contaminants in sterile products. Operators are the largest active source of contamination in a cleanroom.

These two facts underline that the CCS must address both the environment (air, surfaces) and people (gowning, behavior).

Practical Application

In CDMO practice, client auditors often review EM trend data and ask: "After several consecutive months of passing EM results, have you reduced your disinfection frequency?" The correct answer is that a good EM trend is evidence that controls are effective and should not be used to justify lowering control standards. Instead, use the trend data to keep refining the sampling strategy and to identify new risk areas.

  1. Why is the EM program defined as neither preventing nor controlling contamination? What is its real role in the CCS?
  2. How do seasonal changes affect the CCS of a cleanroom? Give at least two specific risk scenarios.
  3. If a cleanroom's EM data show zero counts for 12 consecutive months, what does this mean? Can the monitoring frequency be reduced?

7.1 Environmental Performance Qualification

Original Text

Environmental monitoring performance qualification (EMPQ) is performed to provide documented evidence that the facility design and environmental controls can act together to consistently achieve the desired level of contamination control for the intended manufacturing use. The EMPQ evaluates the CCS environmental control elements including facility and HVAC design, access and flow, cleaning/disinfection, gowning, material transfer, and pest control.

EMPQ is conducted in newly constructed controlled classified areas or after significant changes in existing facilities, that is, changes that could affect the contamination control performance of the environment. The EMPQ sample locations (including CCPs) should be selected based on a risk assessment using guidance from ISO 14644-1:2015 Cleanrooms and Associated Controlled Environments – Part 1: Classification of Air Cleanliness by Particle Concentration. Prior to the EMPQ, the new area should be fully functional with equipment installed and all relevant CCS programs established and underway.

The cleanroom installation and operation qualification are outlined in ISO 14644 and include airflow visualization (smoke) studies, which should be performed under static and dynamic (worst-case or process-relevant) conditions. The data generated from the execution of the EMPQ supports the establishment of the ongoing EM program, which includes the selection of routine sampling locations. For additional information on EMPQ design and site selection, refer to PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2022): Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

EMPQ (Environmental Performance Qualification): Documented evidence that the facility design and environmental controls together, and consistently, achieve the intended level of contamination control. It is not a single test but a comprehensive evaluation of the environmental control elements of the entire CCS, covering:

  • Facility and HVAC design
  • Personnel and material access and flow
  • Cleaning/disinfection procedures
  • Gowning procedures
  • Material transfer
  • Pest control

ISO 14644-1:2015: The international standard for cleanroom classification, used to guide the selection of EMPQ sampling locations.

Analogy

EMPQ is like the hygiene inspection a new restaurant must pass before opening. You cannot wait until customers are already eating to verify that the kitchen is clean. Before opening (that is, before GMP production starts), you must confirm that all equipment is in place, the ventilation system works properly, cleaning procedures have been carried out and are effective, and staff have been trained in hygiene. Only after passing this pre-opening inspection do you get the license to operate (enter routine production).

Key Notes

Key points on timing:

  • New areas: EMPQ must be performed only after all equipment is installed and all CCS procedures are established and in operation
  • Changes to existing facilities: Required only after significant changes that could affect environmental contamination control performance
  • Airflow visualization (smoke study): Must be performed under both static and dynamic (worst-case) conditions

The data generated by EMPQ directly support the selection of sampling locations for the routine EM program, so the quality of the EMPQ determines the effectiveness of daily monitoring.

Practice Questions

  1. What is the relationship between EMPQ and the routine EM program? Why does the quality of the EMPQ determine the effectiveness of EM?
  2. Why must airflow visualization studies be performed under both static and dynamic conditions? What is the risk of performing them only under static conditions?

7.1.1 Environmental Requalification

Original Text

Requalification is performed after controlled classified areas have been modified in ways that involve changes to structure, process flow, or materials of construction. In addition, the area may be requalified after a significant change has occurred, such as a change in the operational use of the area or other contamination control programs (e.g., EM program, sanitization program, gowning). Periodic requalification studies may be performed based on risk assessments, site validation procedures, or changes in the ongoing trend data or as required by local regulations (e.g., current version of Annex 1 recommends requalification of Grade A & B every 6 months and Grade C & D every 12 months.)

The change control for the affected rooms should assess cumulative changes in the rooms that may warrant requalification. An EMPQ study protocol should include the scope and requirements of the requalification, sampling plan, and acceptance criteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Requalification: This is not "repeating the EMPQ" but a confirmatory study targeted at a specific change. Triggers include:

  • Structural changes (replacement of walls, ceilings, floors)
  • Changes to the process flow
  • Changes to materials of construction
  • Changes in area use (for example, from a grade C packaging area to a grade B compounding area)
  • Changes to CCS procedures (disinfection program, gowning procedures, etc.)
  • Changes in trend data

Analogy

Requalification is like the periodic servicing of a car. Even if your car runs fine, you still take it in every six months or 10,000 km. If you replace engine parts (a major change), you need to take it in for inspection immediately; if you only replace the wipers (a minor change), you may not need to. But if you replace the wipers and the tire pressure sensor and also adjust the steering angle, those changes add up and the car should be checked as a whole.

Practice Questions

  1. If a grade B area has undergone three minor repairs in the past 6 months (replacing a ceiling panel, repositioning a return air grille, replacing a UV lamp), is requalification needed? How would you assess it?
  2. Why does Annex 1 call for more frequent requalification for grade A/B (6 months) than for grade C/D (12 months)? Where does the difference in risk lie?

Key Notes

Periodic requalification requirements in EU GMP Annex 1:

This is an explicit recommendation in Annex 1. Note that the change control procedure must evaluate cumulative changes. Each minor change on its own may not require requalification, but the combined effect of several minor changes may.

7.1.2 Environmental Monitoring Critical Parameters

Original Text

The HVAC system and associated air handling units control the cleanliness of the air and provide an environment that is suitable for operators to work safely and comfortably. Procedures should be in place to monitor and trend critical parameters to assess whether the cleanroom is performing as intended. The procedures should also include instructions on how to handle excursions and alarms. Critical parameters are outlined in Sections 7.1.2.1–7.1.2.3.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

HVAC System: The "life support system" of the cleanroom. It controls not only air cleanliness but also provides a safe and comfortable working environment for operators. The three critical parameters (air patterns, differential pressure, and temperature/humidity, described in the following subsections) are all controlled by the HVAC system.

Excursions: Events in which a critical parameter goes outside its set limits. Procedures must include clear guidance on handling excursions and responding to alarms.

7.1.2.1 Air Patterns, Air Movement, and Air Changes

Original Text

Airflow patterns in critical zones and the background environment need to be studied during qualification to show that air turbulence does not interfere with aseptic processes during static or dynamic conditions. There are two types of air flow patterns: turbulent flow and unidirectional flow.

Turbulent airflow is used in most classified areas other than Grade A environments; it is designed to dilute particles and microorganisms in cleanrooms and keep them suspended in the air until they are removed with new "clean" air. The number of air changes per hour of a cleanroom is related to the extent to which potential contaminants can be removed from the room. Each grade of cleanroom should meet a preset number of air changes per hour based on a risk assessment performed as part of the EMPQ. Airflow visualization studies may be conducted in areas of turbulent airflow such as cleanrooms to optimize the layout, use and monitoring locations.

Unidirectional airflow is applied in higher classified areas (i.e., Grade A) and is designed to sweep particles and microorganisms away from critical operations, ensuring that the "first air" is never contaminated before it comes in contact with the product (the critical zone). Airflow visualization studies are designed to demonstrate that unidirectional airflow is delivered to the working position by the BSC or barrier system, and that the product is only exposed to "first air" during operations. Airflow visualization studies are also performed in areas that adjacent to the critical zone to ensure air is not moving into the aseptic area, e.g., air entering through the mouseholes or depyrogenation tunnel into a barrier system, or air passing through the air curtain of a BSC. PDA Technical Report No. 62: Recommended Practices for Manual Aseptic Processes and EU GMP Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products also discuss airflow in aseptic manufacturing areas.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Turbulent Airflow: Used in most cleanroom areas such as grades B/C/D. The principle is to "dilute" airborne particles and microorganisms with a large volume of air changes, so that they are suspended and carried out with fresh air. The higher the air changes per hour (ACH), the greater the dilution capacity.

Unidirectional Airflow: Used in grade A critical zones. The principle is entirely different: not dilution but "sweeping." Air flows in a uniform, parallel manner and "blows away" particles and microorganisms from the critical operating area, ensuring that the product is exposed only to first air.

First Air: Clean air that has left the HEPA filter and has not yet contacted any object or person. During critical operations, the product may be exposed only to first air, which is one of the core principles of sterility assurance.

Analogy

Imagine two ways of cleaning a floor:

  • Turbulent flow = rinsing a bathroom with water: A large volume of water is flushed in, the dirt is carried along and diluted by the flow, and finally washed out through the drain. The more water, the cleaner the rinse.
  • Unidirectional flow = sweeping with a broom from one end to the other: All the dust is pushed in one direction and is not stirred back and forth. You stand in front of the broom (the position of first air) and never touch the dust.

Mouseholes (transfer openings) are like gaps under a door: without the correct airflow direction, dust from the corridor outside will get into the operating room through the gap.

Key Notes

Key checkpoints in airflow visualization (smoke study):

  • Grade A area: Confirm that unidirectional airflow actually reaches the working position and that the product is exposed only to first air
  • Areas adjacent to grade A: Confirm there is no ingress of outside air (for example, through mouseholes, depyrogenation tunnels, or the air curtain of a BSC)
  • Turbulent flow areas: Optimize the layout and the placement of monitoring points
  • Must be performed under both static and dynamic conditions

Practice Questions

  1. Why does grade A use unidirectional flow rather than greatly increasing the air changes of turbulent flow? What is the fundamental difference in how the two protect the product?
  2. If a smoke study shows a small area of vortex in the filling zone, what risk does this represent? How should it be handled?

7.1.2.2 Differential Air Pressure

Original Text

Air pressurization will ensure airflow from a higher to a lower cleanliness level by maintaining positive pressure in the areas of higher classification. Air visualization performed in both static and dynamic conditions will demonstrate appropriate pressure differential. In viral-vector manufacturing, guidance on differential pressure between rooms of different classification can be found in U.S. FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing — Current Good Manufacturing Practice, EU GMP Annex 1 Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products, and PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2021): Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program.

In the case of high-potency products, both active egress from the area and contamination of the product should be prevented. This can be achieved by applying air locks with different pressures between areas of different classification. Pressure differentials should be monitored with an alarm mechanism for minimum and maximum pressure limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Pressure Differential: By maintaining positive pressure in the higher-grade area, air always flows from the cleaner area to the less clean area. This is the physical barrier that keeps external contaminants out.

High-Potency Products: Face a dual challenge: external contamination must be kept out of the product (positive pressure protection), and the high-potency material must be kept from spreading to the outside environment (negative pressure containment). The solution is to use air locks with different pressures to build a pressure gradient between areas of different classification.

Analogy

Differential pressure is a man-made control of "wind direction." Imagine a building where every floor has a different air pressure: the top floor has the highest pressure and each floor down is slightly lower. Air then always flows from top to bottom, and dust cannot flow back upward. The pressure differential principle in a cleanroom is exactly the same: the cleanest room has the highest pressure, and the "air door" always pushes outward.

High-potency products are more like a biosafety laboratory (BSL): you do not want outside bacteria to get in and contaminate your experiment, and you do not want the dangerous pathogens inside to get out. This calls for a "sandwich" air lock, with a buffer zone in the middle and different pressures controlling the airflow in each direction.

Key Notes

Differential pressure monitoring must be equipped with an alarm system: Minimum and maximum pressure limits must be set, and an alarm must trigger immediately when the differential pressure deviates. This is not a suggestion but a basic requirement. Loss of differential pressure means the contamination barrier has collapsed and can result in the loss of an entire batch.

Practice Questions

  1. If you were designing a sterile filling area that also produces high-potency anticancer drugs, how would you plan the pressure strategy? Draw the pressure gradient for each area.
  2. Why must differential pressure monitoring set both a minimum and a maximum limit? What problems can excessively high differential pressure cause?

7.1.2.3 Temperature and Relative Humidity

Original Text

Products may be affected by temperature and humidity, and these parameters are important for operator comfort as well. Shivering or excess perspiration on the part of an operator may result in the release of a higher number of particles into the cleanroom than an operator's garments can retain, which may contaminate the environment. Temperature and humidity levels also have an impact on microbial proliferation. Both parameters should be monitored against their set limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The dual role of temperature and humidity: Temperature and relative humidity affect several aspects:

  • Product: Some products (such as biologics and protein drugs) are sensitive to temperature and humidity
  • Personnel: Operators who are too cold will shiver and those who are too hot will sweat; both release more particles into the cleanroom, beyond what the cleanroom garment can retain
  • Microbiological: High temperature and high humidity favor microbial proliferation

Analogy

Temperature and humidity control is like setting the temperature in an operating room. Surgeons wear heavy gowns and gloves; if the room is too warm they sweat heavily and their hands may slip, and if it is too cold their fingers stiffen and fine manipulation suffers. Aseptic operations work the same way: an operator's comfort directly affects their risk level as a source of contamination. A sweating operator can generate several times the normal number of particles.

Key Notes

Temperature and humidity are often treated as "secondary parameters" and overlooked, but this section states explicitly that they directly affect contamination control. Note in particular that once operators are gowned (cleanroom garment, gloves, goggles), their perceived temperature is much higher than the ambient temperature. The cleanroom temperature setpoint therefore cannot consider product needs alone; it must also consider the comfort range of a fully gowned person.

Practice Questions

  1. If the relative humidity in a grade B area rises from 45% to 70%, what contamination control risks might result?
  2. When writing the CCS document, under which control element should temperature and humidity monitoring be classified? How should the action limits be set?

7.2 Environment and Utility Disruption and Recovery Plan

Original Text

When a disruption occurs in a controlled, classified environment or to a critical utility, the risk of contamination is inherently higher than during daily operations. For this reason, a containment strategy for disruptions and a recovery plan should be established with the aim of reducing the contamination risk.

A disruption to the controlled, classified environment or critical utility may be planned or unplanned:

  • Planned disruptions are deliberate, scheduled events, for example, construction projects, annual shutdown, preventive maintenance, equipment installation
  • Unplanned disruptions are unexpected events, such as leaks in critical utilities, loss of power, or equipment repair, and should be addressed immediately

The level of containment and recovery should be commensurate with the risk. It is useful to define tiers of risk and outline the appropriate containment strategy and response plan for each tier. An example is presented in Section 17.0–Appendix III.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Disruption: Any interference with the clean environment or critical utilities (such as the water for injection (WFI) system, clean steam, compressed air, and HVAC). Disruptions can be classified as:

  • Planned: Scheduled events such as construction work, annual shutdown maintenance, preventive maintenance, and equipment installation
  • Unplanned: Sudden events such as utility leaks, power outages, and emergency equipment repairs, which must be addressed immediately

Recovery Plan: Defines the steps needed to return from a disruption to a state of control. The level of containment and recovery should be commensurate with risk.

Tiers of Risk: Disruption events of different types and severity are graded into tiers, with each tier corresponding to a different containment strategy and response plan. See the examples in Section 17.0 Appendix III.

Analogy

Disruption and recovery plans are like a hospital's emergency response tiering system:

  • Planned disruption = scheduled surgery: All resources are prepared in advance, the schedule is set, all relevant personnel are notified, and cleaning and recovery are carried out afterward according to plan.
  • Unplanned disruption = emergency admission: There is no warning; emergency procedures must be started immediately, with risk assessed as you respond.

Risk tiering is like hospital triage: not every disruption requires full requalification. Minor ones may need only enhanced monitoring, while serious ones may require stopping the line, cleaning, and requalification before production can resume.

Key Notes

"Commensurate with Risk" is the key principle:

  • Overreaction (a full line stop and requalification for every minor disruption) = wasted resources and reduced capacity
  • Underreaction (only minimal handling of a serious disruption) = contamination risk, possibly leading to a product recall
  • The correct approach is to establish a risk tier matrix in advance so that all personnel can make quick, consistent judgments when an event occurs

Annex 1 specifically emphasizes that planned disruptions must also have formal control procedures; controls cannot be relaxed just because an event was scheduled.

Practical Application

As a CDMO, your facility may serve multiple clients at the same time. When an HVAC abnormality occurs (for example, an AHU failure causes a loss of differential pressure in a grade B area for 30 minutes), you need to:

  1. Use the predefined risk tier matrix to determine which tier the event belongs to
  2. Activate the containment strategy for that tier (for example, isolate the affected area, suspend operations)
  3. Carry out recovery procedures (for example, additional cleaning and disinfection, enhanced EM sampling)
  4. Assess the impact on all batches in process and notify each client
  5. Complete the deviation investigation, document the root cause, and update the CAPA

A plant without a pre-established risk tier matrix falls into the chaos of ad hoc judgment every time, and different shifts may handle the same event in completely different ways.

Practice Questions

  1. Design a three-tier (low/medium/high) risk tier matrix for a sterile filling plant, listing the typical events, containment strategy, and recovery requirements for each tier.
  2. How should planned disruptions (such as annual shutdown maintenance) and unplanned disruptions (such as a sudden power outage) each be documented in the CCS document? How do the recovery requirements differ?
  3. If the WFI system has a temperature excursion during filling (dropping below 70°C), what tier of disruption is this? What immediate actions should be taken?

Section Summary

  • Environmental control is the foundation of the CCS: Environmental contaminants (seasonal mold, spore-formers, operators' skin bacteria) can spread through air and surfaces and directly challenge facility cleaning, equipment sterilization, and aseptic operation controls
  • EM is "measurement," not "control": The EM program provides discrete snapshots of environmental quality to measure the performance of controls and warn of loss of control, but a good EM trend must never be used to justify lowering control standards
  • EMPQ is the cornerstone of EM: EMPQ is performed in cleanroom areas after new construction or significant change, and its results directly determine the sampling strategy and monitoring locations of the routine EM program
  • Requalification has clear triggers: Changes to structure, process, or materials of construction; changes in area use; changes to CCS procedures; changes in trends; and the periodic requalification required by Annex 1 (every 6 months for grade A/B, every 12 months for grade C/D)
  • Three critical HVAC parameters: Air patterns (turbulent dilution vs. unidirectional sweeping), differential pressure (positive pressure protection plus two-way control for high-potency products), and temperature/humidity (product stability, operator comfort, and microbial proliferation)
  • Disruption and recovery must be planned in advance: Distinguish planned from unplanned disruptions, establish a risk tier matrix, and make the level of containment and recovery commensurate with risk, avoiding both overreaction and underreaction

8.0 Personnel Training and Qualification

Original Text

Before individuals can enter manufacturing areas, they should have received appropriate training in a standard curriculum for manufacturing personnel. The curriculum should be designed to the role the trainee will be expected to perform. Personnel training and qualification programs should include at a minimum an explanation of the underlying principles and rationale for the controls regarding:

  • Basic Microbiology (see Section 8.1)
  • Personnel, personnel flow, and associated requirements (see Section 5.2; Section 15.0, Table 6)
  • Material and waste flow (see Section 5.3; Section 15.0, Table 4)
  • Environmental controls (see Section 7.0)
  • Cleaning and Disinfection (see Section 5.1.4)
  • Process design (see Section 4.0)

A key element of a microbial contamination control strategy is a routinely scheduled, validated cleaning and disinfection process. The microbiology training program should encompass a clear explanation of the difference between cleaning and disinfection in that the former is purposed to remove physical and chemical residues and the latter is purposed to kill microorganisms. The training program should include an overview of the different cleaning and disinfectant agents and why each are used.

Basic microbiology, personal hygiene, gowning, and cleanroom behavior are core topics within a robust contamination control training program. Operators performing activities in the aseptic processing areas should have additional aseptic operator training and meet qualification requirements, including reviewing the airflow visualization studies. Table 8.0-1 highlights the mindset shift toward holistic CCS relating to personnel.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Personnel Training and Qualification: This is the systematic control mechanism in the CCS for people, the largest source of contamination. TR90 requires that, before personnel enter manufacturing areas, they complete a standard curriculum covering core topics such as microbiology, personal hygiene, gowning, and cleanroom behavior.

Cleaning vs. Disinfection: The purpose of cleaning is to remove physical and chemical residues; the purpose of disinfection is to kill microorganisms. The training program must clearly distinguish between these two concepts, because many operators confuse them.

Analogy

Think of a hospital's infection control program. Before medical staff enter the operating room, they need to know not only "how" to do things (the SOP) but also "why" they do them. A nurse who follows steps mechanically may not know how to respond when something unexpected happens, whereas a nurse who truly understands how microorganisms spread can make the right judgment even in non-standard situations. This is the core difference between the "non-holistic" and "holistic" approaches in Table 8.0-1.

Key Notes

The key mindset shift: The traditional view holds that "as long as the SOPs and batch records are followed precisely, contamination will not occur." The holistic approach holds that following procedures alone is not enough: personnel must understand the specific hazards in the whole process and combine this with CCS practices, quality culture, and continual improvement to prevent contamination.

Training courses should be "designed to the role," which means that personnel in different positions receive training of different depth rather than one-size-fits-all training.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, personnel training is even more challenging because of the variety of client products and frequent process changeovers. An effective approach is to build a "modular training matrix": the basic modules (microbiology, hygiene, gowning) are mandatory for all personnel, while the advanced modules (aseptic technique, specific processes) are assigned according to role. This ensures comprehensive coverage while remaining flexible enough for different product lines.

  2. In your facility, is the training on cleaning and disinfection clearly differentiated? Can operators correctly explain the difference between the two?

8.1 Basic Microbiology

Original Text

Microbiology is the science and study of microorganisms. Microorganisms can potentially impact pharmaceutical facilities, manufacturing processes, and finished drug products. An education program of basic microbiology should be integrated into the employee training that explains the various types of microorganisms (minimally, bacteria, yeasts, and molds). This would describe how microorganisms may cause an impact through contamination, including proliferation, and their potential to release toxins (including endotoxins), and why this can impact patient safety based on the types of finished drug products, patient population and the route of drug administration.

The program should explain the main sources of microorganisms, including people, utilities (e.g., water, gases), the environment, and raw materials. A comprehensive microbiology training program would also include a description of viruses and prions, their origins, their sources, and how and where they might impact the manufacturing process and finished drug product quality. This should also include any requirements for TSE and BSE statements for all materials that pose a potential risk of contamination from prions.

When employees understand the sources and potential consequences of contamination from microorganisms, they are more capable to prevent contaminations and build practices that support a proactive quality culture related to contamination control.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Basic Microbiology: The minimum requirements for microbiology training cover three major groups of microorganisms:

  • Bacteria: The most common source of contamination; they can multiply rapidly and release endotoxins
  • Yeasts: A type of fungus, commonly found in the environment and on the human body
  • Molds: Another type of fungus, which can spread through the air as spores

Advanced training should also cover viruses and prions, the latter being associated with TSE (transmissible spongiform encephalopathy) and BSE (bovine spongiform encephalopathy).

Analogy

Microbiology training is like teaching new drivers to recognize road hazards. You cannot just tell them "stop at red, go at green"; you also need them to understand why running a red light is dangerous (the consequences of an accident), what potential dangers are on the road (pedestrians, cyclists, construction zones), and how to judge correctly in different situations. When operators truly understand the sources and hazards of microorganisms, they shift from "passively following rules" to "actively preventing contamination."

Key Notes

The four major sources of microorganisms: People, Utilities (such as water and gases), Environment, and Raw Materials. Remembering these four sources is the foundation for understanding the entire CCS.

Proactive Quality Culture: TR90 states explicitly that when employees understand the sources and consequences of contamination, they are better able to prevent it proactively. This is not just knowledge transfer but culture building.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does microbiology training need to cover the concepts of "route of drug administration" and "patient population"?
  2. In your CDMO facility, is there a material management procedure addressing TSE/BSE risk? How is it integrated into the training program?

8.2 Personal Hygiene

Original Text

The training program should explain how people and their actions are recognized as one of the greatest sources of microbiological contamination within the pharmaceutical industry. Therefore, the program should stress the requirements for maintaining a high standard of personal hygiene and how to effectively perform handwashing. The topic of hygiene should list health conditions that would restrict personnel from operating in a cleanroom.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Personal Hygiene: TR90 states explicitly that "people and their behavior" are among the largest sources of microbial contamination in the pharmaceutical industry. The training program must cover:

  • The requirement to maintain a high standard of personal hygiene
  • Effective handwashing techniques
  • The list of health conditions that restrict personnel from entering the cleanroom

Analogy

It is like a restaurant chef who must follow strict hygiene standards: you would not want a chef with a cold preparing your sushi in an open kitchen. Likewise, an operator with an infectious illness or a skin wound becomes an active source of microbial contamination in the cleanroom. Health condition restrictions are not discrimination; they are a necessary measure to protect patient safety.

Key Notes

People are the largest source of contamination: This is one of the most important points of awareness in the entire CCS. A normal person can shed thousands to millions of particles per minute, and poor hygiene habits greatly increase that number. Personal hygiene training is therefore not a formality but the first line of defense in contamination control.

Practice Questions

  1. Which health conditions restrict personnel from entering the cleanroom in your facility? Does the list cover skin diseases, respiratory infections, gastrointestinal illnesses, and so on?
  2. Has the handwashing procedure been validated? Are operators assessed regularly on their handwashing technique?

8.3 Gowning

Original Text

Appropriate garments and the process of gowning provides a barrier between personnel and the cleanroom and, therefore, it should be covered in detail within a training program. The program should detail observing wear and tear, use of non-shedding materials, number of uses, frequency of changes, sterilization program, etc. A description of the types and layers of garments to be worn and an explanation of how gowning prevents contamination are necessary components. Personnel assigned to execute cleaning and disinfection SOPs should regown prior to conducting aseptic operations in critical areas. Importantly, instructions for the process of gowning that would permit successful qualification of personnel in gowning should be described explicitly.

For detailed gowning requirements, refer to EU GMP Annex 1 Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products and PDA Points to Consider for Aseptic Processing: Part 1 (2015).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Gowning: Gowning is the key procedure for creating a physical barrier between personnel and the cleanroom. The training program should cover in detail:

  • Wear and Tear: Inspect garments for integrity before use
  • Non-shedding Materials: Ensure the garments themselves do not become a source of particles
  • Number of uses and replacement frequency: Clearly define the service life of each garment
  • Sterilization Program: The sterilization cycles and methods for garments
  • Garment layers and types: Different cleanliness grades call for different gowning requirements

Analogy

The gowning procedure is like an astronaut putting on a space suit: every step has a purpose, and every layer provides a specific protective function. A small hole in a space suit could endanger the astronaut's life. Similarly, if cleanroom garments are damaged or worn incorrectly, microorganisms could breach this barrier and threaten the safety of the product and the patient.

Key Notes

Regowning after cleaning and disinfection: TR90 specifically emphasizes that personnel who carry out cleaning and disinfection SOPs must regown before performing aseptic operations in critical areas. This is because their garments may have been contaminated during the cleaning and disinfection process.

Qualification-oriented training: Gowning instructions must be clear enough for personnel to pass gowning qualification successfully. This means training is not just a "demonstration" but requires clear step-by-step instructions and assessment criteria.

Practice Questions

  1. Does your facility clearly specify the number of uses and the washing/sterilization cycles for gowning garments? Are these limits supported by validation data?
  2. Do personnel who perform cleaning and disinfection regown before entering aseptic operations? If not, what risks does this create?

8.3.1 Gowning Qualification

Original Text

Gowning qualification is the demonstration that a person is capable of consistently donning cleanroom garments and maintaining the barrier properties to ensure the exterior of the garment is not contaminated with microorganisms. Qualification should include multiple observations of the person donning the gown and taking multiple samples from the exterior of the garment after gowning to demonstrate the presence or absence of any microorganisms. Sample sites of the gown should be determined by a risk assessment that considers the specific operation. A documented rationale should explain the acceptance criteria for the personnel gowning qualification process. Personnel are prohibited from Grade A and Grade B cleanroom entry without having successfully completed gowning qualification.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Gowning Qualification: This is a formal assessment procedure that demonstrates personnel can don cleanroom garments consistently and correctly and maintain their barrier properties. The key elements include:

  • Multiple Observations: Passing once is not enough; multiple observations are needed to confirm consistency
  • Multiple Samples: Samples are taken from several locations on the outer surface of the garments for microbiological testing
  • Risk Assessment: Sampling locations should be determined based on a risk assessment of the specific operations
  • Acceptance Criteria: Documented acceptance criteria and their rationale must be in place

Key Notes

Mandatory requirement for Grade A and Grade B: TR90 states explicitly that personnel who have not successfully completed gowning qualification are "prohibited" from entering Grade A and Grade B cleanrooms. This is a non-negotiable requirement.

Scientific basis for sampling locations: Sampling is not random but is determined by risk assessment. For example, the forearms and chest are usually high-risk areas because they are closest to the product and the critical operations.

Analogy

Gowning qualification is like a pilot's skills check: you cannot judge a pilot as qualified from a single landing. The examiner needs to observe performance under different conditions many times to confirm that the pilot's operation is consistent and reliable. Likewise, consistency in gowning is the real quality assurance: gowning qualification must verify whether a person can still gown correctly under pressure or when rushed.

Practice Questions

  1. How many consecutive successful observations does your facility require for gowning qualification? Are the sampling locations based on a documented risk assessment?
  2. If an experienced operator fails gowning qualification, does your procedure define a clear course of action?

8.3.2 Gowning Disqualification and Requalification

Original Text

A documented program should also be established to provide the criteria that disqualifies personnel from cleanroom entry based on insufficient gowning technique and/or exceeding established microbial counts and/or trends from personnel monitoring. A matrix approach should be considered in developing the plan for disqualification, such as multiple alert-level excursions in a prescribed period, multiple action-level deviations in a prescribed period, or a combination of both. Operators can also be disqualified for observation of poor aseptic/cleanroom behavior.

If disqualified, a return to accessing cleanrooms would be contingent upon appropriate corrective actions being taken and successful completion of qualification (see Section 8.3.1). Additionally, requalification of gowning should be performed routinely on an annual basis or return from a long leave / extended absence for personnel to demonstrate their continued capability in gowning.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Disqualification: When personnel gowning technique is inadequate or microbial counts exceed the criteria, their cleanroom access qualification should be withdrawn. TR90 recommends using a matrix approach to establish disqualification criteria, for example:

  • Multiple alert-level excursions within a defined period
  • Multiple action-level deviations within a defined period
  • A combination of the above
  • Poor aseptic/cleanroom behavior observed

Requalification: This should be performed periodically (annually), or after a long leave or extended absence.

Analogy

This is like a driving-license demerit point system: a license is not revoked for a single violation, but judged cumulatively according to the severity and frequency of violations. An alert-level excursion is like a minor violation costing 1 point, and an action-level deviation is like a serious violation costing 3 points. When the total reaches a certain level, cleanroom access is temporarily "revoked" and can be restored only after retraining and reassessment. Annual requalification is like a regular health checkup: even when there are no problems, you still need to confirm periodically that the competence is being maintained.

Key Notes

Advantage of the matrix approach: A matrix avoids "one-size-fits-all" decisions. A single excursion may be a random event, but multiple excursions within a short period may indicate a systemic problem. This tiered assessment is more scientific and reasonable.

Prerequisites for reinstating qualification: After disqualification, personnel must (1) take appropriate corrective action and (2) successfully complete qualification before cleanroom access is restored. Both conditions are required.

Practical Application

In a CDMO facility, where staff turnover is relatively high, it is advisable to establish an electronic personnel qualification tracking system that automatically integrates environmental monitoring data and personnel monitoring results. When a disqualification criterion is triggered, the system automatically locks access rights and notifies the relevant supervisor. This ensures an immediate response and prevents human oversight from allowing unqualified personnel into the cleanroom.

Practice Questions

  1. Does your facility currently use a matrix approach to evaluate personnel disqualification? What trigger conditions does the matrix include?
  2. Is the completion rate for annual requalification 100%? How do you handle personnel who miss their annual requalification because of scheduling or other reasons?

8.4 Cleanroom Behavior

Original Text

To successfully prevent contamination in manufacturing areas, it is critical for operators to always observe appropriate cleanroom behaviors, including, but not limited to, the list below. It should be noted that these behaviors apply even when using barrier technologies:

  • Move carefully: Moving slowly and deliberately will prevent unnecessary air turbulence in a critical area thus minimizing the risk of particle-generation or shedding. The principle of slow, careful movement should be followed throughout the cleanroom, including awareness of engineering controls and locations of air returns.
  • Minimize conversation: Speaking should only occur when necessary and never in a critical area.
  • Maintain unidirectional airflow ("first air"): Unidirectional airflow is designed to protect sterile equipment surfaces, container-closures, and product. Disruption of the path of unidirectional flow air in the critical area can pose a risk to product sterility.
  • Perform interventions carefully: Using the correct tools and equipment and performing interventions in a manner that does not compromise sterility of the product ensures that unidirectional airflow over sterile product and sterile components will not be interrupted. When using a restricted access barrier system (RABS) system, barrier doors should be opened only when necessary and for the shortest time possible.
  • Position body: Keeping the entire body out of the path of the unidirectional airflow will prevent airflow from being blocked over critical surfaces. Personnel should not reach across open containers, exposed product, or components and product contact parts/surfaces.
  • Maintain proper gown control: Prior to and throughout aseptic operations, operators should not engage in any activity that poses an unreasonable contamination risk to the gown, such as unnecessary contact with walls, floors, and cleaned surfaces. After initial gowning, sterile gloves should be regularly sanitized or changed, as appropriate, to minimize the risk of contamination. If damage (e.g., hole, tear) is detected when donning the gown, the gown should be replaced immediately. If damage is detected during manufacturing, the operator should exit the cleanroom and regown.
  • Protect sterile parts: Parts should be staged and grouped for setup to allow for consistent setup order—from top to bottom, back to front—for the protection of all sterile parts. During setup, sterile product contact parts should remain protected until setup is completed. Between uses, sterile tools should be held under Grade A conditions and maintained in a manner that prevents contamination (e.g., placed in sterilized containers) or should be replaced. Parts should be sterilized together, whenever possible, to minimize aseptic connections during setup.
  • Minimize surface contact: Personnel should not come in direct contact with sterile product, containers, closures, or critical surfaces with any part of their gowns or gloves. Sterile tools should always be used, where possible.
  • Manage gloves: Regular glove sanitization techniques and frequency and re-gloving activities should always be followed. At a minimum, single gloves should be worn in supporting areas; sterile double-gloves should be worn in all conventional aseptic processing areas. A fresh pair of sterile gloves should be worn upon each entry to Grade A clean room. Gloves should be checked for integrity upon donning and prior to performing an intervention. If damage (e.g., hole, tear) is detected when donning the glove, the glove should immediately be replaced. If damage is detected during manufacturing, the operator should follow an established risk-based procedure to replace the glove while limiting any contamination hazard to the operation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Cleanroom Behavior: TR90 lists nine behavioral principles, each directly related to controlling contamination risk. Note in particular that these behavioral requirements apply equally when barrier technologies (such as RABS or isolators) are used.

The core principles can be grouped into three categories:

  • Airflow protection: Move with care, maintain first air, and use correct body positioning
  • Barrier maintenance: Control gowning, manage gloves, and minimize surface contact
  • Sterility protection: Perform interventions with care, protect sterile parts, and minimize talking

Analogy

Imagine walking through a room full of spider webs: every movement must be slow and deliberate, because any sudden movement disturbs the air and destroys those fragile structures. In the cleanroom, what you are protecting is not spider webs but the "sterile air curtain" formed by unidirectional airflow. Any unnecessary movement, talking, or improper body positioning can break this protective barrier.

Key Notes

The first air principle: This is one of the most important concepts in aseptic operations: air flowing from the HEPA filter should not contact any other surface or obstruction before it reaches the product. Operators' hands, arms, or bodies should never block the path of first air to critical surfaces.

Tiered glove requirements:

  • Supporting areas: a single layer of gloves at minimum
  • Traditional aseptic processing areas: sterile double gloves
  • Each entry into Grade A: change to new sterile gloves

Assembly sequence principle: Assemble from top to bottom and from back to front, so that sterile parts already assembled are not contaminated by subsequent operations.

Practical Application

In a CDMO facility, where operators may rotate between different product lines, it is advisable to create a "cleanroom behavior quick reference card" for each line that shows the line-specific airflow direction, the location of air returns, and the correct posture for critical interventions. Combined with regular video playback training, this lets operators "see their own movements" and improve on their own.

Practice Questions

  1. Does your facility clearly document the first air direction and airflow path at each operating station? Can operators point out these locations accurately?
  2. Is the door opening time of the RABS monitored and recorded? How do you ensure doors are opened "only when necessary and for the shortest possible time"?
  3. When a glove breach is found during operation, does your risk assessment procedure include evaluating the impact on the product already processed?

8.4.1 Aseptic Operator Training and Qualification

Original Text

Aseptic processing depends on personnel operating in a manner that does not disturb airflow, minimizes the generation of particles, and does not introduce bioburden into the process through inappropriate handling of product contact equipment or components. Aseptic operator trainees should only be permitted to perform interventions upon successful participation with a process simulation. Participation in the process simulation requires that the operator perform a pre-identified set or subset of critical tasks that they would perform during batch production, and the result of the process simulation was successful. Where there are practical limitations or an aseptic process simulation is not required, operators can be qualified outside of an aseptic process simulation provided the qualification exercise is representative of worst-case process conditions.

After an operator has been initially qualified, the facility should schedule an annual refresher training program as well as participation in routine aseptic process simulation (i.e., media-fill study). Access to the aseptic processing area should be controlled (e.g., badge access, etc.) and a procedure should be established for disqualification and timely revocation of access.

Disqualification should result in retraining and requalification, reassignment, or permanent removal from the cleanroom. Personnel would be considered disqualified from performing in an aseptic filling process if they had participated in a media-fill study that resulted in the detection of positive (failing) units and the root cause is determined to be inappropriate aseptic behavior. Additionally, disqualification should be considered if personnel participate in a commercial production lot that does not meet the requirements for sterility testing and the root cause is determined to be inappropriate aseptic behavior.

In addition to training and qualification/disqualification, there should be regular oversight of personnel following established, written procedures and performing good cleanroom behaviors and aseptic technique during manufacturing operations. Routine oversight through an observation program of aseptic operators should form part of the aseptic-operator training and qualification.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Aseptic Operator Training and Qualification: This is a higher-level requirement than for general cleanroom personnel. The key elements are:

  • Process Simulation: Operators must successfully perform their intended critical tasks in a media-fill study to be qualified
  • Worst-case Process Conditions: When process simulation is not possible, the qualification exercise must represent worst-case conditions
  • Annual Refresher: After initial qualification, operators must take part in refresher training and routine media fills periodically
  • Access Control: Entry to aseptic processing areas is controlled, for example by access cards

Key Notes

Triggers for disqualification:

  • A media-fill study in which the operator participated has a positive (failed) unit, and the root cause is improper aseptic behavior
  • A commercial production batch in which the operator participated fails the sterility test, and the root cause is improper aseptic behavior

Options after disqualification:

  • Retraining and requalification
  • Reassignment to another role
  • Permanent removal of cleanroom access

Note: Disqualification is not punitive; it is a necessary risk management tool. The focus is on root cause analysis and corrective action.

Analogy

Aseptic operator qualification is like the assessment of a surgeon: you cannot go into the operating room on books and exams alone; you must first perform the procedure successfully in a simulated operation. The media-fill study is the aseptic operator's "simulated surgery." If a problem occurs in the simulation (microbial growth), it means the product could also be contaminated in actual operations. Annual refresher training and media-fill participation ensure that operator skills do not degrade over time.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, scheduling media-fill studies is a challenge because every operator must participate without disrupting the commercial production schedule. Recommended practices:

  • Build a "qualification calendar" that plans media-fill participation for all personnel 6 months in advance
  • Use product changeovers or annual shutdown periods to schedule media fills
  • Ensure that the tasks each operator performs in the media fill are fully consistent with their routine work
  • Link the access control system to the qualification database so that expired qualifications automatically lock out access

Practice Questions

  1. Does your facility require all aseptic operators to participate in a media fill every year? How do you handle operators who miss it because of scheduling?
  2. When a media fill fails, can your root cause investigation procedure effectively distinguish between "operator behavior issues" and "equipment/environment issues"?
  3. Is operator access authorization linked in real time to qualification status?

Original Text

An aseptic-observer program is a structured process through which aseptic operators are trained to routinely assess and evaluate one another's performance and compliance to all aspects of training and qualification, especially with respect to cleanroom behaviors and aseptic practices. The aseptic-observer program not only identifies areas for improvement but also reinforces observed best practices. An aseptic-observer program can follow the seven simple steps illustrated in Figure 8.4.2-1.

Figure 8.4.2-1🔍 — Aseptic-Operator's Observation 7-Step Program

Step 1: Qualify Operators
All personnel included in the aseptic-observer program should be fully qualified.

Step 2: Identify and Train Aseptic Observers
Aseptic-behavior observers are trained individuals with experience in aseptic behaviors and practices who are respected by the operators.

Step 3: Observe Aseptic Behaviors
Observers use a defined expectations list of behaviors as described in Section 8.4. Behaviors and interventions performed during manufacturing should be consistent with interventions qualified during aseptic process simulations.

Step 4: Report and Provide Feedback
Observers should immediately give feedback to operators on their behaviors as part of continuous improvement. Observations discussed with personnel and frontline management should include overall observations, identification of any observation trends, and potential corrective actions. Potential corrective actions might include refresher training, procedural revisions for clarification, equipment modifications, or use of tools.

Step 5: Improve Aseptic Processes and Behaviors
Barriers to improvement (e.g., process/line configuration, material availability, procedure) should be addressed, and corrective actions should be identified and implemented.

Step 6: Analyze and Trend Observations
Periodically, long-term trending should be performed to determine root cause and to proactively address the most frequent observations. Trend results should then be communicated to operators, management, and support personnel according to site practices. An overall risk assessment tool may also be used for this purpose.

Step 7: Maintain Program
Periodically, the observation program itself should be reviewed to determine if changes are needed to adapt to personnel, facility, and/or process changes. Modifications may include updates to the defined expectations list of behaviors (see Step #3), communication of results, data trending, or effectiveness of corrective actions.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Aseptic Observer Program: This is a structured peer-assessment procedure in which trained aseptic operators periodically evaluate each other's performance and compliance. The program has two purposes:

  • Identify areas for improvement
  • Reinforce observed best practices

The seven-step cycle forms a complete PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act) improvement loop.

Key Notes

Core logic of the seven-step program:

  • Steps 1-2 (establish the foundation): Ensure that both participants and observers are qualified and respected
  • Steps 3-4 (observation and feedback): Observe using a standardized checklist of behavioral expectations and give feedback in real time
  • Steps 5-6 (improvement and trending): Remove barriers to improvement and perform long-term trend analysis to detect systemic problems
  • Step 7 (maintain the program): Periodically review the effectiveness of the program itself and adjust it

Key success factor: Observers must be "respected by the operators." If observers are seen as "monitors" rather than "peer coaches," the program loses its value for building culture.

Analogy

The aseptic observer program is like a sports team's "film review": coaches and players watch game footage together, not to blame anyone for mistakes but to identify what can be improved and to reinforce good performance. The best teams have not only good players but also a culture of continuously learning from each other and improving. Likewise, the best aseptic teams have not only qualified individuals but also mechanisms for continuous observation, feedback, and improvement.

Practical Application

Practical recommendations for establishing an effective aseptic observer program:

  • Digital observation forms: Use electronic forms on tablets instead of paper records to allow real-time entry and later trend analysis
  • A culture of positive feedback: Observation reports record not only deviations but also good practices, creating positive reinforcement
  • Cross-analysis with environmental monitoring data: Combine observer reports with environmental monitoring data to identify correlations between personnel behavior and environmental data
  • Quarterly trend reports: Compile observation data each quarter and report the main findings and improvement progress to management and the operating teams

Practice Questions

  1. Does your facility currently have a formal aseptic observer program? If so, what are the observation frequency and coverage?
  2. What are the selection criteria for observers? Do they ensure that observers are "respected by the operators"?
  3. Are observation results trended regularly? What are the most common types of deviations? Do these deviations correlate with environmental monitoring data?

Section Summary

  • People are the largest source of contamination: TR90 repeatedly emphasizes that personnel and their behavior are the largest source of microbial contamination in pharmaceutical manufacturing, so personnel training and qualification are a core element of the CCS.
  • Six mandatory courses: Basic microbiology, personnel flow, material and waste flow, environmental control, cleaning and disinfection, and process design. This is the minimum training required of all manufacturing personnel before entering the production area.
  • Four core topics: Basic microbiology, personal hygiene, gowning, and cleanroom behavior. These four form the backbone of a contamination control training program.
  • Full lifecycle management of gowning: From qualification (multiple observations + multiple samples) through disqualification (matrix approach) to requalification (annual basis), forming closed-loop management.
  • Nine cleanroom behavior principles: Move with care, minimize talking, maintain first air, perform interventions with care, use correct body positioning, control gowning, protect sterile parts, minimize surface contact, and manage gloves.
  • Advanced requirements for aseptic operators: Operators must successfully perform critical tasks in a media fill to be qualified, and annual refresher training and media-fill participation are ongoing requirements.
  • The seven steps of the aseptic observer program: From operator qualification through observation, feedback, improvement, and trend analysis to maintenance of the program itself, forming a closed loop of continual improvement.
  • The shift from "non-holistic" to "holistic" thinking: No longer assuming that "following SOPs is enough," but pursuing a holistic approach built on understanding hazards, building quality culture, and continual improvement.

9.1 Equipment Design

Original Text

Process contamination risks that are not controlled by equipment design may result in additional risks that need to be mitigated by other controls, such as creation of procedures, use of sterile tools, segregation, or shorter hold times. Not putting these other controls in place could lead to higher operational costs and higher risk for the life of the product.

For example, if the decision is made during process development to leave an in-process step as manual and open, all the surrounding control elements, including the personnel controls and all environmental controls (e.g., cleaning, gowning, flow), should be intensively built up to prevent contamination. In addition, more quality control testing/monitoring will be required at that step, and EM will need to be done every time that step is performed. The CCS should be reevaluated for risk mitigation from development to commercial phase to improve the controls as manufacturing scales up.

Items to be considered during the equipment design phase of CCS include:

  • Equipment is a closed system wherever possible
  • Equipment is for sanitary use (e.g., 316SS versus a lower grade of stainless steel, avoid any parts that may generate particles)
  • Fixed probes (e.g., for active viable air and total airborne particle sampling in an isolator) locations determined by an EM risk assessment and airflow visualization studies
  • Surfaces are smooth and easy to clean
  • Equipment is designed to minimize routine human intervention in critical areas
  • Barrier equipment designed with interlocks to prevent operator mistakes and/or alert operator about component malfunction
  • Ease of access and compatibility with operator access (in isolator or RABS) during aseptic assembly, as well as when performing interventions, to prevent operators leaning over open product or components
  • Parts are designed to be sterilized as a unit to minimize connections during setup
  • Sterile pathways covered during setup, removing covers only at the end
  • Mechanical and electrical adjustments designed to be made outside the aseptic processing area
  • Are plans for pipes, ducts, and other utilities to be installed designed to not create recesses or have unsealed openings and/or surfaces that are difficult to clean?
  • Through-the-wall passages for pipes and tubing sealed or gasketed, with no openings that expose wall interiors
  • All designs result in slopes for drainage
  • Exhaust filters allow exhaust to meet area classification where it is being discharged (e.g., Grade A or B)
  • Source air for air-break filters on autoclaves or lyophilizers drawn from the cleanroom rather than from the plant area
  • Electronics are covered or provided with a wipeable surface that is resistant to disinfectants (e.g., equipment computers or keyboards)
  • Equipment can be easily protected during storage
  • Ways to measure alert/action alarm conditions for all critical operating parameters

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Design Drives Cost: TR90 states at the outset that if contamination risk is not effectively controlled at the equipment design stage, it must later be compensated for with more SOPs, aseptic tools, segregation measures, and shorter hold times. These remedial measures not only increase operating costs but also increase risk over the product lifecycle.

Closed System: Designing equipment as a closed system wherever possible is the first principle. A closed system means the product is not exposed to the external environment, eliminating the risk of microbial and particulate contamination at the source.

Interlock: Interlocks on barrier equipment can prevent operator errors (such as opening a door at the wrong time) or warn of equipment component failure. This is a concrete embodiment of "equipment error-proofing."

Analogy

Equipment design is like the foundation and structure of a house. If the foundation is poor (poor equipment design), you must keep adding alarm systems and hiring more security guards (more SOPs, more monitoring), and the house is still unstable. A house with a solid foundation (good equipment design), on the other hand, is easy to maintain and comfortable to live in. In the pharmaceutical industry, this difference can mean hundreds of thousands to millions in cost per batch.

Key Notes

The Cost of Manual and Open: If a process step is designed as "manual and open," TR90 clearly states that all surrounding controls must be "comprehensively strengthened." This includes:

  • Strengthen personnel controls (stricter gowning and behavior rules)
  • Strengthen environmental controls (higher-grade cleaning, airflow management)
  • Increase the frequency of quality testing/monitoring
  • Perform EM (Environmental Monitoring) every time the step is executed

From the development stage through the commercial stage, the CCS should be continuously re-evaluated to improve control.

Key Takeaways from the Design Checklist

The 18 design considerations listed in TR90 can be summarized into four principles:

  • Isolation principle: closed systems, barrier equipment, interlocks, reduced human intervention
  • Cleanability principle: smooth surfaces, hygienic-grade materials (316SS), drainage slope, sealed penetrations
  • Monitorability principle: fixed probe positions, Alert/Action alarm conditions, wipeable electronic equipment
  • Maintainability principle: mechanical/electrical adjustments made outside the aseptic area, equipment easy to store under protection

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Your CDMO is evaluating the introduction of a new filling line. The client's product requires an open, manual addition step. Following TR90 guidance, you should:

  1. First assess whether the step can be eliminated by changing to a closed system design
  2. If it cannot be avoided, calculate the additional cost of "comprehensively strengthened control" (personnel, EM, QC testing)
  3. Pass this cost on in the quotation to the client, or suggest that the client invest in a closed system to reduce long-term operating costs

  2. List three items on your equipment that do not meet the TR90 design checklist, and propose improvements.
  3. Why does TR90 require that "parts should be designed to be sterilized as one unit" rather than connected one by one on site?
9.2 Equipment Cleaning Validation

Original Text

Cleaning validation is essential, but it is also limited in its value to predict microbial control over the lifespan of equipment due to the timing and amount of validation testing and the limitations of traditional microbiological testing. Historically, cleaning validation has primarily focused on the removal of product residue and less on prevention of biofilms. Additionally, sampling techniques and culture-based microbiological testing are known to have limited detection power for a range of microorganisms.

Equipment validation is often performed on new equipment that has not been exposed to the broad range of factors that can reduce the effectiveness of cleaning. Existing equipment is revalidated on a defined schedule and/or if the validated conditions are no longer relevant (e.g., new product introduction, new equipment configuration). Factors that reduce the effectiveness of a cleaning procedure include biofilm-producing organisms, years of residue build-up, normal wear, and surface damage.

Some cleaning regimens can be effective for years until a worst-case biofilm-forming organism is introduced, for example, Arthrobacter russicus, which can develop a biofilm that is difficult to remove in a low-nutrient environment in as little as three days. PDA Technical Report 69 provides a thorough guideline on biofilm prevention, including scenarios of contamination from equipment. Practical considerations for equipment cleaning are presented in Section 15.0, Table 8. For further information on cleaning validation, refer to PDA Technical Report 29 and PDA Technical Report 49.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Limitations of Cleaning Validation: TR90 raises an important but often overlooked point here: cleaning validation is necessary, but its ability to predict microbial control over the entire service life of the equipment is limited. The reasons include:

  • Validation is limited in timing and test volume (usually only 3 batches)
  • Traditional microbial test methods have limited detection capability
  • Validation is usually performed on new equipment, which has not yet been exposed to the various degradation factors of long-term use

Biofilm: A protective community structure formed by microorganisms on equipment surfaces. Once formed, its resistance to cleaning agents and disinfectants increases substantially, making routine cleaning procedures ineffective.

Analogy

Cleaning validation is like the factory inspection of a new car: every indicator is perfect when the car is new, but you cannot conclude that it will never need servicing. As the car ages, carbon build-up in the engine (= residue build-up), rubber aging (= surface damage), and even unusual road conditions (= worst-case biofilm organism) can make a previously effective maintenance routine insufficient. Biofilm is like limescale in a water pipe: once formed it is hard to remove completely, and it keeps getting thicker.

Key Notes

The Arthrobacter russicus case: TR90 specifically names this microorganism, which can form a hard-to-remove biofilm in a low-nutrient environment in only three days. This case shows that:

  • Your cleaning procedure may work effectively for years, until it meets a worst-case biofilm-forming organism
  • You cannot assume the cleaning procedure will always be adequate just because "there has never been a problem"
  • Control of clean hold time is critical, because any residual moisture can become a breeding ground for biofilm

Cross-references: PDA TR69 (biofilm prevention) and PDA TR29 and TR49 (cleaning validation) provide more detailed guidance.

9.3 Equipment Ongoing Control

Original Text

While equipment can be well designed and validated, routine and ongoing control of equipment during use is also an important consideration in the CCS strategy. Equipment that is not controlled effectively can contaminate the product, harbor biofilms of microorganisms and act as a reservoir for microbial contamination, which can be directly introduced into the process stream.

Contamination control considerations for the control phase include:

  • Prior to use, are processing vessels and components cleaned and sanitized? How is the clean status of equipment identified and verified? Is the cleaning/sanitization status readily identified with labels/tags on each piece of equipment? How do operators know the clean hold time of the equipment?
  • Are sterilized holding tanks and any contained liquids held under positive pressure or appropriately sealed to prevent microbial contamination?
  • Is sterilized equipment discarded or removed from the area when the packaging or cover is not integral, as evidenced by holes, rips, or tears or when unexpected condensate is present? Do site procedures for aseptic filling-line setup include a check for the presence of wet components and state that these should not be used?
  • Is the equipment included in the preventive maintenance program? Does the program include elastomer management and replacement?
  • Has the time interval between the washing, drying, and sterilization of components, containers, and equipment been qualified, as well as the time interval between their sterilization and use?
  • Is the equipment visually confirmed to be dry prior to wrapping or covering?
  • Are appropriate protective coverings applied to items for sterilization/depyrogenation so that sterility is maintained post-sterilization?
  • Is sterilized equipment clearly differentiated from nonsterilized items (e.g., color-change indicator)?
  • Are presterilized materials that cannot be sterilized via passage into the Grade A space (e.g., via double-door autoclave, dry heat oven) double-wrapped, at a minimum, allowing layer removal when transferred into the clean areas? Are sterile surfaces exposed only in the critical area?
  • Is dirty equipment removed as soon as possible and cleaned within the dirty equipment hold time?
  • Are doors to RABS open only when necessary and for the shortest duration? Is there a validated recovery time set for airflows?
  • Is there a transfer plan in place to ensure equipment storage does not result in the introduction of contamination when returning it to the manufacturing area?

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reservoir for Microbial Contamination: Poorly controlled equipment becomes not just a one-time source of contamination but a persistent microbial "nest." Once a biofilm forms on the inner walls of equipment or in piping, it continuously releases microorganisms into the process stream and becomes the root of recurring contamination.

Clean Hold Time vs. Dirty Hold Time：

  • Clean Hold Time: The maximum permitted time between cleaning/sterilization of equipment and its use
  • Dirty Hold Time: The maximum permitted time between the end of equipment use and the start of cleaning

Both must be validated, because exceeding either time limit can lead to microbial proliferation or biofilm formation.

Analogy

Ongoing equipment control is like managing a household refrigerator:

  • Clean hold time = the expiry date label on food (once expired, it cannot be used)
  • Dirty hold time = dishes must not sit in the sink too long without washing (they grow mold)
  • Status label = the date label on food in the refrigerator (clearly shows the status)
  • Unexpected condensate = water droplets on food packaging (the food may have spoiled)
  • Color-change indicator = an egg freshness tester (you know the status at a glance)

Key Notes

The 12 checkpoints can be grouped into five control areas:

  • Status control: cleaned/sterilized status labeling, color-change indicators, visual confirmation of dryness
  • Time control: clean hold time, dirty hold time, time interval between sterilization and use
  • Integrity control: discard torn packaging, double-bagging of pre-sterilized materials, RABS door opening control
  • Positive pressure control: sterilized tanks kept under positive pressure or sealed
  • Transfer control: equipment storage and transfer plan for return to the manufacturing area

RABS Door Opening Control

TR90 specifically notes that RABS doors should be opened only when necessary, for as short a duration as possible, and that a validated airflow Recovery Time is needed. This means that after each door opening, you must wait for the airflow to return to a qualified state. This is one of the core requirements of EU GMP Annex 1 for RABS operation.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Your facility has just finished a filling batch, and the next batch will not start for 16 hours. The operator cleaned the equipment at the end of the shift and attached a "Cleaned" label. Under the TR90 control requirements, you should ensure that:

  1. The "Cleaned" label includes the cleaning date/time so that the operator can check against the clean hold time
  2. The equipment was visually confirmed to be dry before it was wrapped/covered
  3. If the clean hold time would be exceeded before the next batch starts, the equipment is re-cleaned/re-sterilized
  4. The equipment storage area has controls to prevent the introduction of contamination

Practice Questions

  1. How does your facility distinguish sterilized from unsterilized equipment? Besides color-change indicators, what other methods are there?
  2. If a small hole is found in the packaging of a sterilized part but the part looks clean, how would you handle it? Why?
  3. Why does TR90 require that "wet parts should not be used"? What is the relationship between moisture and microbial contamination?

9.4.1 Use of Single-Use Equipment

Single-use bags and product flow paths are increasingly being incorporated into manufacturing processes because they can provide a closed-system manufacturing environment. For example, they can be used for raw materials and sterile drug substance and as part of the sterile filtration process to drug product. These systems may be irradiated and designed to allow processing to occur without exposure to the external environment. Checking the sterilization cycles for this equipment upon receipt is very important (10). Observations for leaks or holes before and during use is very important. These systems are often linked with tube welders to connect different single-use systems (SUS) without breaching the sterile product pathway. It is essential the welders are used properly with the correct technique and maintenance (correct blades, wafers, etc. changed at appropriate intervals) and welds inspected prior to use. The risk to the process from this equipment is that leak/integrity testing often cannot be performed prior to and after use. Therefore, vendors for this equipment work to develop such safeguards to reduce these risks. More information on SUS can be found in PDA Technical Report No. 66: Application of Single-Use Systems in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing.

Single-use rapid-transfer ports can be used to dock isolators for the transfer of sterile materials. For example, stoppers may be sterilized directly into these single-use containers and subsequently transferred into a Grade A isolator.

9.4.2 Use of Barrier Technology

Barrier separation technology is preferred for use in a CCS for operations that are not fully closed, as it is increasingly important to separate the operator from the process. The use of isolators and RABS units support this practice, as interventions occur using special RABS or isolator gloves integrated within the system. The design of such equipment is important; for instance, the gloves should be placed appropriately where the interventions to be performed do not require the operator to reach over sterile product or components. A glove test strategy, combining visual and physical leak tests, should be defined for isolators and RABS. Where RABS are used, the timing, frequency, and duration of door-openings are restricted to limit risk of contamination.

9.4.3 Product Pathways

The product pathway is the environment in which the product is exposed within the system; it may include piping, vessels, and other equipment. Assessment of the control of contamination includes a closure risk analysis of the product pathway. Closed systems offer the lowest risk. Any open steps in the product pathway should be evaluated for improvements that may reduce or eliminate the risk. This may involve the use of SUS or rapid transfer ports, sterile-to-sterile connections, or equipment that is designed for closed-system dispensing and sampling.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Single-Use Systems (SUS): The greatest advantage of SUS is that they provide a closed manufacturing environment, eliminating the need for CIP/SIP and the risk of cross-contamination. TR90 also points out their core risk: it is usually not possible to perform leak/integrity testing before and after use.

Tube Welder: Used to connect different SUS without breaking the sterile product pathway. Weld quality directly affects the integrity of the product pathway, so regular replacement of the blade/wafer and pre-use inspection of weld joints are essential.

Barrier Separation Technology: For operations that cannot be fully enclosed, isolators and RABS (Restricted Access Barrier Systems) are the preferred solutions in the CCS. The core purpose is to separate the operator from the process.

Product Pathway: The environment to which the product is exposed within the system. Performing a closure risk analysis is the key method for assessing the contamination risk of the product pathway.

Analogy

SUS = disposable tableware: Just as a restaurant using disposable tableware eliminates the risk of poorly washed dishes, you must make sure the packaging is intact (no holes) and check it before use. The challenge with SUS is that for some of the "packaging," you cannot fully test its seal integrity before use.

Barrier Technology = space station glove box: Astronauts handle hazardous materials through dedicated glove boxes on the space station, and the glove positions must be designed at reasonable working angles so that astronauts do not have to "lean over" the object they are handling. Likewise, the glove positions of an isolator/RABS must be designed so that the operator does not need to reach over sterile product or parts.

Key Notes

Key control points for SUS:

  • Confirm sterilization cycle data on receipt
  • Observe for leaks or holes before and during use
  • Correct use technique and maintenance of the tube welder
  • Weld joints must be inspected before use

Glove Test Strategy: For isolators and RABS, a glove test strategy combining visual inspection and physical leak testing must be defined. Gloves are the weakest link in a barrier system, and any pinhole leak can compromise the aseptic environment.

Product Pathway risk hierarchy: Closed system (lowest risk) > Sterile-to-sterile connection > SUS/RTP > Open step (highest risk). Any open step should be evaluated for whether it can be improved to a lower-risk option.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Your facility uses SUS for solution preparation and connects to the sterile filtration system with a tube welder. A microbial deviation recently occurred, and the investigation showed a minor leak at the weld joint. You should:

  1. Immediately check the tube welder maintenance records (were the blade/wafer replaced on schedule?)
  2. Review the welding SOP and operator training records
  3. Evaluate whether to increase the frequency of pre-use visual/physical inspection of weld joints
  4. Consider working with the supplier to develop a pre-use integrity test method

9.5 Maintenance

Original Text

Regular equipment maintenance is the key to preventing failures of the cleanroom environmental control systems (HVAC) and production equipment and ensuring the cleanrooms and equipment are operating in their validated state. Maintenance is a risk management practice used to maximize production and minimize loss and waste (see Section 15.0, Table 5). Section 17.0: Appendix III contains case studies of contamination related to the lack of adequate maintenance.

Maintenance activities can also pose contamination risks if not done correctly, starting with making appropriate design choices that reduce the amount of, or increase the ease of, maintenance based on failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA). The best design practice is to build a gray space adjacent to the classified areas that allows access to the equipment to minimize maintenance and calibration personnel working in the classified areas. For example, autoclave, dry heat tunnels, or ovens could be installed in a manner that provides remote access to the equipment spaces. These gray spaces may comprise adjacent corridor access spaces or an interstitial space above the equipment and systems.

Maintenance sometimes involves opening areas that are not cleaned routinely (e.g., behind HEPA filters, electrical panels). If performed during operations, maintenance activities may increase the risk of product, environment, and equipment contamination. Routine maintenance reduces unexpected failures which, in turn, reduces the risk of contamination. Developing a control strategy for preventive and corrective maintenance is important to ensure that the validated state, product sterility, or contamination control is not compromised. The design of the equipment (single-use disposable versus multiuse) and the validated cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization processes, in conjunction with a comprehensive preventive maintenance plan, are critical components of the microbial control strategy.

If certain sections of the cleanroom need to be segregated for maintenance, temporary gray space enclosures (TGSE) can be considered instead of shutting down the entire cleanroom. TGSE requires careful planning and specific procedures to ensure controls are in place and being followed by maintenance personnel and contractors. Planning needs to define traffic patterns into and out of the TGSE area, include monitoring for pressure differential to ensure the enclosure is maintained at a pressure lower than the surrounding classified areas, and provide specific instructions to personnel for gowning and degowning as they enter and exit the TGSE area. The frequency and type of cleaning and sanitization activities performed around the TGSE could be increased to control contamination that may be associated with the TGSE and activities occurring there. Section 16.3 discusses a related case study.

Minimizing traffic in and around the TGSE area during operations is also important. Heightened EM around this area should be considered to provide information about whether the containment process is working as planned. Environmental data, such as airborne monitoring, that shows increased levels in the adjacent classified areas would indicate that the containment, personnel pathways, and gowning enhancements should be reassessed to improve the contamination control. Prior to setting up a maintenance TGSE, a process should be established to recover the area upon completion of those activities. This would typically include cleaning to remove any construction or gross contamination that may have occurred, followed by a comprehensive disinfection process to reestablish the environmental control. Depending on the specific maintenance or contamination activity, a specific cleaning and disinfection plan should be considered before releasing the areas for routine operation. For standard shut-down situations, it is common practice to perform a triple-cleaning and disinfection cycle followed by EM before releasing the areas for routine operation.

Devising a comprehensive preventive maintenance plan for parts that are reused, including elastomeric components (e.g., hoses, diaphragms, gaskets, O-rings), probes, agitators, valves, and filters, is crucial. Routine visual inspection of surfaces, including product contact surfaces, should be performed; such a program enables the early detection of rouging or any other deterioration of process equipment, which can prevent particulate contamination in the product and avoid the loss of cleaning effectiveness. The design of piping and valves should prevent steam condensate from collecting and possible backflow that might lead to contamination. Continuous assessments of change control, work orders, and other process improvements should be conducted to ensure that their individual or cumulative effects do not impact the CCS.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The Dual Nature of Maintenance: TR90 reveals the inherent contradiction of maintenance activities: regular maintenance can prevent equipment failure and contamination, but maintenance activities themselves, if poorly controlled, can also introduce contamination. This is why the maintenance strategy must be integrated with the CCS.

Gray Space: A non-classified area built next to the cleanroom so that maintenance personnel can access equipment from behind without entering the cleanroom. This is a best practice of "eliminating risk through design."

TGSE - Temporary Gray Space Enclosure: When part of a cleanroom needs maintenance, a temporary enclosure can be set up instead of shutting down the entire cleanroom. This is a compromise between "maintenance needs" and "production continuity."

Rouging: Iron oxide deposits that form on stainless steel equipment surfaces through long-term use. They cause particulate contamination and reduce cleaning effectiveness.

Analogy

TGSE = temporary partition during restaurant renovation: Imagine a restaurant that is still open needing to renovate one corner of its kitchen. The restaurant does not close entirely; it uses temporary partitions to separate the construction area from the dining area and the rest of the kitchen. But this requires:

  • The partition is well sealed (= pressure differential monitoring)
  • Construction workers have a dedicated entry and exit route (= defined traffic flow)
  • Construction workers wear protective equipment (= gowning procedure)
  • Enhanced cleaning around the partition (= increased cleaning and disinfection frequency)
  • After construction, thorough cleaning and disinfection before the area is returned to use (= triple-cleaning cycle + EM)

Key Notes

TGSE control checklist:

  • Define personnel entry and exit routes
  • Monitor pressure differential (the TGSE area pressure must be lower than the surrounding classified area)
  • Dedicated gowning/degowning procedure
  • Increase the cleaning and disinfection frequency of the surrounding area
  • Strengthen EM monitoring
  • Minimize personnel movement around the TGSE
  • Establish an area recovery procedure

Triple-Cleaning and Disinfection Cycle: The standard practice for recovery after a shutdown. This is not simply "cleaning three times" but a complete "cleaning + disinfection" cycle repeated three times, after which EM must confirm that the environment has returned to a qualified state before release.

Management of Elastomeric Components

TR90 particularly emphasizes the preventive maintenance program for Elastomeric Components (gaskets, O-rings, diaphragms, hoses, etc.). These parts are the most prone to aging and degradation in the equipment, and once they lose their sealing function they become a point of entry for contamination. Regular visual inspection of product-contact surfaces allows early detection of rouging or other degradation, avoiding particulate contamination and reduced cleaning effectiveness.

Risk Balance in the Maintenance Strategy

Maintenance strategy considerations:

  Risk of not performing maintenance:
    Equipment failure → unplanned downtime → contamination risk ↑ → product loss

  Risk of maintenance itself:
    Opening non-routine areas → particle/microbial release → environmental contamination

  Optimal balance:
    Preventive maintenance (PM) > corrective maintenance (CM)
    Gray space design > maintenance inside the cleanroom
    TGSE area isolation > shutdown of the entire cleanroom

  Recovery criteria:
    Triple clean/disinfect → EM confirmation → release

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: An HVAC fan in your grade B cleanroom needs urgent repair, but two filling lines are currently running commercial batches. You decide to use a TGSE. Under TR90, your action plan should include:

  1. Define the TGSE boundary and install a temporary enclosure, ensuring the pressure differential is lower than the surrounding grade B area
  2. Establish a dedicated entry/exit route and gowning procedure for maintenance personnel
  3. Add EM sampling points and increase the sampling frequency around the TGSE
  4. Increase the cleaning and disinfection frequency around the TGSE
  5. After the repair, perform the triple-cleaning cycle + EM and release the area only after the results are confirmed acceptable
  6. Include all EM data in the batch record review

Practice Questions

  1. Does your facility have a gray space design? If not, which maintenance activities most often require maintenance personnel to enter the cleanroom?
  2. How is the replacement frequency of your elastomeric components (O-rings, gaskets, etc.) determined? Is it based on time, number of uses, or condition inspection?
  3. If EM data around the TGSE show a rising microbial level, what response actions should you initiate?
  4. Explain why TR90 requires "continuous evaluation of the individual or cumulative effects of change control, work orders, and other process improvements," and what this means for the CCS.

Section Summary

  • Holistic Approach: Equipment "design, validation, and ongoing control" must be planned as one complete chain; the three are interlinked and none can be omitted. "Validated" does not mean "effective forever."
  • Design drives cost: Investment at the equipment design stage can greatly reduce later operating costs and risks. Design decisions such as closed systems, hygienic design, interlocks, and gray space have a far-reaching impact on the long-term effectiveness of the CCS.
  • Limitations of cleaning validation: The predictive power of cleaning validation is limited by test timing, quantity, and methods. Biofilm (e.g., Arthrobacter russicus can form within 3 days) is a long-term threat. Control of clean hold time is the key line of defense.
  • Five areas of ongoing control: status control, time control, integrity control, positive pressure control, and transfer control. Each has specific checkpoints.
  • SUS and barrier technology: SUS offer the advantages of a closed system but integrity testing is limited; barrier technology (isolator/RABS) is the preferred option for non-closed operations; the product pathway requires a closure risk analysis.
  • The dual nature of maintenance: Maintenance is both a means of preventing contamination and a potential source of it. The TGSE is an effective tool for balancing maintenance needs with production continuity, but it requires strict control of pressure differential, traffic flow, and EM, as well as a triple-cleaning cycle recovery procedure.
  • Elastomer management: Elastomeric parts such as O-rings and gaskets are the weak link in equipment sealing and must be included in the preventive maintenance program. Early detection of surface degradation such as rouging can prevent particulate contamination.

10.0 Product Containers and Closures

Original Text

Container closure systems are an essential means of controlling microbial and foreign-particulate contamination of excipients, APIs, DS, process intermediates, and finished DPs (19). In the case of sterile products, a failure of container closure systems integrity may impact patient safety due to the potential for microbial ingress and product contamination. Throughout Section 10.0, the term container closure (CC) and packaging are used interchangeably.

"A CC system is required to 'not be reactive, additive, or absorptive so as to alter the safety, identity, strength, quality, or purity of the drug beyond the official or established requirements.'"

In addition, the CC system should "provide adequate protection against foreseeable external factors in storage and use that can cause deterioration or contamination of the drug product" (20).

Therefore, the CC system should be designed to ensure that the material is protected from factors that may impact product quality over its shelf life, including light, evaporation, product loss, exposure to gases, absorption of water, or microbial and particulate contamination (21).

CC system for finished DP are frequently composed of primary and secondary container closures. The primary CC comes in direct contact with the DP and represents the main physical barrier preventing interaction of the product with the exterior environment. The CC system may include a delivery device assembled into the primary container closure for product functionality. Secondary packaging may help protect the primary CC system from microbial or foreign particulate contamination due to physical damage during handling and shipping.

CC system and the associated container closure integrity (CCI) is critical for the prevention of product contamination throughout the shelf life of a finished DP. Figure 10.2-1🔍 provides an overview of CCI considerations.

Degrazio described in detail the main factors to consider when developing a CC system platform for sterile product packaging (22). Establishment of this framework is based on the six main aspects defined in a typical Ishikawa or fishbone diagram—materials, equipment, process, measurement, environment, and people (Figure 10.2-1🔍). Degrazio clearly states that establishing a well-designed, comprehensive framework offers the opportunity to minimize redundant CCI studies when expanding to incorporate new or additional products. Considering all these details within a CC system and a CCI program is recommended. For additional information, refer to EU GMP Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products (2022).

Figure 10.2-1🔍: Fishbone Diagram Conveying a Comprehensive View of the Factors that have an Impact on Assuring Adequate CCI (Reprinted with permission, ©2018 Degrazio (22))

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Container Closure System (CC System): This is the last physical barrier between the drug product and the outside environment. A CC system must not only keep microorganisms and particles out of the product, but must also not interact with the drug itself (it must be non-reactive, non-leaching and non-absorptive/non-adsorptive toward product components).

Container Closure Integrity (CCI): The ability of the CC system to maintain its sealing function throughout the product shelf life. A CCI failure means microorganisms may gain access to a sterile product, which is a direct threat to patient safety.

Primary vs Secondary CC:

  • Primary CC: In direct contact with the drug product and serving as the main physical barrier (for example, vial + stopper + aluminum seal)
  • Secondary CC: Protects the primary packaging from physical damage (for example, outer carton, blister pack)

Analogy

A CC system is like vacuum-sealed food packaging. Picture a vacuum-packed bag of coffee beans:

  • Primary packaging (primary CC) = the foil bag in direct contact with the beans. It must be completely sealed, must not react with the coffee, and must keep air and moisture out
  • Secondary packaging (secondary CC) = the outer carton, which keeps the foil bag from being punctured in transit
  • CCI = the quality assurance that the foil bag stays under vacuum all the way from the factory to the consumer

If the foil bag has a tiny hole (a CCI failure), air and moisture get in and the coffee spoils. In the pharmaceutical world this is not just a quality problem; it is a patient safety problem.

Key Notes

The dual regulatory standard: A CC system must meet two conditions at the same time:

  1. Do not alter the drug product (inert): non-reactive, non-additive and non-absorptive
  2. Protect the drug product (protective): guard against light, evaporation, gas exposure, moisture uptake, and microbial and particulate contamination

These requirements come from USP and FDA regulations (Reference 20) and are basic items that must be demonstrated in the product application. EU GMP Annex 1 (2022 version) further strengthens the expectations for CCI.

Figure Analysis

Figure 10.2-1🔍 Ishikawa Diagram: This diagram systematically analyzes all the factors that affect CCI across six dimensions:

  • Materials: material properties and compatibility of containers and closures
  • Equipment: design and maintenance of processing equipment such as filling and capping machines
  • Process: the effect of manufacturing steps on the CC system
  • Measurement: CCI test methods and specifications
  • Environment: the effect of storage and transport conditions
  • People: operator training and behavior

Degrazio stresses that a well-established CC/CCI framework reduces repeated studies when expanding to new products, which is of particular commercial importance for a CDMO.

Practical Application

CDMO platform strategy: For a CDMO, building a standardized CC system platform (for example, a standard combination of vial specification + stopper + aluminum seal) can greatly reduce the CCI validation workload when onboarding a new client product. Once the platform has been validated, a new product needs only a gap assessment rather than a complete repeat of all CCI studies.

  2. If a CDMO produces three presentations (vials, prefilled syringes and IV bags), how could it use the Ishikawa diagram to build a shared CCI control framework?
10.1 Primary Container Closures

Original Text

Components attached to the CC system and those frequently used to seal it, ensuring a continuous physical barrier to microorganisms, are frequently polymeric (e.g., vial stoppers, administration ports on IV bags); however, other materials of construction (e.g., metal syringe needles and injector pens) are also used. The potential for microbial and particulate contamination of CC materials should be understood and controlled.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Polymeric Components: In the CC systems of sterile drug products, most of the components critical to the sealing function are polymeric materials (such as elastomeric rubber and plastics), because their elastic deformation allows them to form a tight fit between the container and the closure.

Common primary CC components include:

  • Vial stoppers: made of butyl or halobutyl rubber
  • Administration ports: the injection/infusion ports on IV bags
  • Metal syringe needles: made of stainless steel
  • Injector pens: contain composite metal and plastic components

Analogy

Primary container closures are like the weather seals around the doors and windows of a house. The doors and windows themselves may be metal or wood (rigid materials), but what actually keeps wind and rain out is the rubber seal (the polymeric component). The seal must:

  • be elastic enough to fill irregular gaps
  • be weather-resistant, so it does not age and crack over time
  • not release harmful substances into the room

In the same way, a vial stopper must generate sufficient sealing force (compression force) when it is fitted to the vial neck, and must maintain that seal throughout the shelf life.

Key Notes

The deeper meaning of "understood and controlled": The wording in TR90 is very precise here. For the microbial and particulate contamination potential of CC materials, it is not enough to "control" (controlled) it; it must first be "understood" (understood). This means:

  • The source and level of bioburden in the raw materials must be understood
  • The particulates that may be generated during manufacturing and sterilization must be understood
  • The effect of storage conditions on material properties must be understood
  • Effective control measures can be established only on the basis of this understanding

Practice Questions

  1. Why are sealing components mostly made of polymeric rather than metal materials? Think about the sealing principle from a materials science perspective.
  2. For a CDMO handling several container types (vials, prefilled syringes, IV bags), how should the risk levels be differentiated in incoming raw material control?
10.2 End-to-End Container Closure and Container-Closure Integrity

Original Text

Comprehensive, holistic, and end-to-end control of the CC system and CCI is essential in sterile products because of the critical role they play in the physical prevention of microbial or particulate ingress into the finished DP. The main aspects and controls associated with CC system and CCI fit together cohesively within the overall contamination control architecture (Figure 3.0-1🔍) and are described in this section. CC system integrity testing is normally reserved for DP but may also be warranted for DS or intermediates as a one-time qualification. (20)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

End-to-end control: This concept stresses that CC/CCI control is not the job of any single step. It is lifecycle management that runs from container design, material selection and process development through commercial filling to the end of the product shelf life.

Contamination control architecture: CC/CCI is a key subsystem within the overall CCS (contamination control strategy). Together with environmental monitoring, personnel control, equipment maintenance and other subsystems, it forms a complete contamination defense system (see Figure 3.0-1🔍).

One-time qualification: For drug substance (DS) or intermediates, CCI testing generally does not need to be performed routinely as it is for the finished drug product (DP); a one-time qualification is sufficient.

Analogy

End-to-end CCI control is like waterproofing a building. Waterproofing the roof alone is not enough: from damp-proofing the foundation and waterproofing the walls to sealing the windows and the roof membrane, every link must be sound. If you focus on one link and neglect the others, water will still get in at the weakest point. The same applies to CCI: from material receipt, sterilization, filling and capping to transport and storage, every step can be a weak point.

Key Notes

Differences in the scope of CCI testing:

  • Finished drug product (DP): Routine CCI testing is required, usually performed before batch release or in stability studies
  • Drug substance (DS) or intermediates: Only a one-time qualification is needed, unless the risk assessment shows that more frequent testing is required

This distinction matters because it directly affects the design of the testing strategy and the allocation of resources.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 specifically emphasize the three adjectives "comprehensive, holistic, and end-to-end"? What aspect does each of them stress?
  2. Under what circumstances might the CCI of a DS or intermediate need to go beyond a "one-time qualification" and be tested more frequently?
10.2.1 Design and Development of Container Closure

Original Text

CC system design and development should begin in the development phase of the product and include a justified selection of container components. Early development of the CC system will ensure safety during clinical studies and minimize noncompliance to specifications during the first formal stability studies. Many selection criteria are based upon the chemical considerations and compatibility of the CC system; however, functional integrity preventing microbial ingress should also be considered, and the selection and suitability of a robust CCI test should be started during the product development phase.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Justified selection: The selection of CC components cannot rest on experience or convention alone; it needs a scientifically justified rationale. This includes material compatibility data, functional test results and an assessment of how well the components fit the product characteristics.

Functional integrity: Beyond chemical compatibility, the CC system must also have the physical function of preventing microbial ingress. This aspect is often overlooked but is essential: many teams focus too heavily on chemical extractables and leachables (E&L) testing while neglecting verification of the sealing function.

Analogy

Early development of a CC system is like the architectural design stage of building a house. If the door and window specifications and the waterproofing scheme are settled on the drawings, construction goes smoothly. But if poorly sealed windows are found only after the house is built, tearing out and redoing the work costs more than ten times as much.

Drug development is the same: if a problem with the CC system is found only at the clinical trial stage (for example, a CCI failure in a stability study), you must not only select a new container and repeat the compatibility studies, but may also have to repeat the clinical batches, which is a huge loss of time and money.

Key Notes

The timing of "early development": TR90 explicitly requires CC system development to start in the "development phase" rather than waiting for the commercial stage. Specifically:

  • Preclinical/Phase I: preliminary CC selection and compatibility screening
  • Phase II/III: CC system confirmation and CCI test method development
  • Commercial transfer: final confirmation of CC specifications and CCI method validation

The earlier you start, the better you can avoid regulatory compliance problems and the risk of stability failures later on.

Practical Application

Key considerations for CDMO technology transfer: When a client transfers a product to a CDMO, the complete technical documentation package for the CC system (including the selection rationale, compatibility data and CCI test methods) is a core part of the technology transfer documents. If the client's CC development is incomplete, the CDMO must identify the gaps at receipt and ask for them to be filled; otherwise it may meet major obstacles at the process validation stage.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 specifically emphasize that the selection of CCI test methods and the assessment of their suitability should begin during product development? What are the risks of waiting until the commercial stage?
  2. If a client brings a product to a CDMO with only chemical compatibility data and no functional CCI test data, what action plan would you recommend?
10.2.2 Container-Closure Integrity Test Method Development and Method Validation

Original Text

In designing and developing the CC system and CCI specifications, the dimensional and material requirements, with any acceptable tolerance, are generated for all container components. Documented assessments of microbial ingress risks and any failure mode-associated microbial risks should be completed to ensure the establishment of appropriate processes and procedures for the preparation, handling, processing, and shipping.

Prior historical experience, knowledge, and proven performance should be leveraged to determine and develop the CC system, CCI, and CCIT. Providing a rationale that supports the choice of CC system, CCI, and CCIT design control decisions to the eventual commercial manufacturing facility is imperative as these data will assist in resolution of any potential noncompliance and in any change controls.

Although CCI is most important for the prevention of microbial ingress, the CC system should be adequate to prevent water vapor transmission from raising the water activity of the product to levels that may support microbial proliferation in low-bioburden products and affect the quality of lyophilized products (23).

PDA Technical Report No. 27: Pharmaceutical Package Integrity and USP General Chapter <1207.2> Package Integrity Leak Test Technologies provide a thorough comparison of various test methods, including the advantages and limitations of different testing techniques. In addition, the CCIT method should be validated in the equipment that will be used during routine testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

CCI specifications: These cover the dimensional requirements and material requirements for all container components, together with their acceptable tolerances. These specifications are the foundation documents of CCI control.

CCIT (Container Closure Integrity Test): Test methods used to verify the sealing function of the CC system. Common methods include:

  • Vacuum decay
  • High voltage leak detection (HVLD)
  • Helium leak detection
  • Microbial challenge / immersion
  • Dye ingress

Water activity: The amount of free water in the product that is available to microorganisms. If the CC system cannot block water vapor transmission, the water activity of the product may rise to a level that supports microbial growth; the effect is most significant for lyophilized products.

Analogy

Selecting and validating a CCIT method is like choosing a way to detect leaks in a house. You could use:

  • Visual inspection (similar to dye ingress): simple, but may miss tiny cracks
  • An infrared thermal camera (similar to vacuum decay): more sensitive, and can detect problems invisible to the naked eye
  • A pressurized water test (similar to microbial challenge): closest to real conditions, but more time-consuming

The key point is that the detection tool you choose must have been calibrated and validated for your own building materials and structure; you cannot simply apply someone else's data. Likewise, a CCIT method must be validated on the equipment actually used.

Key Notes

Special risks for lyophilized products: Water vapor transmission is a critical risk factor for lyophilized products. A lyophilized cake has very low moisture content, and if the CC system lets water vapor slowly seep in, the result can be:

  • moisture uptake and collapse of the lyophilized cake (cake collapse)
  • increased water activity, which may support microbial proliferation
  • degraded product stability and a shortened shelf life

For lyophilized products, therefore, CCI must prevent not only microorganisms but also water vapor, which places stricter demands on the sealing specifications.

Regulatory References

This section cites two important reference documents:

  • PDA TR No. 27: Pharmaceutical Package Integrity, which provides a comprehensive comparison of the various CCIT methods
  • USP <1207.2>: Package Integrity Leak Test Technologies, which analyzes the advantages and disadvantages of the different test technologies

These are the authoritative reference guides for CCIT method development and validation, and practitioners should be familiar with their content.

Practical Application

A preventive mindset toward change control: TR90 stresses that the rationale for CC/CCI/CCIT design decisions must be documented and that this data must be passed on to the final commercial manufacturing site. This matters especially in a CDMO setting: when a client needs to change container suppliers or modify sealing parameters, this original design rationale is the basis for the change assessment. Without a complete design rationale, any change can turn into a regulatory nightmare.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does the CC system of a lyophilized product need to prevent not only microbial ingress but also water vapor transmission? What additional requirements does this place on the CCI specifications?
  2. If you had to select a CCIT method for a new product, what factors would you consider in deciding between a deterministic method (such as vacuum decay) and a probabilistic method (such as microbial challenge)?
  3. Why does TR90 emphasize that a CCIT method must be validated on the "equipment actually used" rather than relying solely on validation data provided by the supplier?
10.2.3 Container Closure Integrity During Process Validation

Original Text

Functional CCI should be demonstrated throughout the shelf life of the finished product and should consider how the manufacturing process may impact the container, the closure, or the container-closure combination. The process should be designed to accommodate normal dimensional variances. The impact of the different elements of the manufacturing process should be considered, including the elements outlined in Table 10.2.3-1🔍, and CCI should be demonstrated at worst-case manufacturing operations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Functional CCI throughout shelf life: CCI is not a one-time confirmation; it must be maintained throughout the entire shelf life of the product. This means the stability study protocol should include CCI testing time points.

Dimensional variances: Both containers and closures have manufacturing tolerances. The process must be designed to ensure a good seal across the normal tolerance range, including the combinations of maximum and minimum dimensions.

Worst-case manufacturing operations: CCI validation must be performed under the most challenging process conditions, for example:

  • extreme tolerance combinations of container and stopper dimensions
  • maximum and minimum capping force
  • maximum filling speed
  • extreme storage scenarios (such as temperature and vibration during transport)

Analogy

Worst-case validation is like a car crash test. Automobile manufacturers cannot test safety only under "ideal conditions"; they must verify that the airbag deploys properly at the harshest crash angles and the highest test speeds.

CCI validation works the same way: you cannot test only with "perfect" containers and stoppers. You must identify the extreme combinations of dimensional tolerance (for example, the largest vial neck + the smallest stopper) and show that the seal remains intact under the most demanding process conditions. If it passes in the worst case, normal conditions are even less of a concern.

Key Notes

Potential effects of the process on CCI: TR90 mentions that Table 10.2.3-1🔍 lists the process elements to be considered. Although the table content is not detailed in this section, typical process factors that affect CCI include:

  • Filling parameters: fill volume, filling speed, needle position
  • Capping parameters: capping force, capping speed, centering accuracy
  • Sterilization conditions: the effect of autoclave temperature and pressure on containers and stoppers
  • Lyophilization process: vacuum level and the mechanical effects during stoppering
  • Transport conditions: the effect of vibration and temperature changes on sealed products

Each of these factors can lead to a CCI failure under extreme combinations of normal manufacturing tolerances.

Practical Application

CCI strategy in process validation: In process validation (PV) batches, CCI is usually one of the critical release tests. The CDMO should clearly define the following in the PV protocol:

  • The sampling plan for CCI testing (including worst-case samples)
  • The acceptance criteria
  • Consistency with the routine CCIT method
  • How to demonstrate that the extreme combinations of dimensional tolerance also meet the sealing specification

Practice Questions

  1. Why must CCI be validated under "worst-case manufacturing operations"? What are the potential risks if validation passes only under standard conditions?
  2. When drawing up the stability study protocol, at which time points would you schedule CCI testing, and why?
  3. If CCI fails at an extreme dimensional-tolerance combination during process validation, what would you do? Tighten the container specification or adjust the process parameters?

  • The dual role of the CC system: A container closure system must be both "inert" (not reacting with the drug product) and "protective" (guarding against external factors such as microorganisms, particulates and water vapor)
  • Primary vs secondary packaging: The primary CC is the main barrier in direct contact with the product, while the secondary CC provides physical protection during transport and handling
  • Ishikawa diagram methodology: Systematically analyzes the factors affecting CCI across six dimensions: materials, equipment, process, measurement, environment and people
  • The importance of early development: CC system design and CCIT method selection should start during product development to avoid regulatory compliance problems later on
  • CCIT method validation: Must be performed on the equipment actually used, with reference to the guidance in PDA TR27 and USP <1207.2>
  • Special considerations for lyophilized products: The CC system must prevent not only microbial ingress but also water vapor transmission, which would raise water activity
  • Worst-case validation: CCI must be validated at the extreme combinations of dimensional tolerance and under the most demanding process conditions
  • End-to-end control: CCI is lifecycle management from design and development to the end of shelf life, not the job of any single step

11.0 Quality Systems

Original Text

This section addresses how quality systems provide the framework that drives proper function of all aspects of the CCS, including trending and metrics, investigations, corrective and preventive actions (CAPA), change control, QRM, quality agreements, and governance. ICH Q10 provides further details and implementation strategies.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Systems: The quality system is the "backbone" on which the whole CCS operates. It is not a single document or procedure, but a set of interlinked management mechanisms that includes trending, investigation, CAPA, change control, quality risk management (QRM), quality agreements and governance.

ICH Q10 (Pharmaceutical Quality System): ICH Q10 is the pharmaceutical quality system guideline issued by the International Council for Harmonisation. It gives pharmaceutical companies an integrated quality management model that covers the whole product lifecycle. TR90 specifically notes that ICH Q10 provides further detail and implementation strategies.

Analogy

A quality system is like a hospital infection control plan. A hospital cannot prevent hospital-acquired infections with a single rule; it needs a complete system: monitoring data (trending), investigation of abnormal events, improvement actions (CAPA), management of process changes, risk assessment, quality agreements with outside suppliers, and so on. All of these are interlocked, and none can be left out. The quality system in the CCS plays exactly this role.

Key Notes

This section is the key to understanding how the CCS "comes alive". The earlier sections discuss the technical elements of contamination control (facilities, personnel, process, environmental monitoring and so on), while the quality system in this section is the management engine that keeps those elements improving and coordinated. Without a sound quality system, even the best technical controls will degrade over time.

11.1 Trending and Metrics

Original Text

Metrics, including key performance indicators (KPIs), permit firms to respond to signals from the CCS. Metrics should effectively assess the holistic performance of the CCS and include both predictive and reactive measurements. Each metric should be assessed during the periodic review; Table 11.1-1🔍 presents some examples.

The goal of trending is to permit a holistic analysis of control information over a period of time or across different circumstances. Such analysis facilitates identification of patterns, potential influences, and genuine root causes of nonconformances.

Metrics, KPIs, and data relevant to routine monitoring should be categorized and trended to monitor the holistic performance of CCS, for example:

  • Root causes of nonconformances (e.g., control designs, training, knowledge, communication)
  • Control charting of both variable data (e.g., numerical quantities and composition of microbial flora recovered in lower classification cleanroom) and attribute data (e.g., presence or absence of colonies recovered in Grade-A location monitoring)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Predictive Metrics: These are "forward-looking" indicators used to assess the health of the system and to give a signal before problems occur. Examples include quality culture KPIs, capital reinvestment plans, progress of risk mitigation, on-time CAPA completion rate, and adherence to the validation and qualification schedule.

Reactive Metrics: These are "after-the-fact" indicators that reflect problems that have already occurred. Examples include facility deviations, process nonconformances, product nonconformances (such as a sterility test failure), environmental monitoring trend excursions, and unplanned maintenance.

Holistic Analysis: The aim of trending is not to review single events one by one, but to identify patterns, potential contributing factors and true root causes across time and across different situations.

Analogy

Imagine you manage a baseball team. Predictive metrics are like "training attendance", "fitness test scores" and "hours of fielding practice": they tell you whether preparation for future games is on track. Reactive metrics are like "errors", "hits allowed" and "runs conceded": they are results that have already happened. A good coach (quality manager) looks at both kinds of metrics rather than waiting to react until the team has lost.

Key Notes

Table 11.1-1🔍 is an important reference for audit preparation. Regulatory inspectors often ask: "What are your CCS performance indicators, and how do you track them?" If you have only reactive metrics (number of deviations, number of failures), the inspector will question whether your system lacks a forward-looking view. A mature CCS must have both.

Pay particular attention to the predictive metric "quality culture KPIs". EU GMP Annex 1 strongly emphasizes the importance of quality culture, and TR90 brings it into a quantifiable metrics framework.

The left column (predictive) focuses on system inputs and preventive measures, and the right column (reactive) focuses on actual outcomes and deviations. Only a cross-analysis of the two gives a complete picture of CCS health.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, we recommend building a CCS dashboard that presents predictive and reactive metrics visually. For example, track monthly: CAPA closure rate, environmental monitoring excursion trends, validation schedule adherence rate, and number of unplanned maintenance events. These data can be used directly for management review and for responses to customer audits.

11.2 Investigation

Original Text

Investigations related to CCS may arise from multiple sources for e.g. product complaints, product or process testing, process validation, cleaning validation, technical studies, environmental or utility monitoring excursions, data trending, or other deviations. Investigation is one feedback loop of the CCS that ensures continuous improvement and refinement of the CCS control elements (see Figure 3.0-1🔍).

Investigations are used to identify sources of microbial contamination within the facility or detect a breakdown of the overall CCS and, as a result, they should be conducted in a holistic fashion, without a predetermined investigational outcome, even when perceived as one-off events. Where possible, investigation strategies should be standardized in a written plan to streamline the process and hasten a resolution (e.g., EM investigations).

Microbial risk assessments are an important component of the CCS and the investigation process. These should be used to understand potential weak points in the CCS and identify the root cause. The microbial risk assessment is a proactive measure used to support process understanding, especially during the investigational process. PDA Technical Report No. 88: Microbial Data Deviation Investigations in the Pharmaceutical Industry describes a detailed process for conducting microbial deviation investigations and the parameters surrounding them.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Feedback Loop: Investigation is part of the CCS feedback loop. When monitoring data, deviations or complaints trigger an investigation, the findings feed back into the CCS and drive improvement and refinement of the control elements. This is the continual improvement cycle depicted in Figure 3.0-1🔍.

Holistic Fashion: An investigation should not focus only on surface symptoms. Even an apparent one-off event must be analyzed from the perspective of the overall CCS, avoiding a predetermined investigational outcome.

Microbial Risk Assessment: This is a proactive measure used to understand potential weaknesses of the CCS and to support process understanding. It is used not only in deviation investigations but should also be performed regularly as part of routine maintenance.

Analogy

An investigation is like a doctor's diagnostic process. When a patient presents with symptoms (a deviation), a good doctor does not jump to a conclusion but examines the patient comprehensively (holistic fashion), rules out the various possibilities and finds the real cause (root cause). If a doctor simply prescribes a painkiller for every headache (treating the surface) without checking for high blood pressure or a brain problem (systemic causes), the patient's health will only get worse. CCS investigations work the same way.

Key Notes

"Without a predetermined investigational outcome" is one of the deficiencies most often cited in audits. A common failure pattern is that, after an environmental monitoring excursion, the investigation report always concludes "probably an operator handling issue" without genuinely exploring other possible root causes such as facility, equipment, materials or system design.

TR90 specifically cites PDA TR88 as detailed guidance on microbial deviation investigations, which shows how the PDA technical reports complement one another. In practice, we recommend referring to TR88 as well when building a standardized investigation process.

Practical Application

For a CDMO, we recommend developing a standardized EM investigation template that covers: event description, impact scope assessment, a list of possible root causes (facility, personnel, equipment, materials, methods), microbial identification results, CCS weakness analysis, and CAPA recommendations. This speeds up the investigation process while ensuring completeness and consistency.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 emphasize that investigations should be conducted in a "holistic fashion" rather than only for a single deviation event?
  2. What is a "predetermined investigational outcome"? Give a practical example of why this is inappropriate practice.
  3. What role should microbial risk assessment play in a deviation investigation? How does it relate in sequence to the deviation investigation?
11.3 Corrective And Preventive Actions

Original Text

CAPAs are one of the feedback loops that enable ongoing improvement and refinement of the CCS based on the information gained during investigations and routine monitoring. Figure 3.0-1🔍 provides a visual illustration of this concept.

When determining the appropriate CAPAs, the entire CCS should be considered, including the elements that will be directly affected by the CAPA and all other CCS elements that may be indirectly affected. CAPA effectiveness checks should be built into the majority of CAPAs and tracked as a quality metric. The most effective CAPAs will likely enhance multiple controls in the CCS across the facility and a range of processes and products. Effective CAPAs that truly strengthen the CCS and prevent contamination may also lead to a reduction in testing and oversight, for example, closing a process step. Incorporating any new knowledge and learning derived from the CAPA back into the CCS is an important step, one that can be taken during the periodic lifecycle review described in Section 12.0.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Actions): CAPA is one of the most central improvement tools in the quality system. A corrective action addresses a problem that has already occurred, while a preventive action prevents similar problems from recurring. Within the CCS framework, CAPA is the bridge that turns investigation findings into actual improvement.

CAPA Effectiveness Checks: Implementing a CAPA is not the end of the matter. Every CAPA should have a follow-up effectiveness check mechanism to verify that the action actually solved the problem. This should be tracked as one of the quality metrics.

Reduction in Testing: This is a very important concept. When a CAPA genuinely strengthens the CCS and prevents contamination, a company can reasonably reduce certain testing and oversight, for example by closing out a process step. This reflects a risk-based management philosophy.

Analogy

CAPA is like the acceptance inspection after a house repair. If your house leaks (a deviation), you find the cause of the leak (investigation) and make the repair (CAPA), but you never check whether it really stopped leaking (effectiveness check), then the next heavy rain may cause a leak again. And if you not only fixed the leak point but also strengthened the waterproofing of the whole roof (strengthening the overall CCS), you may even be able to reduce the frequency of future inspections (reduction in testing).

Key Notes

TR90 emphasizes that CAPA should consider the "whole CCS", including both directly and indirectly affected elements. This means a good CAPA not only solves the immediate problem but also considers its knock-on effects at the system level. For example, if a CAPA changes a cleaning procedure, the impact on other CCS elements such as environmental monitoring, personnel training and validation status should be assessed at the same time.

The step of "feeding new knowledge from CAPA back into the CCS" is often overlooked. TR90 recommends completing this work in the periodic lifecycle review described in Section 12.0, so that the CCS remains a continually evolving living document.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does TR90 say "the most effective CAPAs may enhance multiple controls across facilities, multiple processes and multiple products"? Explain with a practical example.
  2. How can a CAPA lead to a "reduction in testing and oversight"? What preconditions are needed for this to be implemented reasonably?
  3. If the effectiveness check of a CAPA fails, what should the next step be?
11.4 Change Control

Original Text

There is an inherent risk of contamination when changes are made to any part of the CCS. Any change to product, process, or facility that may impact the level of risk related to CCS contamination controls warrants an ad-hoc risk review. When a change is made to product, process, or facility, the impact of the contamination should be evaluated by SMEs on the system being changed, contamination control, and quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Change Control: Any change to a product, process or facility can alter the risk profile of the CCS. The change control procedure ensures that the potential impact on contamination control is fully assessed before the change is implemented.

Ad-hoc Risk Review: Unlike periodic risk assessments, an ad-hoc risk review is an additional risk review triggered by a specific change. It ensures that every change that may affect the CCS undergoes an appropriate risk assessment.

SMEs (Subject Matter Experts): The contamination impact assessment of a change should not be done by a single department. It requires the participation of multiple experts: experts on the system being changed, contamination control experts and quality experts.

Analogy

Change control is like a structural assessment during a home renovation. If you want to cut a window into a wall (a process change), even though it affects only one wall, you must have a structural engineer assess whether it will affect the load-bearing structure of the whole house (the CCS). You cannot skip the assessment because it is "just a small change", since sometimes an apparently small change can compromise the integrity of the entire system.

Key Notes

TR90 uses the term "inherent risk" to describe the contamination risk introduced by a change, which highlights an important principle: change itself is a risk. This does not mean a change will necessarily cause a problem, but that every change inherently carries the risk of upsetting the balance of existing controls. Therefore, even if the intent of a change is improvement, its potential negative effects must be assessed.

In an audit, regulators will pay particular attention to the following. Have all changes affecting the CCS undergone an appropriate risk assessment? Do the change control records have a dedicated field for assessing the impact on contamination control?

Practice Questions

  1. List three types of change that appear unrelated to contamination control but could actually affect the CCS.
  2. Why does TR90 require the contamination impact assessment of a change to be done jointly by multiple SMEs? What is the risk of an assessment by a single department?
11.5 Quality Risk Management

Original Text

When initially developing a CCS, the organization should review the existing risk assessments that relate to all contamination control elements to understand what risks have been identified, how those risks are currently being reduced, and any mitigations that have been planned. Upon completion of this review, the firm should create a plan for performing risk assessments on the topics with the least amount of risk knowledge and the greatest potential of contamination risk based on the manufacturing processes. The plan should be realistic, consider that risk assessments require a team approach and, as living documents, understand that they will require periodic reevaluation. This process is described in ICH Q9.

When performing new risk assessments, additional contamination risks will be identified along with mitigation plans that enhance the CCS. This creates a feedback loop where the CCS identifies the areas that need risk assessment, and the existing and new contamination risk assessments inform the CCS. Figure 3.0-1🔍 shows QRM as one of the foundational elements, suggesting that the QRM element of CCS is a single document. In practice, QRM is a culmination of multiple, focused contamination-control risk assessments that address different product lifecycle phases, processes, and levels of risk. No single risk assessment tool is appropriate for all risk questions; a combination of appropriate risk tools should be used to achieve an effective QRM. As an example, for the manufacturing process, a design FMEA would be the appropriate tool to identify the best process for preventing contamination and other failures; whereas a HACCP is appropriate to determine whether the surrounding control strategies are sufficient and to identify the in-process sample locations that will monitor the effectiveness of the design and controls. When changes are made, a less formal risk tool may be used as part of the change control process to assess whether the change will introduce new risks.

Other risk assessment tools (e.g., the Preliminary Hazard Analysis outlined in ICH Q9) can be employed as appropriate (27).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Risk Management, QRM: QRM is one of the foundational elements of the CCS. It is not a single document but the sum of multiple risk assessments focused on different aspects of contamination control. ICH Q9 provides the methodological framework for QRM.

Living Documents: A risk assessment is not a one-time exercise that is "completed and filed away". Risk assessments are living documents that need to be reassessed periodically and kept up to date as new knowledge, new data and new changes emerge.

Design FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis): Used at the process design stage to identify potential failure modes and their effects on product quality; it is one of the best tools for preventing contamination.

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points): Originating in the food safety field, it is used in pharmaceutical manufacturing to determine whether the perimeter control strategy is sufficient and to identify sampling locations in the process for monitoring the effectiveness of the design and controls.

Analogy

QRM is like a city's disaster prevention plan. A city cannot use a single method to protect against every disaster: earthquakes require building codes (similar to FMEA), floods require drainage systems and monitoring stations (similar to HACCP), and fires require fire regulations and sprinkler systems (similar to Preliminary Hazard Analysis). QRM in the CCS works the same way: different risk assessment tools are used for different aspects of contamination risk, and together they form a complete risk management system.

Key Notes

TR90 states clearly: "no single risk assessment tool is applicable to all risk questions". This is a common pitfall in practice: some companies use only one FMEA template for every situation. The correct approach is to select the most suitable tool according to the nature of the risk question.

  • Process FMEA is the most commonly used tool and applies to almost all elements
  • Monitoring HACCP is particularly suited to environment, materials and utilities, which are areas that require ongoing monitoring
  • For "novel or high-risk" items (such as new single-use consumables or new equipment), an additional dedicated FMEA is needed

Practical Application

For a CDMO, QRM is more complex because the same facility may manufacture products for many different clients. The recommended approach:

  • Establish a "CCS Risk Assessment Registry" that records all completed and pending risk assessments
  • Following the framework in Table 11.5-1🔍, complete a foundational risk assessment for each contamination control element one by one
  • Set a reassessment interval for each risk assessment (for example every 2-3 years or upon a major change)
  • Include the execution status of risk assessments in the predictive KPIs for tracking

11.6 Quality Agreements and Vendor Management

Original Text

To ensure that a firm's contamination risks and controls from vendor-supplied materials and services are actively managed, quality agreements should be in place between the firm and the vendor. Quality agreements and the associated vendor management programs are critically important because a firm relies on a consistent supply of materials and on dependable, compliant services. The vendor's CCS practices should be evaluated as part of the vendor qualification.

Once qualified and approved, vendors should be audited on a frequency commensurate with the criticality of the supplied materials or service. Vendor audits represent the means of monitoring within the contamination control architecture. Routine vendor audits should include those elements of their CCS that are fundamentally important to the material or service supplied.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Agreements: A formal agreement between a pharmaceutical company and a supplier that defines each party's responsibilities and obligations for quality management. Within the CCS framework, quality agreements should specifically include clauses related to contamination control.

Vendor Qualification: When selecting suppliers, their CCS practices should be evaluated. This goes beyond looking at the supplier's GMP certificate; it means understanding the supplier's specific practices and capabilities in contamination control.

Commensurate with Criticality: Audit frequency should be proportional to the criticality of the material or service supplied. A supplier of materials that come into direct contact with the product should be audited more frequently than a supplier of non-critical services.

Analogy

Vendor management is like a restaurant's control over its ingredient suppliers. A high-end restaurant does not simply buy ingredients at the market; it signs quality contracts with reliable suppliers (quality agreements), visits the supplier's farm or warehouse regularly (audits), and confirms the source of the ingredients and how quality is controlled. Seafood suppliers (high criticality) are audited more often than seasoning suppliers (low criticality), because the quality risk of seafood is greater.

Key Notes

TR90 specifically mentions that the supplier's "CCS practices" should be evaluated. This means not just checking whether the supplier holds GMP certification, but understanding in depth how it controls contamination. For example: do the supplier's raw materials/components have appropriate microbial controls? Does its production environment have an environmental monitoring program? Does it have change control and deviation handling procedures?

This is especially important for a CDMO: the CDMO's clients (sponsors) are also one of its "suppliers", because the drug substance (DS), formulation information and process parameters that the client provides all affect the CDMO's CCS. Therefore, the quality agreement with the client also needs to cover contamination control clauses.

  2. How should the frequency of supplier audits be determined? How is "commensurate with criticality" operationalized in practice?
  3. In the CDMO model, which contamination control clauses in the quality agreement between client and CDMO are most easily overlooked?
11.7 Quality Controls

Original Text

All quality control laboratories that generate data to measure or monitor contamination control elements should be governed by GMPs. The potential for erroneous results should be minimized and thoroughly investigated as part of any contamination deviation (see the Governance and Oversight section of PDA Technical Report No. 88: Microbial Data Deviation Investigations in the Pharmaceutical Industry).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Control Laboratories: All laboratories that generate CCS-related monitoring data (such as microbiology and physicochemical laboratories) must operate under GMP control. This ensures the reliability and integrity of the data.

Erroneous Results: The potential for a laboratory to produce erroneous results should be minimized. When an erroneous result occurs, it should be thoroughly investigated as part of the contamination deviation. This covers both laboratory contamination (false positive) and method failure (false negative).

Analogy

A quality control laboratory is like a forensic laboratory in a court case. If the forensic laboratory itself does not operate properly (does not follow GMP), the evidence it produces (monitoring data) has no credibility. Even if the detective (process engineer) and the prosecutor (quality assurance) have done excellent work, one unreliable forensic report is enough to destroy the credibility of the whole case (the CCS).

Key Notes

This is a very short but extremely important provision. TR90 again cites PDA TR88 as reference guidance for investigating microbial data deviations. In practice, the GMP compliance of the QC laboratory directly affects the credibility of all CCS monitoring data. If the QC laboratory's environmental monitoring, sterility test or endotoxin test results are unreliable, the basis for every CCS decision is shaken.

Key point: "GMP governance" of the laboratory does not stop at having SOPs. It also includes personnel training and qualification, equipment calibration and maintenance, culture media quality management, control of environmental conditions, and assurance of data integrity.

Practical Application

We recommend that a CDMO's microbiology laboratory establish the following key controls:

  • Perform regular environmental monitoring of the laboratory itself to ensure that the laboratory is not a source of contamination
  • Establish a standardized "erroneous result investigation" process that covers both false positive and false negative scenarios
  • Include the laboratory deviation rate in the CCS reactive metrics for tracking
  • Ensure that laboratory personnel understand how the data they generate affect overall CCS decisions

Practice Questions

  1. Why are erroneous results from the QC laboratory treated as part of a CCS deviation? How does this differ from ordinary laboratory deviation handling?
  2. What effects do false positives and false negatives each have on CCS decisions? Which risk is greater, and why?

12.0 CCS Governance and Effectiveness Review

Original Text

The CCS should include a governance structure to oversee the effectiveness of the contamination controls and to escalate control issues. The person(s) or body governing the CCS should have:

  • Appropriate microbiology and process expertise to understand the meaningfulness of the data outputs, for example, quality control in-process and product-release testing, EM, and utility monitoring
  • Clear responsibility to perform regular assessments of the contamination controls related to process, product, personnel, and facility/utility and to drive proactive improvement
  • Authority to respond to potentially adverse trends or events, both proactively and reactively
  • Clear pathways for escalation to the top site management

The CCS lifecycle begins during the design of the facility and process. Further, the CCS should be considered during quality-by-design activities and formally documented as a prerequisite to GMP manufacturing. Thereafter, the quality systems as outlined in Section 11.0 drive refinement and continuous improvement in the CCS over the life of a product.

The CCS should also be reviewed periodically (preferably reviewed annually) for effectiveness to ensure it remains current with the process and aligned with industry standards, specifically the potential need to adopt new, more effective technologies. The periodic review should be done by a multi-departmental team to monitor the effectiveness of contamination controls related to the process, product, personnel, and facility/utilities, including, but not limited to, evaluating quality trends, contamination events, change control, and validation activities. A practical way to achieve this and reduce the administrative burden is to formally monitor these elements throughout the year in regular meetings of the multi-departmental team that will be involved in the periodic review.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

CCS Governance Structure: This is a formal organizational structure and allocation of responsibilities used to oversee the operation and effectiveness of the entire Contamination Control Strategy (CCS). A governance structure is more than a committee; it is a complete framework covering "who is responsible", "who has authority to decide" and "how issues are escalated".

CCS Lifecycle: The CCS is not a static document. It begins to take shape at the facility and process design stage, is formally taken into account in Quality by Design (QbD) activities, and is formally documented before GMP manufacturing begins. It is then continually refined through the quality system (such as CAPA, change control and annual review) across the entire commercial lifecycle of the product.

Multi-departmental Team: The effectiveness review of the CCS cannot be carried out by the quality unit alone. It requires the joint participation of Quality Assurance (QA), Quality Control (QC), Manufacturing, Engineering, Microbiology and other departments, because contamination control touches every aspect of process, personnel, facility and utilities.

Analogy

Think of CCS governance as being like a hospital Infection Control Committee. Such a committee does not meet only when a hospital-acquired infection occurs; it has a standing meeting mechanism and regularly reviews infection data trends, environmental cleaning audit results, hand hygiene compliance rates and so on. Its members include infectious disease physicians, head nurses, the head of the cleaning department, pharmacy and other cross-functional staff. When an adverse trend appears in any indicator, the committee has the authority to demand improvement directly and, when necessary, to escalate to the hospital director. The CCS governance structure follows exactly the same design logic: routine monitoring, cross-functional collaboration and a clear escalation path.

Key Notes

Explicit requirements of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022): Annex 1 raises CCS governance and periodic review to a regulatory requirement. During site inspections, auditors will look at:

  • Is there a designated owner or committee for the CCS?
  • Does that owner/committee have sufficient microbiology and process expertise?
  • Are there records of regular (ideally annual) effectiveness reviews?
  • Does the review cover quality trends, contamination events, change control and validation activities?
  • Is there a clear mechanism for escalating issues to senior management?

The lack of a formal governance structure is a common audit finding. Many companies have a CCS document but lack a clear definition of "who manages it" and "how it is managed".

Practical Application

Special challenges for CDMOs: For a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization), CCS governance is more complex, because the same facility may manufacture different products for several clients at the same time. A CDMO needs to consider:

  • Generic CCS vs product-specific CCS: Facility-level contamination controls (such as environmental monitoring, personnel training, and cleaning and disinfection procedures) usually have one generic CCS; but for specific products (such as a cell therapy product vs a small-molecule injectable), additional product-specific supplementary documents may be needed.
  • Client involvement: Some clients may ask to take part in CCS reviews, particularly for high-risk or high-value products. The governance structure needs to provide a mechanism for client interaction.
  • Year-round segmented monitoring: The "year-round segmented monitoring" approach recommended in TR90 is especially practical for CDMOs. Rather than waiting until year-end for one large review, indicators are tracked continuously in monthly or quarterly cross-functional meetings, so the annual review only needs to consolidate them.

Key Concepts

Three-layer literature structure: The reference system in TR90 can be understood as three concentric layers:

  • Outermost layer -- Regulatory Guidance: the ICH Q series, EU GMP Annex 1, FDA guidance, etc. These are the "must comply" regulatory expectations and define the compliance baseline for the CCS.
  • Middle layer -- Industry Standards: ISO standards, USP general chapters, etc. These provide concrete technical methods and acceptance criteria, and are the bridge that turns regulatory requirements into workable procedures.
  • Innermost layer -- Technical Guidance: PDA technical reports and Points to Consider. These reflect industry expert consensus and give the most detailed "how-to" guidance to help companies actually establish and maintain a CCS.

Analogy

Imagine you are an architect designing a building that must meet regulations. The ICH Q series and EU GMP Annex 1 are like the "building law", telling you which standards must be met (such as seismic coefficients and fire codes). ISO standards and USP general chapters are like the "building material specifications" and "construction standards", telling you how strong the concrete must be and how far apart the rebar must be spaced. PDA technical reports are like the "architect's handbook" and "collection of project case studies", sharing the design experience and best practices of other architects on similar projects. You need all three, but they are used at different times: regulations tell you what you "must achieve", standards tell you "how to measure", and technical reports tell you "how to do it".

Key Notes: Role of the ICH Q Series in the CCS

TR90 lists 6 ICH guidelines, which form the quality management backbone of the CCS:

  • Q5A(R1) -- Viral safety: For the CCS of a biologic, viral clearance/inactivation is one of the core contamination controls.

  • Q7 -- GMP for APIs: GMP requirements for active pharmaceutical ingredients; the CCS needs to control contamination starting from the raw material end.
  • Q8(R2) -- Pharmaceutical development: The theoretical basis of Quality by Design (QbD); the CCS should be designed in already at the product development stage.
  • Q9 -- Quality risk management: The methodological core of all CCS risk assessments.
  • Q10 -- Pharmaceutical quality system: The quality system framework within which the CCS operates, covering CAPA, change control, management review, etc.

Practical Application

  • When troubleshooting: When you meet a specific technical problem (such as a sterilization validation failure, an adverse environmental monitoring trend or a container closure integrity issue), go directly to the PDA technical report on that topic; it will usually offer ideas for troubleshooting and solutions.
  • When driving continual improvement: Periodically check whether new versions of regulations or technical reports have been published. For example, ICH Q9(R1) was issued in 2023 and EU GMP Annex 1 formally came into effect in August 2023 -- the CCS should be updated accordingly.

Table 15.0-1🔍: Structure of the Manufacturing Areas🔍

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Closed Process: A process in which a complete physical barrier separates the product from the external environment. Materials enter and leave only through closed connectors, and personnel have no direct contact with the product or product-contact surfaces. This is the most powerful engineering measure for contamination control.

Open Process: A process in which the product is exposed to the surrounding environment and must be protected by multiple barriers (gowning, aseptic technique training, airflow control, etc.). The contamination risk of an open process is significantly higher than that of a closed process.

Single-Use Equipment: Equipment that is discarded after use, which eliminates the risk of cross-contamination caused by inadequate cleaning. Its integrity can be verified by a microbial ingress test, with a detection sensitivity of 1-3 μm.

Analogy

Think of a closed process as a space station: all supplies come in through sealed hatches, the inside is completely isolated from the outside, and the astronauts need not worry about the vacuum beyond. An open process is like preparing food at an open-air market: you must rely on multiple protections such as tents, windshields, gloves, and masks to reduce dust and contamination.

Single-use equipment is like disposable tableware: use it once and throw it away, with no need to worry whether the dishes were washed properly. Reusable equipment is like restaurant china: economical, but every wash must meet hygiene standards.

Key Points

The key message of this table is: closed process + single-use equipment = the ideal end-to-end contamination control design.

A special note for biological products: for the handling of seed stocks and cell stocks, and for the performance of sterility tests, the report explicitly states that isolator technology is the preferred equipment. This reflects the strict requirements of EU GMP Annex 1 for the sterility testing environment.

A key weakness of reusable equipment: system integrity cannot be conveniently verified after assembly. A pressure-hold test is a commonly used method, but it cannot confirm that the system is completely sealed.

Practical Application

When designing a CDMO facility, give priority to using closed systems with single-use equipment all the way from the downstream purification steps of the drug substance (DS) through to fill and finish of the drug product (DP). This not only lowers contamination risk but also greatly simplifies the cleaning validation burden and shortens batch changeover time.

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Multiuse Facility: A facility in which multiple products are manufactured. Special attention must be paid to cross-contamination prevention, particularly the control of adventitious agents and viruses in biological products.

Adulterated: When product quality is compromised by the introduction of unintended impurities, the product is considered "adulterated" regardless of the source of contamination, which is a serious violation from a regulatory standpoint.

A multiuse facility is like a central kitchen serving many kinds of dishes: however large the kitchen is, if work areas are not strictly separated, seafood allergens could contaminate vegetarian dishes. A single-product facility is like a shop that makes only one dessert: the cross-contamination risk is naturally lower, but quality control between batches still needs attention.

The report emphasizes: whether the facility is multiuse or single-product, the basic requirements for contamination prevention are the same. Vigilance must not be relaxed just because a facility makes a single product. Impurities inadvertently introduced from upstream unit operations are unacceptable under any circumstances.

For multiuse facilities handling biological products, segregation of areas is the key measure for ensuring control of adventitious agents and viruses in downstream materials.

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Sterile Hold Time: The maximum time that equipment may be stored after sterilization before use. Beyond this time, the equipment must be re-sterilized. This time must be established through validation.

Microbial Proliferation: When storage conditions (temperature, humidity, nutrients) support microbial growth, even a very low initial bioburden may multiply substantially during storage.

Analogy

Managing the storage of materials is like managing a household refrigerator: food must be sealed, the temperature controlled, and the contents checked regularly, or bacteria will multiply and the food will spoil. Key Points

Sampling and dispensing areas "should not increase the bioburden of raw materials". This seemingly simple requirement in practice calls for strict environmental control and operating procedures. Many contamination events occur precisely during sampling or dispensing.

Storage of cleaned equipment also requires validation: confirm that bioburden and endotoxin levels do not increase during storage. This usually involves periodic monitoring and a defined maximum storage time.

Table 15.0-4🔍: Path of Materials, Equipment, or Waste🔍

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Unidirectional Flow: Materials, personnel, and waste all move in a single direction, so that the paths of clean items and contaminated items never cross. This is a basic principle of facility design.

Sporicide: A disinfectant capable of killing bacterial spores. General disinfectants may not kill spores effectively, so a sporicide must be used wherever a high level of disinfection is required.

Pass-through Autoclave: A sterilizer installed in the wall between a clean area and an unclassified area. Waste is loaded from the clean side and, after sterilization, removed from the unclassified side, which avoids a backward contamination path.

Analogy

Managing the flow of materials is like an airport security process: passengers and baggage each have a dedicated route; checks must be passed layer by layer before entering the controlled area (removing outer packaging = taking off shoes at security); waste has its own exit and must never flow back into the departure hall. Packaging materials that cannot be disinfected, such as cardboard and wood, are like prohibited dangerous items and must never be allowed into the clean area.

Key Points

A few practical points that are easily overlooked:

  • Materials must never touch the floor: the floor is the surface with the highest bioburden
  • Cart wheels must also be disinfected: wheels contact the floor and are an important route for spreading contamination
  • Carts should preferably be dedicated to areas of a specific cleanliness grade: this further reduces the risk of cross-area contamination
  • Personnel airlocks and material airlocks should be separate: people are the biggest source of contamination and must be separated from material paths

Practical Application

In a CDMO facility design review, complete floor plans of material flow, personnel flow, and waste flow should be drawn to confirm that the three paths do not cross. The transfer procedure at each classification border should be documented and include a disinfection step.

Practice Questions

  1. Why are cardboard, wood, and open-cell foam prohibited from entering the clean area? Analyze from the standpoint of particles and microorganisms.
  2. When designing the waste path, where should a pass-through autoclave be installed to "minimize unnecessary exposure"?

Core Concepts

16.0 Appendix 2: Examples for Assessing Impact of Microbial Excursions

Original Text

Limiting the ingress and proliferation of microorganisms in the manufacturing process is the most effective approach to preventing microbial failures. This can be effectively achieved through a holistic CCS as described in this technical report. Detailed information and guidance on conducting microbial deviation investigations can be found in PDA Technical Report No. 88: Microbial Data Deviation Investigations in the Pharmaceutical Industry.

For nonsterile process intermediates, where microbial control is important, the presence of bioburden above the established limits often requires a product quality impact assessment. The assessment should be scientifically sound and consider the potential for microbial proliferation, potential microbial by-products, impact to the quality of the material at the affected process step(s), and the removal capability of the process to determine if the product is safe. Two detailed examples of this type of impact assessment are presented in this appendix.

A product quality impact assessment is not appropriate for in-process steps that are expected to be free of bioburden, even where sterility testing is not performed, for example, in a cell-culture step.

Microbial by-products are produced during cellular metabolism and secreted extracellularly in the late exponential or post-exponential (stationary) phases in a cell-density-dependent manner. For example, pathogenic strains of Staphylococcus aureus secrete exotoxins in the late exponential or stationary growth phases at a minimum concentration of 105–106 CFU/mL, and Bacillus cereus secretes exotoxins at a minimum concentration of 105 CFU/g. Supporting documents are presented in Section 16.2.

Assessing the process removal capability of potential by-products can be achieved by comparing the characteristics of microbial by-products against the downstream process-removal steps. This includes molecular size of by-products against size-exclusion steps, heat tolerance of by-products against process-heating steps, and isoelectric point of by-products against validated-chromatography steps.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Product Quality Impact Assessment: When in-process microbial monitoring results exceed the action level, a systematic approach is needed to evaluate the potential impact of the excursion on product quality and patient safety. This is not a simple pass/fail decision; it is a science-based risk assessment.

Microbial By-products: Harmful substances that microorganisms may produce while proliferating, including:

  • Endotoxin: a cell wall component of Gram-negative bacteria
  • Exotoxin: toxic proteins actively secreted by microorganisms
  • Protease: enzymes that can degrade protein drug substances
  • Mycotoxin: fungal metabolites

Analogy

Think of a microbial excursion assessment as a food safety contamination investigation. If a restaurant finds trace bacterial contamination in an ingredient, you would not throw away all the food immediately. Instead, you would ask: (1) Can these bacteria multiply to large numbers under the current storage conditions? (2) Even if they multiply, will they produce toxins? (3) Will the later cooking steps kill the bacteria and their toxins? Only the answers to these three questions allow a scientifically sound decision on disposition.

Key Takeaways

Key threshold: The secretion of microbial by-products is cell-density-dependent. Most pathogenic by-products are not secreted until the microorganisms reach 105 CFU/mL or more, a result of the quorum sensing mechanism. This means that an excursion with a low microbial count is generally lower risk when the process conditions do not favor proliferation.

Note the exception: For process steps that are supposed to be sterile (such as cell culture), even a trace of microorganisms means this impact assessment method does not apply; it should be treated as a critical deviation.

The Three Elements of Impact Assessment

Risk determination = f(likelihood of proliferation, by-product potential, downstream removal capability)

Likelihood of proliferation: Do the process conditions (temperature, pH,
           time, bacteriostatic properties) allow the microorganisms
           to reach late log phase?

By-product potential: Can the organism produce toxins/proteases?
           (check the KEGG BRITE database)

Downstream removal: Can the subsequent process steps remove the
           microbial cells and their potential by-products?

16.1 Example 1: Microbial Impact Assessment

Original Text

This example presents a methodology for impact assessment of in-process action level bioburden events, described first in a workflow diagram (Figure 16.1-1🔍), then using a detailed example (Table 16.1-1🔍). Establishing the affected process steps is a key part of the assessment. Recommended actions to take are:

  • Establish the boundary for impacted process steps using worst-case assumptions where information is lacking.
  • Include the process steps between the known ingress point and the next bioburden removal/reduction step.
  • If the ingress point is not clearly known, evaluate the process step(s) between the previous and next bioburden removal/reduction steps, OR between the previous sample point and next sample point that yields acceptable results.

Hypothetical Example

In this hypothetical example, action level Stenotrophomonas maltophilia was recovered from a chromatography eluate at the end of a three-day hold step and directly before 0.2-µm filtration (Result: 120 CFU/10mL, Action level >100 CFU/10mL).

The eluate had been 0.2-µm filtered into a stainless-steel hold-vessel, stored at 2–8 °C for three (3) days and then sampled for bioburden and endotoxin before being 0.2-µm filtered at the start of the next step.

The root cause was identified to be stagnant condensate in a stainless-steel filter-housing between the chromatography skid and the elute hold-vessel. Preventive actions were identified to replace the stainless-steel filter-housing with a single-use, gamma-irradiated filter cartridge.

The quality impact assessment of the affected batch included three risk factors:

  • Likelihood of Proliferation to Late Exponential Phase in the Process Stream: This is considered because microorganisms secrete by-products in a cell-density-dependent manner; the worst-case minimum density is 105 CFU/mL. Supporting documents are presented in 16.2.

    Low likelihood: The eluate matrix and temperature prevented growth of S. maltophilia in the process stream (documented in Table 16.1-1🔍). This is supported by the low-cell density seen in the bioburden result, which is four orders of magnitude below the minimum level associated with exotoxin secretion. Endotoxin levels were confirmed to be <0.05 EU/mL, confirming low-cell density of this endotoxin-producing microbe.
  • Microbial By-Product Potential: This is considered because microbial proliferation is a higher risk if the microorganism is capable of producing toxins or proteases.

    Yes for by-product potential: S. maltophilia is capable of generating undesired by-products, such as endotoxins, exotoxins, and proteases, based on a search of the KEGG BRITE database (documented in Table 16.1-1🔍).
  • Severity Based on Downstream Process Removal Capability: The severity of impact is based on whether the subsequent process steps can reduce or eliminate the contaminating cells and potential by-products.

    Low: The downstream process removes S. maltophilia microbial cells and there is direct evidence that potential microbial by-products are not affecting product quality (documented in Table 16.1-1🔍).
In conclusion, this event was determined to be "acceptable" meaning it did not impact product quality or patient safety. The rationale is that the organism could not grow to late exponential phase in the process stream; therefore, it would not secrete negative by-products before the microbial cells were removed via filtration. The downstream bioburden results also demonstrated the removal of the microbial cells and absence of endotoxin, and an accelerated stability results demonstrated no damage from protease activity.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Establishing the Boundary

The first step of an impact assessment is to define the affected process scope. The report provides a three-tier approach:

  1. Best case: The point of microbial ingress is known, so the scope runs from the ingress point to the next removal step
  2. Typical case: The point of ingress is unknown, so the scope runs from the previous removal step to the next removal step
  3. Most conservative: Nothing is known, so the scope runs from the previous acceptable sampling point to the next acceptable sampling point

Core principle: when information is insufficient, apply worst-case assumptions.

Analogy: The Detective Method

This assessment process is like a detective working a crime scene:

  • The culprit = Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (a waterborne Gram-negative rod)
  • The crime scene = stagnant condensate in the stainless steel filter housing
  • Evidence collected = bioburden result of 120 CFU/10 mL and endotoxin <0.05 EU/mL
  • Motive analysis = Can it multiply to large numbers under the process conditions? Which by-products can it produce?
  • Case closed = The process conditions prevented proliferation, and downstream steps removed the residual cells

Key Takeaways: The Three Key Pieces of Evidence in This Example

1. The temperature does not support proliferation: The optimum growth temperature of S. maltophilia is 35°C, but the storage temperature is 2–8°C, well below its minimum growth temperature of 10°C, so it cannot multiply.

2. The count is far below the toxin secretion threshold: The detected level was 120 CFU/10 mL (that is, 12 CFU/mL), which is four orders of magnitude below the 105 CFU/mL needed for exotoxin secretion.

3. Endotoxin corroborates the low count: As a Gram-negative organism, S. maltophilia releases endotoxin. The result of <0.05 EU/mL further confirms a low cell density.

Root Cause Analysis and CAPA Highlights

Root Cause: Stagnant condensate in the stainless steel filter housing became a breeding ground for microorganisms. This is a classic "dead leg" problem.

CAPA (corrective and preventive action): Replace the stainless steel filter housing with a single-use, gamma-irradiated filter cartridge. This fundamentally eliminates the accumulation of condensate.

This case is a good illustration of the continual improvement spirit of the CCS: every deviation is an opportunity to strengthen contamination control.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, the impact assessment of a microbial excursion bears directly on the batch release decision. If the assessment concludes "Unacceptable", the batch must be rejected, and the loss can reach millions of dollars. A structured, reusable assessment tool (such as the table in this example) therefore not only ensures scientific rigor but also greatly shortens the investigation and speeds up the batch release decision.

The final risk for each dimension = Severity × Probability × Controls, and all three are Low.

Key Takeaways: Supporting Evidence in Example 2

Compared with Example 1, the chain of evidence in Example 2 is more concise but still covers the key elements:

  • Control of proliferation: short hold time (<24 hours) + low temperature (2–8°C)
  • Physical removal: the UF/DF process can remove microorganisms of about 0.2 μm
  • Downstream purification: the multi-step purification (TFF, column extraction) has removal capability
  • Test confirmation: downstream bioburden and endotoxin results are both acceptable
  • Product integrity: the protein profile of the final product is normal, with no change

Practical Application: Choosing an Assessment Method

In CDMO practice, you may need to use different assessment methods for different clients' products. Key considerations include:

  • The Example 1 approach (Vulnerability Matrix) is better suited to high-risk products that need detailed documentation or to strict regulatory markets (such as the EU Annex 1 requirements)
  • The Example 2 approach (FMEA style) is better suited to organizations with a well-established QRM framework, and the assessment is faster
  • Whichever method is chosen, it should be predefined in the CCS document or an SOP to ensure consistency

Discussion Questions

  1. In Example 2, why is the severity of "Number of microorganisms" rated "Medium" rather than "Low"? What is the logic behind this rating, even when the count is not high?
  2. If the microorganism in the UF/DF flush solution were Bacillus cereus (a spore former), how would this assessment differ?
  3. Comparing the two examples, which method better fits your organization's QRM framework? Explain your reasoning.

Section Summary

  • A microbial excursion does not mean batch rejection: a science-based impact assessment allows a reasonable batch release decision to be made while ensuring patient safety
  • Secretion of microbial by-products is cell-density-dependent: most toxins are not secreted until ≥105 CFU/mL, a result of the quorum sensing mechanism
  • The three assessment elements: likelihood of proliferation (temperature, pH, time, bacteriostatic properties), by-product potential (check KEGG BRITE), and downstream removal capability (filtration, chromatography, heat treatment)
  • Two assessment methodologies: the Vulnerability Matrix (Example 1) vs the FMEA-style Severity × Probability × Controls (Example 2); both are acceptable methods
  • Documentation and scientific basis: every determination must be supported by scientific literature, database queries or internal study data
  • Continual improvement of the CCS: every deviation is an opportunity to strengthen the contamination control strategy (such as the change to a single-use filter in this example)
  • Refer to PDA TR88 for more detailed guidance on investigating microbial data deviations

17.1 Case Study 1: Contamination Related to Equipment Maintenance

Original Text

Reference: Case Studies of Microbial Contamination in Biologic Product Manufacturing by Kalavati Suvarna, PhD, Patricia Hughes, PhD, Richard L. Friedman; American Pharmaceutical Review, January 1, 2011

One case involved contamination of a fermenter used in the manufacture of a protein product secreted by a bacterial host. The contaminant was identified as Bacillus cereus (a Gram-positive spore-forming rod). A second case involved the contamination of a fermenter used in the manufacture of a recombinant protein by Paenibacillus curdlanolyticus (a Gram-variable spore-forming rod). A systematic approach was used during the investigations to identify the root cause of the contamination, which included several media simulations to aid in identifying the point of entry into the fermenter. In addition, the investigations involved the manufacture of engineering batches.

After a lengthy investigation in both case studies, multiple potential root causes were identified, including problems with the sampling devices, addition valves, incorrectly fitted components, missing O-rings, incorrect installation and deformation of an air filter after sterilization, and/or inadequate slope of a condensate line.

Immediate corrective actions included the replacement of valve diaphragms in fermenter addition ports, replacement of a membrane valve in the sampling device, and replacement of O-rings on the measuring probes. Enhancements were also made to the sterilization processes of the fermenter and associated transfer lines. A preventive maintenance plan was developed for all fermenter valves. All valves were tagged using a detailed checklist to ensure correct installation. All SOPs were updated, and employees were trained on the revised versions.

The investigations and corrective actions addressed all possible causes of contamination as an unequivocal root cause could not be assigned. In most cases, it is very difficult to identify a definitive assignable cause. Following a systematic approach to determine the root cause is highly recommended. Media simulations help in demonstrating that the sterility of the fermenter is not compromised.

"Recent microbial contamination events at several manufacturing facilities point to breaches in the sterile boundary caused by damaged vent filters, O-rings, diaphragms, and elastomers, and by improperly sloped condensate lines."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Fermenter Contamination: This case involves a fermenter used in biologics manufacturing that was contaminated with Bacillus species (Bacillus cereus and Paenibacillus curdlanolyticus). Both are spore-forming bacteria; spores are extremely resistant and can survive in micro-environmental dead spots under ordinary sterilization conditions.

Media Simulation: Similar to an aseptic process simulation (APS), culture medium is run through the fermenter in place of product to detect where the sterile boundary has been breached. It is a very valuable investigation tool.

Engineering Batch: A non-commercial batch produced specifically for investigation or validation purposes, used to reproduce the problem conditions and verify the effectiveness of corrective measures.

Analogy

The sterile boundary of a fermenter is like the hull of a submarine: any tiny gap (a missing O-ring, a damaged valve diaphragm, insufficient slope in a condensate line) can let in the "water" (microorganisms). Spore formers are like hardy seeds: once they get in through a crack, they can wait for their chance to germinate even if conditions are temporarily unfavorable.

Key Notes

  • Multiple root causes: This case ultimately could not confirm a single unequivocal root cause, which is very common in practice. The correct approach is to identify all possible causes and correct them comprehensively.
  • CAPA scope: Corrective actions covered three levels: hardware (replacing valve diaphragms and O-rings), process (strengthening the sterilization procedure) and system (establishing a PM program, updating SOPs, training personnel). This is a model of a complete CAPA.
  • Common weak points in the sterile boundary: vent filters, O-rings, diaphragms, elastomers and insufficient slope of condensate lines.

Practical Application

For a CDMO, fermenters are high-value equipment, and the cost of downtime for an investigation is very high. Establishing a sound preventive maintenance (PM) program, particularly regular replacement intervals for all valves and seals, is the best investment for avoiding this kind of event. It is advisable to include critical sealing components (O-rings, diaphragms) in the materials management system to ensure adequate safety stock.

Practice Questions

  1. Why are spore-forming bacteria particularly likely to be the culprit in fermenter contamination?
  2. When a single root cause cannot be determined, what strategy should the investigation team adopt to ensure completeness?
  3. What role does media simulation play in root cause investigation? How is it similar to and different from APS?

17.2 Case Study 2: Contamination Related to Blow-Fill-Seal Equipment Design and Maintenance

Original Text

Reference: Commentary Aseptic Processing Contamination Case Studies and the Pharmaceutical Quality System by Richard L. Friedman; PDA Journal of Pharmaceutical Science and Technology, Vol. 59, No. 2, March–April 2005

Background: A firm experienced both sterility and media-fill failures. Stenotrophomonas maltophilia was identified as a sterility failure isolate. Media-fill isolates included Pseudomonas spp. and Acinetobacter spp. The prior sterility history of the blow-fill-seal (BFS) processing line was good.

GMP Issues: Mold plates used to form the primary product container were chilled with cooling water. This demineralized potable water was held in a tank at a low temperature prior to use. When sampled, the cooling water yielded very high microbial counts. Leaks developed in the mold plates, allowing contaminated water to infiltrate into the product, causing nonsterility. Based on this significant breach in equipment integrity, among the most relevant GMP deviations were the unsuitable processing equipment and the lack of an adequate preventive maintenance program.

Quality System Context: The facility and equipment system were the most deficient. The unsuitable equipment and inadequate preventive maintenance program were key factors in the product contamination.

Outcome: Both the sterility failure and media-fill failure were attributed to contamination by cooling water. Pinhole leaks in the mold plates of the aseptic filling machine allowed cooling water to directly contaminate the product. The exact date the problem occurred was unknown, making the corrective and preventive action plan more difficult. Numerous lots were rejected. The firm concluded that frequent visual inspections of BFS molds for leaks had not provided sufficient preventive maintenance, and it implemented corrective measures, including regular testing of molding equipment pressure integrity.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Blow-Fill-Seal (BFS): An aseptic filling technology in which container forming, filling and sealing are all completed within a single piece of equipment. BFS molds require cooling water to maintain the forming temperature.

Pinhole Leak: A tiny perforation in the mold plate that is hard to see with the naked eye but is enough to let microorganisms enter the product along with the cooling water. This is a contamination risk specific to BFS.

Cooling Water Contamination: Demineralized potable water left standing for a long time in a low-temperature tank allows microorganisms to proliferate heavily. The cooling water system is often overlooked, yet it is separated from the product by only a layer of mold plate metal.

Analogy

A pinhole leak in a BFS mold is like a tiny crack in the condenser tubing at the back of a refrigerator: the refrigerant (the contaminant) leaks out slowly and unnoticed. You may not know the problem exists for a long time, until the refrigerator (the product) starts to have problems. "Visual inspection" is like checking only by eye whether the refrigerant tube is leaking, which is clearly not enough; you need a pressure test to find tiny leaks.

Key Notes

  • Equipment design flaw + inadequate maintenance = disaster: The cooling water system is separated from the product by only a metal plate. With inadequate maintenance, pinhole leaks are inevitable once the equipment ages.
  • Limits of visual inspection: The case states explicitly that "frequent visual inspection is not sufficient for adequate preventive maintenance"; quantitative methods such as pressure integrity testing must be used.
  • Difficulty of tracing back: Because the exact date the problem began was unknown, a large number of batches were rejected. This highlights the importance of continuous monitoring: the earlier a problem is found, the smaller its impact.
  • Microbial profile: Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Pseudomonas spp. and Acinetobacter spp. are all typical water-system-associated microorganisms.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can microorganisms in the cooling water system directly affect the sterility of BFS product?
  2. Besides pressure integrity testing, what other methods can be used to monitor the condition of BFS molds?
  3. If you were the head of quality, how would you design a monitoring program for a BFS cooling water system?

17.3 Case Study 3: Contamination Related to Facility Construction

Original Text

Reference: Commentary Aseptic Processing Contamination Case Studies and the Pharmaceutical Quality System by Richard L. Friedman, PDA Journal of Pharmaceutical Science and Technology, Vol. 59, No. 2, March–April 2005

Background: A firm undertook major construction in a cleanroom next to the personnel gowning air lock. The construction occurred over a one-month period and coincided with continued drug product production. Following an initial media-fill failure, the firm's investigation concluded that practices unrelated to the construction were the likely sources of the nonsterile units. The firm corrected the apparent root causes and then performed a repeat media fill. A second media-fill failure occurred. A second thorough investigation by the firm concluded that the contaminants in the media-fill vials had migrated from the area of the construction activity. Spore-forming bacteria (Bacillus sp.) were identified as isolates in both media fills.

GMP Issues: The firm did not adequately assess the risk posed by construction activities.

Quality System Context: The production system was most deficient in this case. With any change in normal, qualified conditions it is essential that it be carefully evaluated by production and quality management. The FDA has seen this scenario with surprising frequency: a firm performs construction in an area that is considerably removed from the aseptic processing room (in some cases, several rooms away) and presumes that the construction will not affect the sterility assurance of the product.

"Unfortunately, it is not uncommon for the contamination to ultimately migrate to the aseptic processing room and into the product. Many sterility failures and media-fill failures have been attributed to contamination from nearby construction."

For example, moving walls is a common culprit in the liberation of spore-formers (most commonly fungi) into the cleanroom environment. These experiences should alert a firm to assess the potential impact of such deviations from normal conditions. Deviation and/or change control systems provide a formal mechanism for evaluating these issues. Written procedures should address returning a facility to normal operating conditions when construction or other activities (e.g., maintenance) are considered to have a potentially adverse impact. In these cases, a firm should either elect not to manufacture product for a specific period or, where appropriate, implement special precautions and increase monitoring to detect any drift in environmental control.

Outcome: Multiple lots were found to lack the assurance of sterility, and those already distributed were recalled. The firm temporarily suspended operations. The firm ultimately restored adequate conditions and resumed aseptic processing following successful media fill-requalification.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Construction Contamination Migration: Construction activities (demolishing walls, modifying piping, etc.) release large quantities of particles and microorganisms (especially spore formers and fungi). These contaminants can migrate along airflow paths, through the HVAC system or because of insufficient pressure differentials, to aseptic operation areas far away.

Change Control: Any activity that deviates from normal validated conditions should be evaluated through a formal change control or deviation system. Construction activity is a major change and requires a risk assessment beforehand.

Analogy

Construction contamination migration is like renovation dust from the next room: even if you close the door of your own room, dust still drifts in through gaps under the door, ventilation ducts and shared corridors. More dangerous still, the walls may hide decades of mold spores that are released in large quantities once a wall is demolished. "It is far away, so it does not matter" is the most dangerous assumption.

Key Notes

  • Common FDA observation: FDA notes that this scenario occurs with "surprising frequency", as firms often underestimate the effect of construction on remote areas.
  • First investigation went in the wrong direction: The firm initially attributed the root cause to "operations unrelated to construction" and only faced the impact of construction after the second media fill failed again, causing losses of both time and batches.
  • Serious consequences: Product recall plus suspension of production, a cost no firm can afford.
  • Correct approach: Production should be stopped during construction, or at the least physical isolation barriers should be set up, environmental monitoring strengthened and additional non-routine sampling performed.

Practical Application

CDMOs often face pressure to carry out facility upgrades without stopping production. Best practice is to set up a cross-functional team (engineering, quality, production) at the construction planning stage, perform a formal risk assessment, and clearly define isolation measures, an enhanced monitoring program and the trigger criteria for "stop production". It is better to proactively halt production for a week than to be forced to recall an entire batch of product.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does "being far away" not guarantee that construction will not affect the environment of the aseptic area?
  2. Why was the conclusion of the first investigation in this case wrong? What does it teach about investigation methodology?
  3. If your plant needs to carry out construction during production, what control measures would you design?

17.4 Case Study 4: Disruption Recovery Program

Original Text

Reference: The PDA TR-90 authoring team developed the following recovery program example based on their professional experiences.

Based on the CCS principle that impact to classified areas should be minimized whenever possible, every effort should be made to plan and schedule routine events such as preventive maintenance, calibration and validation activities, during shutdown periods or times when areas are not in use. The invasive disruption described below is considered an exception.

Two levels of disruption are defined based on the severity of the event and their potential impact to the qualified state:

  • Noninvasive disruption: An activity that does not breach the integrity of a controlled classified area or critical utilities systems and are performed when no activity is occurring in the room, for example, caulking repair, light-switch or outlet repair (no cutting), equipment calibration.
  • Invasive Disruption: An activity that breaches the integrity of the controlled classified area or critical utilities systems and are performed when processing is occurring in the room, for example, opening an electrical panel in a classified area, welding or cutting pipes in critical utilities.

Disruption Recovery Plan: The following steps may be followed for the shutdown/startup process:

  1. Develop a plan to contain the disruption and recover afterward through cleaning/disinfection and monitoring
  2. Determine the environmental control measures for the equipment, facilities and personnel
  3. Execute the activities in the plan
  4. Confirm the completion of activities
  5. Verify EM results to demonstrate the control and post-recovery environmental control measures (level of post-recovery measures should be commensurate with the risk; may be reduced for noninvasive disruptions)
  6. Resume manufacturing

Specific actions to be taken as part of the shutdown include:

  • Create temporary barriers around the area (protect equipment and utilities and segregate the affected area)
  • Determine which activities may continue, and which must be stopped
  • Define a sequence of activities or steps to be conducted
  • Provide instructions for the alternative movement of materials, equipment, and people
  • Train personnel involved in the shut-down activities how to prevent contamination

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Disruption Recovery Program: A predefined management procedure for handling events that disrupt classified areas or critical utilities. Its core principle is tiered management: applying controls that correspond to the severity of the disruption.

Noninvasive vs Invasive Disruption:

  • Level 1, noninvasive: Does not breach the integrity of the classified area and is performed when no operations are under way (for example calibration, caulking repairs)
  • Level 2, invasive: Breaches the integrity of the classified area and occurs while production is under way (for example opening an electrical panel, pipe welding)
  • Level 3, utilities: Involves maintenance or repair of critical utility systems

Analogy

A disruption recovery program is like the "emergency repair SOP" of a hospital operating room:

  • Level 1 = changing a light bulb: can be done between surgeries, with a simple wipe-down
  • Level 2 = repairing ceiling pipework: temporary partitions, vacuuming and full disinfection are needed before the room can be used again
  • Level 3 = repairing the water supply system: the piping must be isolated and disinfected, and testing must pass before the water supply is restored

Key Notes

  • Planning priority principle: Routine events (PM, calibration, validation) should be scheduled during shutdown periods wherever possible, to minimize the impact on classified areas.
  • Use of sporicide: At Level 1, targeted disinfection with a sporicide is needed when dirty surfaces are exposed; at Level 2 it is mandatory. This echoes the spore-former migration problem in Case Study 3.
  • Recovery confirmation = EM acceptance: Before production resumes, environmental monitoring results must confirm that the environment has returned to a controlled state. This is not a formality but a release condition.
  • Commensurate with risk: The intensity of recovery measures should match the risk level of the disruption, avoiding overreaction that wastes resources as well as insufficient action that leads to contamination.

Practical Application

It is recommended that every CDMO site establish a "Disruption Recovery Matrix" that classifies all possible maintenance and construction events as Level 1/2/3 and predefines, for each category: (1) isolation measures, (2) cleaning and disinfection procedures, (3) the environmental monitoring program and (4) release criteria before production resumes. This allows the predefined recovery procedure to be activated quickly when an event occurs, rather than making ad hoc decisions.

CCS Document Template: This template provides a standardized yet customizable CCS document structure. The point is not to "copy the template" but to understand the logic behind each section and fill in specific content according to the actual situation at your own site.

  • Multi-site / multi-product strategy:

  1. Start with Section 3 (Scope): First define the scope clearly: which products, which areas, which part of the supply chain.
  2. Make good use of the Process Map: Presenting the process flow and control points graphically in Section 5.1 is more effective than a text description.
  3. Reference rather than copy: Each element needs only 1-2 paragraphs of summary, followed by references to the detailed SOPs/reports/risk assessment documents.
  4. Update regularly: The CCS is a living document; every major change, deviation event or annual review should trigger an update.
  5. Cross-functional collaboration: Assign the quality unit to own the CCS document, but writing it requires input from teams such as production, engineering, microbiology and validation.

TR90 Complete Report Summary

Complete Report Summary

  • The core spirit of the CCS: A Contamination Control Strategy is not a static document but a lifecycle system covering design, construction, operation, monitoring and improvement. It requires understanding the relationships among all control measures from a holistic perspective.
  • Risk-based approach: TR90 consistently stresses that a CCS must be driven by risk assessment: identify contamination sources, assess risk levels, design corresponding control measures and continuously monitor their effectiveness. Risk is not a one-time assessment but a dynamic cycle.
  • The fifteen CCS elements: From facility design, environmental monitoring, process design, equipment maintenance, personnel control, raw material management, utilities, alarm systems, container closure, supplier management and quality systems, through to governance and performance review, each element is a part of the overall strategy.
  • The dual role of environmental monitoring (EM): EM is both a "detection tool" and a "trend analysis tool". A single data point confirms the immediate status, while long-term trends reveal systematic drift. The core of a Contamination Recovery Program is to confirm recovery through EM.
  • Equipment maintenance is a hidden critical risk: Of the four case studies, three involved maintenance-related failures that directly led to contamination events. A Preventive Maintenance Plan and a Disruption Recovery Program are indispensable components of a CCS.
  • Integrated use of microbiological data: From microbial identification and environmental trending to Microbial Impact Assessment, TR90 shows how to integrate microbiological knowledge into quality decisions.
  • Principles for writing a CCS document: 30-50 pages, factual descriptive tone, reference rather than copy, and design for the dual audience of inspectors and employees. The document structure should cover all 15 elements and adjust the emphasis according to process characteristics.
  • Continuous improvement is the ultimate goal: A CCS is not a tool for passing inspections but an engine that drives continuous improvement in manufacturing quality. Every deviation investigation, CAPA implementation and annual review is an opportunity to improve the CCS.
