Introduction

Original Text

Cell and gene therapies are classes of advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMP) that include numerous different types of products including nucleic acids, viruses, genetically modified microorganisms, and others. Gene therapies are relatively novel technologies that tend to have extremely complex and not well-understood mechanisms of action but hold great potential for treating a variety of serious diseases, unmet medical needs, or simplification of administration and treatment (i.e., "one shot" gene therapy treatments versus a lifetime of chronic treatment for many diseases). The potential applications are diverse and numerous and may reach far beyond any current forms of therapies in terms of disease-state targets, as illustrated by the impact of the handful of currently approved successes. Notable, as well, are the abundant early- to late-stage products currently in clinical development and the anticipation of many more to come.

One technology that has shown immense promise within the ATMP space is the use of viral vectors for delivery of a therapeutic gene. Viral vectors have been shown to be useful both in the direct administration of in vivo treatment to patients and in the ex vivo genetic modification of cells that are then administered to the patient. Different viral-vector types have proven effective as gene delivery systems including, but not limited to, adeno-associated viruses (AAV) and lentiviruses, both of which have successfully commercialized products. AAV-based drug products, in particular, have been commercialized successfully in the United States, Europe, and other regions of the world.

Plasmid DNA (pDNA) are used as critical materials (i.e., the "building blocks") in the production of viral vectors and may also be delivered as naked DNA or encapsulated (e.g., in a liposome) for delivery purposes at various steps of a gene-therapy manufacturing process. Thus, ensuring consistent control of the quality in the production and use of plasmids is of great importance in the manufacture of many gene therapies, including cell-based, gene-modified therapies.

The ex vivo modification of cells was not within the primary scope of PDA Technical Report No. 81: Cell-Based Therapy Control Strategy (TR 81), therefore, some points to consider on viral vectors used in gene therapy were deemed to be of value and are addressed herein. In developing this points to consider document, the intent was to not repeat information already available in TR 81, but rather to supplement it. Likewise, when topics such as process validation, sterile filtration, filtration for viral contaminants, analytical method validation, and phase-appropriate GMPs are addressed in this document, these topics should be deemed supplemental to guidance that can be found in existing PDA technical reports.

A team of industry representatives from multiple CMC disciplines comprising product and process development, analytical development, manufacturing, quality assurance, and regulatory affairs was assembled by the PDA ATMP Advisory Board to author this document. This team was tasked not only with identifying the current major problems in the development, qualification, and manufacture of viral vectors that are not adequately addressed in current regulatory guidance, but also with recommending solutions to these problems based on current industry perspectives and best practices with consideration to compliance within a dynamic, global regulatory framework.

This document presents the views of the Parenteral Drug Association on the development, classification, manufacture, control, and testing of plasmids and vectors used in ATMP production. It is designed to encourage further dialog with industry, health authorities, and suppliers of technology and materials while taking into consideration the changes and needs of the modern, global, sterile healthcare-product manufacturing industry. It does not represent a standard or a regulatory guidance.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: What Is an ATMP?

Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) is the collective term for three categories of specialized therapeutic products defined in the EU regulatory framework (EC Regulation 1394/2007):

  • Gene Therapy Medicinal Products (GTMPs): introduce a therapeutic gene sequence to correct, replace, or suppress the function of an abnormal gene.
  • Somatic Cell Therapy Medicinal Products (SCTMPs): use living cells or tissues that have been manipulated ex vivo as the therapeutic agent.
  • Tissue Engineered Products (TEPs): contain or consist of cells/tissues and are intended to regenerate, repair, or replace human tissue.

PtC-11 focuses on the part of GTMPs that uses viral vectors and plasmid DNA (pDNA).

The relationship between viral vectors and plasmid DNA: plasmid DNA is the "raw material" (building block) for making viral vectors, like parts to a finished machine. pDNA is first amplified in large quantities in bacterial cells and then introduced into mammalian cells, where it helps assemble complete viral vector particles. The quality of the pDNA therefore directly affects the quality of the final viral vector (that is, the drug substance).

Analogy: The Viral Vector as a "Courier" for the Therapeutic Gene

Imagine you need to deliver an important letter (the therapeutic gene) into a castle (the patient's cell). The castle walls (the cell membrane) are very strong, and ordinary methods cannot get through. You need a special "messenger" who can not only cross the wall but also make sure the letter is handed to the right recipient.

A viral vector is this "messenger courier". Viruses that evolved in nature are born with the ability to infect cells. Scientists modify a virus by removing its dangerous disease-causing genes and keeping its ability to "enter cells", then load it with the therapeutic gene, which creates a viral vector.

Plasmid DNA is like the courier company's "production instruction manual", the blueprint used to make these couriers. A poor-quality manual leads to defective couriers that cannot deliver the letter accurately.

AAV (adeno-associated virus) is good at entering non-dividing cells such as neurons and muscle; lentivirus can integrate into dividing immune cells (such as T cells), which makes it especially suitable for producing CAR-T cell therapies.

Key Point: PtC-11 Is Not a Regulatory Standard!

The document states explicitly: "It does not represent a standard or a regulatory guidance."

For CDMO professionals, this is an essential premise to understand:

  • PtC-11 is a set of "best practice recommendations" compiled by PDA from the input of multiple CMC industry experts. It represents industry consensus, not a regulatory requirement.
  • Regulatory inspectors will not issue a Warning Letter directly on the basis of this document; the legally binding requirements are the EU GMP Annex 1, PIC/S Annex 2A, and FDA CMC guidance documents that it cites.
  • A CDMO should treat this document as a "thinking framework" and a "starting point for discussion". It can be cited when discussing strategy with the client (MAH), but regulatory submissions should be based on the official guidance.
  • This document supplements, rather than replaces, PDA TR 81 (control strategy for cell therapy) and other existing PDA technical reports.

Practical Application, CDMO Perspective: Why Does ATMP Manufacturing Capability Matter Strategically?

The ATMP market is growing rapidly. As of 2025, more than 30 ATMP products have been approved worldwide, with over a thousand candidates in the clinical pipeline. For a CDMO, building ATMP manufacturing capability early has significant commercial meaning:

  • High barriers are a moat: viral vector manufacturing is technically complex (it requires BSL-2 biosafety-level facilities, suspension cell culture, and ultracentrifugation purification), and not every CDMO is able to take it on; early entrants enjoy a scarcity premium.
  • High stickiness: once an ATMP client selects a CDMO, switching is often difficult (technology transfer is costly and regulatory filings are complex), so the share of long-term orders is higher than for small-molecule drugs.
  • Regulatory risk needs proactive management: the classification of pDNA and viral vectors (raw material vs. drug substance) differs between regulatory jurisdictions (discussed in depth in Topic 1 later). A CDMO that fails to understand these differences may expose its clients to unnecessary regulatory risk.
  • TR 81 + PtC-11 is dual-track required reading: CMC staff who are familiar with both documents can give clients more complete strategic advice, which is a source of competitive differentiation.
Scope

Original Text

This Points to Consider document is focused on the use of viral vectors for the purpose of the delivery of a therapeutic gene both in vivo and for ex vivo modification within the overall classification of ATMPs. The topics addressed are diverse and include plasmid and viral vector categorization and control, quality by design (QbD), control strategies, filtration, comparability, platform technologies, and potency assays. Separately and together, these topics address a broad range of development, manufacture, and control issues that may be encountered in industry. The topics are organized in a question-and-answer format, presenting a specific problem statement and an accompanying recommendation, inclusive of the recommended points to be considered with the goal to assist the reader in formulating a strategy to address the topic.

One concept that emerges throughout these discussions is the need for applying a science-driven and risk-based approach to providing solutions for current problems associated with the development and manufacture of viral vectors. That approach is embraced by regulatory agencies for the development of gene therapies, including those using viral vectors, as presented in the EU GMP Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products and the Guideline on the Risk-based Approach according to Annex I, Part IV of Directive 2001/83/EC (1). Annex I, Part IV specifically refers to a risk-based approach as the recommended methodology to design the organizational, technical, and structural measures to comply with GMP regulations. PIC/S Annex 2A: Manufacture of Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products for Human Use echoes the EU guidelines, noting that quality risk management principles are particularly important for this class of materials (2).

"In summary, the topics presented in this Points to Consider reflect the current thinking regarding some of the major challenges that have been identified by the PDA community regarding the development, control, and manufacture of gene therapy products using viral vectors. Nonetheless, this team acknowledges that there are many more topics that the industry faces for this diverse class of therapies."

Analogy: In Vivo vs. Ex Vivo, Two "Delivery Strategies"

The delivery approaches of gene therapy can be compared to two quite different logistics models:

In vivo (gene therapy inside the body) - "direct delivery to the door": the viral vector is injected directly into the patient's body, like a parcel delivered straight to the recipient's doorstep. The vector finds the target cells on its own inside the patient and completes the gene transfer. AAV is the most common vector for in vivo gene therapy (for example, Zolgensma, used to treat spinal muscular atrophy).

Ex vivo (gene modification outside the body) - "processed at the factory, then shipped": cells (such as T cells) are first collected from the patient, their genes are modified with a viral vector in a laboratory outside the body, and after the quality of the processed cells is confirmed, they are infused back into the patient. CAR-T cell therapies (such as Kymriah and Yescarta) are the typical ex vivo model. Lentivirus is the most commonly used vector for ex vivo modification because it can integrate the target gene into the genome of dividing T cells.

The scope of PtC-11 covers both models, so its content is a useful reference for CDMOs in different types of ATMP business.

Key Point: Science-Driven and Risk-Based, the Core Philosophy Throughout the Document

"Science-driven and risk-based approach" is the central line of thinking that PtC-11 emphasizes repeatedly, and it is the common tone of all current advanced therapy regulatory guidance:

  • EU GMP Annex 1 (manufacture of sterile medicinal products) and its associated risk-based approach guidance require manufacturers to design organizational, technical, and structural GMP compliance measures based on scientific rationale.
  • PIC/S Annex 2A (manufacture of ATMPs) specifically points out that Quality Risk Management (QRM) principles are especially important for these materials, directly echoing ICH Q9.
  • Practical significance for a CDMO: when regulatory guidance does not provide a clear answer (often the case in the ATMP field), the risk assessment document itself becomes the basis of compliance. Being able to clearly document "why this approach is scientifically justified" matters more than simply following a fixed procedure.

Q 1.1 - Classification Framework for Plasmids and Viral Vectors

Original Text

Plasmid and viral vectors (i.e., vectors that consist of or are derived from biological material designed to deliver genetic material) express the desired gene product that is introduced into the ATMP manufacturing process at different stages of production. In some cases, the purified genetic material (i.e., plasmid or vector) is administered directly to a patient whereas in other cases, the genetic material, most commonly a viral vector, is used to genetically modify the cells ex vivo as part of the cell-therapy manufacturing process. This leads to a situation wherein similar material can be classified as a raw material, starting material, critical starting material, or an active substance (drug substance or active pharmaceutical ingredient) with each governed by different GMP requirements within different regulatory frameworks. The regional regulatory jurisdiction(s) will dictate the classification requirement, and therefore, the governing GMPs.

A starting material may be deemed a critical starting material depending on an assessment of risk and the particular regulatory jurisdiction. A critical classification may drive tighter controls within the quality system. For example, in the European Economic Area, a viral vector that is used to modify the cells ex vivo will be considered a critical starting material and follow EU EudraLex - Volume 4 - Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) Guidelines, while in the United States, it may be considered a drug substance (3). In some jurisdictions, such as Australia, the difference is even more significant: if the vector is administered in vivo to the patient, it will be considered a medicine and follow the PIC/S code of GMP PE-009 (which is largely aligned with EU Volume IV with part 4 being a notable exception) (4). However, if the same viral vector is used to modify the cells ex vivo, it will be considered a critical starting material and is regulated by a completely different code of GMPs (refer to the Australian Code of Good Manufacturing Practice for Human Blood and Blood Components, Human Tissues and Human Cellular Therapy Products) (5).

Considering that various regulatory agencies have different GMP and CMC requirements, it is extremely important to be aware of these nuances to determine the correct classification for each material used during manufacturing in order to develop the appropriate control strategy.

While it is not possible to provide a comprehensive framework for categorizing vectors and plasmids, as there are too many jurisdictions with varying requirements, it is possible to show approaches that would facilitate classification of the material, establish the CMC and GMP requirements, and build an appropriate control strategy. That approach can then be adapted for other jurisdictions.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Starting Material: a raw material used in the process that, after further processing, becomes part of the drug substance or final product; it is not administered to the patient directly.

Critical Starting Material: a special category of starting material that is defined as requiring enhanced control because it has a significant impact on the safety, efficacy, or quality of the final product.

Drug Substance / Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient: the substance that directly constitutes the active part of the medicinal product; it must meet the highest level of GMP requirements and is submitted as a separate CMC section in the filing.

In vivo gene therapy: the viral vector is injected directly into the patient's body, and the vector itself is the drug substance.

Ex vivo genetic modification: the patient's cells are first modified outside the body with a viral vector, and the modified cells are then infused back into the patient. In this scenario, the FDA regards the vector as a drug substance, while the EMA regards it as a critical starting material.

Analogy

Imagine a Michelin-starred restaurant. Take the same truffle (the viral vector). If it is plated and served directly to the guest, it is the "main dish" (the drug substance, subject to the strictest food safety rules). But if the chef first uses the truffle to cook a sauce that is then poured over the main dish, the truffle is only an "ingredient" (a starting material).

What makes it more troublesome is that the very same truffle counts as a "premium ingredient" (critical starting material) in a kitchen in Paris (EU), but counts directly as the "main dish" (drug substance) in a kitchen in New York (FDA). The chef (the CDMO) must decide which handling procedures and documentation this truffle needs according to the hygiene regulations of the city where the restaurant is located.

Key Notes: Compliance Challenges for CDMOs

For a CDMO, the same viral vector process may face completely different regulatory requirements when serving different clients:

  • US clients: the vector must be manufactured under Drug Substance GMP, which requires complete batch records, a drug substance specification, and a DS section in the IND filing
  • EU clients: the vector is regarded as a critical starting material and the GMP requirements are relatively less stringent, but the ATMP manufacturer must audit the CDMO through the Quality Agreement to confirm that appropriate controls are implemented
  • Australian clients: the same vector used in vivo versus ex vivo falls under completely different regulatory frameworks

Practical advice: before accepting a project, confirm the regulatory requirements of all of the client's target markets, and use the "strictest" market as the baseline manufacturing standard, to avoid clinical trial delays or being required to remanufacture because of a misclassification.

Q 1.2 - Available Regulatory Guidance

Original Text

Some guidance for classifying vectors and plasmids is available from U.S. and European regulators, respectively. In particular, the first two guidelines listed below can be considered primary references, though the FDA guidance refers to investigational products while the EMA guidance focuses on approved products. While neither guidance provides a clear indication that the principles outlined can be applied in a phase-appropriate manner, industry consensus and informal regulatory authority commentary suggest that a phase-appropriate strategy should be implemented.

  • U.S. FDA Guidance for Industry: Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Control (CMC) Information for Human Gene Therapy Investigational New Drug Applications (INDs)
  • U.S. FDA Guidance for Industry: Considerations for the Development of Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T Cell Products
  • U.S. FDA Guidance for Industry: Human Gene Therapy Products Incorporating Human Genome Editing
  • European Medicines Agency (EMA) Guideline on the Quality, Non-Clinical and Clinical Aspects of Gene Therapy Medicinal Products (EMA/CAT/80183/2014)
  • EMA Guideline on Quality, Non-Clinical and Clinical Aspects of Medicinal Products Containing Genetically Modified Cells (EMA/CAT/GTWP/671639/2008)
  • EMA Questions and Answers on the Principles of GMP for the Manufacturing of Starting Materials of Biological Origin used to Transfer Genetic Material for the Manufacturing of ATMPs (EMA/246400/2021)
Note: The EMA Q&A (EMA/246400/2021) is based on the PIC/S Annex 2A revision. PIC/S Annex 2A (Sections 5.20–5.29) includes clauses that can be used systematically to classify the material. Those principles are mandated only where PIC/S Annex 2A is approved, but the authors believe these principles can be scientifically applied to build a strategy that can be defended for multiple jurisdictions.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Overview of the Main Regulatory Documents

The following six documents are the main regulatory basis for the classification of plasmids and viral vectors, and you should be familiar with the scope of each:

  • FDA Gene Therapy CMC IND Guidance: the core reference for the CMC section of a US IND application; it applies to the clinical trial stage and mainly addresses manufacturing requirements for gene therapy drug substances
  • FDA CAR-T Guidance: specific to CAR-T cell products; it makes explicit the requirements for the viral vector as a DS
  • FDA Genome Editing Guidance: extends to genome editing (such as CRISPR) products
  • EMA/CAT/80183/2014: the EMA's comprehensive guideline on the quality, non-clinical, and clinical aspects of gene therapy medicinal products; it applies to marketing authorization applications (MAA)
  • EMA/CAT/GTWP/671639/2008: addresses medicinal products containing genetically modified cells and is the EMA's main reference for CAR-T type products
  • EMA Q&A EMA/246400/2021: dedicated to answering questions on the GMP principles for ATMP starting materials; the risk factor analysis in Question 5 is the core of the practical guidance

Key Point: PIC/S Annex 2A, the Most Practical Multi-Jurisdiction Tool

Among the many documents, PIC/S Annex 2A (Sections 5.20–5.29) has special practical value, for the following reasons:

  • Recognition across multiple jurisdictions: PIC/S members include more than 50 regulatory authorities such as the FDA, EMA, TGA, Taiwan TFDA, and Japan PMDA. A classification strategy built on the PIC/S Annex 2A framework is more likely to be accepted in several markets at once
  • Systematic classification provisions: Sections 5.20–5.29 provide systematic provisions that can be used directly to determine material classification, filling the gap left by the FDA and EMA guidance in the classification of "starting materials"
  • Basis of the EMA Q&A: the EMA Q&A (EMA/246400/2021) was written on the basis of PIC/S Annex 2A, so mastering the former helps in understanding the latter
  • Phase-appropriate spirit: industry consensus (although not yet explicitly stated in writing) holds that the principles of PIC/S Annex 2A can be applied progressively according to the clinical phase

Practical advice: when drafting a CDMO's SOP for classifying viral vectors or plasmids, use PIC/S Annex 2A Sections 5.20–5.29 as the backbone of the classification framework, and then layer on the specific FDA or EMA requirements according to the client's market.

Analogy: The Hierarchy of Regulatory Documents

These regulatory documents are like a "traffic law system":

  • PIC/S Annex 2A = the international road traffic convention (basic rules that many countries follow in common)
  • FDA Gene Therapy CMC Guidance = detailed driving rules of a particular US state (stricter and more explicit)
  • EMA guidelines = driving rules of the EU member states (a common framework, but with detail differences between countries)
  • EMA Q&A = the FAQ of the EU traffic enforcement authority (explaining how the rules are applied)

A product developed for several national markets at once must have a driving strategy designed to "satisfy the strictest conditions" in order to travel legally on the roads of every country.

Q 1.3 - Classification Principles from Regulatory Guidance

Original Text

While principles from the regulatory documents identified in Q 1.2 are summarized here, each regulatory body offers subtle differences on various points and sponsors should carefully check the latest guidance available in the applicable jurisdiction(s). When targeting multiple jurisdictions, the sponsor should consider the most restrictive regulatory environment where they intend to market their product. This approach should also consider that jurisdictions with less restrictive requirements may still have jurisdiction-specific requirements that would also need to be fulfilled. For example, when manufacturing a critical starting material, establishing a master virus seed stock or master cell bank will enable the sponsor to enter each jurisdiction without delaying the program to remanufacture the critical starting material. In fact, in Europe, having a master stock/bank is becoming required, even at an early stage. Therefore, it would be advantageous for the firm to rapidly progress to reference stocks/banks with an eye towards manufacturing the master stock/bank as quickly as possible.

For mRNA- or DNA-based in vivo gene therapies, which are directly administered to patients, the mRNA or DNA used for injection is the active drug substance(s), and the formulated, filled product in the primary container is the drug product. In these situations, the starting materials include the plasmid used at the start of the drug-substance manufacturing process and the bacterial master or working cell bank that is used to expand the plasmid prior to routine drug-substance manufacturing.

Similarly, for an in vivo viral-vector gene therapy, the plasmid used to transfect production cells or to make the master virus seed stock and master cell bank for vector production is the starting material, and the actual viral-vector produced is the active substance.

Finally, for ex vivo genetically modified cells, the FDA considers both the viral vector and the genetically modified cells as active drug substances, so the sponsor must file two drug substance sections. The EMA, however, considers genetically modified cells the only active substance requiring a drug substance section. Intriguingly, the EMA Q&A document specifies that the vector is a starting material for which the principles of GMP for starting materials are applicable (6). Question 2 in the EMA document asks: "What does principles of GMP mean?"

GMP requirements for ATMP active substances (drug substances) and ATMP finished products are described in Part IV of the GMP Guide. Some requirements of Part IV can also be relevant for the starting materials.

However, the main differences with regard to the starting materials are:
• Not all GMP aspects described in Part IV of the GMP Guide are required.
• According to the current legal framework, neither recurring inspections nor GMP certifications by the responsible authorities are required.
• As a result of this situation, the ATMP manufacturers have the responsibility to verify that appropriate GMP requirements are implemented for the manufacturing/testing of the starting materials according to the methodology described in questions 4 and 5 of the document.

The EMA document asserts that the sponsor is accountable to apply a risk-based analysis to identify which parts of Part IV of the GMP Guide are applicable, and that the sponsor's analysis, supplier qualification, and starting-material quality are assessed during clinical trial application, marketing authorization, variation application, and GMP inspections of the ATMP manufacturer.

Aside from the nomenclature differences between the FDA and EMA guidances, for any sponsor considering clinical trials in both the United States and European Union, the viral vector should be treated like an active substance that is manufactured according to GMPs and should be filed as a separate drug substance section within the IND application for FDA. This standard will meet the EMA requirements and thus provide access to both marketplaces as needed.

Key Concepts: Three Types of Gene Therapy Products

PtC-11 Figure 1.3-1 divides gene therapy products into three types, and the logic for classifying materials is completely different for each:

  • Type 1 - In vivo direct injection of mRNA/DNA: the mRNA or naked DNA itself is the drug substance (DS), and the plasmid DNA and the bacterial Master Cell Bank (MCB) are starting materials. Typical examples: mRNA vaccines, lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated DNA therapies
  • Type 2 - In vivo viral vector gene therapy: a viral vector such as AAV is the DS, and the plasmids used to produce the vector and the Master Virus Seed Stock are starting materials. Typical examples: Luxturna (retinal dystrophy), Zolgensma (spinal muscular atrophy)
  • Type 3 - Ex vivo cell modification (CAR-T, etc.): this is the most complex type. The FDA regards both the lentiviral vector AND the modified T cells as DS; the EMA regards only the modified T cells as the DS, and the lentiviral vector is downgraded to a critical starting material

Key term: the Master Virus Seed Stock is the reference batch used for large-scale production of the viral vector. Once established and confirmed, it serves as the traceability basis for all subsequent production batches, and when it is exhausted a new seed stock must be re-established and confirmed.

Analogy: The FDA and EMA Naming Divergence

The classification difference between the FDA and EMA is like the US and the UK using different names for the same ingredient:

  • The FDA calls the lentiviral vector (used in CAR-T production) the "main dish (Drug Substance)", requiring a high-specification kitchen (CGMP) and a separate menu page (the DS filing section)
  • The EMA calls the same lentiviral vector a "premium ingredient (Critical Starting Material)", with relatively looser kitchen requirements, but the client (the ATMP manufacturer) is responsible for confirming that the supplier's kitchen meets the quality requirements

Both care about food safety; the regulatory route is simply different. The key point is this: if your restaurant (the CDMO) serves both American and European guests, you must handle this ingredient to the American standard, because the American standard is higher, and that will naturally also satisfy the European requirements.

Practical Application: A CDMO Strategy for Serving Both US and EU Clients

Principle: always manufacture the viral vector as a Drug Substance.

In practice:

  • Establish and maintain all production records to FDA CGMP (21 CFR Parts 210/211) specifications
  • Establish a separate drug substance specification (DS Specification) for each vector, with a complete test list covering identity, purity, strength, and quality
  • Submit a separate DS CMC section in the IND application (to meet FDA requirements)
  • The same set of documents will automatically satisfy the EMA requirements for a "critical starting material" (the EMA requirements are only a subset of the FDA requirements)
  • Establish the Master Virus Seed Stock and Master Cell Bank early, to meet the increasingly strict EU expectations for early-phase clinical trials

This strategy saves not only compliance cost; more importantly, it avoids the clinical trial delay caused by having to remanufacture a seed stock (which can take 6–18 months).

Key Point: The EMA's "Transfer of Responsibility" Logic

The logic behind the EMA's design is worth understanding in depth: because EU regulations currently do not require periodic official inspections of, or GMP certification for, starting material manufacturers (such as CDMOs), the regulator transfers this responsibility to the ATMP manufacturer (that is, the CDMO's client):

  • The client (the licensed ATMP manufacturer) must perform supplier qualification audits on the CDMO
  • The client must define the responsibilities of both parties through a Quality Agreement
  • The client's audit results and the quality of the starting material will be reviewed in the CTA (clinical trial application) and in GMP inspections

This means that even if the CDMO considers itself GMP compliant, if the client has not established an appropriate supplier management procedure, both may be cited with a deficiency in an EMA inspection.

Q 1.4 - GMP and Regulatory Requirements for Vectors as Drug Substances and Starting Materials

Original Text

The FDA CMC guidance states that:

"You should describe whether the vector DS will be formulated into the DP for administration of the genetic material (section V.B.3.b., 'Batch Formula (3.2.P.3.2),' of this guidance) or whether it will be formulated as a bulk DS for ex vivo genetic modification of cells (as outlined in section IV.B., 'Drug Substance and Drug Product,' of this guidance). As an active pharmaceutical ingredient, an appropriate level of control should be applied to each DS, and each DS should be manufactured under current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) conditions that are appropriate for the stage of development (Ref. 11) (7)."

Therefore, clearly the FDA considers the vector an active pharmaceutical ingredient, equivalent to a drug substance, for which phase-appropriate GMP are required.

The EMA provides a more explicit list of factors to include in the risk-based analysis of the starting-material manufacturing which may be useful to consider for this question. These considerations apply to any vector manufacturing process, whether performed by the sponsor or by a contract manufacturing organization. Question 5 of the EMA Q&A directs that consideration should be given to (6):

  • Cross-contamination with other vectors or genetic material
  • Adventitious agents, for example:
    • Transmissible spongiform encephalopathy (TSE) such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease from human donors, or bovine spongiform encephalopathy from bovine serum raw materials
    • Viral contamination or recombination into replication-competent virus
  • Microbiological controls for sterility, mycoplasma, or endotoxin
  • Impurities:
    • Originating from raw materials added to the process (e.g., antibiotics)
    • Generated as part of the process (e.g., addition of transfection enhancers)
    • Carried over into the process (e.g., residual plasmids that are carried over from the vector production into the cell-therapy process)
  • Environmental controls
  • Storage and transportation conditions, including supply-chain complexity
  • Stability

The overall process must be taken into consideration when evaluating the starting materials for any active drug substance that is similar in size to, or larger than, a small virus. This is equally true for a directly administered gene therapy as it is for a viral vector used in ex vivo genetic modification.

For these active substances, removing all risk from adventitious viruses (e.g., parvovirus) from the manufacturing process is not possible and therefore, the adventitious-agent control strategy for the starting material is paramount. While both the FDA and EMA accept animal- and human-derived raw materials (e.g., bovine or human albumin serum), they should be avoided where possible or be gamma-irradiated, unless that process impacts the product quality.

In another example, human-derived raw materials used to manufacture starting materials may have different donor-screening requirements depending on jurisdiction. Sponsors and the ATMP manufacturer should pay special attention to the donor location, screening, testing, and pool size, as well as viral removal and inactivation steps during processing. For example, additional testing may be required for the raw material itself or a negative test may be required from the donor. The control strategy should again address the most stringent requirements from the intended jurisdictions.

As stated in the FDA CMC guidance, "As the sponsor of the IND, you are ultimately responsible for the safety of subjects in the clinical investigation (21 CFR 312.3); therefore, we recommend that you and the [Contract Manufacturing Organization] CMO understand and document your respective responsibilities for ensuring product quality" (7).

To ensure the safety of subjects, it is imperative that the sponsor receive all necessary information and documentation from the CMO required to complete the IND sections relevant to the manufacturing process to properly assess and control product quality and safety. The responsibilities for this knowledge transfer should be delineated within the context of a Quality Agreement to ensure that each organization involved in manufacturing is aware of their activities and how these activities will be conducted in compliance with GMPs. The 2016 FDA Guidance for Industry: Contract Manufacturing Arrangements for Drugs: Quality Agreements provides a framework for establishing a Quality Agreement and includes examples of how quality management principles can be used to aid in the management of a contract manufacturing relationship (8).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Seven Factors of the EMA Q&A Question 5 Risk Analysis

Question 5 of the EMA Q&A requires the manufacturer to perform a risk assessment of starting material manufacture, covering the following seven areas:

  • Cross-contamination: cross-contamination with other vectors or genetic material; tight control is especially needed in multi-product facilities
  • Adventitious agents: including TSE (such as bovine spongiform encephalopathy BSE and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease CJD) and viral contamination (including the risk of forming replication-competent virus, RCV)
  • Microbiological controls: testing and control of sterility, mycoplasma, and endotoxin
  • Impurities: impurities originating from raw materials (such as antibiotic residues), the process (such as transfection enhancers), or carry-over (such as residual plasmid)
  • Environmental controls: cleanroom classification, personnel control, and process environmental monitoring
  • Storage and transportation: including supply chain complexity, where cold chain management is especially critical
  • Stability: stability data for the starting material that support the setting of the shelf life

Key Point: Adventitious Agents, a Risk That Cannot Be Completely Eliminated

The most distinctive challenge of viral vector processes is that the risk of adventitious viruses (adventitious agents) cannot be completely eliminated.

The reasons are as follows:

  • A viral vector is itself a biological macromolecule and cannot be terminally sterilized, because heat or radiation would destroy the vector's function
  • Animal-derived raw materials commonly used in the process (bovine serum albumin, human albumin) may carry latent viruses or TSE agents
  • Small viruses such as parvovirus are highly resistant to common viral clearance steps (such as nanofiltration)
  • Viral recombination may generate Replication-Competent Virus (RCV) during production, which is a major safety risk for gene therapy

The adventitious agent control strategy must therefore use a multi-layered defense: raw material screening → supplier qualification → in-process testing → final product testing. The failure of any single layer can lead to a serious patient safety problem.

Practical Application: Essential Elements of a Quality Agreement for Outsourced Manufacturing

When a CDMO manufactures a viral vector for an ATMP manufacturer, the Quality Agreement is the core document linking the regulatory requirements and the commercial relationship. The key clauses that must be covered are as follows:

  • Division of responsibilities: clearly define who is responsible for raw material procurement, batch release, deviation investigation, change control, and stability studies
  • Documentation obligations: the CDMO must provide all batch records, test results, and equipment qualification reports required for the IND CMC section
  • Adventitious agent control: clearly specify the adventitious agent test items, methods, result acceptance criteria, and the procedure for handling non-conforming product
  • Cross-contamination prevention and control: the CDMO's cleaning validation and changeover procedures must be open to audit by the client
  • Change notification: any process or raw material change by the CDMO that affects product quality must be notified to the client before implementation (the notice period is typically 90 days or more)
  • Right to audit: the client has the right to perform periodic GMP audits of the CDMO (usually once a year)
  • Multi-jurisdiction compliance statement: the CDMO should declare that its process meets the regulatory requirements of the target markets listed by the client

Starting outsourced manufacturing without a sound Quality Agreement is one of the most common types of deficiency in FDA 483 observations and EMA GMP inspections.

Analogy: Phase-Appropriate GMP

Phase-appropriate GMP is like a student's journey of growth:

  • Phase I clinical: like starting university. The teacher is relatively relaxed about the report format, but basic academic integrity (sterility, safety, identity confirmation) cannot be compromised
  • Phase II/III clinical: like graduate school. A stricter thesis format and more detailed methodology are required (process validation, systematic controls)
  • Commercial: like entering the workforce. Every "report" (batch) must meet the most complete professional standard, and any deviation must be explained in writing

But note that at every phase, "Identity" and patient "Safety" are non-negotiable bottom lines, just as academic integrity runs through the whole of one's schooling.

Q 1.5 - Special Considerations for Classification of Plasmids

Original Text

Plasmids are critical raw materials for most gene-therapy-based ATMPs, as shown in Figure 1.3-1. It is imperative that the plasmid contains the desired transgene and associated genetic elements (e.g., promoters) which have the correct nucleotide sequence since any mutation, deletion, or insertion could significantly impact the expressed protein sequence, function, or expression level. This is true for both DNA-containing therapies and mRNA therapies, where the plasmid is reverse-transcribed into the mRNA.

Assessing the classification of plasmid DNA is also important in the context of viral vectors (either for direct injection as a gene therapy or for ex vivo genetic modification of cells) as the viral-vector manufacturing process may use three or more plasmids to mitigate the risk of recombination that could form a replication-competent virus. As examples, AAV- and lentivirus-based vectors have a common manufacturing approach that entails the co-transfection of host cells using multiple plasmids to separately encode the gene of interest, the viral structural proteins, and the viral helper genes required to assemble the viral vectors. As a result of the multiple plasmids, there are multiple sources of error or cross-contamination that could impact patient safety and product efficacy.

Many manufacturers outsource plasmid DNA manufacturing to contract development and manufacturing organizations (CDMOs) which, in turn, manufacture plasmids for different clients with different virus types and sub-types. Potential cross-contamination could occur due to shared equipment and materials, personnel flow, or insufficient cleaning during changeover. Indeed, clinical trials have been put on hold due to contamination of research-grade plasmid used in producing clinical materials. The sponsor needs to ensure that risks are assessed, preventive actions are taken to avoid such potential cross-contaminations, and that appropriate methods are established to confirm and characterize the identity and quality of the plasmids being used in the sponsor's manufacturing process.

The FDA Guidance for Industry: Human Gene Therapy for Neurodegenerative Diseases includes the expectation that plasmids manufactured in a multiproduct manufacturing facility should be tested for the presence of other contaminating plasmids which may have been co-purified (9). Implementation of such testing may pose logistical challenges to the sponsors as CDMOs typically do not share information regarding other clients' production schedules, let alone proprietary information (e.g., plasmid sequence) that might be needed to detect contamination.

As part of the Quality Agreement expectations, sponsors should require their CDMOs to demonstrate robust changeover procedures from product to product, validate cleaning and sanitization processes, and ensure other procedural controls are in place to prevent cross-contamination. Identity and/or purity assays should include criteria that can detect the occurrence of cross-contamination. Sponsors may also consider sequencing the CDMO product periodically, whether a plasmid or viral vector, as part of raw-material receipt by the sponsor's ATMP drug product manufacturer to support a holistic control strategy established to ensure product identity.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Complexity of Multi-Plasmid Systems

To understand why AAV and lentivirus need multiple plasmids, you first need to understand the logic of viral vector manufacturing:

Why are three or more plasmids needed?

If all the viral genes were placed on a single plasmid, the host cell after co-transfection could assemble replication-competent wild-type virus (RCV) on its own, which is an extremely serious safety hazard. The industry practice is therefore to "split" the key genes across three or more plasmids:

  • Transfer Plasmid: carries the therapeutic gene of interest (the transgene) and its regulatory elements (promoter, etc.)
  • Envelope Plasmid: encodes the viral envelope protein (which determines the tissue tropism of the vector, that is, which cells it targets)
  • Packaging Plasmid: encodes the viral structural proteins and the enzymes required for replication, but lacks the packaging signal and so cannot form infectious virus on its own

Only when all of the plasmids are present in the host cell at the same time can a viral vector be assembled; no single plasmid can form infectious virus, which lowers the risk of RCV formation.

Key Point: Plasmid Cross-Contamination at CDMOs, Where Real Clinical Holds Have Occurred

PtC-11 states explicitly: "clinical trials have been put on hold because of the use of contaminated research-grade plasmid to produce clinical material."

This is not a hypothetical risk but an event that has actually happened. Typical scenarios in which cross-contamination occurs:

  • Shared equipment: a CDMO's fermenters, piping, and filtration systems are used for the plasmid production of several clients, and inadequate cleaning carries plasmid from the previous batch over to the next
  • Shared consumables: reagents, buffers, and resins may be cross-used between different batches
  • Personnel movement: operators moving between different products may carry trace contaminants
  • Inadequate changeover: rigor in cleaning validation is insufficient, so complete removal of residual plasmid from the previous batch cannot be assured

The potential impact on patients is extremely serious: if another client's plasmid gets mixed in, the patient may receive a product containing unknown genetic elements, leading to unpredictable gene expression or immune reactions.

FDA expectations (FDA Neurodegenerative Disease Gene Therapy Guidance): plasmids produced in multi-product facilities must be tested for co-purified contaminating plasmids. This requirement is very challenging in practice, because a CDMO normally does not disclose the plasmid sequence information of its other clients.

Practical Application: Sequencing Strategy for Plasmid Identity Confirmation

PtC-11 recommends that ATMP manufacturers periodically perform sequencing when receiving plasmids or viral vectors supplied by a CDMO, to support the overall identity control strategy. A feasible sequencing strategy framework is as follows:

  • At receipt of each batch (Receipt Testing):
    • Restriction Enzyme Digest (RED): quickly confirms that the plasmid configuration is consistent; low cost, and can serve as a routine batch release test
    • Partial Sequencing of key regions: Sanger sequencing of key regions such as the promoter, the transgene coding sequence, and regulatory elements to confirm there are no mutations
  • Periodic Full-Length Sequencing:
    • Use Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) for whole-plasmid sequencing, with a recommended frequency of once per quarter or once every N batches
    • NGS can detect at the same time: sequence mutations, insertions/deletions, and mixed sequences (that is, foreign DNA from cross-contamination)
    • Build a plasmid Reference Database, and compare each sequencing result against it
  • Enhanced testing for the first batch after changeover:
    • For the first plasmid batch after a CDMO changeover, require full-length NGS as an additional condition for routine batch release
    • Require the CDMO to provide the changeover Cleaning Validation Report
  • Supporting Quality Agreement clauses:
    • Explicitly require the CDMO to disclose the categories of other products manufactured in the same facility (the identity of the clients need not be disclosed, but the virus type must be, so that the cross-contamination risk can be assessed)
    • Require that the CDMO's changeover procedures be available for review during audits

Analogy: The Sequence Integrity of Plasmid DNA

Think of plasmid DNA as a precise musical score. The "performance" of gene therapy (viral vector assembly, gene expression) depends entirely on the accuracy of this score:

  • A single wrong note (a single-base mutation) can throw the whole piece out of tune (loss of protein function)
  • A deleted bar (a sequence deletion) can make the performance collapse midway (treatment failure)
  • Passages mixed in from another composer (cross-contamination) can make the audience hear music that is completely unpredictable (the patient receives unintended gene expression)

Sequencing is carefully proofreading every page of the score before the performance, confirming that every note is correct. For gene therapy, "proofreading the score" is not optional but a basic safeguard of patient safety.

Q 2.1 - What is QbD and how does it apply to viral vectors and plasmids?

Original Text

Quality-by-design (QbD), first developed by Dr. Joseph M. Juran, has evolved greatly since its inception and today it is supported by such widely recognized standards as the International Council for Harmonisation's Quality Guidelines ICH Q8(R2): Pharmaceutical Development, ICH Q9: Quality Risk Management, and ICH Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System. The guiding principle of QbD asserts that quality should be designed into a product and into its manufacturing process. By designing the product and process properly, understanding is increased, thereby simplifying the troubleshooting of process and product problems in the future. This perspective is encouraged by the FDA and by other global regulators. This enhanced process knowledge, in turn, simplifies risk-based quality decision-making, as the risk-based decisions are made within the framework of the effective boundaries of the process.

The major deliverables of QbD include:

  • Quality target product profile (QTPP) that identifies the critical quality attributes (CQA)
  • Product design that includes identification of critical material attributes (CMA)
  • Process design that includes identification of critical process parameters (CPP)
  • Control strategy that links CPPs and CMAs to the delivery of CQAs
  • Process capability
  • Continuous quality improvement

Like large-molecule biotechnology products and small-molecule conventional therapies, viral vectors and plasmids benefit from a structured QbD approach to development and continuous improvement. Product development teams make use of prior platform experience and scientific knowledge (e.g., industry standards, published examples) as well as perform studies as needed (e.g., design of experiment) to create the body of knowledge (subject to rugged data analysis) which supports the product throughout its lifecycle. That data is then subject to knowledge management principles and is updated and enhanced by production experience and stability results to support commercialization.

At the end of each phase of pharmaceutical development, stage gates should be defined to encourage revision of the QTPP, control strategy, and control ranges. Final revision of the overall initial control strategy should be performed at the conclusion of the process performance qualification (PPQ). Lifecycle maintenance of the control strategy will then be required until product retirement.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The QbD Deliverables System

QbD is not a single tool but a set of interlocking knowledge-building elements. Understanding the cause-and-effect relationships between the deliverables is the key to mastering the whole quality system:

  • QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile): defined by the MAH/Sponsor, it describes "what quality standard the product must ultimately meet in order to be used safely and effectively in patients". It is the starting point of all subsequent work.
  • CQA (Critical Quality Attribute): derived from the QTPP, it represents those physical, chemical, and biological characteristics that, if outside the range, would significantly affect product safety, potency, or purity.
  • CMA (Critical Material Attribute): which characteristics of materials (for example excipients and starting materials) affect the CQAs? This is the core question of product design.
  • CPP (Critical Process Parameter): which operating parameters in the process (temperature, pH, multiplicity of infection MOI, etc.) directly affect the CQAs? This is the core question of process design.
  • Control Strategy: writes all of the links above into an executable control framework, ensuring that the CQAs of every batch of product are within the acceptable range.

The whole chain, QTPP → CQA → CMA / CPP → control strategy → process capability → continual improvement, is a cycle rather than a linear system.

Analogy: The Architectural Blueprint

The traditional approach to quality control is like "build the house first, then call an inspector to find fault with it", so quality is only "inspected in" at the very end. In the world of ATMPs this is extremely costly: a single batch of viral vector can cost millions of dollars to produce, and if it fails acceptance the loss cannot be recovered.

The QbD mindset is: "when drawing the design blueprint, design in all of the safety standards, structural requirements, and electrical codes." In this way every step of construction has clear quality boundaries, and when a problem arises the root cause can be located quickly, instead of panicking just before shipment.

For a viral vector, the QTPP is the "building permit", the CQAs are the "structural safety requirements", the CPPs are the "construction specifications", and the control strategy is the "acceptance checklist".

Key Point: Stage Gates

PtC-11 specifically emphasizes that a stage gate should be set at the end of each clinical development phase (Phase I → II → III → commercial) to re-review the QTPP, the control strategy, and the scope of controls. This is not red tape, but is needed because:

  • Knowledge in early development is limited, and many CQAs and CPPs are defined on the basis of assumptions or a small amount of data.
  • As more batches accumulate and process understanding deepens, the control ranges should be refined (not relaxed).
  • The final control strategy should receive its last revision after PPQ (process performance qualification) is completed, and then enter the lifecycle maintenance stage.

If a stage gate is skipped, an inadequately defined CQA may be discovered late, requiring expensive comparability studies to explain it to the regulator.

Q 2.2 - MAH responsibilities when working with a CRO or CMO

Original Text

The determination of responsibility for QbD is commonly part of the business agreement and Quality Agreement between the (future) registration holder and the contract research organization (CRO) or contract manufacturing organization (CMO). However, the latitude to shift responsibility from the marketing authorization holder (MAH) or the sponsor to the CRO or CMO is limited as some responsibilities cannot be delegated and must stay with the MAH or sponsor (e.g., disposition decisions). The EMA Reflection Paper on Good Manufacturing Practice and Marketing Authorisation Holders can help in understanding the guiding principles. This reference applies to the European Economic Area (EEA), and compliance with it has brought demonstrated success with other jurisdictions.

When making use of a CRO for the development of the product, the QTPP should be prepared by the Sponsor (i.e., future MAH) and reviewed with the CRO. Since the QTPP represents the development target from a quality perspective, alignment and mutual understanding on its content are essential. Typically, it is then the responsibility of the CRO to conduct the necessary studies to determine the preliminary CQAs, CMAs, and CPPs. The (future) MAH will be required to periodically assess and verify that the recordkeeping associated with the development activities are sufficient to support the development, technology transfer, product registration, and future product disposition.

Ideally, the Quality Agreement should specify how the documentation is developed, recorded, and archived as appropriate to the phase of development. In particular, a written agreement should be struck that identifies the work (both manufacturing and analytics) to be performed under the GxP principles pertaining to the appropriate phase of development/clinical-batch production. It is critical to ensure that there is an explicit agreement about who owns the data and important to define that the Sponsor retains access to the intellectual property (IP) associated with the developed methods or processes. Without an explicit agreement, a risk exists that a successful product may become legally difficult to progress. (For more information, see PDA TR 56 (Revised 2016): Application of Phase-Appropriate Quality System and cGMP to the Development of Therapeutic Protein Drug Substance.)

When the (future) MAH has concluded product development and transfers the product to a CMO for commercialization, the sending unit (MAH) must provide the QTPP, CQAs, CMAs, and CPPs established during development to the receiving unit (CMO). These specifications and parameters will be codified in the technology transfer protocol(s) and then will be introduced into the GMP documents for the CMO (e.g., specifications, master batch records, analytical methods, and analytical method and process validation protocols). Additional updates to the control strategy may be made as the specifics of the equipment, automation, and controls are explored during transfer and subsequent validation. Document control processes will ensure that appropriate change control and version control are maintained for the controlled documents that define the specifications and control strategy.

Comparability will be required for any new site or significant change in product or process. (See Topic 5.) Process capability will then be established with the technology transfer and process validation, becoming the initial benchmark for future continued process validation and annual product review. (For more information, see PDA TR 65 (Revised 2022): Technology Transfer.)

"Without an explicit agreement, a risk exists that a successful product may become legally difficult to progress."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point: IP Ownership, the Most Easily Overlooked Legal Risk

This passage of PtC-11 is very direct: "If an IP agreement is not specified in advance, a successful product may be legally difficult to take forward."

In the ATMP field this risk is especially real, for the following reasons:

  • In early clinical stages many Sponsors rely on an external CRO to develop analytical methods (for example virus titration and potency assays), and these methods are often the CRO's core technology assets.
  • If the Quality Agreement does not explicitly state that the MAH has the right to access, use, or obtain these methods, then when the MAH wants to switch suppliers or manufacture in-house it may face litigation or be held to ransom by the CRO.
  • When reviewing a marketing application, regulators (such as the EMA and FDA) require submission of complete analytical method validation reports; if the MAH has no access to these documents, the application may be unable to be completed.

Practical advice: state explicitly in the Quality Agreement: data ownership, licensing of method use, access to raw data, and data transfer provisions in the event of supplier bankruptcy or termination of the collaboration.

Practical Application, CDMO Perspective: Checklist Before Taking Over a Technology

As the receiving party, a CDMO should confirm that the following have been obtained from the MAH before signing the technology transfer agreement:

Original Text

Before determining which aspects of QbD apply, it is important to understand whether the material will be considered a starting material or an active substance (e.g., drug substance), and the jurisdiction in which it will be regulated. If regulated as an active substance, in specific jurisdictions, the requirements of a CMO as described in Q 2.2 may apply.

However, if regulated as a critical starting material, the requirements may differ. Critical starting materials are required to have an incoming specification and suitable test methods to determine, among other things, the identity, purity, and whatever attributes of potency or functionality can be assessed at this stage in production. There may be requirements for viral and microbiological safety of the material as well. However, risk assessments may be appropriate to evaluate the criticality of the starting material and whether all these requirements should apply. As with all supplied materials, it may be possible to receive some or all of these test results based on the supplier's Certificate of Analysis (CoA) and Certificate of Conformance, provided the supplier has been qualified (and/or audited) specific to their manufacture, control, and testing and subjected to an appropriate risk assessment.

Identity-testing, however, is a phase-appropriate GMP requirement and the nature and extent of the identity-testing performed should be defined and justified. For example, in some cases, partial sequencing may provide sufficient discrimination of identity without having to perform testing of the full-length sequence.

In cases where the manufacturing platform or active substance has not been developed on behalf of the (future) MAH for a specific clinical trial or application, the supplier (i.e., the CMO who supplies the viral vector or plasmid) may hold the intellectual property rights. Global regulatory health authorities may not have a final standardized guidance to address how such a product will be filed and reviewed: therefore, it is important to actively communicate with the CMO and the regulatory health authority in question. In some cases, the supplier may not be willing to provide the same level of detail about the process to the (future) MAH as a true CMO, which may be appropriate given the phase of the product's development. In these cases, the basic details should be made available by the supplier to support a clinical trial dossier. In other cases, regulators may communicate that those details of a manufacturing platform process should be shared to support a marketing authorization. In any case, onsite auditing will be required to inspect the original source data that supports those technical details.

Viral vectors and plasmids are considered critical to the manufacturing process. Even in a jurisdiction that regulates these as starting materials, compliance to the manufacturing process and manufacturing conditions of the supplier are essential quality elements. Therefore, the quality management program of the (future) MAH should appropriately classify the supplier as critical and require a stringent onsite audit to ensure material quality and adherence to GMPs in a phase-appropriate manner. In addition, due to the assigned criticality, some jurisdictions may reserve the power to conduct risk-based inspections of the facility to ensure GMPs are followed.

It is advantageous for these materials to be "banked", which means that characterization of the material, stability during storage, and effective inventory management principles to reduce the risk of mix-up are all essential elements of the supplier- and self-audits. In addition, qualification of the analytical methods and equipment that established the master/working bank(s) must be completed. All these controls should be implemented in a phase-appropriate manner. (For more information, see PDA TR 56 (Revised 2016).)

Whether the viral vector or the plasmid is considered an active substance or a starting material, change control management must be established by the supplier and, as part of the Quality Agreement, details of product, process, or analytical changes must be made known to the MAH or Sponsor prior to change implementation so any impact to the registration or clinical trial application (or its equivalent) may be assessed. Key Concepts: Identity Testing Is the Minimum GMP Requirement

Whatever the regulatory classification of the starting material, identity testing is the minimum requirement that cannot be waived in any GMP setting. The logic behind this is intuitive: you must be able to confirm that "this is indeed what I ordered", otherwise all quality control is empty talk.

For viral vectors and plasmids, the depth of the identity testing method depends on the development phase (phase-appropriate GMP):

  • Early clinical (Phase I/II): partial sequencing is usually sufficient to confirm identity, and full-length sequencing is not required.
  • Late clinical to commercial (Phase III+): more complete sequence confirmation and functional testing may be needed to support the marketing application.

Tests for purity, potency, functionality, viral safety, and so on are also important requirements, but through risk assessment it can be decided whether to rely on the supplier CoA in place of in-house testing, provided that the supplier has passed a full qualification audit.

Analogy: Sourcing Ingredients

Imagine a top restaurant that buys ingredients from an outside farm instead of growing them itself. Even though the farm does the growing, the restaurant must still:

  • Confirm on receipt that "these are indeed the organic tomatoes I ordered, not another variety" (identity testing).
  • Require the farm to provide pesticide residue reports and certificates of origin (CoA / CoC).
  • Visit the farm in person periodically (onsite audit), and not just read the reports.
  • Be notified in advance, before the farm changes its growing method (change control).

If the farm holds an exclusive growing recipe (IP), the restaurant may not be able to know every detail, but must at least know enough to ensure food safety; the regulator (the health department) will also inspect the farm directly, not just the restaurant.

Key Point: When the Supplier Holds the IP, an Onsite Audit Is Still Mandatory

PtC-11 states explicitly that even if a supplier is unwilling to disclose full process details on the grounds of IP protection, onsite auditing is still necessary. This is because:

  • Regulators (such as the EMA and FDA) may require inspection of original source data, which can only be verified on site.
  • The Quality Agreement should include a clause giving the MAH the right to conduct onsite audits, even when the supplier holds the IP.
  • Some regulators retain the authority to conduct risk-based inspections of supplier facilities; the MAH must make sure the supplier is prepared for this.

If a supplier refuses to accept an onsite audit, this in itself is a major red flag in supplier qualification, and the MAH should reassess whether to continue the collaboration.

Practical Application: Change Notification Clauses in the Quality Agreement

PtC-11 requires that, whether the vector or plasmid is classified as a drug substance or as a starting material, the Quality Agreement must establish the supplier's change control notification obligations.

In a CDMO setting, it is recommended that the Quality Agreement explicitly cover the following change notification scenarios:

  • Product level: sequence modifications, changes in the cloning strategy, introduction of a new cell line.
  • Process level: changes in culture medium composition, adjustments to purification steps, equipment replacement.
  • Analytical level: any modification or replacement of a method used for release testing.
  • Facility level: change of manufacturing site, downgrade or upgrade of the GMP classification.

Recommended notification timelines: major changes should be notified to the MAH at least 60 days before implementation; urgent changes should be notified immediately, with supporting documentation supplied afterwards.

Q 3.1 - Control Strategy Requirement and Timing

Original Text

Q 3.1. Are control strategies required for viral vectors and plasmid manufacture? If so, at what stage should the control strategy be available?

Viral vector and plasmid manufacture, whether defined as a critical starting material or as a drug substance, requires a robust, holistic control strategy to drive product quality. The control strategy should link the CPPs and CMAs directly to the CQAs. By understanding which process parameters have a direct impact on the CQAs, appropriate process monitoring and controls can ensure reproducible and predictable delivery of the viral vector or plasmid. PDA Technical Report No. 81 Cell-Based Therapy Control Strategy (TR 81) describes the approach for cell-based therapy control strategies; the strategies and concepts described in PDA Technical Report No. 60 Process Validation: A Lifecycle Approach (TR 60) apply to all pharmaceutical products. The principles discussed in TR 81 and TR 60 can broadly be applied to viral vector and plasmid control strategies as well.

In addition to the principles mentioned above, phase-appropriate implementation may be warranted. For example, the control strategy may consist of preliminary factors to be monitored and assessed in a phase-appropriate manner. As experience with the production of Phase 1 and Phase 2 material is gained, the control strategy may mature while regional differences may dictate how early the control strategy must be formalized. As additional product and process knowledge is gained through clinical results and through continued small-scale and lifetime studies, process ranges must be established and CPPs defined, so that by the time pivotal clinical batches are produced, product and process consistency are assured. When creating a holistic control strategy for plasmid and vector production, inclusion of microbiological and viral control aspects, in addition to the core manufacturing process elements, is important. The microbiological and viral control strategies can either be prepared as separate documents or they may be written within the overall process control strategy. Either approach is acceptable, provided that all stages of the process and all material handling steps are included in the approaches.

Viral and microbiological controls should be in place for the first human clinical trial as they have a direct impact on patient safety. Therefore, these attributes of the control strategy may need to mature earlier than some of the other parameter definitions. In the EU, sterilization and sterility methods are required to be validated at the time of Phase 1 for an Investigational Medicinal Product Dossier. For example, if a vector is defined as a sterile critical starting material, safety-indicating methods (such as sterility and endotoxin) must be qualified. Requirements for a mycoplasma safety-indicating method may vary based on regional requirements. A risk assessment may be needed which takes into consideration the intended market and existing controls. Additionally, the initial control strategy will need to be refined and revised as additional product knowledge and process understanding are gained.

Analytical methods and their associated reference standards should mature along with the control strategy. As CPPs are defined for each associated CQA, the analytical methods that confirm CQA achievement must be demonstrated to be reproducible, accurate, and precise. In this manner, should late-stage process changes or process parameter changes be introduced (within or between sites), comparability and bridging can be accomplished with a high degree of assurance. (See Topic 5.)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Maturity Trajectory of a Control Strategy

Control Strategy is a living document that describes how the process ensures that the CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes) of every batch meet specification. Its core logic is:

  • CMA (Critical Material Attributes) → Which properties of the raw materials affect final quality?
  • CPP (Critical Process Parameters) → Which process parameters, if they deviate, will impact the CQAs?
  • CQA (Critical Quality Attributes) → The quality standards that must ultimately be met (e.g., potency, purity, sterility)

The three form a causal chain: material attributes and process parameters together determine product quality. The control strategy makes every node in this chain explicit and puts monitoring and control measures in place.

Phase-appropriate means that the control strategy does not need to reach commercial-grade rigor at Phase 1. It should mature progressively with clinical development, from preliminary, qualitative monitoring, to clearly quantified CPP ranges, to full process validation.

Analogy - How a Hospital's Infection Control Program Grows

Imagine a hospital under construction.

  • Phase 1 (outpatient clinic opens): Basic hand-washing rules and staff health monitoring are enough. The infection control program is a first draft, focused on the highest-risk items (such as the direct safety of patients exposed to transmissible pathogens).
  • Phase 2 (inpatient wards open): Environmental microbial monitoring and cleaning procedure validation need to be added, and the program becomes more formal and complete.
  • Phase 3 (operating rooms and ICU open): The entire infection control program must be fully in place, including air management, validation of aseptic technique, and environmental certification.

The same applies to the control strategy for a viral vector: as the study moves to later stages (Phase 1 → Phase 2 → pivotal study), the depth and rigor of the strategy must be stepped up. The key point: microbial and viral controls must be in place from Phase 1, because they bear directly on subject safety and cannot be left to "strengthen later".

Key Point - EU vs US Phase 1 Expectations (Essential for Dual-Market CDMOs)

This is one of the most critical regulatory differences for a CDMO serving both the EU and US markets:

  • EU Phase 1 (IMPD requirements): Sterilization methods and sterility methods must be validated by Phase 1. If the product is defined as a "sterile critical starting material", safety-indicating methods such as sterility and endotoxin must be qualified. This is a mandatory requirement.
  • US Phase 1 (FDA expectations): It is sufficient to demonstrate capability; formal completion of simulation challenge studies is not required. Full validation is typically completed in Phase 2 and later.

Practical impact: If a client files an EU IND/IMPD first, the CDMO must complete the more stringent validation work (including APS/APV) before Phase 1. If the filing is for an FDA IND only, capability demonstration can be established initially, lowering the start-up cost. A CDMO with a dual-market strategy should clarify the client's target markets at the very start of the project, to avoid the high cost of catching up on validation later.

In addition, the qualification requirements for mycoplasma test methods differ by region and should be assessed through risk assessment according to the client's target market.

Practical Application - Maturing Analytical Methods in Step with the Control Strategy

The text stresses that "Analytical methods should mature along with the control strategy", which has far-reaching commercial implications for a CDMO:

  • If CPPs have been defined but the corresponding CQA analytical methods are still not reproducible, there will be no data to support a site transfer or comparability exercise, which may draw regulatory challenge.
  • Establishing reproducible reference standards and analytical platforms early is the foundation for later comparability bridging studies. For similar products from multiple clients (such as AAV), platform analytical methods can greatly reduce cost.
  • Refer to PDA TR81 (Cell-Based Therapy Control Strategy) and TR60 (Process Validation Lifecycle) for more detailed strategy frameworks.

Original Text

Q 3.2. What aspects of microbiological control apply to vector and plasmid manufacture?

Phase-appropriate microbiological- and viral-control principles should be applied regardless of whether the product is considered a starting material, active substance, or final product. During the definition of the QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile), it should be established whether the viral vector or plasmid is intended to be sterile or low bioburden. This definition will guide which aspects of microbial control to apply to the process.

After classification as sterile or low bioburden, the microbiological control strategy should then be established based on a systematic assessment of each process step. The assessment should consider the intrinsic and extrinsic sources of contamination with a focus on any open processing. The control strategy must identify appropriate active controls (e.g., incoming testing or in-process treatment, such as filtration) to limit microbiological and viral contamination. In addition, monitoring requirements (e.g., environmental monitoring, bioburden sampling) should be defined and implemented as passive measures to ensure that the state of control is maintained. The intent is to establish both active and passive measures that provide confidence in the assurance of the claim of either sterility or low bioburden.

Aseptic controls are critical requirements for operation. For low-bioburden processes, control strategy development can focus on which aseptic controls are essential to support a compliant low-bioburden material. Open processing and manual manipulation steps should be a particular focus in the microbiological control strategy. Certain factors in functionally closed processes, as described in EU Annex 1, should also be considered.

Whether the end product is considered sterile or low bioburden, all basic elements of a contamination control strategy (CCS) should exist, including:

  • Environmental monitoring program (see PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2022) Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program)
  • Cleaning and sanitization of the environment (see PDA Technical Report No. 70 Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities)
  • Specified personnel and material flow that prevent cross-contamination
  • Gowning
  • Access control
  • Environmental controls (e.g., temperature/humidity monitoring, pressure cascades, routine certification of HEPA filters)
  • Control of raw materials
  • In-process and final-product testing
  • Cross-contamination controls, including viral material

Additional elements of the CCS should be risk-based and specific to the process and the end claim. Examples of differences in control strategy elements, based on a product sterility claim, are presented in Table 3.2-1.

Within a given control strategy, there may be different approaches to achieve the same level of quality. For example, some firms include screening patient materials for bioburden and endotoxin in their control strategy while other firms set aside retention samples to be used in the case of a manufacturing investigation. The utility of the latter approach is that it speeds any possible investigation without consuming day-to-day resources for low-risk, single-use equipment with closed processing. The former approach demonstrates an active surveillance program to detect potential contamination which could be introduced to the facility.

In addition to supporting multiple approaches to the same issue, control strategies may mature or change as development progresses or as experience is gained. For example, during clinical-trial eligibility screening, patients are tested for multiple viruses, serving as a beneficial control. Once commercial production begins, some facilities consider adding adventitious-virus testing, thereby enhancing the surveillance and control. In both cases, the regulatory requirements form the minimum basis of the control strategy.

Many viral vectors and plasmids cannot withstand terminal sterilization or sterile filtration, which puts them in a high-risk category due to the reliance on the aseptic processing to safeguard the sterility of the final product. For sterile processes, aseptic process simulation (APS)/aseptic process validation (APV) is expected to demonstrate appropriate control over aseptic operations. This is the case for both drug substance and drug product, although the power of the study may be less with drug substance. In some jurisdictions, such as the United States, the expectation at Phase I is that the control strategy demonstrates the capability of the aseptic process but does not strictly require a simulation run with a challenge fluid. As the product progresses to Phase II and beyond, the first full validation will be conducted. For other jurisdictions, such as the EU, it is expected in Phase I that the process is fully validated at this early time-point.

Whether water trial or media simulation, the expectation is that the process simulation is risk-based, with an emphasis on all open and manual manipulations as well as sterile holds. Where unit operations or manufacturing steps are considered "functionally closed" (e.g., steps that are closed during processing but not fully closed during support activities such as sampling), simulating these steps is recommended so data can be generated that demonstrates the necessary level of control over the process. As with all aseptic simulation activities, it should be remembered that it is not possible to validate poor aseptic technique, and that the methodology is probabilistic. The APV/APS provides confidence that the process is capable, while the routine monitoring provides the assurance of the day-to-day controls.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point - Sterile vs Low Bioburden: One Decision, Global Impact

The text stresses that whether the product is "sterile" or "low bioburden" must be determined at the QTPP definition stage. This is far more than a field in a document: it is the fork in the road for the entire control strategy:

  • Low bioburden pathway: The control strategy is relatively flexible, with a lower level of rigor and less validation work (for example, release on a supplier CoA may be allowed, and gowning requirements are less stringent).
  • Sterile/aseptic pathway: Requires the full set of measures: a Grade A/ISO 5 environment, APS/APV, qualification of aseptic gowning, and continuous environmental monitoring for every batch. Cost and complexity rise sharply.

Once the classification is set, all subsequent SOPs, facility design, EM program and validation plans must match it. Changing the classification midway through development (for example, from low bioburden to sterile) triggers a large amount of retroactive validation work, delaying the timeline and increasing cost. Therefore, this decision must be made when the QTPP is defined, and backed by sound scientific justification.

Core Concept - The Nine Foundation Elements of a CCS

Whether sterile or low bioburden, the Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) must cover the following nine elements. This is the underlying framework of PDA TR90, and also the site-wide, systematic approach required by EU Annex 1:

  1. EM Program (Environmental Monitoring): Per PDA TR13 (Revised 2022), set alert limits, action limits and investigation procedures.
  2. Cleaning & Sanitization: Per PDA TR70, ensure the environmental microbial load is under control.
  3. Personnel & Material Flow: Physical and procedural measures to prevent cross-contamination.
  4. Gowning: Personnel are one of the greatest sources of contamination risk in aseptic operations.
  5. Access Control: Restrict unauthorized personnel from entering cleanrooms.
  6. Environmental Controls: Temperature and humidity, pressure cascades, periodic HEPA certification.
  7. Raw Material Control: Incoming testing or acceptance criteria for supplier CoAs.
  8. In-Process & Final Testing: Active monitoring to verify the state of control.
  9. Cross-Contamination Controls: In particular, segregation controls for viral materials.

Key Point - Reading Table 3.2-1: The Core Differences at a Glance

The key differences in the comparison table are condensed below, to help you quickly grasp the real divide between the two pathways:

  • APS/APV: Specific to the sterile pathway; not required for the low bioburden pathway. This is the largest gap between the two in validation workload.
  • Gowning requirements: The sterile pathway requires full aseptic gowning qualification; the low bioburden pathway is less stringent.
  • EM intensity: The sterile pathway requires continuous monitoring during open operations for every batch; the low bioburden pathway needs only routine monitoring to confirm room classification.
  • Final product testing: The sterile pathway requires a sterility test + endotoxin; the low bioburden pathway requires bioburden + endotoxin (no sterility test).
  • Open-operation environment: The sterile pathway mandates Grade A/ISO 5; the low bioburden pathway has no such requirement.

Practical Application - How to Document the Sterility Classification Decision in the QTPP

When reviewing an IND/IMPD, regulators pay particular attention to "why this product was defined as sterile rather than low bioburden". The CDMO should help the client prepare the following documentation in support:

  • Product characteristics rationale: Explain why terminal sterilization or sterile filtration is not possible (for example, viral particles are inactivated by high temperature or by filtration shear forces).
  • Route of administration analysis: High-risk routes such as intravenous and intraocular injection usually drive the sterility requirement; a low bioburden pathway for intramuscular or subcutaneous injection needs a stronger scientific rationale.
  • Risk assessment: Apply ICH Q9 methodology to evaluate the degree of patient harm if the product is not of sterile quality.
  • Regional regulatory differences: The same product may have to be defined as sterile in the EU because of IMPD requirements, while a low bioburden classification may be acceptable under a US IND; this needs to be explained in the QTPP.

Analogy - APS Is a Full Dress Rehearsal Before Surgery

Many people misunderstand Aseptic Process Simulation (APS), thinking it is merely walking through the process and swapping the culture fluid for growth medium. Its spirit is closer to this:

The full dress rehearsal that a surgical team performs in the operating room before a real operation: not just reciting lines, but:

  • All personnel must perform the operations while actually wearing sterile surgical gowns
  • All open steps and manual operations must be simulated (including sampling, bag changes, etc.)
  • "Functionally closed" steps (such as opening and closing a sampling port) should also be included in the simulation, because although normally sealed, each time they are opened for sampling they are a potential contamination point
  • The result is probabilistic: passing an APS once does not mean every batch is completely sterile; it only shows that the process has sufficient capability to control contamination risk

Remember: APS cannot validate poor aseptic technique. If personnel's operating habits are themselves a problem, no number of passed APS runs can mask the underlying risk.

Key Point - "Cannot Be Terminally Sterilized" = Highest Risk Level

The text states explicitly that many viral vectors and plasmids cannot withstand terminal sterilization or sterile filtration, which places them in the high-risk category for sterility assurance.

What this high-risk status means:

  • No safety net: Conventional drug products can be steam-sterilized (121°C) after filling; even if there is slight contamination during filling, terminal sterilization provides additional assurance. Viral vectors have no such "second line of defense".
  • Aseptic operation is the only safeguard: Every open step and every manual operation is the only point at which the sterility of the product is decided; once an error occurs, it cannot be remedied.
  • Higher regulatory expectations: EU Annex 1 and FDA sterile products guidance both place stricter aseptic operation controls on products that cannot be terminally sterilized.
  • AAV is an exception: AAV can usually be sterile filtered and is therefore in a relatively lower risk category. Lentiviral vectors, rHSV, retroviral vectors and the like usually cannot be filtered because of their larger particle size or sensitivity to shear.

Practical significance for a CDMO: when evaluating a new client's product, first confirm whether it can be sterile filtered, to determine whether a full aseptic processing facility and full APS/APV support are needed.

Q4.1 - Should filtration be considered as a part of the initial strategy of development and control?

Original Text

For contamination control of proteins and biologically active materials, the use of filtration technologies is commonly preferred over other technologies (e.g., steam sterilization, gamma sterilization, X-ray) because it tends to reduce the impact on the product's quality attributes.

Microbial and viral filtration should be included in the design of the manufacturing process from the earliest conceivable timepoint so that its introduction later in development does not introduce any unintended impact on product quality attributes. Establishing filtration for a process may be challenging, as lentiviral, rHSV, and gamma-retroviral vectors may be damaged by filtration or result in such low potency that a lot may be unusable. While AAV vectors can typically be filter-sterilized, potential product changes due to filtration, such as aggregation, should be characterized.

PDA Technical Report No. 26 Sterilizing Filtration of Liquids (TR 26) and Technical Report No. 41 (Revised 2022) Virus Retentive Filtration (TR 41) provide in-depth discussions of the theoretical and technical elements associated with the filtration of fluids with the goal of removing microbial and viral contamination. EMA Annexes 1 and PIC/S Annex 2A state compliance expectations when the drug product cannot be filter-sterilized, as in the case in cell therapies. Where filtration is not possible, aseptic processing is expected. In addition, the sterile filtration or aseptic processing of starting materials is also expected (e.g., media used for producer cells, transfection agents, other additives).

The decision to aseptically produce without filtration must be accompanied by a risk assessment, including results of the filter development study from the specific product or from platform experience, that demonstrate the product was irreparably harmed by the process of filtration. For more detailed information, see FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing — Current Good Manufacturing Practice and EU Annex 1.

"Microbial and viral filtration should be included in the design of the manufacturing process from the earliest conceivable timepoint..."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Why Is Filtration the Preferred Option for Biological Products?

Filtration is the preferred contamination control method for biologically active materials (such as viral vectors and plasmids), because it is a physical separation process that requires no heat, radiation or chemical reaction, and therefore causes minimal damage to the molecular structure of the product.

By comparison:

  • Steam sterilization: High temperature and pressure inactivate almost all proteins and viral vectors
  • Gamma sterilization: Radiation breaks nucleic acid strands and destroys the genetic material of the vector
  • X-ray sterilization: Similar to gamma; it severely damages biologically active materials

The core advantage of filtration: It physically removes microbial and viral contamination without compromising the product's critical quality attributes (CQAs).

Analogy - Filtration Is Like Straining Soup, Not Boiling It

Imagine you have simmered a pot of carefully prepared stock, with delicate aroma and nutrients (representing the activity of the viral vector). Now you find there may be impurities in the soup (representing microbial contamination).

You have two options:

  • Option A: Boil it again and again (steam sterilization) - The impurities are indeed killed, but the aroma and many of the nutrients of the soup disappear with them
  • Option B: Strain it through a fine-mesh sieve (sterile filtration) - The impurities are retained on the sieve, and the flavor and nutrients of the soup are almost fully preserved

For biologically active materials, "filtration" is that fine-mesh sieve that protects the flavor. But for vectors such as LV (lentivirus), whose particles are so large that they easily get stuck in the sieve, even filtration itself can cause losses. That is the challenge of this question.

Key Point - LV / rHSV Are the Most Challenging Vectors

Lentiviral vector (LV), recombinant herpes simplex virus (rHSV) and gamma-retroviral vector are the three types of vector that are the most difficult to filter in ATMP development, for the following reasons:

  • Large particle size: LV is about 80–120 nm, close to the retention range of a 0.22 µm filter membrane, and is easily retained or damaged by shear forces
  • Fragile structure: The lipid bilayer of an enveloped virus is easily ruptured under high differential pressure
  • Severe potency loss: Filtration often causes a large drop in activity units (transducing units, TU), or even loss of the entire batch

By comparison, AAV (adeno-associated virus) is a non-enveloped virus with a more robust structure and can usually be filtered, but the risk of aggregation still needs to be assessed.

Compliance note: If the decision is not to filter, it must be supported by a risk assessment report and filtration study data (or platform experience data), and must explain that the product is indeed irreversibly harmed by filtration.

Key References

  • PDA TR26: Sterilizing Filtration of Liquids - the theoretical and technical basis of sterilizing filtration of liquids
  • PDA TR41 (Revised 2022): Virus Retentive Filtration - complete guidance on virus-retentive filtration
  • EMA Annex 1 / PIC/S Annex 2A: Compliance expectations when the product cannot be filtered (e.g., cell therapies)
  • FDA Guidance：Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing — cGMP
Q4.2 - What are the key considerations for the control strategy when filtration is considered and when filtration cannot be performed?

Original Text

The following are key elements to deliberate when filtration is considered:

  • Intended purpose of the filtration step (e.g., sterilization, bioburden reduction, viral removal)
  • Type of filter and its material of construction (e.g., depth filter, casted membrane, hollow fiber, cartridge)
  • Process conditions (e.g., temperature, pressure differential, flow rate, nature of the fluid, flush volume)
  • Nature and level of microbial and/or viral contamination
  • Quality impact on the product (e.g., yield, adsorption, viability/activity, inactivation, matrix reactivity, aggregates, extractables/leachables)

These elements must be evaluated as part of the control strategy when filtration is identified as the mechanism to remove adventitious agents (e.g., microbial and viral contamination). These elements should also be considered as part of the demonstration that filtration was not possible if a risk assessment is planned for that purpose. (See PDA TR 26 for additional information.)

Whether the plasmid or vector will be filtered or aseptically manipulated without filtration, the control strategy is expected to minimize opportunities for adventitious agent contamination. To ensure that the control strategy is sufficiently robust, a thorough evaluation of the process from feed to final product is required to ensure the proper controls are in place to prevent adventitious agents from being introduced into the product stream. Each step should be evaluated to identify aseptic manipulations and specify the requirements for either reduced bioburden or sterile/virus-free components.

In early development laboratories, aseptic processing may seem to be impractical, even though GLP and/or GxP quality systems are in place. If vector is produced in an R&D, academic, or process-development lab and later used in first-in-human trials, however, careful consideration through a risk assessment and an appropriate quality assurance review is warranted to evaluate the potential for microbial and viral contamination and patient risks.

PIC/S Annex 2A provides guidance on the design and separation of ventilation systems that should be considered when multiple replication-competent vectors are being developed in parallel with other processes.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Risk Assessment Requirements for a "Do Not Filter" Decision

When a product cannot be sterile filtered, the control strategy must fill this gap. PDA PtC-11 explicitly lists five key elements that must be systematically evaluated when considering filtration or assessing "cannot be filtered":

  • Purpose of filtration (Purpose): Is it sterilization, bioburden reduction, or viral removal? The purpose determines the membrane pore size and validation strategy chosen
  • Filter type and material (Filter type/material): Depth filters, cast membranes, hollow fibers and cartridges each differ in retention mechanism and compatibility
  • Process conditions (Process conditions): Temperature, differential pressure, flow rate, fluid properties and flush volume all affect filtration efficiency and product recovery
  • Nature and level of contamination (Contamination level): The known level of background microbial and viral contamination determines the safety margin required
  • Impact on product quality (Quality impact): Pay particular attention to extractables and leachables (E&L), aggregates, and loss of activity/potency

Key Point - Early R&D-Grade Material Used for FIH Studies Is a High-Risk Compliance Area

This is a known compliance risk area in regulatory inspections. Many CDMOs and academic institutions produce vectors during early development in R&D or process development laboratories, which often:

  • Lack formal aseptic manufacturing facilities
  • Use R&D-grade (non-GMP-grade) raw materials
  • Have not performed complete validation of microbial control

However, once the material is planned for use in a first-in-human (FIH) study, GMP requirements apply immediately. At that point the following are needed:

  • A formal risk assessment of the patient risk from microbial and viral contamination
  • Quality assurance (QA) review to confirm the appropriateness of the manufacturing process

Regulatory warning: There are cases at both the FDA and EMA where non-compliant early vector manufacturing led to delayed IND/CTA review, or even clinical holds.

Analogy - A Control Strategy Is Like a Food Safety Management System

Imagine ingredient management in a restaurant: whether or not the ingredients are finally "cooked at high temperature (= filtration/sterilization)", every step from receiving to serving needs corresponding contamination prevention measures.

If the chef says today, "this dish cannot be heated (= the vector cannot be filtered)", then every link, including the ingredient supply chain, kitchen hygiene and tableware disinfection, needs tighter management. This is exactly the spirit of a control strategy that assesses the whole flow from incoming materials to final product.

Q4.3 - What is the impact of sterile filtration on plasmids and vectors?

Original Text

Removal of microbial contamination is commonly attained by means of 0.1 µm to 0.22 µm membranes. The structure of plasmids and vectors make them susceptible to aggregation during certain processing conditions. Close attention must be paid to steps prior to the filtration that could result in the formation of aggregates as the solutions approach the membrane. If aggregates are formed, they may be retained on the filter resulting in reduced yield and potency.

Working with membrane vendors during development may enable the reduction of harm to the product (e.g., preventing product loss, the formation of aggregates, loss of potency). Fine-tuning the product chemistry and the membrane characteristics may yield improved results. For example, with lentiviral vectors, performing scaled-down studies with 43 mm disc membranes from several different vendors may be beneficial to study the differences in impact that different membranes impart. The use of scaled-down studies economizes on the amount of vector used during development when it is in short supply.

Often dual-layer, asymmetric polyethersulfone (PES) membranes are successful candidates for developing a lentiviral control strategy which includes final filtration.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Application - A Scaled-Down LV Filtration Study Strategy for CDMOs

For a CDMO, filtration development studies for lentiviral vectors (LV) are an important service capability. PDA PtC-11 provides a concrete and economical experimental design framework:

Why use a 43 mm disc membrane?

  • The effective filtration area of a 43 mm disc is about 14.6 cm², far smaller than a production-scale cartridge
  • A proportionally scaled-down experiment consumes only a small amount of precious vector, greatly reducing development cost
  • Membranes from multiple suppliers can be compared in parallel, quickly screening out the best candidate

Why is a PES dual-layer asymmetric membrane the first choice?

  • Polyethersulfone (PES): Low protein adsorption, suitable for high-value biological products such as viral vectors
  • Dual-layer: The upstream layer retains large particles (aggregates) and the downstream layer completes the fine filtration, reducing the risk of clogging
  • Asymmetric: The pore size narrows gradually from the inlet face to the outlet face, increasing retention efficiency and extending membrane life

Commercial implication for a CDMO: If a client's vector cannot be filtered, the entire batch may be unusable, with great loss. Investing in a scaled-down study up front is the best investment for reducing the risk of failure at final scale.

Key Point - Pre-Filtration Steps Are Equally Critical

PDA PtC-11 specifically stresses that the cause of aggregation is often not the filter membrane itself but the process conditions before filtration. The following need particular monitoring:

  • Buffer exchange conditions
  • Shear forces in the concentration step
  • Storage temperature and time (freeze-thaw cycles)
  • Changes in fluid pH and ionic strength

If aggregates have already formed before filtration, they will be retained on the membrane, directly causing reduced yield and potency loss, for which the filter membrane itself is not "responsible".

Analogy - Membrane Screening Is Like Choosing the Track for a Specific Athlete

Different filter membranes are like running tracks of different materials: some are friendly to a long-distance runner (the large-particle LV), while others easily trip them up (retention). A scaled-down study lets the athlete trial-run every track before the real race, to find the most suitable one. The economy of the 43 mm disc lets you complete the track selection at the lowest "trial run cost".

Q4.4 - Why is the use of nanofiltration to mitigate viral contamination relevant?

Original Text

Virus-retentive filters are designed to remove potential viruses by means of size exclusion; the nominal pore size in these filters is 0.1 µm. These filters are used in ATMPs to process culture media or buffers used to culture or expand cells where viral contamination will be detrimental to the product, and they should be considered as part of the overall adventitious virus control strategy.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Role of Nanofiltration in ATMP

Nanofiltration, more precisely called virus-retentive filtration, is used in ATMP manufacturing in a different setting from ordinary sterile filtration:

Application: It is used not to filter the product containing the viral vector directly, but to filter culture media and buffers, which, once contaminated with virus, would seriously jeopardize cell culture or virus amplification.

Principle: With a nominal pore size of 0.1 µm, it retains virus particles by size exclusion, removing adventitious viruses that may be present in the culture medium.

Position in the control strategy: As one part of the overall adventitious virus control strategy, it works together with source material testing, supplier qualification and other measures to form a layered defense.

Key Point - Nanofiltration vs. Sterile Filtration

Two concepts that are easily confused:

  • Sterile filtration: Uses a 0.22 µm (or 0.1 µm) membrane to remove microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) and ensure the product is sterile. The 0.22 µm membrane is the most common standard for sterile filtration.
  • Virus-retentive filtration / Nanofiltration: Uses special membranes with a pore size of 0.1 µm or smaller to remove virus particles; typically used for virus removal from culture media/buffers, not for rendering the final product sterile.

In ATMP manufacturing, both may appear in the process, but they are applied to different fluids and serve different protective purposes.

Analogy - Nanofiltration Is the Virus Safety Net of the Ingredient Supply Chain

Continuing the restaurant analogy: nanofiltration is like virus testing and filtering of the water source and sauces before ingredients enter the kitchen. However skilled the head chef (final product filtration) is, if the supply chain (culture media/buffers) itself carries virus, the cell culture of the whole batch may be contaminated, causing irreparable loss.

Q4.5 - Does the sterile filter have to be the last step in the process before filling?

Original Text

Ideally, the point of filtration should come after all significant additions and manipulations of the product, which naturally places the filtration step as the last activity prior to filling. If a different position within the process is warranted (e.g., based on harm to the product or excessive product loss during filtration), a risk assessment may be possible to support an earlier position for filtration in the process.

The final position for the filtration and its justification should be part of the formal control strategy.

"The final position for the filtration and its justification should be part of the formal control strategy."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Design Principles for Filter Placement

The ideal position for sterile filtration is the last step of the process, immediately before filling. The logic of this design is:

  • All operations after filtration are potential recontamination points
  • If there are additions or manipulations after filtration, the product has been exposed to a potentially contaminated environment without the protection of filtration
  • "Last-step filtration" confirms sterility at the checkpoint closest to the patient

Key Point - Filtration Not at the Last Step Must Be Clearly Documented in the Control Strategy

If the filtration step must be placed earlier (earlier position) because of product harm or excessive product loss, then:

  • A risk assessment must be performed to quantify the incremental contamination risk of earlier filtration
  • The risk assessment results and alternative control measures (compensating controls) must be clearly documented
  • The choice of filtration position and its justification must be included in the formal control strategy document

Regulatory insight: In FDA and EMA GMP inspections, the position of the filtration step and its justification are among the items always checked. An earlier-filtration decision that is not adequately documented is a common source of FDA 483 observations.

Analogy - Last-Step Filtration Is the Final Door Lock Before Leaving the House

In aseptic manufacturing, filtration is like the last lock on the door before you leave. Ideally, you lock the door (filtration) at the very moment you step out (filling), ensuring no intruder is left inside the house.

If for some reason (say, the lock is so heavy that it would damage the door frame, representing product harm) you must lock the door earlier, then you need other safety measures (surveillance cameras, alarms, representing compensating controls) for the period between the door being shut and actually leaving, and you must record in writing why you did it this way.

Q4.6 - In early vector development, what is the timing for the decision on whether to aseptically process or sterile filter?

Original Text

The decision to filter or to aseptically process should be made as early as practicable in the design of the product. The earlier the decision is made, the easier it will be to ensure that the appropriate control elements are included in the CCS and that the necessary unit operations are protected as required to fulfill the design requirements. However, the decision to not include a sterile filtration step requires justification, appropriate risk assessment, and data to support the risk assessment, therefore, the timing may depend on the collection of data and justification of the approach.

Ultimately, the final approach should be codified within the QTPP, the control strategy for the process, the microbiological and virological CCS, and the facility controls.

Early vector development commonly occurs in shared facilities that may have more than one standard for processing: therefore, careful procedural controls should be in place for each material developed that reflect the appropriate use of those materials (e.g., animal studies, tox studies).

Different regulatory authorities have varying stances on sterilization process validation and the timing relative to the development of clinical materials. For instance, in Europe there is an expectation that sterilization validation will be complete and available prior to Phase I (see Q 4.1). The regulatory requirements for the jurisdictions selected by the MAH may therefore influence the timing of the decision on aseptic processing or filtration.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Rationale for Deciding Early

"The earlier you decide, the easier it is to implement" is the core logic of this question. The choice of filtration or aseptic processing directly determines:

  • Facility requirements: Whether a high-grade aseptic facility such as RABS / isolator is needed
  • Equipment configuration: Whether a filtration system and integrity testing equipment (integrity tester) need to be installed
  • CCS architecture: The overall design of the contamination control strategy (from the microbiological and virological perspective)
  • Validation plan: The timeline for filter validation or aseptic process simulation (APS)

If the decision is made late in development, facilities, equipment and process may need major modification at very high cost.

Analogy - Early Decisions Are Like Choosing Between Timber and Reinforced Concrete Before Building a House

The choice between filtration and aseptic processing is like the first structural system you decide on when building a house: timber (light but more limiting) or reinforced concrete (heavy but highly flexible).

Once this decision is made, all subsequent design (piping, equipment, validation plan) must be built around it. If you change your mind when the house is nearly finished, much of the earlier work has to be torn down and redone, with enormous loss of time and money.

Q4.7 - When sterile filtration is not an option, at what point in the process is aseptic processing needed?

Original Text

In an aseptic process, the materials used in the process are sterilized separately (e.g., filtration, heat, irradiation) and then brought together in an aseptic manner. The point where aseptic manipulations must be conducted should be as far downstream in the process as practicable. In this manner, there is a high degree of sterility assurance for the individual components and the aseptic processing steps (the aseptic boundary) are kept to the absolute minimum.

"The point where aseptic manipulations must be conducted should be as far downstream in the process as practicable... the aseptic processing steps (the aseptic boundary) are kept to the absolute minimum."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Structural Principle of Aseptic Processing

The core logic of aseptic processing is "sterilize separately, combine aseptically":

  1. Each individual component (container, buffer, active ingredient) is sterilized separately by an appropriate method (filtration, moist heat, irradiation, etc.)
  2. The sterilized components are assembled in a controlled aseptic environment
  3. From the start of the assembly operation to the completion of final sealing, this entire segment is the scope of the aseptic boundary

Key design principle: The smaller the aseptic boundary, the better. Every operational step inside the aseptic boundary is a potential contamination risk point; the larger the boundary, the more risk points have to be controlled, and the harder it is to achieve sterility assurance.

Therefore, aseptic operations should be deferred as far downstream as practicable, so that upstream processing is completed under non-sterile conditions and the aseptic environment is entered only for the last few critical steps.

Analogy - The Aseptic Boundary Is Like the Open Area of an Operating Room

Imagine a surgical operation: the operating room (the aseptic boundary) is the space where contamination is controlled with extreme strictness. The smaller the operating room and the shorter the operation, the fewer the opportunities for contamination.

When designing an aseptic process, the goal is to shrink the "operating room (aseptic boundary)" to the minimum: have the pre-operative preparation (upstream processing) done in outer spaces as far as possible, and enter the strictest aseptic zone only for "the surgery itself (the last few aseptic operation steps)".

In other words: do not turn the whole hospital into an operating room; keep only those few square meters around the operating table at the highest aseptic standard. This is the essence of "deferring the start of the aseptic boundary as far as possible".

Key Point - The Practical Significance of Minimizing the Aseptic Boundary

For ATMPs that cannot be sterile filtered (such as LV vectors), the optimization goal of process design is:

  • Within what is reasonably practicable, complete as many process steps as possible (cell culture, virus production, initial purification) outside the aseptic boundary
  • Have the point of entry into the aseptic boundary as close as possible to the final filling step
  • For each individual component entering the aseptic boundary, ensure that each has already met sterilization requirements (individual filtration, autoclaving, irradiation, etc.) before it is brought in and combined within the aseptic boundary

Compliance note: The definition and scope of the aseptic boundary must be clearly documented in the contamination control strategy (CCS) and validated through aseptic process simulation (APS).

Q 5.1 - How are bridging studies different from comparability studies?

Original Text

Bridging and comparability are terms commonly used during the development and registration of a new product when comparing and assessing differences between processes or materials. They are used separately (e.g., bridging of reference standards, comparability of lots from different sites) and, more confusingly, can also be used together such as in ICH Quality Guideline Q5E: Comparability of Biotechnological/Biological Products Subject to Changes in their Manufacturing Process where there is a reference to a "comparability bridging study." Therefore, it is important to first define these terms in order to offer clarity about their objectives and execution.

Bridging is the process of relating earlier data to later data in a way that the ability to extrapolate earlier results to later results is maintained. Common usage includes bridging of clinical studies, bridging of reference standards, or bridging from one test method to a new version of that test method.

Comparability is the process of comparing technical data from a reference population to a new population for the purpose of ensuring equivalence in quality, efficacy, and/or safety. Common usage includes comparability of product manufactured by different manufacturing sites, particularly when launching at a new site. It may also include comparability (comparing) product quality attributes and patient safety data after a significant process change.

In many situations, bridging is achieved when analytical comparability of product quality or analytical method outputs before and after a change is demonstrated. When comparability is not demonstrated by only analytical testing, additional nonclinical and clinical bridging studies are needed.

"A study performed to provide nonclinical or clinical data that allows extrapolation of the existing data from the drug product produced by the current process to the drug product from the changed process." — ICH Q5E on comparability bridging studies

In early phases of development, establishing in-house or interim reference standards that are useful for the development work, but are not intended for commercial use, is common. When establishing a primary reference standard and subsequent working reference standard, conducting bridging studies of the reference standards to be able to relate lot test results from early safety and clinical work to the testing from batches that represent the commercial process is essential.

Similarly, test methods might evolve throughout the development and commercialization program as they are refined or improved (e.g., enhanced method controls or automation, improved precision, greater limits of quantification or detection). Bridging of methods ensures that results from earlier generations of test methods can be interpreted in the context of results generated with the new versions of the test methods.

Nowhere is bridging more critical than for stability studies. Data generated with reference standards or test methods at earlier timepoints must be preserved through an understanding of the bridging to the current methods and, therefore, the results at the current and future timepoints. Often stability studies can be performed with both the old and new methods (and old and new reference standards) in parallel from the point of introduction of the new method through the endpoint of the study.

Comparability is performed to assess the quality, efficacy, and/or safety attributes of a lot(s) against other lot(s) that are differentiated by process, site of manufacture, raw material, or other potentially significant difference.

Some important points to consider for bridging and comparability include the following:

  • Ensure that bridges and comparability are always maintained to clinical materials, especially the pivotal lots that will demonstrate efficacy, safety, and/or immunogenicity
  • Keep careful track of which lots were tested with which version of the assay or reference standard and know what those differences might mean in terms of bias or offset
  • Establish written guidelines for when CoAs will or will not be updated based on the introduction of new test methods or new reference standards, and ensure that there is a clear signifier on the CoA that traces to its version that permits ready traceability to the testing that was used to generate the values
  • Ensure that with each intended change there is a planned strategy for bridging stability data to new methods and reference standards to preserve the integrity of earlier timepoints, before the change is implemented
  • Use bridging and comparability protocols to define the criteria by which comparisons and conclusions are to be made
  • Ensure that an inventory of clinical lots, reference standard lots, and other representative lots are set aside and maintained for the purpose of establishing the essential bridges required for the program

Establishing standard operating procedures that include these requirements is one way to ensure success in demonstrating that product, processes, reference standards, and/or analytical results are consistent and traceable.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Bridging = a dictionary that translates across eras

Imagine you looked up a word in an old 1980s dictionary, but the definition of that word has since been updated. "Bridging" is like lining up the definitions in the old and new dictionaries to confirm that the meaning you found back then still holds today. Past stability data and reference standard test results all need this kind of "translation" to remain acceptable today.

Comparability = a blind side-by-side tasting of two wines

The same winery produces wine in two different vintages (or at two plants in different countries), and you taste them side by side to see whether the quality and flavor are the same. That is comparability: comparing two batches of product made under different conditions at the same time, to confirm equivalence.

Key Point: The Stability Bridging Plan Must Be Drawn Up in Advance

The source text specifically stresses that the stability data bridging strategy must be planned "before the change is implemented".

This is one of the most common mistakes in the industry: only after the analytical method has been upgraded and the new reference standard put into use does the team realize that the stability time-point data of the past three years cannot be linked to the new method. By then it is too late to fix: the old batches may have exceeded their shelf life or the samples may be used up, so a bridging study cannot be done at all.

Practical conclusion: before a method change or reference standard update, the change control document must include a "stability bridging plan" section.

Practical Application: Retention Sample Inventory Management Strategy at a CDMO

For a CDMO, managing a client's retention sample inventory is a high-value, high-risk activity. The following management strategies are recommended:

  • Clinical Lot Inventory: Set up a dedicated register recording, for each clinical batch, the sample location, remaining quantity, expiry date, and the test versions already performed
  • Reference standard version tracking: The CoA of each batch should state the reference standard version used at the time, and a version-to-batch cross-reference should be established in the LIMS
  • Analytical method version mapping table: Maintain a matrix of which batches were tested with which method version, so that after a method upgrade it is quick to identify which batches need a bridging study
  • Explicit SOP provisions: Define when a CoA needs to be reissued and when it is enough to add a version note, to avoid different QA staff interpreting this differently
  • Approval for use of retention samples: Every draw on the retention sample inventory needs a written protocol stating the purpose of use, projected remaining quantity, and replenishment plan

Core Concept: Bridging vs. Comparability

These two terms are often used interchangeably in industry, but there is a clear conceptual distinction:

The "comparability bridging study" of ICH Q5E combines the two: comparability is used first to confirm equivalence, and the bridging study then allows extrapolation of prior nonclinical/clinical data.

Q 5.2 - How can a change control strategy compare or utilize test results from two sites, two batches, or clinical to PPQ material?

Original Text

Demonstration of comparability of product quality attributes and patient safety data from product manufactured under different conditions (e.g., different development stages, different site of manufacture, different analytical methods) is critical to prove that previous preclinical toxicology, nonclinical, and clinical study results are still relevant. Further, demonstration of comparability will prove that the same control strategy, specifications, and shelf life can still apply to the post-change products. Therefore, ICH Q5E is also applicable to ATMPs.

For analytical comparability, typically three levels are involved:

The first level is lot-release testing, where comparability criteria should be established based on historical data generated from materials manufactured before the change. In many cases, the comparability criteria might need to be tighter than the release specifications. To meet the predefined comparability criteria, not only should all lots made from the new process meet release specifications, but they also should be within the comparability criteria.

The second level is characterization testing, wherein additional characterization assays should be performed in addition to the lot-release testing to demonstrate there are no other quality attribute changes that are not detected by lot-release testing. A significant change in any quality attribute (critical or noncritical) may suggest there may be other underlying changes that could pose risks to product safety and efficacy.

The third level is stability comparability, which could include stability studies at recommended storage conditions and/or accelerated/stress stability studies. Comparability of stability (both degradation rate and profiles) should justify that the previously established shelf life can still be applied to the post-change product. In addition, accelerated/stress stability studies are considered a "pressure test" to reveal any differences that may not be detected under long-term storage conditions during routine analysis. Such forced degradation studies are commonly done for therapeutic protein products during a comparability assessment.

The stringency of the comparability exercise increases as the product progresses into later stages of development. Comparability of early preclinical/toxicology to first clinical materials enables the first-in-human clinical trial, while comparability between pivotal clinical materials and validation (process performance qualification (PPQ)) is essential to a smooth Biologics License Application (BLA) review and approval process.

Many ATMPs are for rare disease indications, so the number of lots manufactured at early stages may be limited and it could be difficult to establish meaningful comparability acceptance criteria with such a limited data set. Increasing the number of replicate tests for the same material could help set criteria that take assay variability into consideration, which is especially important for critical, yet highly variable, biological assays (e.g., titer and potency).

Unique challenges exist for ATMP comparability studies. The quantity of ATMP samples is typically limited and may not be sufficient for a comprehensive comparability testing plan. However, new analytical technologies (e.g., charge detection mass spectrometry, small-volume subvisible particle method) that consume only small sample volumes are advantageous as their use will minimize the amount of product used from the manufactured lot.

As the focus of the process/product comparability exercise is to demonstrate product comparability, certain practices can be adopted during analytical testing:

  1. Test pre- and post-change products side-by-side within one laboratory and within one run/plate/sequence, if possible, to minimize assay variability. Assay setup can be adjusted to accommodate such needs (e.g., splitting two samples equally between two plates instead of using one plate for each sample).
  2. Use reagents (especially critical reagents) from the same vendor or even use the same lots where possible.
  3. When side-by-side testing is not possible due to material scarcity and product stability, it is beneficial to include a historical sample(s) (e.g., reference standard or assay control) in the testing of post-change products to monitor potential assay drift and justify the apparent difference it causes.

Core Concept: The Three-Level Comparability Framework

Under the ICH Q5E framework, analytical comparability has three progressive levels, with rigor increasing at each level:

Level 3: Stability Comparability
Includes accelerated stability and forced degradation studies, confirming that the shelf life still applies
▲ Most rigorous

Analogy

The three-level framework = the three checkpoints of vehicle safety certification before a car leaves the factory

Level 1 (lot release) = the standard pre-delivery safety check, confirming that basics such as steering and brakes work, but this is only the minimum threshold. Level 2 (characterization testing) = more comprehensive engineering tests, including vibration testing and material fatigue analysis, to find latent problems the standard check cannot see. Level 3 (stability) = a long-term road simulation test, confirming that the car will be as reliable five years later as when new, which corresponds to the "stress test" concept of accelerated stability. Only when all three checkpoints are passed is it truly comparable.

Key Point: The Unique Challenge of Scarce ATMP Samples

Comparability studies for ATMPs (advanced therapy medicinal products) face a fundamental challenge that conventional biopharmaceuticals do not: extremely limited sample quantity.

For a rare-disease gene therapy product, only 2-3 batches may have been produced in early Phase I, with just a few vials per batch. These limited samples must support analytical method development, reference standard establishment, stability studies and nonclinical toxicology studies, in addition to comparability testing, which is simply not enough.

Industry coping strategies include:

  • Use low-consumption techniques such as charge detection mass spectrometry
  • Increase the number of replicate tests on the same sample, using statistical methods to compensate for the small number of batches
  • Establish an assay control early, as a bridge for comparison across time points

Practical Application: Details of Executing Side-by-Side Testing

"Same plate, same reagents, same time" is the golden rule of comparability testing. In practice:

  • Same run/plate/sequence: Place the pre-change and post-change samples on the same ELISA plate or in the same mass spectrometry sequence, eliminating run-to-run reagent differences and instrument drift
  • Sample plate-splitting technique: If one plate cannot hold all the samples, split each of the two samples across two plates (half on each), rather than putting one sample on an entire plate. That way, even if there is plate-to-plate variation, both samples are affected symmetrically
  • Critical reagent lot control: For critical reagents such as antibodies and cell lines in particular, reserve enough of the same lot in advance for the whole comparability study period, to avoid introducing extra variables from a lot change midway
  • Alternative when same-plate testing is not possible: Include a historical reference standard or assay control in the new batch's testing to monitor assay drift, and quantitatively calculate the effect of the drift on the test results

Key Point: BLA Review Risk - The Consequences of a Failed Pivotal Comparison

The source text specifically warns that even the most thorough comparability assessment cannot fully eliminate the risk that regulators will later request an in vivo assessment.

A more serious situation: if comparability between pivotal clinical batches and PPQ (process performance qualification) batches is not adequately established, regulators may request the following during BLA review:

  • A supplementary bridging clinical study, which may delay the launch by one to two years
  • Revision of the release specification, requiring resubmission of technical documentation
  • A post-approval commitment attached to the approval conditions

Conclusion: the return on investment (ROI) of comparability studies is very high; investing adequately in a comparability plan up front saves far more time and cost than remediating afterwards.

Q 5.3 - What approaches can be used when it is necessary to change test methods during a comparability protocol?

Original Text

Changing test methods during product development is common. This is especially true for ATMPs as these therapies are relatively new and the analytical methods and associated controls necessary to fully monitor and assure product quality are still lagging behind other biological products, such as monoclonal antibodies.

Test methods should be defined and understood prior to assessing comparability. Therefore, it is undesirable to change both the methods and the attribute to be studied for comparability (e.g., site of manufacture); however, it is understood that ongoing development may necessitate changing or updating test methods at times. When updates are necessary, method bridging can be used as one approach to understand changes in test methods. Method bridging studies can be accomplished by testing the same set of samples by both methods. The method bridging is important to demonstrate method comparability. Including all relevant sample types (especially the stability sample) is essential to ensure thorough understanding of the method differences.

While less than ideal, it is acceptable to have bias between methods as long as the impact of the bias to the control strategy—in-process controls, release and stability specifications, and comparability criteria—is adequately assessed. Well-characterized method bias should be factored in when setting up comparability acceptance criteria if historical data generated using the old method will be used to compare against new data generated with the new method.

Both process comparability exercises and method bridging studies highlight the importance of keeping retention samples. This is extremely challenging for ATMPs since, during early development, the limited material available is needed for various purposes by multiple teams (e.g., analytical development, reference standard, stability, preclinical studies). For some of the assays, a platform approach or a product independent sample/control can be adopted. For a host protein or a DNA assay, for example, a sample from a different product using the same or similar manufacturing platform process could be used in the method bridging study.

Sample stability after long-term storage needs to be taken into consideration when side-by-side retesting is performed on historical samples together with recently generated samples. Differences may arise from the stability storage of the historical sample retained. In such situations, historical results or profiles at, or close to, time-zero samples should be leveraged to demonstrate comparability.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: How Method Bridging Works

The essence of method bridging is to use a "common sample set" as a bridge connecting the result spaces of the old and new methods. The steps are:

  1. Select the bridging sample set: It should include a representative variety of sample types: high, mid and low value samples; different batches; and stability samples (especially important)
  2. Parallel testing: Test the same set of samples with both the old and new methods at the same time, to obtain paired data
  3. Calculate method bias: Statistically analyze the systematic bias and the difference in precision between the two methods
  4. Assess the scope of impact: Evaluate the effect of the bias on the release specification, the stability specification, and the comparability acceptance criteria
  5. Update the control strategy: If needed, adjust the corresponding acceptance criteria, and document the size and direction of the bias

Key principle: a bias between methods is acceptable, but it must be a well-characterized bias, not an unknown uncertainty.

Analogy

Historical stability samples = old wine slowly changing in the bottle

Imagine you seal a bottle of wine, planning to taste it five years later to see whether it is still the same as five years ago. The problem is that even if you change nothing, the wine itself keeps evolving in the bottle: the tannins soften and the aroma changes. When you re-analyze it five years later with a new tasting technique, does the difference come from the "change in tasting technique" or from the "natural evolution of the wine in the bottle"?

Historical stability samples of ATMPs face the same problem: long-term frozen storage also causes slight sample degradation. Therefore, when using historical samples for method bridging, the "storage effect" must first be subtracted, so that the source of the difference can be attributed correctly.

Key Point: A Platform Approach as an Alternative When Retention Samples Are Scarce

When ATMP retention samples are completely inadequate to support a method bridging study, a platform approach offers a practical alternative strategy:

Core idea: For "product-independent" analytical attributes, samples of a "different product on the same platform" can serve as surrogate material for the bridging study.

Examples of applicability:

  • Host cell protein (HCP) testing: If two different AAV products both use HEK293 cells as the production system, the HCP standard of one product can provide surrogate samples for HCP method bridging of the other
  • Residual host cell DNA testing: DNA samples from the same cell line source can serve as shared method bridging material for different products

Limitations and cautions: This alternative applies only to the purpose of "method performance verification" and cannot replace comparability conclusions that depend on specific product characteristics. Regulatory acceptance needs to be assessed case by case.

Key Point: The Golden Rule - Do Not Change the Method and the Process at the Same Time

The source text states explicitly: "it is undesirable to change both the methods and the attribute to be studied for comparability"

This is a golden rule, and the logic behind it is:

If, during a comparability study, you change both the "process/site being compared" and the "analytical method used for the comparison", then any difference observed cannot be clearly attributed: is it caused by the process difference, or by the method difference? The two are confounded, and the comparability conclusion loses its persuasiveness.

Practical recommendation: If the process change and the method upgrade must happen at the same time, complete method bridging first, to establish the equivalence relationship between the old and new methods, and only then begin the process comparability assessment. That way, even though the methods differ, the results can be converted to a common basis through the known bias relationship.

Q 6.1 — How can platform technologies be used to improve quality and efficiency in the development of ATMP products?

Original Text

A platform is a model process (or model analytics) from which variations are developed. The term "platform" was adopted as the model represents the jumping-off point for any new development efforts. For example, a model process would have identical unit operations for the majority of the process steps with the same process controls (the process backbone) with only targeted changes, such as an adjustment to an individual process parameter or setpoint, to adapt to the needed conditions specific to the biological entity (e.g., strain).

Within the industry, the term platform may also be applied to specific unit operation equipment technology. Although used in this context, the terminology may be imprecise in some cases, as the use of a particular piece of equipment or group of equipment does not ensure that the same process or analytics are maintained. While these equipment "platforms" can help to standardize and streamline qualification, validation, and overall control strategies, they may not provide the same level of regulatory efficiency that are being described in this section.

The development of multiple products based on one or more common technologies, or platforms, offers an approach to effectively use prior knowledge from existing products as new products are developed. The interest in pursuing platform technologies has risen significantly in the last several years, particularly for ATMPs, as these products are typically developed under aggressive timelines due to the nature and promise of the therapeutic effect. The use of platforms can allow for:

  • Standardization during manufacturing process development
  • Standardization during development of analytical methods
  • Establishment of harmonized CQAs and the associated risk-based assessments
  • Establishment of consistent process and analytical control strategies
  • Harmonization of regulatory submission content and technical support documents
  • Reduced costs and timelines associated with development activities
  • Reduced time for review and approval by regulators (when prior art has already been approved)
  • Efficient process qualification approaches (e.g., the inserted coding region in a common viral-vector or plasmid drug substance is produced and purified using the same overall production and control processes)

The U.S. government has pilot programs promoting the development of platform technology (e.g., the "Paving the Way for Rare Disease Gene Therapies" project) as it is understood that the benefits to patients from the therapies, and the possible shortening of the time to market (which for traditional development pathways has been more than 7–10 years), is in the best interest of the public.

The use of a technology platform also contributes to enhanced process knowledge on an ongoing basis as data from additional molecules become available and can be used to further characterize and refine a platform.

The use of platforms is consistent with QbD (see Topic 2) and robust control strategies (see Topic 3) as there is a heavy emphasis on prior knowledge and a reliance on effective knowledge management to collect and organize the critical data that ensures appropriate conclusions can be drawn to strengthen the knowledge of the design space (i.e., the combination of critical material attributes and critical process parameter ranges that define the successful product manufacture) and the performance of the process.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Process Platform vs. Equipment Platform

Process Platform: A standardized model process with the same unit operations, the same process control logic (process backbone) and consistent analytical methods. For each new product, only minimal parameter adjustments are needed for the specific biological entity (for example, a different serotype or gene of interest, GOI), so the process does not have to be designed from scratch. This is the "true platform" that PtC-11 emphasizes in this section.

Equipment Platform: Standardization of specific equipment or equipment sets only (for example, the same brand of bioreactor or chromatography system). The document states explicitly that the same equipment does not mean the same process. An equipment platform can help simplify validation and the control strategy, but the regulatory efficiency it brings is far smaller than that of a process platform.

In one sentence: An equipment platform is "the same kitchen"; a process platform is "the same recipe, steps and quality checks, with only the ingredients changed".

Platform Economics: Quantified Advantages in Cost and Timeline

Traditional ATMP drug development from IND to marketing typically takes 7–10 years; platform technology can compress time and cost in several dimensions:

  • R&D cost: Shared CQA definitions, risk assessment frameworks and analytical method development greatly reduce duplicated work.
  • Validation/qualification cost: A matrix approach to PPQ allows Process Performance Qualification to be completed with fewer batches.
  • Regulatory timeline: Approved prior art makes reviewers familiar with the platform architecture, which in theory can shorten the review cycle.
  • Compounding knowledge: Data from each new molecule feed back into the platform, strengthening understanding of the overall design space and creating a virtuous cycle.

For a CDMO, this is not only a scientific question but also one of commercial competitiveness: a CDMO that can offer clients a "platform service" has a clear advantage in winning new clients.

Analogy: A Platform Is Like a Standardized Restaurant Kitchen

Imagine a restaurant chain in which every branch has identical kitchen equipment, layout and workflow (this is the "process backbone"); the chef only changes ingredients or adjusts a few seasoning steps for each menu (these are the "targeted adjustments").

When a new branch opens, there is no need to redesign the kitchen or retrain staff on every procedure; they only need to learn what differs in the new menu. This is the value of a platform: maximum reuse, minimum difference.

By contrast, in non-platform development every new product requires designing the process and running the studies from scratch. It is like renovating the kitchen every time you open a restaurant: slow, expensive, and hard to keep consistent in quality.

Key Point: The Regulatory Strategic Significance of Prior Art

The "first-mover advantage" is especially important in regulatory filings. Once a company's AAV platform has been approved by FDA or EMA for the first time, regulators are familiar with the platform architecture when later products on the same platform are filed. Reviewers' questions focus on the "differences" rather than the "fundamentals", which shortens review time.

This is the core meaning of "reduced time for review and approval by regulators (when prior art has already been approved)" in the document. For a CDMO, if it can establish the platform prior art first on behalf of a benchmark industry client, the regulatory path will be smoother when other clients later use the same platform.

The US government's PaVe-GT (Paving the Way for Rare Disease Gene Therapies) program, set up at NCATS (National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences), is a concrete policy-level expression of support for this direction.

Practical Application: How a CDMO Can Present Platform Value to Clients

When a CDMO talks with a new gene therapy client whose AAV serotype matches the CDMO's existing platform, the platform advantages can be presented as follows:

  • Shorter timeline: The core process design does not need to be validated from scratch; only the GOI-specific process adjustments need to be confirmed.
  • Lower cost: Most regulatory documentation can be reused, and PPQ may use a matrix approach to reduce the number of batches.
  • Assured knowledge base: The platform has accumulated process data from multiple molecules, so the design space is better characterized and the risk is lower.
  • Regulatory predictability: Platform elements already have precedent, which reduces uncertainty.
Q 6.2 — What types of ATMPs are most likely to benefit from the application of platform technologies?

Original Text

For different ATMPs, the decision to use one or more platforms is driven by different factors including stage of development (proof of concept versus licensed product), cost, target disease, and intellectual property.

ATMPs are a highly diverse group of products and, consequently, there is a range in the extent to which platform technologies can be applied. AAV vectors are an example of a product class that makes extensive use of platform technologies, where the manufacture of an AAV vector may use the same production methods and controls as the manufacture of a different product with the same vector serotype and different gene-of-interest (GOI) sequence. Different technologies for manufacturing AAVs have been used to develop a range of products, making it possible to use this prior knowledge to develop an understanding of how the process needs to be controlled. In contrast, an ex-vivo genetically modified cell product relies upon lot-specific starting materials and a number of different technologies are used to create a wide range of custom product types that are classified as ATMPs.

For pDNA used as a drug substance, FDA and EMA have accepted efficient PPQ approaches when multiple pDNA expressing different antigens are a) produced from the same backbone plasmid vector, b) produced with identical unit operations with the same master batch-record (MBR) and testing, and c) are combined in a single drug product. In such examples, regulatory agencies have agreed to fewer than three consecutive runs for each plasmid individually, based on a matrix approach for PPQ of the platform. In another example, in the vaccine industry, the production of drug substances with different serotypes or strains are not independently validated, but specific parameter targets are adjusted and confirmed via initial production batches.

Similarly, for CAR-T products/lentiviral vector (LV) platforms, four or more different vectors that share a common backbone and yet differ in the cell-surface-receptor-coding region and potency testing, have been filed in an IND as a platform using QbD principles, avoiding repetitive CMC sections in each Biologics License Application (BLA).

Platform technologies are used extensively both in the production and testing of ATMPs. For some products, individual manufacturing or testing activities may be based on the platform and, in other cases, platform technologies may account for the majority of all activities. For AAV vectors, for example, the platforms may differ based on such elements as cell lines, AAV serotypes, and/or construction methods.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Application: Justifying a Matrix PPQ to FDA/EMA (pDNA CDMO Scenario)

Suppose a CDMO produces the pDNA for three of Client A's mRNA vaccines. The three plasmids share the same backbone and differ only in the insert (antigen sequence). How can the CDMO seek a matrix PPQ (fewer than 3 consecutive batches per plasmid) from the regulators?

All three conditions must be met (precedent accepted by FDA/EMA):

  1. Same backbone: All three pDNAs use the same backbone plasmid vector, with written confirmation that the vector backbone sequence is identical.
  2. Same unit operations + same MBR: The manufacturing flow is identical (fermentation, cell lysis, purification), using one master batch record with only the pDNA identification information filled in differently.
  3. Combined in a single drug product: The three pDNAs are ultimately combined into one drug product.

Once these conditions are met, the CDMO can apply to FDA/EMA to replace individual validation with a platform PPQ matrix design, using fewer batches (for example, 1 batch per pDNA plus matrix cross-over batches) in place of the traditional requirement of 9 batches per pDNA, saving considerable time and cost.

Key Point: CAR-T IND Platform Filing - A Strategy to Reduce BLA Duplication

For CAR-T/LV platforms, PtC-11 describes a strategy: at the IND stage, 4 or more vectors that share a backbone are filed as a "platform", with the CMC section (process description, analytical methods, reference materials, etc.) written once in the IND. Each subsequent BLA then only needs to reference this platform CMC information and supplement it with its own clinical data and specific differences (for example, the different cell surface receptor coding regions and the potency assay).

This strategy avoids rewriting the same CMC content in every BLA, saves substantial filing resources, and lets regulators quickly locate the differences, improving review efficiency.

Key prerequisite: The shared elements of the platform CMC must have clear boundaries and a referencing framework established at the IND stage, so that it can be extended smoothly to the BLAs later.

Q 6.3 — What are the considerations for developing and using platforms for ATMP manufacturing?

Original Text

Any platform-leveraging approach should make use of strong risk-based rationales for all variations in processes and process control. The magnitude of the risk should drive decisions regarding when supporting studies may be required.

There is a need to carefully evaluate all changes from the baseline platform during development to determine which foundational studies may need to be repeated, in whole or in part, due to platform changes. For example:

  • Changes to upstream processing may result in the need to recharacterize cell-expansion curves, feed regimes, end of production cells, or limit of in-vitro cell age
  • Changes to downstream processing or the purification process parameters might require reevaluation of baseline studies such as small-scale viral clearance and small-scale resin lifetime studies
  • Changes at any point in the process may result in an incremental change to the analytical methods, so it is important to ensure that appropriate bridging and comparability are performed (see Topic 5)

While a platform may be able to be filed as a common control strategy, it is important to remember that individual products will require separate BLAs related to the individual product's clinical-trial results and control-strategy-specific variations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Risk Assessment Checklist for Departures from the Platform

Any departure from the baseline platform must go through a formal risk assessment process. The key questions of the assessment framework are:

  • Nature of the departure: Is it a change in upstream (cell culture), downstream (purification) or the analytical methods?
  • CQAs affected: Which CQAs could this departure affect, and by what mechanism?
  • Risk level: Based on the degree of impact on patient safety, define whether foundational studies need to be repeated.
  • Supplementary studies required:
    • Upstream change → cell expansion profile, feeding strategy, end-of-production cell characteristics, in vitro cell age limit
    • Downstream/purification change → small-scale viral clearance studies, resin lifetime studies
    • Any process change → analytical method bridging and comparability studies (see Topic 5)
  • Documentation requirements: All decisions on departures must be supported by a written risk assessment; verbal decisions are not acceptable.

Key Point: Viral Clearance Studies - Not Exempt Even for a Platform

Many people mistakenly believe that using an existing platform exempts them from all validation. This is a dangerous misconception.

Viral clearance studies and resin lifetime studies are the most critical safety studies in a purification platform. When any change occurs in the downstream process (purification process parameters), these studies must be re-evaluated; even a small adjustment of operating conditions (for example, elution pH or salt concentration) can affect the log reduction value (LRV) of viral clearance.

Regulators (FDA, EMA) are highly sensitive to this type of safety study. If a submission cannot provide an assessment of the effect of downstream changes on viral clearance, it is likely to receive a deficiency letter.

Recommendation: In the CDMO's platform governance document, define in advance which downstream parameter changes are the thresholds that "trigger a repeat", so that costly viral clearance studies are not found to be necessary only shortly before marketing.

Practical Application: CDMO Platform Governance Framework (When to Use the Core Platform, When to Deviate, How to Document)

For a CDMO providing AAV or pDNA platform services, a three-tier governance structure is recommended:

Tier 1: Core Platform
The existing data package defining the unit operations, MBR framework, CQA list and foundational studies (viral clearance, resin lifetime, etc.) shared by all clients. New clients draw on it directly, with no need to repeat the foundational studies.

Tier 2: Permitted Deviations
Pre-assess which parameters can be adjusted within a specified range (for example, MOI, culture temperature ±1°C) and define the minimum validation work required for each deviation (for example, 1 engineering run is sufficient). This tier requires a written risk assessment template for clients to use.

Tier 3: Major Deviations
Changes outside the permitted range (for example, changing the cell line or the purification strategy) require a full process development and validation program. Such cases no longer count as a "platform service", and the contract scope and fees should be renegotiated with the client.

A clear governance framework not only supports regulatory consistency but also, commercially, protects the CDMO from bearing unbudgeted study costs caused by excessive client customization.

Key Point: Platform Control Strategy vs. Individual BLA - Do Not Confuse the Two

PtC-11 states clearly at the end of Q6.3 that even when multiple products share the same platform control strategy, each product still needs to submit its own BLA. The reasons are:

  • Clinical trial results (safety and efficacy data) cannot be shared between products.
  • Product-specific differences in the control strategy (for example, different release specifications arising from different GOIs) must be explained in each BLA.

In other words, a platform lets you "do it once, use it many times" at the CMC level, but the clinical data package is always product-specific. This line must be clearly recognized when planning the BLA filing strategy, so that the platform CMC referencing relationship is not confused with the review obligations of each individual product.

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO is evaluating whether to use its existing AAV8 platform for a new AAV9 client. The common process backbone of the platforms is largely the same, but the different serotype results in a different capsid protein composition. Please answer:
    (a) Is this a "true process platform reuse" or an "equipment platform reuse"?
    (b) If the downstream purification steps (for example, the affinity chromatography resin) need to be adjusted, which foundational studies must be re-evaluated?
    (c) If the CDMO wants to argue to FDA that viral clearance studies do not need to be repeated, what scientific justification must it provide?
  2. A client plans to develop 3 mRNA vaccines that use the same pDNA backbone but different antigen inserts, and are finally made into a combined drug product. Design a matrix PPQ plan that states clearly:
    (a) the three core conditions accepted by FDA/EMA that must be met;
    (b) the minimum number of PPQ batches required and how they are allocated;
    (c) whether it affects eligibility for the matrix PPQ if one of the pDNAs changes the resistance gene of the expression plasmid midway.
  3. A company has 5 CAR-T products, all based on the same LV backbone but targeting different tumor antigens. The company wants to use a platform IND strategy to reduce BLA duplication. Please answer:
    (a) Which shared elements should the platform CMC include in the IND?
    (b) What must be provided separately in each BLA and cannot be covered by referencing the platform?
    (c) If the third product needs a change to the LV potency assay, does the platform IND need to be amended? What are the consequences?

Section Summary

  • Precision in defining a platform: A "process platform" means the same unit operations + process backbone, with only minimal adjustments for the specific biological entity; an "equipment platform" standardizes equipment only and offers less regulatory benefit. The two must not be used interchangeably.
  • Platform economics: Platform technology can shorten the 7–10 year development cycle and significantly lower development and filing costs through shared CQA definitions, matrix PPQ and harmonized regulatory documents; the PaVe-GT program (NCATS) is the US government's policy endorsement of this direction.
  • AAV and pDNA platforms are the most applicable: FDA/EMA have clearly accepted a pDNA matrix PPQ (same backbone + same MBR + combined drug product); AAVs of the same serotype with different GOIs can broadly share a platform. CAR-T/LV can reduce BLA duplication through a platform IND strategy; the degree of platformization of ex vivo cell products is limited by starting material that differs from batch to batch.
  • A departure from the platform must have a written risk assessment: Upstream change → reconfirm cell expansion characteristics; downstream/purification change → re-evaluate viral clearance and resin lifetime studies; any process change → analytical method bridging (Topic 5).
  • Platform control strategy ≠ shared BLA: A platform allows a shared CMC architecture, but each product's clinical data package and the specific differences in its control strategy must still be presented in full in its own BLA; this is an individual obligation that cannot be substituted.

Q7.1 — Key Attributes of an Effective Product-Specific Potency Assay

Original Text

Potency is an expression of the activity of a drug substance or drug product in terms of the amount or concentration required to produce a defined effect. Clinical efficacy, on the other hand, is a measure of the therapeutic effectiveness of the drug in humans. Regulators desire a product-specific potency assay that provides a demonstrable link between potency and clinical efficacy. As such, the development team should first identify the relevant biological property linked to the product's in vivo mechanism of action (MOA) and then establish a method to provide a quantitative measure of that biological property. The assay must demonstrate that its performance remains unaffected by expected variability in product purity or matrix effects.

Other than safety, potency is the most critical quality attribute of a product. The ideal potency assay should reflect the MOA of the drug and demonstrate the product has the expected ultimate "functionality" that correlates with clinical efficacy. For an in-vivo gene therapy product, the ultimate functionality means the action of the expressed (or transcribed) gene of interest (GOI). If the product is a protein, then demonstration of the expected bioactivity (e.g., enzymatic activity) of the expressed protein is the ultimate goal of potency assay development. For an activated cell therapy product, for example, this broadly means that there must be an in vitro analytical method that correlates to in vivo reduction of tumor size or number or reducing the progression to metastasis.

Potency is a CQA for stability, not just release; therefore, an effective potency assay must also demonstrate that it is stability-indicating. To do this, forced degradation studies should be performed which demonstrate the primary degradation pathways of the product. With the samples showing between 10% and 20% degradation for each condition (where possible), as confirmed by orthogonal methods, the potency assay must be able to accurately detect the associated loss in potency.

If the product will be produced across multiple sites, it should be recognized that small differences in process and materials may exist site to site. Ideally, the potency assay should be robust enough to account for these small differences when they do not represent a difference in clinical effect.

To effectively quantitate potency, it is necessary to establish a reference standard against which the response from each lot will be quantitated. Initially, the reference standard may be a well-characterized development lot as an interim reference standard. However, as product development advances, the company must qualify a primary reference standard that will be used for commercial production. Bridging will likely be required to link the quantitation of early lots (and their stability studies) to any new reference standard that is introduced (see Topic 5). It is especially important to maintain a quantitative reference to clinical lots.

"Since development of an appropriate potency assay is complex, it is recommended to collect a significant amount of bioanalytical characterization data on early product lots until it is demonstrated that the developed potency assay is robust to the product and process and is predictive of MOA."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Triangle of Potency, Pharmacological Activity and Clinical Efficacy

Potency: The dose or concentration of drug needed to produce a specific biological effect. It is a biological activity value measurable in vitro, for example the enzymatic activity (IU/mL) of the GOI expressed after an AAV vector infects HEK293 cells.

Clinical Efficacy: The therapeutic effectiveness of the drug in real patients, which can only be demonstrated in clinical trials. For example, the clinical efficacy of a gene therapy for a rare lysosomal storage disease means improved organ function and normalization of biochemical markers in patients.

The key link between the two: Regulators require the potency assay to have a demonstrable link to clinical efficacy. This means:

  • AAV gene therapy (protein replacement): The in vitro enzymatic activity value must be statistically correlated with the enzyme replacement effect in animal models
  • CAR-T cell therapy: The in vitro tumor killing assay must correlate with in vivo results, either tumor shrinkage in animals or in the clinic

In short: potency is the in vitro surrogate of clinical efficacy, and sufficient data must show that the two are related. Without this link, a potency assay is just a number and means nothing to regulatory reviewers.

Analogy: Engine Dynamometer vs. Road Test

Think of a potency assay as measuring a car engine's horsepower on a dynamometer: instead of driving the car on the highway to see how fast it goes, you precisely measure the engine's power output under controlled laboratory conditions.

Dynamometer reading = Potency: A quantitative, reproducible biological activity value measured under controlled conditions.

Actual road performance = Clinical Efficacy: Affected by road conditions, weather and driving habits, and hard to predict precisely during development.

The regulatory "demonstrable link" means you must show that an engine measured at 500 horsepower on the dynamometer really is faster on the road than one measured at 300 horsepower. This correlation must be established with data, not just assumed.

Key Point: Design Essentials of a Stability-Indicating Assay

Many teams mistakenly think potency is only for release. PtC-11 stresses explicitly that potency is a stability CQA, so the potency assay must be able to detect the loss of activity caused by degradation during storage.

Design principles for a forced degradation study:

  • Target for each degradation condition: 10–20% degradation (too much degradation destroys the product structure, and the real degradation pathway is no longer detected)
  • Common conditions: elevated temperature, oxidation (H₂O₂ treatment), light exposure (ICH Q1B), mechanical stress (freeze-thaw cycles)
  • The extent of degradation must be confirmed by orthogonal methods, for example SEC-HPLC for protein aggregation and flow cytometry for the decrease in cell viability
  • Once degradation is confirmed, the potency assay must be able to quantitatively detect the corresponding loss of activity

If the potency assay cannot detect the loss of activity in degraded samples, it is not a stability-indicating assay, cannot be used in stability studies, and will face major challenges at regulatory review.

Practical Application: CDMO Reference Standard Bridging Strategy

In actual ATMP CDMO operations, the evolution of the reference standard usually goes through three stages:

  • Before the Phase 1 IND: Use a development batch as the interim reference standard (interim RS). The batch characteristics must be documented in detail at this point (GMP or research batch, process version, version of the potency assay method).
  • Phase 2/3 transition: Prepare and qualify the primary reference standard (primary RS), supported by a GMP batch, sufficient stability data and a full characterization package.
  • Bridging Study: When a new RS is introduced, a quantitative relationship between the old and new RS must be established by parallel testing, ensuring that the potency data of early clinical batches remain consistent and traceable with the new RS system.

Key practical recommendation: Start building extensive bioanalytical characterization data at the interim RS stage. If the potency assay later needs to be adjusted, these data are the lifeline for bridging. Documentation should be good enough that someone can reconstruct the potency history of every batch 5 years later.

Q7.2 — Acceptable Approaches for Phasing in a Product-Specific Potency Assay

Original Text

Due to the complex nature of ATMP products, developing a potency assay that is truly reflective of the MOA of the product is challenging, especially in the early development stage. Some of the GOIs encode structural proteins that lack clearly indicated bioactivity, while the functions of other GOIs are multifaceted, while some are not yet fully understood. Regulatory agencies are aware of such challenges and typically allow for flexibility in determining the most appropriate potency assay for each product at each development stage. The expectations for the potency assay increase proportionally as the drug development progresses.

A widely accepted approach for potency development is the matrix approach. A combination of various assays is used to assess product potency holistically. It starts with the measurement of a DNA copy number, vector particle number, cell counts, and cell-viability physical attributes that are necessary (but may not be sufficient) for potency. As product development progresses, the matrix evolves, and certain methods may drop out due to complexity or robustness concerns. Other assays developed in parallel that are more reflective of MOA and demonstrate functionality of the product may become more central to the testing scheme. The matrix can comprise both quantitative and qualitative assays. For example, a binding assay can provide quantitative results compared to the reference standard while a cell-based assay can confirm the expected functionality (pass or fail). The correlation between the quantitative and qualitative assays can be established during assay development to justify the use of the combination of quantitative and qualitative assays in routine release.

In specific cases where there is no ability to directly measure MOA, it may be possible to use more than one assay to represent potency, providing regulatory agreement is granted. For example, in the specific case of pDNA or mRNA as the drug product, there is no specific in vitro assay indicating MOA because transcription and translation of the drug product for presentation to the immune system is required in vivo. In these cases, FDA has accepted direct measurement of nucleic acid coupled with integrity of the molecule (e.g., supercoil measurement) as surrogates for potency assay.

The ideal lot-release assay for routine quality control release, needs to balance its capability and reliability. A complicated, research-grade assay that can only be conducted on a sophisticated instrument by a highly trained scientist is not feasible for routine quality control testing of the product. A simple, robust, and reproducible assay is preferred. A robust data package is expected to justify using a surrogate assay for functionality potency assay. A correlation study using a regular drug substance or drug product, an in-process degraded sample, and/or specially constructed products with reduced or elevated potency would be valuable to support the assay choice. Open and timely communication with regulatory agencies on a potency assay strategy is of critical importance for the success of a project. Many clinical trial applications and licensure approvals have been delayed due to potency assay concerns in recent years.

Recently, FDA proposed a multifaceted approach combining manufacturing process design, process control, material control, in-process testing, and potency lot release assays as a systematic potency assurance strategy (Ref 16). Sponsors need to prepare for a holistic overview of potency assurance in addition to a robust, MOA reflective potency assay.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Comprehensive Health Checkup vs. a Single Indicator

A matrix potency approach is like a full health checkup: you do not decide whether you are healthy from a single indicator:

  • Fasting glucose = DNA copy number → necessary but not sufficient
  • Liver function index (ALT/AST) = vector particle count → indicator of structural integrity
  • Cardiac stress test = cell-based tumor killing assay → final functional confirmation
  • Quantitative ECG = binding assay against the RS → quantitative functional benchmark

A single indicator being high or low may be just noise, but the combined picture from multiple indicators truly reflects health. Likewise, no single assay in the matrix can define potency on its own; the assays together form the complete potency picture.

Also, as you age (= as development progresses), the items and standards of the checkup are upgraded, which is the essence of the matrix evolving dynamically.

Key Point: Special Potency Strategy for pDNA/mRNA

When pDNA and mRNA are the drug product, there is a fundamental challenge: their MOA only takes effect after transcription/translation in vivo, so no direct in vitro MOA assay is available.

Combination of surrogates accepted by FDA:

  • Direct nucleic acid quantitation: Confirms that the amount of pDNA or mRNA is consistent with the specification
  • Supercoil measurement (for pDNA): Confirms the supercoiled fraction of the pDNA, because the supercoiled form has the highest transfection efficiency; degraded or open-circular forms have markedly lower potency
  • Integrity assay (for mRNA): Confirms that the mRNA has no degraded or truncated fragments, usually measured by capillary electrophoresis or Bioanalyzer

Important reminder: This is a compromise FDA accepts in specific situations; it is not a general rule. Using surrogates requires submitting a robust data package demonstrating the correlation of the surrogate with potency (for example, the supercoiled fraction against in vitro transfection efficiency, or against in vivo protein expression in animals).

Practical Application: Design Principles for CDMO QC Release Assays

As a CDMO, when a client brings a complex research-grade potency assay and asks to use it for release, you need to help the client rethink the following questions:

  • Executability: Can the assay be performed in a QC laboratory environment (not a research environment) by analysts with standard training?
  • Reproducibility: Do the reproducibility data (%CV) between batches, analysts and instruments meet the validation acceptance criteria?
  • Turnaround time: Cell culture-based functional assays usually take 3–7 days; does this meet the product release time requirements?
  • Data package for a surrogate assay: If a simpler surrogate is proposed, prepare:
    (1) a correlation study of normal vs. degraded samples
    (2) validation data with artificially reduced-potency and elevated-potency samples
    (3) a feasibility assessment for multi-site technology transfer

CDMO strategy recommendation: At the technical feasibility assessment stage before contract signing, proactively discuss the feasibility of the potency assay with the client, so that an unworkable method is not discovered on the eve of the BLA submission.

Key Point: Potency Assay Problems Are the Leading Cause of IND/BLA Delays

PtC-11 states clearly that in recent years many IND submissions and BLA approvals have been delayed because of potency assay problems. This is a warning in regulatory practice that cannot be ignored.

Common delay scenarios:

  • Discovering midway through a Phase 3 trial that the potency assay is not stability-indicating, requiring redesign and bridging
  • Receiving an FDA Information Request after the BLA submission asking for supplementary MOA-link data
  • On the eve of commercial manufacture, the potency assay cannot be successfully transferred to the commercial site
  • The reference standard system was not established, so the potency data of early clinical batches cannot be traced

Solution: communicate early with the regulators. FDA encourages applicants to proactively discuss the potency assay strategy at a Pre-IND Meeting or Type B Meeting and to obtain health authority (HA) agreement early, which saves several times the time and cost of fixing the problem afterwards.

Practice Questions

  1. MOA link: Your client is developing an AAV9 gene therapy whose GOI encodes a structural protein whose function is not yet fully elucidated. How should the potency assay development team respond in the Phase 1 IND to FDA's requirement for a "demonstrable link between potency and clinical efficacy"? Which matrix combination would you recommend, and how would you plan the assay evolution path from Phase 1 to Phase 3?
  2. Stability-indicating design: Your CAR-T product uses an in vitro tumor killing assay as its potency method.   3. pDNA surrogate potency: In the pDNA drug product specification submitted by the client, the only potency method is DNA quantitation (OD260 absorbance). Do you think this method is sufficient? Section Summary

  • Potency vs. clinical efficacy: Potency is a measure of activity that defines the concentration/dose needed for an effect; clinical efficacy is therapeutic effectiveness in humans. Regulators require a demonstrable scientific link between the two; neither can be omitted.
  • Potency is the most important CQA after safety, and it must serve both release and stability, so the design of the stability-indicating assay must be planned together with the forced degradation study.
  • The matrix approach is the industry consensus for ATMP potency development: starting from basic physical attributes, it evolves with clinical progress into MOA-reflective assays, and finally, before the BLA, is consolidated into a simplified, robust commercial release scheme.
  • The pDNA/mRNA special case: FDA accepts nucleic acid quantitation + supercoil integrity (pDNA) or an integrity assay (mRNA) as potency surrogates, but a robust correlation data package is required.
  • Reference standard bridging: The transition from interim RS to primary RS must be planned rigorously, and the bioanalytical characterization data accumulated early are the basis for future bridging.
  • FDA's 2023 potency assurance strategy (Ref 16) proposes a five-layer integrated framework; in the quality agreement the CDMO should define clearly how responsibilities for potency assurance are divided among the parties.
  • Early HA communication is the key to success: Potency assay problems have been the leading cause of IND/BLA delays in recent years, and the potency strategy should be raised proactively at the Pre-IND Meeting.
