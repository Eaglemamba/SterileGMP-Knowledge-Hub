Introduction

Original Text

This document represents the views of the Parenteral Drug Association (PDA). The document is designed to communicate PDA's thoughts and considerations pertaining to the design, operation, and use of Restricted Access Barrier Systems (RABS) for aseptic processing, processes subjected to terminal sterilization and low bioburden, while encouraging further dialog with industry, health authorities, and suppliers of technologies and materials. This document considers the evolution and needs of the modern, global, sterile, healthcare product manufacturing industry. This document does not represent a standard or regulatory guidance.

In 2020, PDA issued Points to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Sterile Pharmaceutical Products in Isolators, which focus on important regulatory and technology updates surrounding isolator design, validation, and operation for aseptic processing. Several other PDA documents equally address many of the principles for working and operating with RABS, including Points to Consider No. 1: Aseptic Processing (Revised 2023) or Technical Report No. 62 Recommended Practices for Manual Aseptic Processes. Additionally regulatory guidance documents as the European Commission's EU Guidelines for Good Manufacturing Practice for Medicinal Products for Human and Veterinary Use Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products (2022), U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing-Current Good Manufacturing Practice (2004) and others are addressing the special requirements for RABS. While many of the recommendations and principles from these documents are equally applicable to RABS, until this point, there was no equivalent guideline or Points to Consider (PtC) dedicated to RABS for which technology and operational specific nuances apply. Hence, a taskforce was established to focus on industry best practices, regulatory and technology considerations directly relevant to the application and use of RABS in an aseptic manufacturing setting.

A fundamental principle of sterile product manufacturing is to minimize the risks of contamination (microbiological and particulate) through appropriate facility, equipment, and manufacturing process design, and qualification. The use of barrier systems, including RABS, to increase the protection of the product is not only recommended but should be considered in the context of an overall contamination control and risk-reduction strategy. RABS technology provides for increased product protection compared to non-barrier systems used for Grade A and Grade B aseptic manufacturing operations. RABS is a very different technology to isolators and, consequently, key design, regulatory and operational differences, and considerations apply, most notably background environments, cleaning, disinfection and decontamination methods, aseptic setup, and strictly defined conditions and controls governing open-door RABS interventions.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Restricted Access Barrier Systems (RABS): RABS is a physical barrier technology used in aseptic processing. It provides an enclosed but not fully sealed environment and uses rigid walls, integrated gloves and unidirectional airflow to separate operators from the Grade A critical zone, thereby reducing the risk of microbial and particulate contamination.

PDA (Parenteral Drug Association): The world's largest technical association for injectable drug products. Its Points to Consider series reflects the consensus view of industry experts; although it is not a regulatory requirement, it is widely adopted in practice as a basis for best practice.

Positioning of this document: This is the first complete PDA guidance dedicated to RABS. Before it, RABS knowledge was scattered across various regulations and technical reports, with no systematic consolidation.

Analogy

Imagine working in the open kitchen of a high-end restaurant. An isolator is like sealing the entire cooking area under a closed glass enclosure, with the chefs working only through gloves. A RABS is more like placing a transparent barrier between the cooking area and the dining area: the chefs work mainly through the integrated gloves, but under strictly controlled conditions they can briefly open a door for specific tasks. Both are much safer than a traditional open kitchen with no barrier, but the design logic and operating rules are very different.

Key Notes

RABS =/= Isolator: The document stresses that RABS and isolators are "a very different technology". The main differences include:

  • Background environment: RABS requires a Grade B background; an isolator can sit in a Grade D background
  • Decontamination method: RABS relies mainly on manual wipe disinfection; isolators use automated VPHP decontamination
  • Degree of sealing: RABS is not fully sealed (it has openings such as mouseholes); isolators aim for complete separation
  • Door opening: RABS allows door-open interventions under strict conditions; isolators in principle are not opened

These differences directly affect facility design, qualification strategy and day-to-day operating procedures.

  • EU GMP Annex 1 (2022): The latest EU GMP requirements for sterile manufacturing, setting out clear but distinct requirements for RABS and isolators. PIC/S GMP Annex 1 is identical.

  • PDA PtC No. 1 (2023): PDA's general guidance on aseptic processing; many of its principles apply equally to RABS.

Glossary

Original Text

Definitions have been provided to help clarify the concepts discussed in this document. While some definitions used vary among companies, geographic location, etc., the definitions described below are consistently used within this Points to Consider document. Where a definition is based on another published source, the source is cited.

Aseptic Process Simulation (APS)

A simulation of the entire aseptic manufacturing process in order to verify the capability of the process to assure product sterility. Includes all aseptic operations associated with routine manufacturing, e.g. equipment assembly, formulation, filling, lyophilization and sealing processes as necessary (1).

Bio-decontamination

A process that eliminates bioburden via use of a sporicidal chemical agent (1).

Note: In the context of open RABS (oRABS), this is a manual treatment process with sporicidal agents.

Biological Indicator (BI)

Test systems that contain viable microorganisms with a defined concentration and resistance to a specific decontamination process. They help monitor whether the necessary conditions were met to kill a specified number of microorganisms for a given process.

Campaign

Processing of multiple lots or batches of the same product serially in the same manufacturing line.

Contact Time

The minimum amount of time that a sanitizer, disinfectant, or sporicide must be left in complete (wet) contact with the surface to be treated in order to be effective (2).

Note: This is typically referred to as "wet contact time".

Critical Zone

A location within the aseptic processing area in which product and critical surfaces are exposed to the environment (1).

Critical Surfaces

Surfaces that may come directly into contact with, or directly affect, a sterile product or its containers or closures. Critical surfaces are rendered sterile prior to the start of the manufacturing operation, and sterility is maintained throughout processing (1).

Decontamination

The overall process of removal or reduction of any contaminants (chemical, waste, residue, or microorganisms) from an area, object, or person. The method of decontamination used (e.g., cleaning, disinfection, sterilization) should be chosen and validated to achieve a level of cleanliness appropriate to the intended use of the item decontaminated (1). See also Bio-decontamination.

Disinfection

The process by which the reduction of the number of microorganisms is achieved by the irreversible action of a product on their structure or metabolism to a level deemed to be appropriate for a defined purpose (1).

Note: In this document, it is understood that sanitizers and disinfectants are used in the same context.

First Air

Refers to filtered air that has not been interrupted prior to contacting exposed product and product contact surfaces with the potential to add contamination to the air prior to reaching the critical zone (1).

Format Parts

Container specific parts (e.g., based on different container sizes) that allow the proper guidance/transfer of product containers through a manufacturing line.

Grade A Air Supply

Air which is passed through a filter qualified as capable of producing grade A total particle quality air, but where there is no requirement to perform continuous total particle monitoring or meet grade A viable monitoring limits. Specifically used for the protection of fully stoppered vials where the cap has not yet been crimped (1).

Note: For the purposes of this document, the term Grade A is understood to be interchangeable with International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 5.

Hold Time

Set durations between qualified events or process steps.

Aseptic Hold Time

A defined and validated maximum time for 'holding' conditions for aseptic preparation and processing.

Clean Hold Time

Duration between when cleaning is implemented to meet a target requirement before the use of parts or areas. This type of hold time would typically apply before sterilization or bio-decontamination processes.

Sterile Hold Time

Duration sterilized items can be held after sterilization and before use.

Intervention

Activities, manipulations, and tasks performed in Grade A, or other critical processing areas, during aseptic operations.

Corrective Intervention

An intervention that is performed to correct or adjust an aseptic process during its execution. These may not occur at a set frequency in the routine aseptic process. Examples include such activities as clearing component jams, stopping leaks, adjusting sensors, and replacing equipment components.

Inherent Intervention

An intervention that is an integral part of the aseptic process and is required for either set-up, routine operation and/or monitoring (e.g., aseptic assembly, container replenishment, environmental sampling). Inherent interventions are required by procedure or work instruction for the execution of the aseptic process.

Critical Intervention

An intervention (corrective or inherent) into the critical zone (1).

Note: An example of this type of intervention would be an open-door intervention when critical surfaces are exposed within the Grade A RABS. This could be a corrective or inherent intervention.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Cleaning and Decontamination Levels

This glossary contains a set of easily confused terms whose hierarchy needs to be clarified:

  • Cleaning: removal of visible soil and residues
  • Disinfection: reduction of the number of microorganisms to an appropriate level, but not necessarily the destruction of spores
  • Bio-decontamination: elimination of bioburden using a sporicidal agent
  • Sterilization: elimination of all viable microorganisms (the highest level)
  • Decontamination: a general term covering all of the above methods

In RABS operations, disinfection and bio-decontamination are normally used rather than sterilization, because the internal surfaces of a RABS are usually disinfected by manual wiping rather than sterilized by an automated system.

Key Concepts - Classification of Interventions

The classification of interventions is one of the most important concepts in RABS operational management:

  • Inherent Intervention: an operation that must be performed as part of the process, such as environmental monitoring sampling or container replenishment. These are foreseeable and planned.
  • Corrective Intervention: an operation performed because of an abnormality, such as clearing a stoppered or jammed vial or repairing a leak. These are unplanned.
  • Critical Intervention: any intervention that enters the critical zone, whether inherent or corrective. In a RABS environment this refers specifically to operations that require opening a door.

RABS operating procedures should be designed to minimize all interventions, especially critical interventions that require opening a door.

Analogy - First Air

The concept of First Air can be understood through a shower: when you stand under the showerhead, water flows directly from above onto you, which is the "first water". If the water hits the wall first and then bounces onto you, it is no longer first water, because it may already have picked up dirt from the wall.

Likewise, air leaving the HEPA filter and blowing directly onto the exposed product surfaces, with no obstacle blocking or disturbing it in between, is First Air. Any object that obstructs first air may introduce contamination before the air reaches the critical zone.

Key Notes - Hold Time Management

Hold time plays a key role in RABS operations. Three types of hold time form a complete chain of time control:

  • Clean Hold Time: completion of cleaning to start of sterilization/decontamination (ensures the cleaned state does not deteriorate)
  • Sterile Hold Time: completion of sterilization to actual use (ensures the sterile state is maintained)
  • Aseptic Hold Time: completion of aseptic set-up to end of the process (ensures the aseptic process remains reliable)

Each hold time must be validated, and exceeding it requires reprocessing. In a RABS environment, because of its not fully sealed nature, these time limits are usually stricter than for an isolator.

Key Notes - Grade A vs ISO 5

The document notes that Grade A and ISO 5 are used interchangeably in the text. Be aware, however, that Grade A is an EU GMP classification (covering both particulate and microbial limits), whereas ISO 5 is a classification under ISO 14644 (particulates only). Grade A is conceptually more stringent because it also includes microbial monitoring requirements.

Grade A Air Supply is a special concept: air quality that meets the Grade A particulate standard but does not require continuous particle monitoring or compliance with the Grade A microbial limits. It is mainly used to protect fully stoppered vials that have not yet been crimped.

Glossary (continued)

Original Text

Isolator

A contained, decontaminated environment meeting Grade A/ISO 5 conditions used for aseptic process manufacturing that provides an uncompromised, continuous isolation of its interior from the external environment. Once decontaminated by a validated cycle, an isolator prevents the microbiological contamination of sterile products and product contact surfaces of the interior by enclosures and the supply of continuous, controlled overpressure of HEPA-filtered air (3).

Closed Isolator Systems

Exclude external contamination from the isolator's interior by accomplishing material transfer via aseptic connection to auxiliary equipment, rather than using openings to the surrounding environment. Closed systems remain sealed throughout operations (1).

Open Isolator

Systems are designed to allow for the continuous or semi-continuous ingress and/or egress of materials during operations through one or more openings. Openings are engineered (e.g., using continuous overpressure) to exclude the entry of external contamination into the isolator (1).

Product Contact Parts
Direct Product Contact Parts

Are those that the product passes through, such as filling needles or pumps (1).

Indirect Product Contact Parts

Are equipment parts that do not contact the product, but may come into contact with other sterilized surfaces, the sterility of which is critical to the overall product sterility (e.g., sterilized items such as stopper bowls and guides, and sterilized components) (1).

Rapid Transfer Port (RTP)

A System used for the transfer of items into RABS or isolators that minimizes the risk to the critical zone. An example would be a rapid transfer container with an alpha/beta port (1).

Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS)

System that provides an enclosed, but not fully sealed, environment meeting defined air quality conditions (for aseptic processing grade A), and using a rigid-wall enclosure and integrated gloves to separate its interior from the surrounding cleanroom environment. The inner surfaces of the RABS are disinfected and decontaminated with a sporicidal agent. Operators use gloves, half suits, RTPs and other integrated transfer ports to perform manipulations or convey materials to the interior of the RABS. Depending on the design, doors are rarely opened, and only under strictly pre-defined conditions (1).

Closed RABS (cRABS)

A RABS for which the barrier prevents air exchange with the surrounding environment. Closed RABS uses a dedicated unidirectional air flow unit (UDAF) system for air supply. Unlike a closed isolator, a closed RABS typically has mouseholes, and commonly has no automated biodecontamination system.

Open RABS (oRABS)

A RABS for which the barrier permits air exchange with the surrounding environment. Passive Open RABS uses the room HVAC for supply. Active Open RABS uses a dedicated unidirectional air flow unit (UDAF) system for air supply but exchanges the exhaust with the room.

Note: Although Open RABS cannot be automatically biodecontaminated, there are some installations where the cleanroom, and all the equipment it contains including the open RABS, is biodecontaminated, but this is a special case.

6-Log Reduction

The reduction of viable microorganisms with the chance of 1:1,000,000 to have a surviving organism.

Sporicidal Agent

An agent that destroys bacterial and fungal spores when used in sufficient concentration for specified contact time. It is expected to kill all vegetative microorganisms (1).

Unidirectional Airflow (UDAF)

An airflow moving in a single direction, in a robust and uniform manner, and at sufficient speed, to reproducibly sweep particles away from the critical processing or testing area (1).

Unidirectional Airflow (UDAF) Unit

A cabinet supplied with filtered unidirectional airflow (previously referred to as a Laminar Airflow Unit or LAF) (1).

Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide (VPHP)

Agent for the decontamination of the interior of the isolator, including all exposed surfaces, fixed isolator glove exteriors (portion extending into the barrier), transfer systems, equipment, and certain supplies, materials, and parts, using a defined and validated gaseous mixture or micro-atomization of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) over a defined and validated period of time, under defined and validated conditions.

Working Position

Position where high-risk operations occur and where product and/or components are exposed (1).

Note: This is sometimes referred to as "working height".

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy - cRABS vs oRABS

Imagine two different surgical environments:

A cRABS is like an operating room with its own dedicated air conditioning system. The doors are normally closed and the air does not mix with the corridor, but there are still small gaps under the doors (mouseholes), and the door can be opened when needed.

An oRABS is like an operating room that uses the corridor's air conditioning. Although it has doors and a barrier, the air is exchanged with the corridor. The air quality of the corridor (the Grade B background) is therefore critical to the cleanliness inside the operating room.

Key Notes - 6-Log Reduction

6-Log Reduction is an important measure of decontamination efficacy. "6-log" means the microbial count is reduced to one-millionth of the original (10-6).

In a RABS environment, a 6-log reduction is usually the acceptance criterion for the bio-decontamination process. Biological indicators (BIs) are used to verify that the decontamination process achieves this level.

By comparison, VPHP decontamination of isolators also usually requires at least a 6-log reduction.

Key Notes - RTP (Rapid Transfer Port)

The Rapid Transfer Port (RTP) is a key device for transferring materials into RABS and isolators. The alpha/beta port design principle is as follows:

  • Alpha port: the fixed part mounted on the RABS/isolator wall
  • Beta port: the matching part mounted on the transfer container
  • When docked, the outer faces of the two ports (the faces that may be contaminated) are sandwiched between them and never come into contact with the inside of the RABS

Using an RTP in a RABS reduces the need to open doors and is an important design feature for lowering contamination risk.

Key Concepts - The Relationship between VPHP and RABS

Note that the definition of VPHP (vaporized hydrogen peroxide) explicitly refers to "decontamination of the inside of an isolator". This reflects an important fact: automated VPHP decontamination is standard equipment for isolators but not for RABS.

RABS (especially oRABS) normally rely on manual wiping with sporicidal agents to achieve bio-decontamination. Although a cRABS is structurally closer to an isolator, it also "normally has no automated bio-decontamination system".

This difference has far-reaching implications for operating procedures, personnel training and qualification strategy.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Suppose your CDMO site currently uses an oRABS for aseptic filling and a client asks for an upgrade to a cRABS. Based on the definitions in this glossary, the main changes you need to consider include:

  • Changing the air supply from a shared HVAC system to a dedicated UDAF
  • Designing the barrier to prevent air exchange (while still keeping mouseholes for container entry and exit)
  • The decontamination procedure may need to be redesigned and validated
  • Hold time parameters may need to be revalidated
  • The procedures and evaluation criteria for interventions need to be updated

Q 1.1 What are the different types of restricted access barrier systems (RABS)?

Original Text

Recommendation

A Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS) is a series of components and processes that enclose and provide a Grade A environment for aseptic processing (e.g., dispensing, filling and closing, lyophilization loading). Due to the protection offered by the barrier system, a RABS can provide a higher level of product and personnel protection than conventional Grade A or Grade B filling operations. See Question 2.3.

All RABS have rigid barriers, glove ports for operator access, a unidirectional airflow (UDAF) unit source providing H14 high efficiency particulate air filter (HEPA) filtered air (i.e., First Air), and a method to transfer materials and supplies in and out of it. Having these features in common, RABS differentiate in three different types of air handling: passive open RABS (oRABS), active oRABS, and closed RABS (cRABS), each offering an increasing level of protection for product and personnel. See Topic 3.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

RABS (Restricted Access Barrier System): A RABS is a system made up of rigid barriers, glove ports, HEPA-filtered unidirectional airflow, material transfer ports and other components. Its purpose is to create a Grade A environment in the aseptic processing area and to separate operators from the critical zone, reducing the risk of microbial contamination.

The core difference between the three RABS types is how the air is handled:

  • Passive oRABS: Air comes from the room's ceiling HVAC system and spills out through the barrier into the surrounding space. This gives the lowest level of protection.
  • Active oRABS: Air is supplied by a dedicated RABS HVAC system, but it still spills out into the surrounding space. This gives a medium level of protection.
  • cRABS (closed RABS): A dedicated HVAC system recirculates the air inside the RABS without spillage, and a pressure differential can be established. This gives the highest level of protection.

First Air: The clean air that has left the HEPA filter and has not yet contacted any surface; it is the first line of defense for protecting sterile product.

Analogy

Imagine an operating room:

  • A passive oRABS is like an operating room that relies only on air conditioning from the corridor, with the door half open: it gives basic protection but is easily affected by the corridor environment.
  • An active oRABS is like an operating room with its own air conditioning but with the door still half open: the air quality is better, but people walking past in the corridor still have an effect.
  • A cRABS is like an operating room with its own air conditioning and the door closed: the air inside is recirculated and separated from the outside, giving the highest protection.

An isolator is like a space station: completely sealed and self-contained, and outside the scope of this document.

Key Notes

Level of protection in ascending order: passive oRABS < active oRABS < cRABS < isolator. The choice of type should be based on a risk assessment that considers product characteristics, process needs, personnel safety and other factors.

PDA states explicitly that "non-barrier technology" is now obsolete and does not meet regulatory expectations. All new lines should use at least a RABS.

Barrier Considerations

Non-barrier technology is obsolete and is not compliant with regulatory expectations. Non-barrier systems have only individual barriers in place, such as polycarbonate, or curtains and aseptic operations are open to the surrounding space of the room.

All types of RABS have a barrier system in place (e.g., translucent rigid barriers) that surrounds the aseptic operations. In oRABS (i.e., passive and active), the barrier system is open to the room via openings below the product level that allow the unidirectional airflow to vent to the surrounding space. This type of oRABS is not sufficiently sealed during operations, therefore the RABS cannot be subjected to a Leak Rate Test to verify enclosure integrity while in operation.

In cRABS, the barrier system is closed to the room such that the unidirectional airflow is recirculated inside the cRABS. The barrier system is sealed, and the cRABS may be subjected to a Leak Rate Test to verify closure integrity even during operations. However, the lack of inflatable gaskets can lead to leaking into the room, and the Leak Rate Test should not be conducted without additional precautionary measures.

Unidirectional Airflow Source

Non-barrier systems and passive oRABS have unidirectional airflow supplied by the surrounding space/room via a ceiling-mounted HVAC system with HEPA-grade filters. Active oRABS and cRABS have unidirectional airflow supplied by a dedicated RABS HVAC system with HEPA-grade filters.

Glove Ports

Non-barrier systems do not have glove ports because the process is open to the surrounding space/room. All types of RABS have glove port(s) installed on the rigid barrier; number and placement will vary. Glove ports allow for aseptic interventions to occur without the need for the operator to enter the RABS (i.e., aseptic processing area).

Rapid Transfer Ports (RTPs)

Non-barrier systems have no material transfer ports because supplies and samples that are transferred into and out of the processing area are done so directly. (See Topic 6 for additional information.) All types of RABS have material transfer port(s) that are integrated into RABS. These ports allow for the aseptic transfer of materials and samples in and out of the RABS.

Note: Although RABS doors can be opened, in the absence of appropriate controls this would potentially allow the introduction of lower-grade air into the Grade A space, potentially contaminating the Grade A processing area environment with lower-grade air. Open-door interventions should be extremely rare occurrences, and proper risk mitigation precautions should be implemented (1).

Environmental Control

Factors such as temperature, humidity, and differential pressure are controlled by the HVAC unit mounted in the surrounding room/space for non-barrier systems and passive oRABS. These factors are controlled by the dedicated RABS HVAC unit for active oRABS and cRABS.

Typically, no positive pressure is present over the processing area compared to the surrounding space. Minimal to no positive pressure is present in passive oRABS over the processing area compared to the surrounding space. In active oRABS and cRABS, positive pressure is established in the processing area compared to the surrounding space. However, in some designs there is the capability to selectively control the air volume in specific areas to enable a positive air flow.

Pressure Cascade

No pressure cascade is possible in non-barrier systems, or oRABS (i.e., active or passive), but this is possible in cRABS.

Bio-decontamination

Bio-decontamination (by gassing or fogging) is possible for non-barrier systems or oRABS only if the room is also bio-decontaminated. In the case of cRABS, it is possible to perform bio-decontamination without the room, however, if the cRABS does not utilize inflatable gaskets, there could be leakage around the gaskets leading to health safety concerns and adequate precautions are needed to ensure personnel safety.

Product

Only nontoxic products are able to be manufactured in non-barrier systems and in oRABS (i.e., active or passive). In cRABS, it is possible to manufacture nontoxic or toxic drug products.

Additional design features should be considered including, but not limited to the following:

  • Design must ensure that all processes (i.e., with the exception of setup) will be designed to avoid open-door interventions.
  • The barrier walls should be rigid, resistant to chemical cleaning and disinfecting agents, and also resistant to mechanical stresses (e.g., scratches/cracking) from cleaning tools. Typical barrier materials are polycarbonate or tempered/safety glass.
  • The positioning of equipment, components, and sensors within the critical zone (e.g., filling needles, open vials) should be considered to ensure that First Air is not disrupted. For fixed machine components (i.e., transport mechanisms), having an effective disinfection program in place is required.
  • The glove positions inside the RABS barrier wall should be appropriate to allow operator arm entry into RABS glove(s) without disruption of First Air above critical surfaces, for example, open vials at the accumulation turntable, above fill needles, above stopper bowl, or above environmental monitoring (EM) equipment. It is key that these locations be determined early in the design process and tested during the mock-up phase of the project to verify operator ergonomics for all intended operations. Having support from experienced operators during this design process is beneficial and recommended.

Note: Any tools that are stored inside the RABS for operator use must be sterilized.

  • To protect the product from particulate contamination, it is recommended that RABS parts and components in the aseptic processing area be constructed of non-shedding, non-leaching, non-corrosive, chemical-resistant materials. It is recommended that all metallic components be constructed of low-carbon 304 stainless steel or better. In the case of metallic product contact and indirect product-contact components, low-carbon 316 stainless steel or better should be used (5). All nonmetallic materials, including RABS gloves, gasketing, seals, and barrier walls and doors need to withstand exposure to various cleaning and disinfecting agents and be suitable for the process.

The following are some points to consider in design and operations of RABS that impact achieving specified environmental control conditions within the RABS:

  • Appropriate lighting to see the equipment, product, and components without degradation of light sensitive product.
  • Rounded corners and smooth materials to minimize the risk of compromising the glove integrity during changes or line interventions.
  • Safety curtain (i.e., light curtain) to detect operator glove entry into the RABS.
  • Alarms for environmental parameters (Temperature/Humidity/UDAF velocity) and door status (door open or closed).
  • EM system viable and total particles in continuous operation during setup and filling operations.
  • EM system (settle plates and active air-monitoring locations) readily accessible via glove ports.

Rationale

The RABS design attributes as described in Table 1.2-1 are beneficial in assuring that environmental conditions are maintained and minimizing microbial contamination associated with direct human interventions in the critical zones.

Q 1.3 What are the design criteria for closed restricted access barrier systems (cRABS)?

Original Text

Recommendation

A cRABS, while not providing the same design constructs as an isolator, does provide some advantages over an oRABS design. The benefits of a cRABS can only be experienced when the RABS remains closed following setup. All qualified interventions should be able to be performed through the glove ports. The need to open the doors should be rare and heavily scrutinized on a case-by-case basis; these types of rare open-door interventions may require a deviation (1, 3, 6).

Differences between cRABS and active oRABS:

  • The rigid barrier completely encloses the Grade A space within the cRABS.
  • Air is recirculated within a cRABS, hence there is no spillover to adjacent space as in an oRABS. (See Figure 1.1-3.)
  • Pressure can be maintained within a cRABS, allowing for better control of a pressure cascade between the RABS and upstream and downstream equipment.

Differences between cRABS and an isolator:

  • If inflatable door seals on a cRABS are not present, a vapor-phase hydrogen peroxide (VPHP) cycle is not recommended without adequate safety precautions.
  • Due to the lack of a positive door seal, the background environment for a cRABS is Grade B to minimize the risk of contamination.
  • In the case of rare, open-door interventions and the potential for not being able to conduct automated biodecontamination for a cRABS, a Grade B background is required to minimize the risk of contamination.

Rationale

The cRABS design attributes are beneficial in providing further assurance of environmental conditions and minimizing microbial contamination associated with direct human interventions in the critical zones.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The position of a cRABS: between an oRABS and an isolator:

  • Compared with an oRABS: the cRABS barrier fully encloses the Grade A space, the air recirculates internally and a pressure cascade can be established
  • Compared with an isolator: a cRABS lacks a positive door seal, so the background environment must be Grade B (an isolator background can be Grade C or D)

VPHP (vaporized hydrogen peroxide): A commonly used surface bio-decontamination method. If a cRABS has no inflatable gasket, VPHP may leak into the surrounding environment and pose a safety risk to personnel, so particular caution is needed.

Analogy

If an isolator is thought of as a fully sealed "space station", a cRABS is like a "high-end submarine": it has sealed hatches, but the door seals are not as tight as on a space station. Therefore:

  • A space station (isolator) can operate safely in space (a Grade C/D background)
  • A submarine (cRABS) needs to operate in shallower water (a Grade B background) so that safety is maintained when the door seals are not perfect

Key Notes

The most critical operating principle for a cRABS: After set-up is complete, the doors must remain closed until the end of the batch. All qualified interventions should be achievable through the glove ports. If a door must be opened, it should be treated as a very rare event, reviewed case by case, and may require a deviation to be raised.

This principle directly affects:

  • The number and positions of glove ports must be sufficient to cover all anticipated operations
  • Equipment design must allow all maintenance operations to be performed from the glove ports
  • Personnel training must ensure proficient work through the glove ports

Q 1.4 What are the design considerations for the placement of the restricted access barrier system (RABS) glove ports?

Original Text

Recommendation

The correct positioning of the RABS glove ports is one of the key elements that should be considered during the design of a RABS. The position of the glove ports plays a vital role in the operation of the RABS and its ability to remain closed during setup and operation. Efforts to minimize the need to use RABS gloves should be taken to reduce the impact or risk to process performance and product sterility. Examples include reducing, eliminating, or repositioning activities, manipulations, and interventions. Figure 1.4-1 distinguishes which part of the RABS are the glove ports and which are the RABS gloves (1).

Figure 1.4-1: Differentiation of Glove Ports and Gloves

When determining where to place/position the RABS glove ports (typically done during mock-up studies), several factors that may pose a risk to the performance of the aseptic process and sterility of the product should be considered such as:

  • Use of a simulation method to review glove port positioning
  • Ergonomics to address personnel fatigue and safety
  • Diversity of personnel height and reach, from which a determined range may inform optimum port positioning
  • Quantity of glove ports
  • Orientation and location of planned and anticipated activities, manipulations, and interventions (i.e., inherent and corrective interventions)
  • Glove port distance to open containers (e.g., vials, syringes, cartridges, EM components) and equipment*
  • RABS gloves range of motion
  • Weight and orientation of materials and items to be handled via the glove port
  • Ability to install RABS gloves and donning RABS gloves in an aseptic manner
  • Orientation of RABS gloves during decontamination/disinfection, aeration, if applicable, and nonuse
  • Proximity to sharp objects or surfaces that can pierce RABS gloves
  • Position of EM apparatus, instruments, and sampling sites

*Note: Interventions cannot be performed directly using the RABS gloves, but rather by using a sterilized tool and/or sterile material for an additional layer of protection where the First Air concept needs to be maintained.

Rationale

With the use of RABS gloves, it is possible to perform interventions inside the RABS without the need to open it. The correct positioning of glove ports enables the operator to reach machine parts without the introduction of the operator gloves and gown, which are potential particle and microbial contamination sources. For this reason, the design and position of glove ports is an important decision point and should be part of the risk assessment and contamination control strategy (CCS).

Generally, a review of the positioning should be performed using a simulation method. Modeling, rendering, 3-D model, virtual reality, mock-up, or any method that allows for demonstration and testing of glove-related procedures with different operators of diverse heights and arm length could be used.

The glove port needs to be positioned to ensure that the RABS gloves have sufficient distance to the open containers (e.g., vials, syringes, cartridges, EM components, etc.) and to the equipment that comes into direct and indirect contact with the product. This allows for a RABS glove entry by the operator without disruption for First Air above critical surfaces.

The design of glove ports should be sufficiently cleanable (i.e., hygienic design) and resistant to the appropriate cleaning/disinfection agents. The design should also allow for the range of motion necessary with the RABS gloves to avoid excessive operator reaching or stretching of RABS gloves.

Restriction of airflow interrupting or interfering with adequate distribution of decontamination chemicals, vapors, or gases (i.e., typically only seen with cRABS) should be avoided.

If applicable, select position of glove stretchers to allow for automatic bio-decontamination of the whole surface (cRABS only).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Glove ports vs. gloves: These are two different components. A glove port is the fixed opening and flange structure mounted on the barrier wall; gloves are the replaceable gloves fitted to the port. The two have different design considerations:

  • Port placement: determines the range the operator can reach; once the barrier wall has been manufactured it is difficult to change
  • Glove selection: material, thickness and size; gloves can be replaced but must match the port design

Mock-up studies: PDA stresses that glove port placement must be verified early in the design through simulation or physical models. Methods include 3D models, virtual reality (VR) and physical mock-ups, and operators of different heights and arm lengths should take part in the testing.

Analogy

Designing glove ports is like designing a car's driver seat:

  • The positions of the steering wheel, gear lever and buttons must allow drivers of different builds to operate comfortably
  • If poorly designed, a tall driver cannot reach the pedals and a short driver cannot see the dashboard
  • Once the driver seat leaves the factory it cannot be changed much, and the same is true of glove ports

So "test drivers" (operators) of all builds must verify the design "before delivery" (before the equipment leaves the factory).

Key Notes

Key design principle - spacing: There must be sufficient distance between the glove ports and open containers (open vials, syringes and so on) to ensure that an operator's arms entering the RABS gloves do not disturb First Air. This is a key point that is often overlooked in design reviews but causes problems in actual operation.

Note: PDA states explicitly that operators should not use RABS gloves to touch product or critical surfaces directly, but should use sterilized tools or sterile materials as an additional protective layer. This is a common operational misunderstanding.

Q 1.5 What are the design considerations for restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves?

Original Text

Recommendation

Quality risk management (QRM) principles should be used to identify, assess, and address special considerations for the safe and effective use of RABS gloves to mitigate the risks posed by the installation and use of RABS gloves during aseptic operation (1).

Once the glove-port positioning is determined, a number of factors that may pose a risk to the performance of the aseptic process and the sterility of the product should be considered. The following considerations should be given to the composition of RABS gloves (3, 4):

  • Quality and design of RABS gloves should be sufficiently cleanable (i.e., hygienic design) and resistant to the appropriate cleaning/disinfection agents and steam sterilization.
  • Glove size, though usually supplied as standard size ranges, should be considered based on intended user population and demographic to ensure that gloves are sufficiently sized and practical for use.
  • Method of physical and visual inspection for integrity (See Topic 4).
  • Ability to confirm or test the integrity of RABS gloves (See Topic 4).
  • Ability to decontaminate, disinfect and, if applicable, aerate RABS gloves
  • Selection of glove material (e.g., ethylene propylene diene monomer (EPDM), chlorosulfonated polyethylene synthetic rubber (CSM)) to allow for resistance during sanitization/decontamination and use; also, to ensure sufficient dexterity to allow for the accurate execution of interventions
  • Thickness of glove material to address dexterity needed to perform task in RABS gloves
  • Thickness of glove material to resist puncture, cracking, or excessive wear
  • Ability to adequately install RABS gloves in glove ports to maintain aseptic status of the line

Additional operational considerations include, but are not limited to:

  • A sufficient number of spare RABS gloves should be available for each run.
  • Expiration date of RABS gloves
  • Exposure to direct sunlight that can degrade RABS gloves or glove seal material
  • Safe position for intermediate storage of RABS gloves in the RABS glove port after or between operations to avoid both the obstruction to First Air during operation, as well as ensuring that the gloves cannot be damaged by moving parts within the RABS

Rationale

Performing the above recommendations will reduce the possibility of leakages due to the tearing of RABS gloves, which impacts the integrity of the RABS. These recommendations are to promote compliance of good aseptic practices/techniques to reduce contamination risks and promote the prevention of open-door interventions.

With the use of RABS gloves, it is possible to perform interventions inside the RABS and to reach machine parts without the introduction of the operator gloves and gown (a potential particle and microbial contamination source). For this reason, the manner of usage, construction materials, and installation procedures is an important decision point and should, at a minimum, be recorded in the CCS.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Selecting RABS glove material is a classic engineering trade-off:

  • EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber): resistant to steam sterilization and chemicals, with good elasticity; a common choice
  • CSM (chlorosulfonated polyethylene synthetic rubber): good chemical resistance, and resistant to ozone and ultraviolet light
  • The thickness trade-off: too thin: easily punctured and damaged; too thick: insufficient dexterity and operator fatigue

Glove integrity testing: PDA treats this separately as Topic 4, which underlines its importance. A glove breach means the RABS barrier integrity has been compromised, which is equivalent to "a breach in the city wall".

Analogy

RABS gloves are like an advanced version of surgical gloves:

  • Surgical gloves must balance sense of touch (thin) and protection (no tears), and so must RABS gloves
  • Surgical gloves come in sizes, and RABS gloves also need to take the hand shapes of the user group into account
  • But RABS gloves must also withstand harsh conditions such as steam sterilization, chemical disinfectants and VPHP, far beyond the requirements for surgical gloves

Also, surgical gloves are discarded after use, whereas RABS gloves are reusable, so there are additional considerations such as expiry period, storage conditions and UV degradation.

Key Notes

Operational considerations that designers often overlook:

  • Spare management: sufficient spare gloves must be available for every run; if a glove is breached during a batch and there is no spare, a door may have to be opened for replacement (the worst case)
  • Glove position when not in use: gloves must not hang in the First Air path and block the airflow, and must not be damaged by moving parts inside the RABS
  • Sunlight exposure: direct sunlight accelerates rubber degradation, which is a real problem in cleanrooms with skylights or large glass windows

PDA stresses that the use, construction materials and installation procedures of gloves should at least be documented in the CCS (contamination control strategy).

Q 1.6 How should the restricted access barrier system (RABS) be designed to minimize the risk posed by interventions?

Original Text

Recommendation

Human interventions (i.e., open-door and/or inherent interventions) within the RABS are required during the setup and assembly of the aseptic filling machine, including the installation of RABS gloves, and during routine manufacturing operations (e.g., aseptic filling with open- and closed-door inherent and corrective interventions).

Risk-based assessments should be used to evaluate the need for and design of human interventions. Where possible, interventions should be eliminated, reduced, or modified to reduce their relative risk. A process should be created to monitor and control the frequency of interventions. Changes to the process should be made to improve the overall robustness of the manufacturing process and should eliminate the need for interventions, when possible.

It is not recommended to approach the need for manufacturing changes with the goal of reducing intervention. Corrective, open-door interventions should be eliminated or occur only as very rare events. Therefore, the prevention of open-door interventions should be a key factor in the consideration for designing a RABS (1, 6).

The positioning of RABS gloves in proper locations should eliminate the need for open-door interventions during filling operations where good aseptic technique applies. Interventions within the RABS (i.e., inherent and corrective) are to be designed to minimize microbiological ingress, including retention, within the RABS. Such factors are proximity to exposed sterile product, components, indirect product contact surfaces, intervention duration, complexity, and frequency.

The following points for consideration are applicable for an aseptic filling line and also for other aseptic working steps (e.g., dispensing, lyophilizer loading/unloading), where a RABS is used:

  • The RABS and the filling line should be designed to allow for interventions (i.e., open and closed door) in the Grade A RABS with due consideration of adherence to proper aseptic technique, for example, to follow First Air working principles for the operator during open-door interventions and also RABS gloves during closed-door interventions. See Question 1.1 for more information.
  • In the case of an open-door intervention that requires direct entry of part of the operator’s body within the Grade A area, design considerations should extend to operator posture and body positioning and ensure that the door openings and location of the RABS glove ports do not prevent risk of operator contact. Tools should be used in place of an operator entering the RABS when possible. Airflow visualization studies should be performed to confirm adequacy under dynamic conditions. Further precautions and controls are required for open-door interventions (1, 6-8). (See Topic 9.)
  • The number and location of glove ports should be designed based on the need for and ease of performance of interventions, in particular, in locations where First Air principles can be followed.
  • Materials and equipment within the RABS should be positioned to avoid crowding and the need to reach over exposed sterile products, materials, and surfaces.
  • The design and positioning of a RABS filling line should prevent the use of swing conveyors to pass from the front to the back of the filling line.
  • The RABS design should consider, under risk assessment, a zone of protection using a UDAF unit to cover, at a minimum, the open-door radius. The positions must be determined by a risk analysis and should consider typical open-door activities, such as the setup of stopper bowls or feeding lanes.
  • The dynamic use of the RABS, where there are inherent and/or corrective interventions being performed and equipment is operating, needs to be confirmed by airflow visualization studies (AVS) to demonstrate that unidirectional flow of air is occurring, and First Air principles are maintained.
  • The use of automated process steps (e.g., sterilization in place (SIP) after installation of the filling line), and intervention-reducing technology (e.g., rapid transfer port (RTP) filling path or components loading as closures, to install EM samplers for preventing mobile air sampler transfer) should be considered to minimize interventions during setup, operations, and sampling.
  • The use of robotics and advanced automation should be considered during the design of a new RABS line to increase the level of sterility assurance by removing operator interventions for critical processes (e.g., the setup of the filling path). When using robotics in the aseptic environment for aseptic filling, the design and operating principles must be carefully evaluated for operation suitability within a cleanroom environment. Robotic systems, regardless of type, bring a certain risk to the unidirectional airflow due to a tendency to generate turbulence. Therefore, robots should not be used for the sake of using robotics, but rather should bring a benefit for the aseptic filling process. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and airflow visualization studies (static and dynamic) should verify the impact of the robot to the unidirectional airflow and its impact on First Air. Besides the robot itself the requirements to meet first air also applies for any tools the robot is carrying and connections made on the robot (e.g., media hoses etc.). In addition to considerations regarding impact to First Air, cleanability, disinfection and accessibility to the robot also require consideration.

Rationale

All interventions into a RABS (i.e., open-door intervention) and within the RABS (i.e., closed-door intervention) in the vicinity of exposed sterile product and critical surfaces can add the risk of microbiological contamination to sterile product. Where possible, the elimination or reduction of these activities through process design, equipment layout, technology, and automation can reduce risk. Where activities cannot be eliminated or reduced, process and equipment design that allows for proper aseptic technique and practices can reduce risk. Where interventions cannot be eliminated or reduced in number, modifying these activities to reduce risk factors can reduce the overall relative risk.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Classification of interventions and risk levels:

  • Inherent interventions: operations expected to occur by process design, such as EM sampling and part adjustments
  • Corrective interventions: operations needed because of abnormal situations, such as clearing jammed vials or adjusting misalignment
  • Open-door interventions: operations that require opening the RABS door; the highest risk
  • Closed-door interventions: operations performed through the glove ports; relatively lower risk

PDA's philosophy for controlling interventions (in order of priority):

  1. Eliminate: use design so that the intervention does not need to occur
  2. Reduce: lower the frequency of the intervention
  3. Modify: change how the intervention is performed to reduce risk factors (for example, using a glove port instead of opening a door)

Key Notes

Robotics and automation are not a cure-all:

PDA takes a cautious view of robots: "robots should not be used for the sake of using robotics, but rather should bring a benefit for the aseptic filling process."

Key challenges of robots in an aseptic environment:

  • Robot movement generates turbulence, which disrupts the UDAF
  • The tools and hose connections carried by the robot must also meet First Air requirements
  • Their effect on airflow must be verified through CFD (computational fluid dynamics) and AVS (airflow visualization studies)
  • Cleanability, feasibility of disinfection and accessibility are additional design challenges

Analogy

Controlling interventions in a RABS is like the design philosophy of an aircraft cockpit:

  • Eliminate interventions = the autopilot handles all routine operations
  • Reduce interventions = the pilot needs to operate manually only at critical moments
  • Modify interventions = even when manual operation is needed, it is done through a control stick rather than by touching the engine directly (glove port vs. opening the door)
  • Open-door interventions = a pilot opening the engine cowling in flight: clearly extremely high risk, done only when absolutely necessary

Key Notes - UDAF Zone of Protection Design

PDA puts forward an important design concept, the Zone of Protection: the UDAF coverage should extend at least over the opening radius of the door. This means that even during an open-door intervention the critical zone is still protected by positive-pressure UDAF airflow, reducing the risk of ingress of lower-grade air from outside.

In addition, the use of a swing conveyor to carry product from the front of the filling line to the back is prohibited, because it would require operators to reach across exposed sterile product, violating the First Air principle.

Practical Application

CDMO new line design scenario: You are designing a RABS for a new aseptic filling line. The client requires a "fully automated filling path set-up" capability.

  • Step 1: Inventory all intervention points: list the set-up, the inherent interventions during the run and the possible corrective interventions
  • Step 2: Evaluate them one by one: which can be eliminated through automation/robotics? Which can be changed from open-door to glove port operation?
  • Step 3: If a robot is introduced, CFD simulation and AVS confirmation of the effect on airflow are required
  • Step 4: For all interventions that cannot be eliminated, design the glove port positions, evaluate the First Air path and develop SOPs

Key point: every decision must be supported by a risk assessment and included in the CCS documentation.

Q 1.7 How can open-door interventions be prevented during filling?

Original Text

Recommendation

RABS are designed with the goal of operating predominately as a closed system. Where open-door interventions may be required, appropriate measures must be taken to mitigate the potential for increased risk to the aseptic process.

Detailed design reviews of the production process undertaken at the onset of design can help avoid or minimize the need for open-door activities during the filling operation (1). These reviews should consider, but not be limited to, the following:

  • Since the open-door barrier manual set-up process presents the highest risk of contamination of the Grade A zone when entering sterilized parts, the design of the set-up operation should be assessed using QRM principles to reduce any unnecessary risk.
  • Following QRM principles, RABS design and operation should avoid the need for open-door interventions so after the last bio-decontamination at set up the barrier doors remain closed until batch end. Primarily inherent and corrective interventions should be via barrier closed and any open-door intervention fully justified and rare.
  • RTPs used for introduction of pre-sterilized process components, consumables, product containers and closures plus EM plates if required to be changed or recovered for incubation during aseptic manufacturing operations.
  • Use of robotic systems and automation that drives intervention free processing including introduction of EM systems that do not rely on growth media plate change (e.g., real time continuous viable monitoring).
  • Design considerations equally extend to consideration of the use and position of glove ports and should be considered in conjunction with the intended operations to define access requirements within the enclosed system and the type of operations for which interventions may be required.
  • Design considerations extend to the use of clean-in-place (CIP)/SIP and single-use systems for fluid or powder pathways.

Periodic reviews of the type, frequency, and duration of interventions for the filling process should be reviewed to inform the next periodic aseptic process simulation (APS) protocol and to otherwise inform a potential engineering or other control solution that may be introduced to eliminate the need for future similar open-door interventions (1, 4, 9).

Notwithstanding these considerations, not all possible events that may be encountered during manufacturing operations can be considered during the design or early stages of RABS introduction for a new product or process. Rather, the intent of a well-designed aseptic program involving the use of barrier technology is to ensure that the RABS protection concept is the primary focus and continuously assessed, with the aim of eliminating or reducing the need to open the barrier during filling activities. When a closed-door intervention is possible to address an issue, an open-door intervention must be prohibited.

Procedural controls should be established to ensure that adequate provisions are included in governing procedures and processes for such unavoidable scenarios and should extend to a detailed review and documentation including, but not limited to:

  • Preassessment of airflow visualization studies (AVS) and positive-pressure differentials to ensure that positive airflow from the enclosure to the external environment can be maintained prior to opening doors. A pre-intervention assessment should also extend to a documented review of what needs to be performed and whether the activity can be considered comparable to a preexisting intervention (1, 4, 9).
  • Consideration of whether the unqualified intervention represents a departure from critical controls established and whether a deviation or formal risk assessment is required in support of finalizing and undertaking the planned intervention. The need for additional EM data, personnel monitoring requirements, sterility sample requirements, particulate-monitoring data and alarm review should be considered. An assessment of whether a media qualification and AVS may be required prior to release of the batch should also be understood and documented.
  • A robust disinfection of the nonproduct-contact surfaces, implicated by the open-door activity using appropriately qualified disinfectants. If necessary, to ensure there is no disinfectant residue, the use of 70% IPA after disinfection should be considered.
  • Consideration of need for batch segregation and/or discarding of any filled product or components that may have been compromised or placed at unnecessary risk during the intervention.
  • Line clearance to eliminate open containers and/or components as applicable that may be in the vicinity of the intervention.
  • Confirmation that Grade A conditions have been reestablished prior to recommencing filling.
"Note: Although not recommended, in rare cases, an unanticipated corrective intervention may be required that necessitates open-door operator intervention (e.g., to mitigate equipment challenges). In such cases, the inherent increased risk to the product requires careful consideration in line with formal QRM principles and should inform future engineering controls or other provisions that may be introduced with the aim of eliminating recurrences or the requirement for the open-door intervention. Before the intervention is executed, it needs to be risk assessed. If the risk-assessment does not support the execution of the intervention, it should not occur."

Rationale

By design, the intent of a RABS or other barrier system is to separate the operator from the critical process zone to reduce the overall risk of contamination. Since interventions within a barrier system pose the highest risk to the aseptic core, the aim of any barrier technology, combined with a system of (engineering) controls, should be to reduce or eliminate any unnecessary interventions. Hence, the use of the barrier technology in conjunction with a system of controls comprising suitable design, robust quality systems, and aseptic oversight is required.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Open-Door Intervention: An operation that can only be performed by opening the RABS door. This is the highest-risk event in a RABS environment because it directly defeats the core protective function of the barrier system, which is to separate people from the Grade A critical zone.

Closed-Door Intervention: An operation completed through the glove ports without opening the door. The risk is significantly lower than that of an open-door intervention, and it is the preferred approach while the RABS is in operation.

Core principle: Where a closed-door intervention can solve the problem, open-door interventions must be prohibited. This is not a "recommendation" but an explicit prohibition.

Analogy

Imagine a delicate heart surgery: the positive pressure in the operating room is like the UDAF airflow protection of a RABS. If the assistant had to open the operating room door every time an instrument was handed in, bacteria from the corridor would flood in. The best approach is to pass instruments in through a pass-through hatch (like an RTP, rapid transfer port), or to have a robot deliver them automatically (like an automated system), so the door never needs to be opened from start to finish.

If the door must be opened (for example, an emergency transfer of large equipment), you must first confirm that the corridor has been cleaned, positive pressure is still maintained and all exposed sterile surfaces are covered. This is the "procedural control" described in the document.

Key Notes

The strict position of EU Annex 1: The EU regulation explicitly requires that open-door interventions during filling be "very rare". The PDA text goes further and states that they should be eliminated at the design stage through QRM assessment. This means:

  • Open-door interventions cannot exist in an SOP as a "standard operation"
  • Every open-door intervention should trigger a deviation investigation or a formal risk assessment
  • The need for additional EM data, personnel monitoring, sterility samples and so on must be evaluated
  • Grade A conditions must be confirmed as restored before filling is resumed

Batch segregation considerations: After an open-door intervention, the affected filled product or components may need to be segregated or destroyed, which is a major commercial loss. Investing at the design stage to eliminate the need for open-door interventions is therefore far more cost-effective than dealing with the consequences afterwards.

Practical Application

Scenario: Your CDMO site is designing a new RABS filling line. The design team asks for 12 glove ports, but the budget is limited. How do you decide?

  • Step 1: List all expected inherent interventions and possible corrective interventions
  • Step 2: Run a QRM assessment for each intervention: can it be eliminated through automation? Can it be replaced by an RTP?
  • Step 3: For the interventions that must still be done manually, confirm that the glove port locations cover all needs
  • Step 4: At the mock-up stage, verify that every intervention can be completed with the doors closed

The upfront cost of investing in RTPs and automation may be recovered the first time it avoids a deviation investigation triggered by an open-door intervention.

Q 1.8 How can contamination risks of Grade A environments and compromise of sterilized component sterility be minimized during setup?

Original Text

Recommendation

Setup, if done through closed doors via the glove ports, requires fewer precautions; however, setup most often requires activities through open doors. If that is the case, there are several ways to minimize the risk:

  • Operate with only one door open at a time.
  • As much as possible, equipment should be assembled prior to sterilization to avoid the need for aseptic connections. Sterilized items should be introduced with protective covers or sterile boxes and those covers or boxes should only be removed after the doors have been closed through the glove ports. For legacy systems, where removal of protective covers after the doors are closed is not possible, alternative approaches must be considered and documented as part of the CCS. (See Topic 6.)
  • Utilize a "top down" approach to line build, when possible, so installation is not occurring over an exposed critical surface (e.g., stopper hopper, stopper slide, stopper bowl).
  • Don additional gowning, such as sterile sleeves or longer gloves (1).
  • Perform operator activities with a primary and secondary operator model: In this model, the secondary operator opens the door and hands material to the primary operator who only operates within the Grade A space. (See Topic 9.)
  • The RABS design should consider, under a risk assessment, a zone of protection using an UDAF unit to at least cover the open-door radius. The positions have to be determined by a risk analysis and should consider frequent open-door activities such as the setup of stopper bowls or feeding lanes. Figure 1.8-1 shows an example for UDAF extension (1, 9).
  • Evaluate the need to take additional (continuous) air samples (viable and total particulates) in the Grade B area with UDAF immediately surrounding the open-door area for the duration of the open-door setup activities (1, 9). (See PDA Technical Report No. 62: Recommended Practices for Manual Aseptic Processes for additional guidance for personnel behavior.)
  • AVS qualification should be done to ensure that there is a UDAF flush away and out of the RABS during setup.
  • Evaluate the possibility of performing VPHP decontamination after setup (cRABS or oRABS with cleanroom).
  • After setup, the use of automatic decontamination reduces the risk of ingress of the operator into Grade A.

Rationale

Open-door activities typically necessary for equipment setup are of a higher risk as personnel must ingress into the Grade A space. All activities that minimize the risk of introducing bioburden into the Grade A environment will reduce the risk to the environment and, subsequently, the product. Such activities include, but are not limited to, the following:

  • Minimizing the number of open doors to reduce the potential disruption of the UDAF within the RABS.
  • Maintaining protective covers on equipment, such as bonnets on a stopper bowl, keeps the sterilized surfaces from being exposed until the RABS doors have been closed and ensuring greater protection. It also allows for the door-open time to be minimized, completing the setup activities through the glove ports in a second step (4).
  • The utilization of additional protective measures (sterile sleeves, longer gloves, etc.) needs to be evaluated based upon an approved risk assessment.
  • Similarly, the primary/secondary operator model safeguards the integrity of the gowning of the primary operator, further reducing the risk of introducing contamination. (See PDA TR-62 for additional information.)
  • Having UDAF immediately adjacent to the doors, so that once the door is open, the area remains covered by Grade B surrounding the open door, will reduce the risk of introducing bioburden into the RABS enclosure.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The setup phase is one of the highest-risk periods in RABS operation, because operators must enter the Grade A area to install equipment components (such as filling needles, stopper bowls and guide rails). Unlike interventions during filling, setup usually cannot avoid door opening entirely.

"Top Down" Approach: Install the upper components first and the lower components afterwards, so that installation actions never take place above surfaces that are already exposed. This is a simple but highly effective contamination risk control strategy.

Primary/Secondary Operator Model: The secondary operator opens the door and passes materials, while the primary operator concentrates on the operations inside the Grade A area. This protects the gowning integrity of the primary operator, who does not need to touch door handles or items in the Grade B area.

Analogy

The setup phase is like moving into a disinfected operating room: you have to carry the furniture (equipment components) in, but every time the door opens, dust from the corridor comes in. The smartest approach is to:

  • Wrap the furniture in plastic film first (protective covers/sterile boxes) and unwrap it after it is inside
  • Open only one door at a time (to avoid cross-flow)
  • Place the ceiling lights first and the tables afterwards (top down)
  • Have one person at the doorway to hand items in (the secondary operator) while the person inside concentrates on placing them (the primary operator)
  • Spray-disinfect everything once the move is complete (VPHP bio-decontamination)

Key Notes

The challenge of legacy systems: The document specifically notes that for older systems where the protective covers cannot be removed through the glove ports after the doors are closed, an alternative approach must be developed and documented in the CCS. This shows that regulators recognize differences in equipment capability, but they do not accept an attitude of "we can't, so we don't". A written alternative and a risk assessment are required.

Post-setup VPHP decontamination: An important advantage of a cRABS is that automated VPHP bio-decontamination can be performed once setup is complete. This step greatly reduces the microbial risk introduced during setup, effectively giving the Grade A area a "reset". An oRABS can also consider this practice if it is combined with a cleanroom.

Practical Application

Setup-phase risk reduction checklist:

  • Have all sterilizable components been pre-assembled before sterilization?
  • Do all sterilized items have protective covers/sterile boxes? Are the covers designed so that they can be removed through the glove ports with the doors closed?
  • Does the assembly sequence follow the "top down" principle?
  • Does UDAF coverage extend over all areas where doors need to be opened?
  • Is there a post-setup VPHP decontamination capability (cRABS)?
  • Do operators wear additional sterile protective equipment (sterile sleeves, extended-length gloves)?

  2. How does the primary/secondary operator model provide higher sterility assurance than single-operator working? Analyze it from the perspective of gowning integrity.
  3. Compare cRABS and oRABS in their post-setup bio-decontamination capability, and the impact of this on CCS strategy.
Q 1.9 What construction design considerations are required for materials and maintenance?

Original Text

Recommendation

RABS surfaces should be manufactured in materials compatible with the environmental classification conditions required for the proposed use.

Construction Materials Considerations:

  • Interior metallic surfaces in direct or indirect contact with the primary packing should be constructed in 316 stainless steel or better and, for the rest of the line, metallic components could be constructed of low-carbon 304 stainless steel or better (1, 6).
  • Interior transparent surfaces made of polycarbonate or glass.
  • Seals joining the RABS with upstream and downstream equipment should take temperature and differential pressures into consideration (e.g., depyrogenation tunnel to filler RABS or freeze dryer).
  • Cleanable with detergents commonly used in the cleanroom with no absorbing or desorbing effect when in contact with them (10).

All RABS surfaces should be resistant to the following conditions and agents to avoid the release of particles:

  • Scratches and abrasions.
  • Detergents and sporicidals used for CIP and cleaning-out-of-place, or disinfection (e.g., turntables, guides) (11).
  • Sterilization process as SIP for fixed-system components (e.g., filling path).
  • External sterilization processes with moist heat or radiation, or chemical sterilization processes for complementary components removable from RABS (e.g., stopper bowl, feeding lanes), or ready to use.

Maintenance Considerations

The internal RABS working areas should be designed to minimize the risk of contamination in critical zones for troubleshooting and repair activities.

The access to maintenance panels and/or mechanical spaces should be outside the RABS and below the operating/processing level (product height) as much as possible. Even if outside of the machine, these areas should not be opened without additional controls being evaluated and implemented (e.g., requires to exchange gowning after those activities).

All maintenance activities for a RABS and related equipment should be scheduled in a plan to occur at the same time, when it is possible, to avoid multiple preventive-maintenance work-order days.

Rationale

The RABS and equipment construction materials should be designed to meet such production requirements as cleanability, sanitization, and practicality of use, with the scope to reduce the contamination risk by particles that could be released from routine use or from eventual damages and abrasion occurred during routine production or maintenance activities.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The logic of choosing 316 vs. 304 stainless steel:

  • 316SS (molybdenum-containing): Used for surfaces in direct or indirect contact with the product. Molybdenum gives stronger resistance to chloride corrosion, which suits environments with frequent use of disinfectants and SIP steam sterilization.
  • 304SS (low carbon): Used for the remaining metal components on the line that do not contact the product directly. It costs less but still offers basic corrosion resistance.

Hygienic Design: All surfaces must be cleanable, must not absorb or release chemicals, and must withstand repeated disinfection and sterilization without generating particles. This is a fundamental GMP requirement for pharmaceutical equipment.

Analogy

Choosing RABS construction materials is like choosing the countertop material for a restaurant kitchen: you would not use wood (it absorbs water and harbors bacteria), you would use stainless steel. But if the kitchen is washed every day with strong acidic cleaners, you need a higher grade of stainless steel (316SS) to prevent corrosion. The transparent panels that guests can see through are like the glass display case in a sushi bar, made of polycarbonate or glass.

Placing the maintenance panels outside the RABS and below product height is like a restaurant putting the plumbing access hatch on the lower part of a wall outside the kitchen: when the pipes are repaired, the food on the preparation counter is not affected.

Key Notes

Temperature/differential pressure considerations for seals: The seals that connect the RABS to upstream and downstream equipment (such as depyrogenation tunnels and freeze dryers) must withstand both high temperature (SIP) and differential pressure conditions. This is an easily overlooked point in design that often causes problems in operation. Seal failure leads to loss of integrity of the Grade A environment.

Consolidated scheduling of maintenance activities: PDA recommends scheduling all maintenance activities at the same time, to avoid repeated maintenance causing repeated potential contamination exposure. This is a pragmatic recommendation: each maintenance event requires additional cleaning and possibly requalification, and doing the work together greatly reduces downtime.

  2. List the chemical and physical conditions that RABS surfaces must withstand. Which material requirements are stricter for a cRABS than for an oRABS, and why?

Q 1.10 What unique design provisions are required for highly potent compounds exposed within closed restricted access barrier systems (cRABS)?

Original Text

Recommendation

Environmental Health Safety vs. Good Manufacturing Practices

When designing a RABS for aseptic highly potent compounds, both the good manufacturing practice (GMP) and environmental health and safety (EHS) aspects are very important. While overpressure of the containment is necessary from a GMP perspective, negative pressure is required from the EHS standpoint to ensure operator safety (6).

Therefore, the pressure differential between the inside of the RABS and the outside environment must be positive. To avoid contamination from the most highly potent exposure area (e.g., filling zone), this zone is operated in negative pressure towards adjacent RABS sections. To make this physically and technically possible, a cRABS is needed. To allow for the creation and maintenance of the pressure differences during operation, it needs at least a cRABS, if recirculating air is used, a dedicated air handling unit (AHU) must be present.

Additional Considerations:

  • Ability to safely change out terminal HEPAs by a safe-change filter system or one-pass aeration systems (with bag-in/bag-out exhaust HEPAs) (See Figure 1.10-1.)
  • Interlocked door sensors and other mechanical devices to ensure operator safety
  • Vacuum systems should be avoided where possible; otherwise, the integration of filters that prevent contamination at the base of the machine should be considered
  • Transfer ports should be present to manage the removal of contained material used for spillage clean-up without having to open the RABS.*
  • Pressure differential-monitoring systems to alarm in case of loss of pressure differential or RABS leakage.
  • Double glass doors are to be avoided (See Figure 1.10-1.)
  • Important for the commissioning of such products is the correct protective equipment of the employees and their behavior in case of an accidental breach.

*Note: This consideration does not apply for monitoring equipment using vacuum, such as total-particle counters or active viable monitoring. The monitoring equipment must be located in the most critical positions according to the EM risk analysis. A strategy to prevent contamination of the vacuum lines and the monitoring equipment must be evaluated (e.g., the particulate filter above the machine plate in the aseptic area) immediately after the sampling head for active viable (6). The sampling head must be treated as any other exposed equipment surface within the RABS and needs to be appropriately cleaned before the sterilization cycle. For particulate measurement systems, a different strategy must be considered. Total particle counters should be dedicated to certain areas and changed in situ to reduce the movement of the equipment and to limit exposure. Particulate filter can be placed after the device to ensure contamination prevention of the vacuum lines and pumps.

Cleaning

A highly preferred method for cleaning a cRABS contaminated with toxic substances is the automatic wash-in-place (WIP) process. The WIP process can either be executed with spray guns mounted inside the RABS for manual wash-down or with a spray ball to achieve an automatic wash down. This means that even hard-to-reach areas can be cleaned more easily while the doors remain closed and, therefore, the risk of operator exposure is minimized. The handling of the wastewater must be considered with a special focus on the draining systems (i.e., EU Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products excludes drains in Grade A or Grade B environments) and toxic water separation. Depending on toxicity, measuring residuals in wastewater is recommended to ensure the RABS environment is free from potential potent residuals. A risk assessment should be performed to determine the proper neutralization of the wastewater from the toxic process and proper design. Nevertheless, the machine and the containment must, of course, be designed in such a way that all parts can be easily cleaned and equipped in a hygienic design.

Rationale

Potent compounds are hazardous to operators and, while product sterility must be maintained, it is equally important to ensure that operators and the environment they operate in can be protected from exposure. Utilization of cRABS, with negative pressure and WIP systems, allows for the operation and cleaning of the system, without ever exposing the operator or the environment outside of the RABS to the compound.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The GMP vs. EHS pressure conflict: This is the classic design problem when handling highly potent compounds (such as ADCs, antibody-drug conjugates, and cytotoxic drugs).

  • GMP requirement: The RABS must be at positive pressure relative to the external environment, to prevent external microorganisms from entering Grade A
  • EHS requirement: Areas containing toxic substances must be at negative pressure, to prevent toxic substances leaking out and harming operators

Solution (pressure gradient design):

  • The RABS as a whole is kept at positive pressure relative to the background environment (meeting GMP)
  • The filling zone (the area of highest exposure) is kept at negative pressure relative to the other sections of the RABS (meeting EHS)
  • This "local negative pressure within overall positive pressure" design can only be achieved with a cRABS, because it requires tightness and an independent air handling system

Analogy

Imagine a hospital that handles radioactive materials: the whole hospital is kept at positive pressure relative to the outside (to keep dirty outside air out), but the radiology procedure room is kept at negative pressure relative to the corridor (to prevent radioactive material leaking into the corridor). This is the concept of "overall positive pressure, local negative pressure".

A WIP system is like installing an automatic car wash inside the RABS: without opening the car doors, the internal spray nozzles wash away all the toxic residues. The wastewater must also go through a dedicated treatment system and cannot be discharged into the ordinary sewer.

Key Notes

Why does only a cRABS apply? An oRABS cannot maintain the tightness and pressure gradient, so it cannot satisfy both the GMP positive pressure and the EHS local negative pressure requirements. For filling highly potent compounds, a cRABS is the minimum requirement (an isolator is the better option).

EU Annex 1 drain restrictions: The EU regulation prohibits drains in Grade A and Grade B environments. Wastewater management for the WIP system is therefore a major design challenge: it requires a special wastewater collection and neutralization system, and conventional floor drains cannot be used.

Safe-Change HEPA Filter System: When replacing HEPA filters contaminated with toxic substances, a Bag-In/Bag-Out (BIBO) system must be used to ensure that operators are not exposed to the contaminated filters. This technology is commonly used in biosafety laboratories and nuclear facilities.

Pressure Gradient Design Logic

Pressure gradient illustration:

External environment (Grade B): baseline pressure P0
        |
        v  (+15 Pa)
RABS outer section:      P0 + 15 Pa
        |
        v  (-10 Pa)
Filling zone (high-exposure area):   P0 + 5 Pa

Result:
- Filling zone > external environment (+5 Pa) --> GMP positive pressure requirement OK
- Filling zone < RABS outer section (-10 Pa) --> EHS negative pressure protection OK

  2. Why does the document recommend avoiding vacuum systems? Where they must be used (such as for particle counters), what protective measures should be taken?

Q 1.11 What are the best engineering design considerations for in-line environmental monitoring (EM)?

Original Text

Recommendation

Monitoring should be performed at locations posing the highest risk of contamination of the sterile equipment surfaces, containers, closures, and product. The selection of monitoring locations and the orientation and positioning of sampling devices should be justified in an EM risk analysis and appropriate to obtain reliable data from the critical zones (1, 4, 12).

In-line Viable Monitoring:

  • Viable air samplers located near critical process zones (e.g., accumulation table, filling station, stoppering section).
  • Consider location near glove ports for ease of plate placement, removal, and storage.
  • Consider installing an EM plate holder for staging multiple EM plates as well as holding the lid of the air sampler, if applicable. This stand should be installed near to the air sampler and within reach via glove ports.
  • Verify active air samplers are part of the calibration program (airflow).
  • Consider First Air requirements for critical surfaces (e.g., initial load of agar plates, replacement of settle plates).

In-line Total Particulate Monitoring:

  • Total particle air sampler located near critical process zones within a one foot radius of the working position (e.g., accumulation table, filling, stoppering) (4).
  • If air samplers have covers, verify sampler is near glove ports for ease of cover placement or removal.
  • Verify air sampler is part of the calibration program (airflow, particulates typically 0.5 μm and 5.0 μm).
  • Ensure that the tube between the sampling probe (isokinetic funnel) and particle counter is <1 m minimizing bends or radii. If deviating from this requirement, particle loss studies need to demonstrate the functionality (1).

If considering the use of an alternate rapid microbiological methods (real-time monitoring via biofluorescent particle-counting), considerations should include, but not be limited to, the following (1, 6, 12, 13):

  • Sampling locations must be representative and close to conventional settle plates to show equivalency of the system for validation and data collection during production.
  • Suitable installation location of the counter to ensure accessibility for retraction of gelatine filter/agar plate for possibility of identifying the autofluorescence unit.
  • Tube length from sampling point to counter needs to be considered and validated by particle loss studies if >1 m.
  • Alarm management for autofluorescence unit hit must be in place and validated.

Temp/RH/DP/Airflow Velocity (6):

  • Sensors should be placed in representative locations—located downstream of UDAF/HEPA filters but above product pathway—so as not to interfere with equipment operation and potential interventions.

Alarm and Notification Systems:

  • Warnings (alerts and alarms) should be in place to alert operators in the event of any deviations from the predefined EM parameters.
  • Where alarm delays are utilized, a duration of delay must be justified.

Data Analysis and Reporting:

  • Consider having the inline EM sensors integral to the RABS control system to facilitate central operator control and integration of EM readings to equipment interlocks (e.g., high particle counts halts filling).
  • Alert levels should be established, and then trended, followed by periodic review.

Rationale

EM should effectively evaluate the state of environmental conditions within the RABS (e.g., total particulates, viables, air quality, airflow and pressure cascade).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Environmental Monitoring is an easily underestimated area of RABS design. EM is not just "putting out a few culture plates" but a complete engineering design problem involving sampling locations, tubing length, calibration procedures, alarm systems and data integration.

Key design principles:

  • Location selection: Monitoring points must be at the locations of highest contamination risk (the filling station, the stoppering area, the accumulation table), not at the locations that are most convenient to install
  • Accessibility: EM equipment must be operable through the glove ports (changing plates, lids and so on), otherwise every operation would require opening the door
  • Tubing limits: The sampling tube of a particle counter should be <1 m long and bends should be avoided, otherwise particle losses will make the data unreliable
  • Isokinetic Sampling: The intake velocity of the sampling probe must match the velocity of the surrounding airflow

Analogy

An EM system is like the smoke detector and air quality monitor in a house. You would not put a smoke detector in a storeroom with no appliances (a low-risk area); you would put it in the kitchen and bedroom (high-risk areas). The detector also must not be installed directly in front of a vent (the smoke would be blown away and missed), nor too far away (the response would be too slow).

The <1 m limit on the sampling tube of a particle counter is like drinking bubble tea through a very long straw: the longer the tube and the more bends it has, the more easily the pearls (particles) get stuck along the way, and the less you get (detect).

Key Notes

The future trend of real-time microbial monitoring: The document mentions biofluorescent particle counting as an alternative rapid microbiological method (RMM). This technology enables real-time, continuous monitoring of viable microorganisms and no longer depends on changing culture plates (changing plates is itself an intervention).

From a regulatory standpoint, however, a new method must still be validated for equivalence against the traditional settle plate method, and the sampling location must be close to the routine settle plates to allow comparison.

EM and equipment interlocks: Integrating EM data into the RABS control system (for example, automatically stopping filling at a high particle count) is an advanced and valuable design concept. It moves the system from "passive monitoring" to "active control" and greatly raises the level of sterility assurance.

  2. What benefits and potential problems might an interlock design that "automatically stops filling at a high particle count" bring? Consider the impact of false-positive alarms.
  3. Compare the advantages and disadvantages of traditional settle plate monitoring and biofluorescent real-time monitoring in a RABS environment, especially the effect on intervention frequency.

Recommendation

Retrofitting a line into a RABS is normally a temporary solution to maintain compliance with GMP. However, retrofitting can be beneficial by increasing sterility assurance while the company works on the longer-term implementation of a full RABS or isolator design.

Typical retrofit scenarios, include:

  • Adding transfer interfaces (e.g. RTPs) to existing RABS

Retrofitting a RABS or transferring system interfaces along the filling line is recommended to address the following needs:

First Air Principle Maintained

When retrofitting, the design of the RABS must work around the existing design of the filling equipment. Key Concepts

Retrofitting: The process of upgrading an existing filling line to a RABS. This is not the ideal option (a new design is better), but for sites that need to improve GMP compliance in the short term it is a pragmatic transitional strategy.

Typical upgrade paths:

  • Conventional A/B filling → oRABS: The most common retrofit scenario. Barrier panels and doors are added around the existing filling line.
  • oRABS → cRABS: More difficult; it requires added tightness and possibly an independent air handling system.
  • Adding an RTP: Adding a rapid transfer port to an existing RABS to reduce the need to open doors.

Interface Design: The connection points between the RABS and upstream and downstream equipment (such as depyrogenation tunnels, freeze dryers and transport carts) are the most critical part of a retrofit design. It must be ensured that the pressure gradient and the First Air principle are not compromised at the interface.

Analogy

A RABS retrofit is like adding airtight windows and air conditioning to a house that is already built. You cannot tear down the whole house and rebuild it (too expensive, too long a production stoppage), but you can upgrade step by step: first replace the ordinary windows with airtight ones (add barrier panels), then add a total heat exchanger (differential pressure control), then install a pass-through hatch (RTP) so that the delivery person does not have to open the front door every time.

The biggest challenge is that the layout of the house is already fixed and you have to find a way to work within the existing space. Sometimes the window position is unsuitable for an airtight window (glove port locations are restricted), and sometimes the wall is too thin to fit a pass-through hatch (structural limitations). This is why the document says "retrofitting may be difficult".

Key Notes

The value of CFD simulation in retrofits: Using 3-D models and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation before the retrofit is an extremely important step. The geometry of a retrofitted RABS is often less ideal than that of a new design (there may be protrusions and irregular corners), and the airflow pattern may show unexpected turbulence or dead zones. CFD simulation can identify these problems before the actual retrofit.

Retrofit strategy for freeze dryer loading/unloading: The document recommends a phased approach: implement a semi-automated system first, then move to a fully automated system. This is a pragmatic recommendation, because freeze dryer loading and unloading is one of the most complex interfaces on a RABS filling line, involving the movement of large numbers of containers and temperature control.

Regulator-driven retrofits: Many retrofit projects are driven by requirements from health authorities (such as an FDA 483 observation or a compliance gap following the revision of EU Annex 1). The retrofit plan must be documented in the CCS and form part of a long-term barrier technology upgrade plan.

  • Can the glove port locations cover all necessary interventions? (Verify with a mock-up)
  • Is UDAF coverage sufficient to cover the door-opening areas?
  • Can an RTP be added to reduce the frequency of door opening?
  • Can the interface pressure gradient with the freeze dryer/tunnel be achieved?
  • Has CFD simulation verified the airflow pattern after the retrofit?
  • Is the retrofit plan documented in the CCS, with a clear long-term upgrade timeline?

Q 2.1 Room Classification Surrounding RABS

Original Text

Q 2.1. What should be the classification of the room surrounding the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

The minimum classification of the room immediately surrounding the RABS must be Grade B. However, a manufacturer may also choose to maintain these areas at more stringent levels of control when assessed for risk, especially in the area immediately surrounding the RABS doors, which may require execution of open-door interventions (i.e., during setup or filling) (1, 4).

Rationale

A RABS provides an enclosed and separated environment designed to reduce the risk of contamination to product, components, and product-contact surfaces when compared to conventional "curtained" aseptic processing operations. However, RABS are not fully sealed from the immediate surrounding environment and, in some designs, allow for open-door interventions by aseptic operators. As such, the adjacent background cleanroom environment must be appropriately classified and maintained to protect the critical processing zone(s) within the RABS; therefore, the cleanroom environment should not be lower than Grade B.

Companies should provide the maximum assurance possible and practicable so that the controlled RABS environment is not impacted by the surrounding environmental conditions since the RABS is not completely isolated from the surroundings, unlike closed isolators, for example.

"Note: The minimum regulatory expectations (e.g., FDA, EMA) for the classification of manufacturing spaces immediately adjacent (i.e., background) to the Grade A critical processing zone is Grade B."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Grade B (Grade B cleanroom): This is the minimum classification required for the environment surrounding a RABS. A Grade B area must meet particle limits close to those of Grade A at rest, and has more relaxed but still strict limits in operation.

The key difference between a RABS and an isolator: A RABS is not a completely sealed system. There are air exchange paths between it and the surrounding environment, which is the fundamental reason the background environment must be kept at a high grade. An isolator is completely sealed, so its background environment can be reduced to Grade C or even Grade D.

Analogy

Think of a RABS as an operating room with a screen window: the screen (the barrier) blocks most contaminants but is not completely sealed. The corridor outside the operating room (the background environment) therefore also has to be kept reasonably clean. If the corridor is dirty and untidy, dust and microorganisms can still get into the operating area through the gaps despite the screen. An isolator, by contrast, is like a space station: completely sealed, and it does not matter that there is a vacuum outside.

Key Notes

The regulatory baseline: Both FDA and EMA explicitly require the background environment of a Grade A critical processing area to be at least Grade B. This is not a recommendation but a mandatory requirement.

The principle of tightened risk control: The document specifically notes that in the areas near RABS doors where door-opening operations may be performed, firms may need to apply stricter controls than Grade B. This reflects the spirit of quality risk management (QRM): adjust the level of control according to the actual risk.

Practical Application

In CDMO operations, clients often ask: "Is the background environment of our RABS line Grade B or higher?" This directly affects the client's confidence in the sterility assurance of their product. If a CDMO can provide tightened environmental control in the door areas (such as additional UDAF protection), it becomes an important competitive advantage, especially with clients developing high-value biologics.

  1. Why can the background environment of a RABS not be reduced to Grade C or Grade D as it can for an isolator? Explain from the perspective of system tightness.
  2. If a CDMO wants to apply stricter control than Grade B in the RABS door areas, what specific measures can it take?

Q 2.2 Environmental Monitoring in Extended UDAF

Original Text

Q 2.2. Which environmental monitoring criteria are required in extended unidirectional airflow surrounding restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Due to the presence of the UDAF around the open-door portion of the RABS, total particulates and viables should always reflect Grade A limits at the working position, at rest. Locations that are found to be critical based on the risk assessment, should have tighter limits than Grade B under the UDAF when the RABS door is in the open position, while in operation.

The remainder of the room should be subjected to sampling frequencies and limits (e.g., total particulates, viable air samples (active and passive)) and surface samples at a frequency appropriate for Grade B and adhering to Grade B limits.

Rationale

In the extended UDF surrounding the RABS, EM should be conducted to demonstrate the maintenance of a protective environment during the times when the doors are opened. The function of the extended UDF is to provide enhanced protection around critical zones where there is a break in the integrity of the Grade A RABS zone (i.e., during open-door interventions). During times when the doors are closed, this environment can be monitored as a more traditional Grade B environment, whereas during open-door interventions, it should be treated as an enhanced Grade B environment with limits (alert and action) defined based on a risk assessment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Extended UDAF / UDF (extended unidirectional airflow): An additional unidirectional airflow system installed on the outside of the RABS doors. When a RABS door is opened, this airflow forms an "air curtain" that prevents contaminants from the background environment from entering the Grade A area.

Enhanced Grade B: A monitoring concept between Grade A and Grade B. During door-opening operations, the ordinary Grade B limits no longer apply to the extended UDAF area, and stricter alert and action limits are needed.

Analogy

Extended UDAF is like the "air door" (air curtain) at an airport security area: when the shop doors are open, the air door creates a downward airflow that stops hot (or cold) air from outside entering the building. In the same way, when a RABS door is opened for an operation, the extended UDAF is the "air door" that blocks external contamination.

Key Notes

Dual monitoring standards: The document puts forward an important operational concept: the same area needs different monitoring standards under different operating states:

  • Door closed: Monitor as standard Grade B, using Grade B limits
  • Door open: Monitor as enhanced Grade B, using stricter alert and action limits defined on the basis of a risk assessment

This dynamic monitoring strategy reflects the requirement of EU Annex 1 (2022) for a Contamination Control Strategy (CCS): the monitoring program must match the actual risk.

Practical Application

In actual CDMO operations, establishing different monitoring standards for "door open/door closed" requires the switching rules to be clearly defined in the EM procedure. It is advisable to integrate door sensors into the SCADA/BMS system to record automatically when doors are opened, so that the times can be correlated with EM data. This is not only a regulatory requirement but also an expression of data integrity.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can Grade A limits not be applied directly to the extended UDAF area when the door is open? Hint: consider the effect of the operation itself on the environment.
  2. If, during a door-opening operation, the particle monitoring data in the extended UDAF area exceeds the action limit for "enhanced Grade B", what measures should be taken?

Q 2.3 Airflow Visualization Studies (AVS)

Original Text

Q 2.3. How should airflow pattern visualization/demonstration be performed in a restricted access barrier system (RABS) for airflow visualization studies (AVS)?

Recommendation

The purpose of airflow visualization studies (AVS) (also known as "smoke testing") is the verification of unidirectional airflow maintained during all required equipment functions and manual interventions. This is aligned to the principle of assuring First Air in contact with product and direct and indirect product-contact equipment. The intention is to demonstrate that all permitted interventions are appropriately designed and can be executed according to the approved methodology or procedure.

AVS should verify protective airflow is maintained at RABS entry 'mouseholes' without compromise to the process environment that protective airflow is applied to.

An additional reason to perform an AVS is to have a visual record of process operations conducted per requirements to allow the following:

  • Used as training material to demonstrate correct process execution. An important aspect about AVS is that they should be exactly representatives of operational conditions, so people should have the right gowning, validated loads and packages should be used.
  • Presenting to internal or external inspectors who may require visualization of a process that may not be (fully) running at the point of inspection.
  • Assess EM sampling site locations and design of interventions or intervention discard strategies.
"Note: It is important to know what the air velocity is at the working position during the airflow visualization testing so that a correlation can be established in routine requalification studies to demonstrate the continued applicability of the AVS."

Airflow Pattern Visualization Process Activity Considerations

It must be recognized that the act of performing airflow visualization disturbs the environment that it is designed to visualize due to the purposeful injection of a smoke or fogging spray into the critical zone that would self-evidently not occur during routine operations.

To minimize the impact of this disturbance in the air supplied to the RABS, the following should be considered:

  1. Develop a Qualification Protocol — Work with affected departments (Manufacturing, Quality Assurance, Engineering, Microbiology) to determine and document the setup, operation, and intervention steps necessary for the process. The test objective is to show unidirectional airflow sweeping over critical surfaces.
  2. Select Smoke-Generation Equipment Type (e.g., Fogger, Cartridge, Dry Ice) — There are multiple acceptable equipment options for the generation of "smoke" for airflow pattern visualization testing, including foggers that use dry ice, vaporization of water for injection (WFI) or deionized water, or liquid nitrogen. The use of foggers that generate glycerin-based vapor is not recommended because they can leave an oily residue on the test area, personnel, and/or floor areas.
  3. Select Water Vapor-Generating Equipment — Water vapor-generating equipment has a variety of generation mechanisms and design styles, including cartridge-based (handheld) and continuous supply via external devices. Depending on the specifics of the application and the RABS design, one of these styles may be the better choice as there would be no residue formation.
  4. Amount of Smoke — The amount of smoke generated should be sufficient to visualize the airflow (and possible turbulence), but not too much smoke to obscure the operators' actions or machine operations.
  5. Smoke Speed — The smoke should be generated at slow speed in the opposite direction to the airflow, at approximately 45° against the direction of a vertical airflow and at a speed that minimizes disturbance. It should be noted that if the smoke flow was in the same direction as the normal airflow, that can reinforce the airflow directionality.
  6. Smoke Generator and Smoke Diffuser (i.e., Manifold) — Movement of the smoke generator and smoke diffuser should be reduced to be as slow as possible, as recommended by good aseptic practices. To limit movements, the use of a smoke diffuser of sufficient size is suggested such that it becomes unnecessary to move it during the AVS.
  7. Smoke Generation Location — The smoke is to be generated as high as possible above the working position, according to the design of the equipment, but not too close to the HEPA filter to avoid filter-clogging due to residue accumulation.
  8. Air Movement at Interfaces — To check air movement at interfaces to the Grade B surrounding environment (e.g., when doors are open), the smoke is injected inside and outside the RABS. This is done at different locations to demonstrate the flush of the air from the Grade A RABS and its demonstrated unidirectionality (when injected inside) and the absence of air ingress (when injected from outside) into the RABS zone.
  9. Wall and Vertical Panel Considerations — Injecting smoke near walls or vertical panels should be avoided, as this may create additional disturbances in airflow that would not occur in routine operations. Note: Air will flow slower along the RABS walls, and there will be minor eddies along the walls. This is to be expected by the application of physics.
  10. Airflow Visualization Study Frequency — The frequency of re-executing an AVS is not timebound in the first instance; it should be based primarily on events (equipment or process change control). As periodic validation review is regarded as a qualification study, the study may need to be repeated for other reasons (e.g., improvements in film-capture quality over time).
  11. Open-door Intervention Considerations — In the case of open-door interventions, airflow visualization qualification should be done to ensure that there is a unidirectional airflow flush away and out of the RABS during the intervention.

Selection of Video Recording Equipment and Cinematography

The record that exists after the execution of airflow visualization testing is a GMP record that assures the data integrity, a video record that may need to exist for many years. As such, careful thought must go into both the selection of the recording equipment and the modalities concerning film cinematography to maximize value of what is shown to the reviewer in the video footage. The following points and recommendations should be followed:

  1. Equipment and Physical Setup — The equipment used and the physical setup to video-capture the performance of the airflow visualization testing must be considered to ensure that the record contains sufficient detail to demonstrate that unidirectional flow is maintained in all required areas. A good strategy to ensure this is to set up multiple cameras at different angles with significantly overlapping fields of view. Where applicable, built-in cameras and recording devices can be used to capture video of smoke studies; however, complete and detailed coverage of the study area must be accomplished.
  2. Visibility of Vapor on the Video Recording — The rear glass doors of the RABS may need to be covered by an opaque backing or a contrasting color in order to facilitate visibility in the final video footage.
  3. Cinematography Considerations — Contextualizing the cinematography by filming a timepiece that indicates the time and date before panning the camera to visualize the smoke directionality is recommended. Filming a schematic drawing of the RABS system and then the equipment section can also assist the viewer in this regard. Ensure that the differential pressure across the filter and velocity were within standard operating limits during the AVS. Each camera shot should be carefully prepared to ensure that no unnecessary activity is performed in the background. The video should be taken using two to four (2-4) different camera angles to facilitate, as much as possible, a three-dimensional view of the unidirectionality of airflow over the critical zones. Best practice is to synchronize the timing of all filmed scenes, so that direct comparison of the airflow visualization can be made from all filmed angles while viewing in a single pane. When footages are edited to be combined into one stream, the original video file, if considered to contain raw data, is to be retained.

Practical Considerations

Similarly, the logistics of the study execution require careful planning so that the captured film footage is integral and does not allow for any misinterpretation or gaps to the record. The following should be considered for implementation:

  • Simulate a walk-through of interventions (dress rehearsal) to assist with seamless professional execution that minimizes the need for extensive editing. Consider the floor communication method (i.e., radios, hand signals) during execution if participating individuals are on opposite sides of a RABS.
  • Simulate all manipulations strictly following good aseptic behavior, including glove decontamination, and limit the personnel in the space to the smoke-equipment operator, aseptic processing operators, video operator, and reviewer/approver.
  • Filling line operation at maximum speed will require airflow visualization studies because of potential impact on protective airflows, including 'First air'.
  • Consider bringing a cleanroom mobile device (e.g., tablet) to the film location so the video footage can be downloaded and reviewed by approvers on the floor immediately after the test study has been performed (subject to room classification).
  • Verify that all support systems (e.g., room HVAC, unidirectional airflow) are in a controlled and qualified state and operational. Verify all personnel are present and gowned per routine procedure.
  • Verify that the materials needed (i.e., smoke vapor source) are available and in sufficient quantities.
  • Verify any smoke-detection equipment that could trigger an alarm and/or evacuation are switched off for the zone being filmed, in collaboration with the site security personnel. Particle and viable counters should be in operation to mimic the routine, but additional precautions may need to be taken to avoid damage to sensor heads.
  • Verify that the room is in an acceptable state (i.e., normal housekeeping, no work in progress in the background of the video, no materials linked to shutdown activity stored in area being studied).

Rationale

Focus of the video reviewer may be diverted from the smoke study evaluation if simulated operations are not done with the expected level of control for the operation to be simulated (e.g., untidy areas, personnel not wearing required gowning). Additionally, abnormal conditions may not be considered representative of the routine when performing AVS.

The integrity of the airflow visualization film footage requires assurance (e.g., date, time, continuity, accuracy) for both inspectional and training purposes. The firm should assess periodically the need to repeat smoke testing to provide assurance that they reflect current operational characteristics.

This assessment should consider the following:

  • Cumulative effect of process and/or facility changes
  • Quality of the video footage being improved over time
  • Improvements in the methodology of smoke generation and insertion techniques

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Airflow Visualization Studies / AVS: Commonly known as "smoke testing". Visible smoke or fog is introduced into critical areas to verify visually that the unidirectional airflow (UDAF) keeps the correct direction during all equipment operations and manual interventions. This is the core verification tool for confirming that the First Air principle is protected.

First Air: Clean air flowing directly from the HEPA filter that has not been blocked or disturbed in any way. First Air must reach the product and product-contact surfaces first, which is a fundamental principle of sterility assurance.

Mousehole: A small opening in a RABS through which containers or materials pass. AVS needs to verify that protective airflow is still maintained at these openings.

Analogy

AVS is like using a "mirror for air" to see what is normally invisible. Just as we can "see" the direction of airflow by exhaling on a cold winter day, a smoke test lets us "see" the direction of air movement in a cleanroom that is otherwise invisible. But note one paradox: exhaling itself changes the surrounding airflow, and in the same way, introducing smoke itself disturbs the environment we want to observe. Learning how to "exhale gently" without disturbing the original airflow is therefore the technical core of AVS.

Key Notes

Key requirements for smoke generation:

  • Glycerin-based foggers must not be used, as they leave oily residues that affect equipment performance and personnel safety
  • Recommended: dry ice, vaporized WFI, atomized deionized water, or liquid nitrogen
  • Injection angle: about 45° against the direction of airflow, at low velocity, to avoid turbulence caused by the injection itself
  • Injection location: as close as possible to above working height, but not so close to the HEPA filter that it causes blockage

GMP requirements for video recording

AVS videos are formal GMP records and must meet data integrity requirements:

  • The start of the video should show a clock displaying the date and time
  • Use 2-4 camera angles to give an approximately three-dimensional view of the airflow
  • Multi-angle videos should be time-synchronized to allow side-by-side comparison
  • If the video is edited afterwards, the original video files must be retained (as raw data)
  • There should be no unrelated activities in the background (maintenance, cleaning, etc.)

The event-driven principle for repeat frequency

The frequency for repeating AVS is not time-based but event-based: it needs to be repeated only when equipment or process changes occur. This differs from the traditional "redo it every year" mindset. However, companies should still evaluate periodically whether a repeat is needed, considering factors such as:

  • The cumulative effect of process/facility changes
  • Whether the quality of the video recording can be improved
  • Whether smoke generation technology has improved

Practical Application

The importance of a "rehearsal": The document specifically recommends a dress rehearsal before the formal AVS. In a CDMO environment this step is critical, because AVS involves cooperation among several departments (production, QA, engineering, microbiology), and any poor communication can mean the video has to be reshot, wasting valuable cleanroom time and resources.

Maximum line speed requirement: The document states that AVS needs to be performed with the filling line running at maximum speed, because high-speed equipment operation may affect the protective airflow. When a CDMO expands capacity, this must be included in the validation plan.

  1. Why is it recommended to inject smoke against the airflow direction (about 45°)? What problems would occur if it were injected with the airflow?

Q 2.4 Personnel Passage Through Grade A RABS

Original Text

Q 2.4. What are the considerations for facility layout design regarding acceptable personnel passage through the Grade A restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Using a conveyor swing arm to lift the process line and doors that close into both sides to protect the line can allow the movement of operators from one side of the line to the other in cases where it is not possible to have two entrances into the room from the Grade B corridor. This movement should always be performed only after the impacted section of the line has been cleared of vials and is preferable in a section of the line where the units have already been filled and are fully stoppered. After personnel passage through the swing conveyor, a disinfection of the RABS interior/swing conveyor is required.

The environment immediately adjacent to the RABS may be designed to provide unidirectional airflow. This will provide protection to the glove ports and the interior of the doors when opened to performed inherent interventions (i.e., sterile equipment installation) or rare open-door corrective interventions. Computational Fluid Design (CFD) modeling may determine the need to have small rigid polycarbonate sheets extending from the ceiling to the top of the door height to ensure the airflow is unidirectional providing the desired protection.

Rationale

The movement of the swing arm might create turbulence in the surrounding air. Every effort must be made to prevent air external to the RABS from entering the RABS to protect units that are not yet integral from contamination.

Using smaller HEPA curtains that reach down to door height would provide some directionality and flush airflow without introducing risk to operators or materials.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Conveyor Swing Arm: An engineering solution that allows one section of the process line to be lifted so that operators can pass from one side of the line to the other. It is an alternative when the facility layout cannot provide two separate entrances.

Computational Fluid Dynamics / CFD: Using computer simulation to analyze air flow patterns. In RABS design, CFD can be used to evaluate whether polycarbonate deflector panels need to be installed between the ceiling and the top of the doors to ensure the airflow remains unidirectional.

Analogy

Imagine a RABS filling line as a bridge over a river: when someone needs to get from one side of the river to the other, they cannot simply wade across (that would stir up the water and bring in contamination). The conveyor swing arm is like a drawbridge that can be raised: first the "river" (the product conveyor) is lifted and the "boats" (the containers) are cleared from the channel, and then the person crosses quickly. After crossing, the "bridge deck" must be cleaned (the inside of the RABS disinfected) before normal passage can resume.

Key Notes

The three prerequisites for personnel passage:

  • Line clearance: All containers in the affected section must be removed
  • Prefer sealed sections: Ideally the passage is made at a section where the product has been filled and fully stoppered
  • Post-passage disinfection: The inside of the RABS and the swing arm must be disinfected

The fundamental principle of facility design: The ideal facility layout provides two separate entrances into the room from the Grade B corridor, so that operators do not need to cross the production line. A swing arm solution should be considered only when the existing facility cannot achieve this layout. This again reflects the "design first" spirit of Quality by Design (QbD).

The value of CFD simulation

CFD modeling is used not only at the design stage but can also:

  • Evaluate the installation position and size of polycarbonate deflector panels
  • Simulate the extent of turbulence generated when the swing arm moves
  • Verify the protective effect of the UDAF during door-opening operations
  • Reduce the number of physical mock-up tests and lower validation costs

For a CDMO, investing in CFD modeling can predict potential problems before a facility modification and avoid expensive physical changes.

Practical Application

In CDMO facility planning, the design of personnel flow is a problem that has to be solved at the very beginning. If the layout means operators frequently need to cross the Grade A area, every passage creates additional disinfection time and line clearance work, which directly affects capacity utilization (OEE). In the capital budget assessment for a new facility design or the modification of an old facility, the investment in "dual corridors/dual entrances" should therefore be compared with "swing arm + the capacity lost at each passage" in a total cost of ownership (TCO) analysis.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is it preferable for personnel to pass through a section where product is "filled and stoppered"? How would the risk change if passage were made in a section that is not stoppered?
  2. The document mentions that CFD simulation can determine whether polycarbonate deflector panels need to be installed. Describe how these deflector panels work.

Section Summary

  • Room classification: The minimum classification of the environment surrounding a RABS is Grade B, a mandatory FDA/EMA requirement. Stricter control may be needed in the RABS door areas, because a RABS is not completely sealed like an isolator.
  • Extended UDAF monitoring: The extended UDAF area outside the RABS doors needs a dynamic monitoring strategy: monitor as Grade B when the door is closed and as "enhanced Grade B" when the door is open, with limits defined on the basis of a risk assessment.
  • Airflow visualization studies (AVS): This is the core tool for verifying First Air and UDAF protection. Key points include: avoiding glycerin-based smoke, injecting against the airflow at 45°, multi-angle video recording, an event-driven repeat frequency, and the data integrity requirements for the video as a GMP record.
  • Personnel passage design: The ideal design avoids personnel crossing the Grade A area. Where necessary a conveyor swing arm solution can be used, but the prerequisites of line clearance, choosing a sealed section and post-passage disinfection must be met. CFD modeling is an important tool for optimizing the airflow protection of the facility.

Q 3.1 Operator Glove Disinfection

Original Text

Q 3.1. Should operator gloves be disinfected prior to entering the restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves?

Recommendation

Disinfection of operator gloves prior to entry into the RABS gloves is an important component of the CCS and should be undertaken prior to each entry into the RABS gloves.

Gloved hands should be disinfected with an approved disinfectant (e.g., sterile isopropanol) prior to donning the RABS gloves (1, 4). The disinfectant can be applied by spraying. It is considered a basic and vital element of hygiene and cleanroom practices to minimize contamination risk or the transfer of contaminants within the clean room in general. It is also important that the risk of transfer of contamination from one operator to another is also considered and mitigated as part of the overall CCS.

Since RABS gloves are typically shared by more than one operator, the risk of contamination transfer from one operator to another through the shared use of the RABS gloves should be minimized by sanitizing the operator's own gloves before they enter into the glove ports or after exiting from the glove ports. The risk identified above (multiple operators using same gloves) is with reference to mitigating risk of contamination transfer between operators. Of course, the risk of product contamination requires equal consideration.

Rationale

RABS gloves are an integral design element and form part of the physical barrier that enables product protection by separating operators from the process.

Disinfection of gloves worn underneath RABS gloves will reduce the microbial load within the RABS gloves, in contrast to nondisinfected gloves. In the case of a pinhole in a RABS glove, a reduced microbial load on the inside of the RABS glove will reduce the chance of microbial ingress into the RABS Grade A environment (14).

RABS gloves are not infallible and, with continued use, may become worn, torn, or perforated and, hence, may potentially compromise the integrity of the system. While controls, including visual inspection (pre- and post-interventions of use) of gloves, provide for some degree of detection of holes or leaks, the identification of a leak through glove integrity-testing is usually undertaken post-batch or post-campaign manufacturing. This requires a retrospective assessment to determine the impact on the batch in the event of a confirmed glove-integrity breach.

For closed-door interventions that require the use of the RABS gloves, the risk profile associated with a potential breach of the Grade A RABS environment owing to a RABS glove integrity failure is high. This can be further compounded in the absence of operator glove disinfection, since the physical barrier between the operator hand (normally afforded through integral RABS gloves) and the Grade A environment is breached. A vector for microbiological contamination is thereby created, with risk profile reduced by ensuring adequate glove disinfection.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

RABS Glove Disinfection: This is the most basic yet most easily overlooked step in RABS operation. Before putting their hands into the RABS gloves, operators must first disinfect their own cleanroom gloves with an approved disinfectant (such as sterile isopropyl alcohol).

Why must it be done "every time"? Because RABS gloves are usually shared by several people. After operator A uses them, operator B uses the same pair of RABS gloves next. If A's gloves carry microorganisms, those microorganisms will remain on the inner surface of the RABS gloves and may cause cross-contamination when B puts them on.

Analogy

Imagine shared RABS gloves as a shared rack of surgical gloves in a hospital. Even if the operating room (the Grade A area) itself is sterile, if each surgeon does not wash and disinfect their hands before putting on the surgical gloves, the inside of the gloves becomes a "relay station" for bacteria, passing them from one surgeon to the next.

More dangerous still, if a glove has a pinhole that is invisible to the naked eye, bacteria from an undisinfected hand can pass straight into the sterile area, just as a hand gets wet when a waterproof glove has a hole in it.

Key Notes

Defense in Depth thinking: Glove integrity testing is usually performed only at the end of a batch or batch activity. This means that if a glove is damaged during production, you can only find out afterwards. At that point, glove disinfection beforehand becomes the critical "last line of defense".

  • Glove disinfection = reduces the microbial load inside the glove
  • Glove integrity testing = confirms after the event whether the glove was damaged
  • Combined = even if a glove is damaged, the risk to the product is minimized because the microbial load inside is low

This is the essence of a Contamination Control Strategy (CCS): not relying on a single control measure but building multiple layers of protection.

Practical Application

The CDMO challenge: In a CDMO environment, products from different clients may be run on the same RABS filling line, and glove disinfection needs to be enforced even more strictly at shift changes or product changeovers. It is advisable to state the following explicitly in the SOP:

  • Sterile IPA must be sprayed before each entry into the RABS gloves
  • At operator handover, the incoming operator must disinfect before entering
  • Document the frequency and method of glove disinfection in the CCS documents

Practice Questions

  1. If an operator performed a closed-door RABS operation without disinfecting their gloves, and it was later found that the RABS glove had a pinhole, how would you assess the impact on the batch?
  2. Why does the document stress that the disinfectant can be applied by "spraying"? What practical advantages does it have compared with immersion?
Q 3.2 Personnel Qualification

Original Text

Q 3.2. How are personnel qualified for executing interventions?

Recommendation

For qualification of personnel for open-door and/or closed-door interventions, an intervention management program should be established. Typically, a phase-wise approach is followed to increase operator proficiency.

Level 1 – Training

A well-rounded personnel qualification training program should include, but not be limited to, the following:

  • Basic elements of microbiology and hygiene with specific focus on cleanroom practices (slow, deliberate movements of body and arms/gloves), principles about contamination control, First Air concept, good aseptic working techniques (i.e., in particular during RABS open-door interventions), protection of sterile products and critical surfaces, and the potential safety implications to the patient if a product is not sterile.
  • Visual airflow studies should be used in training to support the understanding of operator interventions in the aseptic space. Virtual reality-based training modules may be used as a best practice in training to assess operators' skills and mitigate risks by providing immersive, interactive simulations that help them practice real-life situations in a controlled environment.
  • Aseptic gowning and cleanroom behavior training is to be provided. The trainee should perform dry runs of gowning and degowning under the supervision of an experienced trainer, showing that the trainee is able to perform the gowning process appropriately and consistently. Those dry runs can be performed in suitable office spaces or otherwise easy-to-access rooms. Gowning qualification can be initiated in conjunction with a trainer after successful completion of the basic elements of microbiology and cleanroom behavior aspects outlined above.

Level 2 – Gowning Qualification Followed by Demonstration

The aseptic-gowning procedure should be confirmed by a visual and microbial (plating with contact plates) assessment, and the trainee's capability to don the complete sterile gown in an aseptic manner to allow entry in the aseptic area should be evaluated.

Demonstration of Assembly/Machine Setup, Interventions, and Filling Operation

Training is required on site-specific standard procedures, on machine parts setup and interventions, with the help of applicable dynamic airflow visualization studies. Training can be staggered based on intervention complexity, wherein operators first demonstrate competency to perform less complex interventions prior to moving to more complex interventions. Alternatively, the training may be certified as one block, where proficiency in all relevant interventions (irrespective of complexity) must be demonstrated for the operator to be certified.

Personnel should observe the production activities (setup and interventions) during routine commercial batch-manufacturing from the view windows or by viewing remote cameras without entering into the aseptic area. This can be done in parallel with Level 2 training, with supervisory monitoring by experienced and qualified personnel.

Personnel should simulate machine setup and interventions in the aseptic core, with the supervision of qualified personnel, by performing interventions in Grade A when there is no commercial batch-manufacturing (e.g., after completion of the routine commercial batch operations, prior to cleaning of the filling line). Assessment of nonviable and viable particle-monitoring during those simulations may be considered. The number of dry runs to be considered should be based on personnel skill set and prior experience before allowing participation in an APS run.

Level 3 – Practical Assessment in Smaller-Batch and Regular APS

After completing Levels 1 and 2, the personnel should perform setup and interventions with participation in an APS (1, 4, 7, 15). The run can be independent of routine APS, or it can be a routine APS run. The APS run may be smaller or of routine size depending on the size of the operation, the number of personnel to be qualified, and the process complexity oRABS/cRABS and open- or closed-interventions.

A bracketing approach can be applied for setup operations in the case of similarities in manipulations and interventions during the media manipulation run for qualifying more than one operator within the same run, based on the risk-based approach. However, attention should be paid to the number of units filled by an individual operator to be representative of the actual commercial operation.

Choosing a setup process that is more challenging to perform or more complex in execution and grouping interventions to perform with different sets of tools (e.g., using short or long forceps, stopper/seal track adjustments, needle adjustment) is recommended.

For open-door versus closed-door interventions, all open-door interventions and closed-door interventions that are classified as high risk within site-risk assessment documents should be chosen to be performed. When certifying the personnel to perform the aseptic processing activities in commercial batches, the same procedures that were qualified in the APS run should be chosen. Upon the successful result of APS, the data compliance monitoring (continuous viable and total particle), assessment of aseptic techniques, and the behavior of the operator(s) to be qualified for performing aseptic operations, the operator should be qualified.

Within lifecycle management, when any new open-door interventions are added to the system at later stages, quality management tools should ensure that the personnel are qualified to perform the additional interventions and the interventions have been simulated in APS runs, unless it has been otherwise documented in risk assessments with scientific rationale that the new intervention is like the qualified one.

Level 4 – Ongoing Training and Assessment

Upon successful completion of personnel qualification, the operator should be certified to perform aseptic operations, and their aseptic behavior should be monitored on a periodic/continuous basis until a sufficient level of experience is achieved. The assessment should evaluate if supervision of the newly qualified person can be reduced or if further training and additional assessment is required. It is recommended to qualify personnel annually, for both gowning and participation in APS runs.

Disqualification of Aseptic Processing Personnel

In the event when operators are considered disqualified, access to the aseptic area should be removed/prohibited until demonstration of successful requalification. The disqualification criteria may include:

  1. Failure to qualify or meet periodic qualification requirements
  2. Participation in an unsuccessful or failed APS, where their performance was identified as a cause or contribution to the failure
  3. Repeated aseptic processing technique in a manner deemed unacceptable in relation to cleanroom or aseptic-process operations or functions
  4. Failure to maintain gowning qualification
  5. Failure to maintain training requirements
  6. Repeated personnel monitoring excursions
  7. Extended periods of inactivity or absence from the aseptic processing area
  8. Other aspects of performance as identified by a supervisor (e.g., behavioral)

The requalification should be defined on the reason and relative risk associated with the disqualification.

Rationale

The performance of personnel-related activities, manipulations, and interventions in the RABS during aseptic operations can pose a risk to the sterility of the product or adversely affect it if it is not performed in an aseptic manner. Therefore, proper training and qualification of personnel with APS experience is a must.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Intervention Management Program: This is not just a training checklist but a complete system for developing personnel competence. PDA recommends a four-level progressive approach, from basic knowledge to actual operation, to build up the aseptic operating capability of operators step by step.

The four-level qualification system:

  • Level 1 - Training: Microbiology fundamentals, cleanroom behavior, the First Air concept, gowning practice
  • Level 2 - Gowning Qualification + Demonstration: Gowning qualification assessed by microbial contact plates, plus simulated operations outside production time
  • Level 3 - Practical Assessment in APS: Participation in an aseptic process simulation (APS), performing the complete operation under simulated conditions
  • Level 4 - Ongoing Training and Assessment: Annual requalification, including gowning and APS participation

Analogy

This four-level qualification system is like the training of a pilot:

  • Level 1 (ground school): Learning aviation theory, meteorology and flight rules, corresponding to microbiology and cleanroom fundamentals
  • Level 2 (simulator training): Practicing various scenarios in a flight simulator, corresponding to gowning qualification and operating drills outside production time
  • Level 3 (flying with an instructor): Flying in a real aircraft with an instructor alongside, corresponding to actual operations in an APS
  • Level 4 (recurrent training): Even an experienced captain must pass flight checks regularly, corresponding to annual requalification

Just as a pilot cannot skip the simulator and go straight to the aircraft, aseptic operators cannot skip any level.

Key Notes

Forward-looking recommendation on VR training: This is the first time PDA explicitly mentions that Virtual Reality (VR) training modules can be a best practice. It is an important signal of an industry trend: regulatory inspections in future may regard VR training as an advanced training tool.

The importance of observational learning: Level 2 specifically mentions that personnel should observe actual production operations through an observation window or a remote camera rather than entering the aseptic area directly. This "look but don't touch" stage is very important, as it lets operators understand the rhythm and complexity of real operations in a safe environment.

Disqualification Criteria

The document lists eight situations that may lead to an operator being disqualified, covering three dimensions:

  • Skill decay: Failing periodic qualification, lapsed gowning qualification, long periods without entering the aseptic area
  • Operational errors: An APS failure where the operator's performance was determined to be the cause, repeated improper operating technique
  • Monitoring anomalies: Repeated personnel monitoring excursions

Note item 7 in particular: "extended inactivity or absence". This means that even an operator who was fully qualified before must be requalified after a long period without performing operations. Many CDMOs tend to overlook this point.

Practical Application

The CDMO bracketing strategy: The document mentions that a Bracketing Approach can be used for similar operations, qualifying several operators in the same APS. This is especially valuable for a CDMO:

  • Select the most challenging equipment setup procedure to simulate
  • Combine operations with different tools (long forceps, short forceps, stopper track adjustment, etc.)
  • Ensure that the number of units filled by each operator is representative

This reduces the number of APS runs while still ensuring rigorous qualification, which for a CDMO is the best balance of cost-effectiveness.

Practice Questions

  1. An operator is disqualified for "extended absence" (6 months without entering the aseptic area). When requalifying, at which Level do you think they should restart, and why?
  2. Level 2 training mentions that gowning and degowning can be practiced in an "office space". Is such a practice environment sufficient? What are the advantages and disadvantages of each compared with practicing in an actual cleanroom?
  3. The document recommends that annual requalification include gowning and APS participation. If your site runs only two routine APS runs a year but 20 operators need requalification, how would you arrange it?
Q 3.3 Open-Door vs Closed-Door Qualification

Original Text

Q 3.3. Is there a clear distinction between the operator qualification for closed- and open-door operator interventions, and are operators qualified for closed-door interventions allowed to perform open-door interventions (For operators qualified for specific tasks, e.g., primary operator, secondary operator, based on criticality)?

Recommendation

After setup, open-door interventions should be rare events. The personnel authorized to conduct open-door line setup and permitted open-door interventions should be trained and qualified specifically for open-door interventions after they have been permitted to undertake closed-door interventions.

Requirements for qualification of open- and closed-door interventions should be clearly distinguished.

An operator qualified for closed-door intervention is not allowed to perform open-door intervention due to the risk factors associated with aseptic techniques and intervention type. But a person qualified for open-door interventions can be allowed to perform closed-door interventions due to the low risk and considering the similarities in the intervention process. However, this should be clearly documented in the site's quality risk assessment and CCS documents (1).

A qualified-operator list should clearly specify the personnel authorized to perform interventions into the Grade A environment, with open-, closed-, or both interventions and, within that category grouping, can be done based on the similarities (e.g., loading of stoppers and/or seals if the latter is performed in the RABS).

Rationale

Interventions should be designed and performed using proper aseptic techniques to minimize the risk of product contamination. Operators should be first performing closed-door interventions and gaining a skillset at a later stage to perform open-door interventions. (See Question 3.2 for additional information.)

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

This is like the tiered system of driving licenses: someone with a bus license can drive a car, but someone with only a car license cannot drive a bus.

Open-door operations are like driving a bus: they need higher skill, stricter training and more experience. Closed-door operations are the car: basic qualification is still required, but the risk level is lower.

Key Notes

The importance of the Qualified-Operator List:

The document requires a clear "qualified operator list", which must:

  • Clearly show the types of operation each operator is authorized to perform (open-door/closed-door/both)
  • Allow grouping within the same category based on the similarity of operations (for example, loading of stoppers and seals can be grouped together if the operations are similar)
  • Be documented in the quality risk assessment and CCS documents

During regulatory inspections, inspectors often ask to see this list and cross-check whether the people who performed open-door operations inside the RABS that day were indeed on the authorized list.

Practical Application

The CDMO scheduling strategy: In the multi-product environment of a CDMO, it is very important to ensure that at least one qualified open-door operator is present on every shift. It is advisable to:

  • Establish a tiered operator system: Primary Operator (open-door qualified) and Secondary Operator (closed-door qualified only)
  • Add qualification check logic to the scheduling system to ensure coverage of open-door qualified personnel
  • The equipment setup phase requires open-door operations, so qualified personnel must be scheduled for it

Practice Questions

  1. During a night shift, the only person qualified for open-door operations leaves because of an emergency. At that moment an equipment fault occurs that can only be resolved by an open-door intervention. How would you handle it?
  2. The document states that operators qualified for open-door operations "may" perform closed-door operations, but this must be documented in the CCS documents. Why does this "backward compatibility" still need formal documentation?

Key Concepts

The principle of qualification asymmetry: Q3.3 establishes a very important rule: the "one-way compatibility" of operating qualifications:

The logic is clear: open-door operations carry far higher risk than closed-door operations, because when the door is opened the Grade A environment is directly exposed to the Grade B background. Someone who can perform a high-risk operation can naturally perform a low-risk one, but not the other way around.

Q 3.4 Qualified vs Unqualified Interventions

Original Text

Q 3.4. What are the recommendations for qualified and unqualified interventions?

Recommendation

Best Practices for Qualified Interventions

Interventions should be conducted by trained and qualified personnel. (See Question 3.2 on how to qualify.)

An approved standard operating procedure (SOP) containing all defined interventions with step-by-step instructions, mitigation actions (if any), and images (where applicable) should exist (1, 4, 7, 15, 16). A dynamic AVS should be performed simulating all the listed interventions, and these studies should be repeated with a defined frequency as described in Question 2.3.

Simulation of all qualified interventions in routine APS should be performed on a rotational basis; its maximum frequency of occurrence in routine batch-manufacturing should be covered in an initial qualification run and semiannual qualification runs. A rotation policy can be defined in an internal-site risk management process but should occur at least annually.

Management of Unqualified Interventions

When an unqualified intervention occurs, a real-time risk-based impact assessment shall be performed (See Question 1.7 on how to risk assess). If the assessment determines the unqualified intervention can be performed, only authorized personnel (See Question 3.2 on authorized personnel) may execute the unqualified intervention. If the risk assessment does not support the intervention as safe, then the intervention should not proceed and the appropriate steps to end the batch should be taken. As part of batch release, the impact of the unqualified intervention must be assessed for quality and compliance on the batch.

Unqualified interventions identified to become qualified interventions shall have AVS to demonstrate unidirectional airflow and preservation of First Air are not impacted. The unqualified intervention will then be included in the ongoing APS program and upon successful completion the intervention can be added to the local procedure. Unqualified interventions that are similar to qualified interventions may not require additional AVS execution or inclusion in the APS program, to become a qualified intervention, if proven equivalent with supporting technical and scientific rationale. In certain situations, unqualified interventions may not be recommended to become a qualified intervention, based upon special events, and will not require simulation in AVS and APS programs.

Rationale

Interventions should be designed and performed using proper aseptic techniques to minimize impact to the production environment and the risk of product contamination. An unqualified intervention does not have sufficient data to demonstrate the capability of control, hence, simulating the unqualified intervention in APS testing to demonstrate the process capability is recommended.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Qualified Intervention: An operation that has been through the complete qualification process: it has an SOP, an AVS (airflow visualization study) has shown that it does not affect First Air, and it has been simulated and completed successfully in an APS (aseptic process simulation). These operations are "expected to occur", and the system has shown that they can be performed safely.

Unqualified Intervention: An operation that has not been through the complete qualification process described above. It is an "unexpected" operation, for example an equipment fault never encountered before that requires a new way of handling it.

The key distinction: "Unqualified" does not mean "prohibited". The document establishes a complete decision process for handling unqualified operations.

Analogy

Think of this as an airline's flight manual:

  • Qualified Interventions (standard operating procedures): Like all the standard and emergency procedures listed in the flight manual: what to do if an engine fails, what to do if the hydraulic system fails. These have all been trained in the simulator and rehearsed repeatedly.
  • Unqualified Interventions (unknown situations): Like meeting a completely new situation not covered in the flight manual. The captain must assess the risk in real time (real-time risk assessment) and decide whether it can be handled safely or whether to return to the airport (stop the batch).

Moreover, if a certain "unknown situation" starts to occur frequently, the airline should write it into the manual (upgrade it from unqualified to qualified) and run simulator training (AVS + APS) so that all pilots learn to handle it.

Key Notes

Decision tree for unqualified interventions:

  1. Real-time risk assessment: When an unqualified operation occurs, perform a risk assessment immediately
  2. Decision fork:
    • Risk acceptable → performed by authorized personnel, but its impact must be evaluated at batch release
    • Risk unacceptable → do not perform the operation; take appropriate steps to end the batch
  3. Follow-up:Decide whether the unqualified operation should be upgraded to a qualified operation
    • Upgrade needed → perform AVS verification + include in the APS program
    • Similar to an existing qualified operation → equivalence can be shown through a technical justification, and additional AVS/APS is not necessarily required
    • A special event → upgrading to a qualified operation is not recommended

Rotation Policy

Rotational simulation strategy for qualified interventions: The document requires that all qualified interventions be simulated in routine APS runs, but on a "rotation" basis:

  • Initial qualification APS: covers the interventions at their maximum frequency of occurrence in routine production
  • Semi-annual qualification APS: rotates through simulating different interventions
  • Rotation cycle: each intervention is simulated at least once a year

This is a pragmatic approach: if there are 30 qualified interventions, it is impossible to simulate all of them in every APS. Rotation ensures that all interventions are verified within a reasonable cycle.

Practical Application

Intervention management challenges for a CDMO:

Because a CDMO handles the products of many different clients, it may face far more types of interventions than a single-product site. It is advisable to establish the following systems:

  • Intervention database: Manage all qualified interventions in one place, tagging the applicable product/equipment combinations
  • APS rotation matrix: Plan which interventions are simulated in each APS, ensuring 100% annual coverage
  • Unqualified intervention tracking system: Record each occurrence of an unqualified intervention, the risk assessment result and the follow-up decision
  • Upgrade review meeting: Review the unqualified intervention records periodically (for example, quarterly) and decide which should be upgraded to qualified

Practice Questions

  1. During a filling batch, a stopper jam occurred in a way never seen before, and the operator solved the problem with an innovative method (an unqualified intervention). The batch was completed smoothly. What are your follow-up action steps?
  2. The document mentions that some unqualified interventions that are "similar to a qualified intervention" can be shown to be equivalent through a technical justification, with no additional AVS/APS needed. What factors do you think the criteria for judging "similarity" should include?
  3. If the risk assessment concludes that it "does not support the operation as safe", the document says to "take appropriate steps to end the batch". Q4.1 How do you ensure the integrity of RABS gloves?

Original Text

Recommendation

There are various aspects to designing, maintaining, and verifying the integrity of a RABS. The potential loss of integrity can occur at many locations, including the RABS enclosure seams, baseplate openings, doors, glove ports, gloves, transfer ports, UDAF (in the case of an active RABS), and the lack of First Air.

A risk analysis should be conducted to assist in identifying design decisions and operational actions that have the potential for a loss of integrity. (See PDA Technical Report No. 54: Implementation of Quality Risk Management for Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology Manufacturing Operations for guidance on performing a risk analysis.)

As discussed in Questions 1.1-1.6 of this document, quality-by-design techniques can reduce the risk of incursions to RABS by considering process and user requirements, risk analysis, and designs that limit potential points of contamination. However, active monitoring and verification of RABS integrity is key to protecting the product and operator safety, where applicable in cRABS.

Considerations on monitoring glove integrity throughout a campaign is important as post campaign integrity test failures can be highly impactful. Also, integrity monitoring after significant interventions needs to be considered. Monitoring environmental conditions in real time within the oRABS or cRABS is essential to maintaining Grade A conditions to support RABS integrity. These parameters include:

  • Temperature
  • Humidity
  • Differential pressure
  • UDAF velocity
  • EM

In addition to real-time monitoring of these environmental conditions, glove integrity-testing, both visual assessment and a pressure test are required (1, 17). Firms should establish the procedures for this pressure test based on the specifications of their gloves and testing methods. This process should be incorporated into the CCS. (See Topics 4-6 of this PtC.)

A common pathway to RABS contamination is through the use of glove ports and, in particular, the loss of integrity of the glove material (4). While pre- and post-filling EM sampling may detect contamination via the gloves, it is imperative to verify the integrity of the glove itself. Glove integrity-testing should be performed based on an approved risk assessment. An approved risk assessment should determine the frequency of pressure testing, visual inspection, and glove changeout (6, 8). Additional glove visual assessments should be performed prior to each use during both campaign and batch production (3).

Rationale

It is critical for RABS to maintain integrity in order to support Grade A conditions. The RABS gloves design, use, and maintenance are key elements to ensuring appropriate control and overall integrity. RABS-related procedures and programs should encompass the continuous review of system and process improvements that support product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Glove Integrity: the ability of a RABS glove, as part of the barrier system, to maintain its physical seal. Gloves are one of the most vulnerable points in a RABS: they are flexible material that is used repeatedly, stretched and exposed to cleaning agents, so over time they can develop pinholes, tears or wear.

Pressure Test: a physical test for glove leaks using the pressure decay method or the constant pressure method. Compared with visual inspection, a pressure test can detect micron-level holes, which makes it a key means of ensuring the integrity of the grade A environment.

UDAF (Unidirectional Airflow): clean air blown downward from above inside the RABS, providing First Air protection. If a glove or seal fails, external contaminants may enter against the airflow.

Analogy

Think of a RABS as a submarine, and the gloves as the "sealing rings of the mechanical arms" on that submarine. Deep underwater, a leak at any sealing point (hatch, seam, viewing window, mechanical arm interface) can lead to disaster. You would not wait until water floods in to find the problem. Instead, you run regular pressure tests and visual inspections, and confirm that every seal is intact before each mission. RABS glove management follows exactly the same logic: prevention is better than cure.

Key Notes

Gloves are the most common contamination pathway in a RABS: the PtC explicitly states, "A common pathway to RABS contamination is through the use of glove ports." This means glove-related events account for a high proportion of all RABS contamination investigations.

Five real-time monitoring parameters: temperature, humidity, differential pressure, UDAF air velocity and EM. Together they form the "dashboard" of RABS integrity. A deviation in any one of them may indicate a problem with the barrier system.

Monitoring during a campaign: testing cannot be done only after a batch ends. If a glove failure is found only after the whole campaign, multiple batches may be affected, and both the scope of the investigation and the commercial loss will grow.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, the same RABS filling line may serve products for several customers. If the glove integrity test fails after a campaign, all batches in that campaign may be affected, which means products of multiple customers need a retrospective assessment. This is not only a quality issue but also a reputation issue. For this reason, many leading CDMOs also perform glove integrity tests in the middle of a campaign in order to "segment" the risk.

  1. If your RABS runs 5 consecutive batches in one campaign, at which points would you schedule glove pressure tests, and why?
  2. Besides the gloves themselves, where else in a RABS could integrity be lost? List at least 4 locations.
  3. Why can EM sampling "possibly" detect contamination but cannot replace glove integrity testing? How do the roles of the two differ?
Q4.2 What are the considerations for a RABS glove-management strategy?

Original Text

Recommendation

A RABS glove-management strategy is essential to maintaining the RABS environment (i.e., product quality) and providing operator safety. This strategy includes glove selection, integrity testing, and an established glove changeout frequency (6). Interventions should be designed to in a way to minimize the risk of glove damage and loss of integrity. Within a Glove Management Strategy (part of CCS) the regime for glove integrity testing should be specified based on risk and impact of glove integrity breaches. Visual inspection, and where appropriate physical methods of glove integrity testing, should be applied as combined assurance that glove integrity has been maintained through critical process steps of set-up and during routine operations where inherent and corrective interventions apply.

There are multiple considerations with the selection of glove material. (See Question 1.5 for details on glove materials of construction).

Depending on the glove selected, a risk assessment should establish the appropriate and validated integrity test method and frequency (1). The risk associated with the process and product would have the greatest impact on establishing the appropriate integrity-test requirements. The integrity-test method should include both visual and physical testing components as detailed in Question 4.6.

Based on the glove selected and the established integrity-test requirements, the risk assessment should evaluate a preventive maintenance (PM) program to establish the duration of glove changeout and replacement frequency (6). Once in operation, the PM frequency of the gloves should be reviewed on a periodic basis and adjusted based on data acquired from the RABS. For example, if certain gloves are experiencing higher wear due to machine setup and adjustment, operator interventions, and/or cleaning and disinfecting agents, the PM frequency may be increased.

Glove rotation can be considered to minimize the damage or failure rate for reusable-type gloves. Consideration should be given to the location(s) where the glove is subjected for low/high utilization during processing for rotation from batch or campaign operations (e.g., from a high-interventions zone to a low-interventions zone). The rotation policy and its recording procedure is to be defined by QRM for its traceability.

Rationale

The selection of the proper gloves, glove testing, and management of gloves is necessary to ensure operator safety and to maintain the RABS environmental conditions. The firm should evaluate their process and perform a risk assessment to determine an appropriate integrity test that reliably detects glove defects.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Glove Management Strategy: this is more than "changing gloves regularly". It is a complete management system covering material selection, test methods, test frequency, replacement schedule and rotation policy, and it must be included in the CCS (contamination control strategy).

Preventive Maintenance (PM): the key principle is that gloves should not be used until failure. PM frequency should be based on risk assessment and continuously adjusted according to actual data.

Glove Rotation: moving gloves from high-use positions to low-use positions. Like tire rotation, this extends the overall service life and evens out wear.

Analogy

Glove management strategy is like managing the tires of a car. You do not just check whether a tire has blown out (visual inspection). You also measure tire pressure regularly (pressure test), check tread depth (physical test), and decide when to replace according to the degree of wear (PM frequency). A smarter practice is to swap the front and rear tires periodically (glove rotation) so that all four wear more evenly. If your car often drives on rough roads (operations with frequent interventions), you need to check and replace more often.

Key Notes

Three lines of defense (selection + testing + maintenance): none of the three pillars of glove management strategy can be missing. Choosing a suitable material is the first line of defense, regular testing is the detection line, and preventive replacement is the final safeguard.

Data-driven PM adjustment: the PtC stresses that PM frequency is not fixed and should be adjusted dynamically based on operational data (such as wear rate and failure rate). This reflects the spirit of continuous improvement.

Rotation policy must be traceable: glove rotation cannot be done casually. The policy must be defined under QRM and fully recorded to ensure traceability. This is a focus area during regulatory inspections.

Formula

Glove management strategy =
    Material Selection
  + Integrity Test Method
      - Visual Inspection
      - Physical/Pressure Test
  + Test Frequency
      - Risk-Based
  + PM Changeout Schedule
      - Based on supplier recommendations + actual data
  + Rotation Policy
      - Rotate from high-use to low-use positions
  → All included in the CCS document

  1. If your RABS has 20 glove ports, 4 of which are in the filling area (high intervention frequency), how would you design the rotation policy?
  2. Why must the glove management strategy be part of the CCS rather than just a standalone SOP?
  3. Suppose the glove integrity test failure rate of your RABS rose from 1% to 5% over the past three months. What actions would you take?
Q4.3 What should the maintenance program be for RABS gloves?

Original Text

Recommendation

Expectations and practices regarding the inspection and replacement of glove systems can be documented within the planned maintenance program. This should be supported with a quality risk assessment that details the rationale for glove-replacement frequency. (See PDA Points to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Sterile Pharmaceutical Products in Isolators for further details on the glove maintenance program.)

Gloves should not be used until failure. The frequency of PM activities must be established via risk assessment with input from the vendor to determine the optimal frequency of glove changeout. The quality risk assessment should include, but not be limited to:

  • Glove material of construction
  • Frequency of glove use
  • How gloves are used (e.g., handling sharp-edged equipment, stretching)
  • VPHP contact time or concentration, if applicable (delamination can occur due to prolonged VPHP/decontaminant exposure)
  • Type of disinfectant used for inner disinfection of the gloves
  • Ability to clean residual product from the gloves
  • Historical data for loss of integrity

Rationale

Glove integrity can be critical to product quality. A robust risk assessment, including specific process parameters, will provide the information needed for maintenance procedures. Site procedures should define the maintenance and operational procedures based on risk.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Planned Maintenance Program: a pre-planned schedule of glove inspection and replacement. The core principle is "preventive" rather than "reactive": do not wait until a glove tears before replacing it.

VPHP Delamination: vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VPHP) is a common biodecontamination method for RABS, but prolonged exposure may cause delamination of some glove materials, in which the outer and inner layers separate and the glove is severely weakened. This is an often overlooked but extremely important consideration.

Analogy

This is like the maintenance program for an aircraft engine: you would never wait for the engine to fail before servicing it (use until failure). Instead, you set a regular maintenance schedule based on flight hours, number of takeoffs and landings, and environmental conditions (such as salt spray and sand). The same applies to gloves: number of uses (flight hours), type of operation (contact with sharp objects = harsh flying conditions) and VPHP exposure (corrosive environment) are the key factors that decide "how often to replace".

Key Notes

"Gloves should not be used until failure" is the golden rule. If an inspection finds that gloves are replaced only after they tear, an observation is almost certain. The frequency of preventive replacement must be supported by a risk assessment.

Seven risk assessment factors: material, frequency of use, manner of use, VPHP exposure time/concentration, type of disinfectant, ability to clean residual product, and historical failure data. These seven items make up the complete input for the replacement frequency decision.

Role of the supplier: the PtC specifically mentions the need for input from the vendor. Glove suppliers usually provide chemical resistance data and recommended service life for the material, which are important bases for the risk assessment.

Practical Application

Suppose you are the head of quality at a CDMO setting up a glove maintenance program for a new RABS filling line. Your RABS uses VPHP for biodecontamination, each campaign has about 5 batches, and each batch takes about 8 hours. What would you do?

  • First, contact the glove supplier to obtain VPHP compatibility data and the recommended replacement interval
  • Analyze the expected frequency of use and type of operation for each glove port based on the operating SOPs
  • Establish an initial PM schedule (for example, replacement every 30 days or every campaign)
  • Collect integrity test data for the first 6 months and adjust the PM frequency according to trends

Q4.4 What is the response to a glove-integrity failure?

Original Text

Recommendation

In response to a glove-integrity failure, the actions to be taken will vary depending on when and where the failure is identified. The impact on previous batches must be always considered unless there is an approved end of batch glove integrity test available. A risk-based site policy and/or procedure should detail the actions required when a glove failure is discovered (1, 6). Typical responses to a glove failure include, but are not limited to the following:

  • If discovered during line setup and prior to RABS cleaning, disinfecting, and/or bio-decontamination, the nonintegral or damaged glove should be replaced, and the replacement should be documented per the site's procedures.
  • If discovered after RABS cleaning, disinfecting, and/or bio-decontamination but prior to product exposure inside the RABS, the glove should be replaced, disinfected or sterilized, and the action documented per the site's procedures. An investigation, including a root cause analysis of procedures, process, and equipment, is recommended.
  • If discovered during or after a production run, an investigation, including a root cause analysis of procedures, process, and equipment as well as the impact on the filled product, is recommended.

During the investigation process, the evaluation of the following elements should be considered:

  • Time and process step when the glove failure was identified
  • Who identified the glove failure and the associated interview
  • RABS location of the failed glove
  • Failure location on the glove (e.g., fingertip, palm, glove port interface)
  • Activities performed by those wearing the failed glove
  • Review of the gloves and RABS preventive maintenance records, including:
    • Recent glove integrity test results
    • Last glove changeout
  • Review of the batch records, including:
    • RABS gloves Incoming Quality Assurance documentation (e.g., material specifications)
    • RABS setup/glove installation
    • RABS cleaning/disinfecting
    • Filling interventions, both qualified and unqualified
    • Any batch-related deviations
  • Review of the operator's qualification and training records
  • Review of the glove integrity-test system qualification studies
  • Review of the current unidirectional air visualization studies
  • Review of batch EM data, both viable and total particles (See Topic 5.)
  • Review of batch bioburden samples data

The evaluation of these materials and records should be conducted according to the site's QRM system. This glove-integrity investigation should be conducted such that the impact on the product is clearly understood and suitable corrective and preventive actions are put in place (4).

Rationale

When nonintegral gloves or gloves with tears or pinhole leaks are detected, proper QRM must be used to determine the potential for contamination of the Grade A environment. While cleaning and disinfecting prior to the run, as well as continuous First Air, provides a level of protection, all integrity-test failures should be investigated. The investigation should determine the potential impact on process and product quality and whether any other processes or batches are potentially impacted. The reason for including or excluding a product or batch from the scope of the investigation should be clearly justified and documented.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Glove-Integrity Failure: a glove found during an integrity test or visual inspection to have a pinhole, tear, deformation or other defect that affects its seal. This is one of the most impactful deviations in RABS operation.

Tiered Response: the PtC divides the response to glove failure into three time points, with increasing risk:

  • Tier 1 (during setup, before cleaning): lowest risk. Replace the glove and document it.
  • Tier 2 (after cleaning, before product exposure): moderate risk. Replace the glove + disinfect/sterilize + investigate.
  • Tier 3 (during or after production): highest risk. Full investigation + assessment of product impact.

CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Actions): the improvement actions that must be established once the investigation is complete, to ensure that similar events do not recur.

Analogy

Imagine you run a high-end sterile operating room. If a glove problem is found before the surgery starts, the impact is minimal: just change the pair. If it is found after the instruments are laid out but before the patient is cut, you need to change the gloves and re-disinfect the entire operating area, and investigate why it happened. But if the torn glove is found only during the surgery, this is the most serious scenario: you must assess whether the patient has been exposed to infection risk, trace back through the whole procedure, and conduct a full investigation of all instruments and areas that were touched. The tiered response logic for RABS glove failure is exactly the same.

Key Notes

Retrospective impact assessment is mandatory: "The impact on previous batches must be always considered". Unless you have records of a passing glove integrity test at the end of each batch that can narrow the scope to the current batch, all previous batches are within the scope of the investigation.

The investigation checklist is extremely detailed: the PtC lists 11 investigation elements, from personnel (who found it, who wore the glove, their qualification and training records) to equipment (glove position, PM records) to process (batch records, EM data). This is because the root cause of a glove failure can come from many directions.

Exclusions also need a rationale: if you decide a batch is not affected and exclude it from the investigation, you must have it "clearly justified and documented". This is a point that is very easily challenged during regulatory inspections.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: suppose that in the glove integrity test after batch 3 of a 5-batch campaign, a pinhole is found in one glove.

  • Scope of impact: if no glove integrity test was done at the end of the first two batches, batches 1-3 are all within the scope of the investigation
  • Customer notification: if these 3 batches involve products of different customers, you need to notify all affected customers
  • Batch release: until the investigation is complete, the affected batches should be placed on hold for release
  • Commercial impact: possible supply delays, loss of customer confidence and additional testing costs

This is why more and more CDMOs choose to perform glove integrity tests at the end of every batch, to narrow the risk window.

  1. Why does the investigation of a glove failure need to review the results of the airflow visualization study (AVS)? How does AVS help assess the risk to the product?
  2. Suppose the investigation finds that the glove pinhole is at a fingertip, and that the glove was most recently used to adjust a filling needle. How does this information affect your risk assessment?
  3. Design a simplified "decision tree for glove integrity failure response" that covers the response actions for all three tiers.
Q4.5 How do you prevent RABS glove damages after installation?

Original Text

Recommendation

To keep the gloves in good condition during the processing of a batch or maintenance intervention, any operation that could compromise glove integrity should be avoided. For example, tools with sharp edges (e.g., scissors), should be those with rounded or covered tips designed specifically for cleanrooms.

Any operation that may overstretch gloves should be avoided, using instead activities within the specific glove range of action or using another glove in closer proximity of the intervention. When not in use, RABS gloves should be retracted to avoid possible interference and damage due to moving parts as well as disruption to First Air. When the gloves are retracted, best practice is to keep the fingers of the gloves extended for ease of the next use.

See Topic 1 of this PtC for RABS design considerations related to gloves.

Glove Replacement Criteria

Gloves should be replaced whenever the relative conditions are different from the technical characteristics defined in manufacturer data sheets or from the internal risk assessment that could compromise the RABS aseptic internal conditions and product quality (1, 3).

Glove replacement frequency is based on the following recommendations, which include, but are not limited to:

  • Physical attributes
  • Manufacturer expiry date
  • Color variation from specification
  • Loss of elasticity
  • Viable and total particle counts trending higher

Prior to removal of gloves from the RABS, a visual inspection and/or pressure test should be conducted based on an approved risk assessment of any damage. Gloves should be removed gently from the RABS station and carefully placed on a sterile or sanitized cart or in sterile pouches. Any other hard or sharp object should not be placed over the gloves after removal from RABS station as that may damage the glove during handling for washing and sterilization processes.

Rationale

Integrity of the gloves is key in order to maintain a Grade A environment within the RABS enclosure. Maintaining glove integrity includes procedurally controlled glove use and replacement, as well as both periodic and routine glove inspection or testing based on an internal risk assessment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Glove Retraction: when a RABS glove is not in use, it should be retracted outward (turned back into the inside of the glove port), so that the glove does not hang inside the RABS where it could be damaged by moving parts or disturb the First Air flow. Best practice is to keep the fingers extended during retraction so the glove can be donned quickly next time.

Glove Replacement Criteria: the PtC gives five replacement triggers: change in physical properties, exceeding the supplier's expiry, color deviation, loss of elasticity and rising particle count trends. These are all objective indicators of glove performance deterioration.

Color Variation: a glove color that deviates from specification may indicate material degradation (for example chemical attack or UV aging), and is an early warning signal that is easy to catch by visual inspection.

Analogy

Protecting RABS gloves is like caring for a pair of fine leather gloves: you would not use them to carry bricks (avoid contact with sharp objects), you would not stuff them into a pocket that is too small (avoid over-stretching), and when not in use you would lay them flat in a dedicated box rather than toss them on the desk (retract the gloves and store them properly). When it is time to replace them, you would first check their condition (visual/pressure test), then carefully remove them and put them in a protective bag (sterile pouch), rather than stuffing them into a pocket and crushing them. The whole process calls for gentle handling.

Key Notes

Prevention is better than detection: the core idea of Q4.5 is "control at the source". Rather than spend large resources investigating after a glove has been damaged, prevent the damage at its root: use round-tipped tools, avoid over-stretching, and retract gloves when not in use.

Particle trends are a hidden indicator: "Viable and total particle counts trending higher" is a very subtle replacement trigger. The glove itself may look intact, but if particle counts near it keep rising, this may suggest that the glove material is degrading or shedding particles.

Glove removal also needs an SOP: the PtC specifically emphasizes precautions when removing gloves (remove gently, place on a sterile cart or in sterile pouches, do not place sharp objects on the glove). This is because reusable gloves may need to be cleaned and re-sterilized after removal, and damage during removal leads to unnecessary waste.

Practical Application

Operator training focus: in a CDMO environment, glove-use training for new operators should include the following hands-on exercises:

  • Tool selection: show the difference between cleanroom round-tipped scissors and ordinary scissors, and explain why sharp tools cannot be used
  • Reach range: practice the reach of each glove port so that operators understand when to use "the closer glove" rather than over-stretching
  • Retraction technique: demonstrate the correct way to retract gloves, including how to keep the fingers extended
  • Abnormality recognition: show real samples of glove deterioration such as color change, loss of elasticity and surface roughening

  1. Why do gloves need to be retracted when not in use? What problems could arise if a glove hangs inside the RABS all the time?
  2. Suppose you are drafting a glove replacement SOP. Of the five replacement criteria, which can be found by visual inspection, and which require data trend analysis?
  3. The PtC says removed gloves should be placed on a "sterile or sanitized cart or in sterile pouches". Why do even gloves that are to be discarded need careful handling? (Hint: consider the need for integrity testing)

Q 5.1 — Environmental Monitoring Frequency

Original Text

Q 5.1. What should the environmental monitoring (EM) frequency be in a closed or open restricted access barrier system (cRABS or oRABS)?

Recommendation

The frequency of EM for oRABS and cRABS should be the same; continuous viable and total particle monitoring is required for the full duration of critical activities, including setup (1). (See Question 5.5.)

Critical activities start at the point when a product-contact part and/or a primary container or closure are transferred and are exposed to the inside of the RABS. Where the RABS is subdivided into discrete zones, the point where critical activities start and end may well be different within the different zones; hence, the continuous EM may start at different times in each zone (4).

Continuous viable (i.e., a combination of active and passive air monitoring) and total particle monitoring should begin at the start of the aseptic setup and should continue up until the end of filling. Typically, this is the end of the batch or campaign.

The RABS should be well designed to minimize the need for interventions involving opening the doors. By exception and where required, the doors should only be opened under strictly defined conditions, which should be procedurally defined and pre-assessed as part of the QRM. The conditions for the opening of the doors and controls in place to mitigate risks should be considered and documented as part of the overall CCS.

Appropriate controls should be in place to minimize the risk posed by the intervention through the opening of the doors and should extend to consideration of further disinfection steps. Additionally, controls should extend to the need to undertake a partial line clearance, for example, to discard exposed primary packaging containers or other direct product-contact parts located close to the intervention (e.g., vials, syringes, stoppers located on the stopper track). While sterility testing cannot be used to rationalize poor practices or otherwise support the undertaking of a high-risk intervention, based on risk, additional sterility samples should be considered as part of an additional control measure for critical interventions (4).

The same EM program applies to both product manufacture and APS.

Rationale

There is no difference in expectations around the frequency of EM inside oRABS and cRABS.

Once a critical surface is exposed inside the RABS, that surface is potentially at risk of contamination, so continuous EM is required.

The requirement for continuous EM ceases once the last unit is closed for the batch or campaign provided any used product-contact parts are reprocessed, and any sterile components are cleared prior to reuse of the line.

In the rare event that the RABS door is opened after line setup is completed, the critical zone is put at an elevated risk of contamination from the person(s) intervening and from the surrounding cleanroom. Additional controls should be considered to reduce risks of such interventions and should extend to consideration of additional disinfection steps and partial line clearances.

Monitoring should not interfere with production processes in a way that raises the level of risk by introducing an activity in the critical zone, which defeats the purpose of the EM.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Continuous Viable and Total Particle Monitoring: both EU Annex 1 and this document require "continuous" monitoring in the grade A environment. Here "continuous" means monitoring may not be interrupted from the start of aseptic setup until the last container is sealed.

Viable Monitoring: a combination of active air sampling and passive air monitoring (settle plates).

Total Particle Monitoring: a particle counter continuously detects particles of ≥0.5 μm and ≥5.0 μm.

Critical Activities: these begin at the moment product-contact surfaces or primary packaging components are exposed inside the RABS. If the RABS has separate zones, the start time of critical activities may differ between zones.

Analogy

Imagine you are an infection control nurse in a hospital operating room. Air quality monitoring must start the moment the sterile packaging of the surgical instruments is opened, and continue until the wound is fully closed and the dressing is in place. You would not skip monitoring because it is a "minor surgery". The same is true of RABS EM: whether oRABS or cRABS, the starting point and requirements for monitoring are exactly the same.

Key Notes

  • oRABS = cRABS: the EM frequency requirements are exactly the same, with no difference. This is a common misunderstanding among manufacturers.
  • Monitoring ≠ interference: the document specifically reminds that the monitoring activity itself should not introduce additional risk into the critical area, otherwise it defeats the purpose of EM.
  • APS applies equally: the EM program for aseptic process simulation (APS) must be consistent with actual production.
  • Response after an open-door intervention: additional disinfection steps and partial line clearance should be considered, for example discarding exposed vials or stoppers.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, a customer may ask: "Can our cRABS filling line have fewer EM sampling locations than an oRABS?" The answer is no. The document clearly states that the EM frequency requirements for both are the same. However, the advantage of a cRABS is that open-door interventions are less frequent, so there are fewer actual deviation events and investigations, which lowers quality costs in the long run.

  2. Under what circumstances can continuous EM be stopped? List the conditions that must be met.

Q 5.2 — Surface Monitoring in RABS

Original Text

Q 5.2. Which surfaces should be monitored in a restricted access barrier system (RABS) (open or closed)?

Recommendation

Firms should conduct a risk assessment to define which surfaces of a RABS to monitor after use. Swabs should be used for complex geometric surfaces; contact plates should be used for flat surfaces. Sampling is only representative; not all surfaces can be sampled (e.g., not all filling needles and not all other surfaces above the critical surfaces).

Typically, based on risk, firms will sample:

  • Direct- and indirect-contact parts
  • Sterile tools that contact or breach First Air over contact parts or sterile primary packaging during the batch/campaign
  • RABS intervention gloves used during the batch/campaign for critical interventions*
  • RTP doors, if used, where the door breaches First Air over a critical surface
  • Parts above the critical surfaces that cannot be sterilized

*Note: When RABS intervention gloves are used ambidextrously, considerations should be given to EM sampling on multiple sides of gloves. This should be assessed using QRM principles.

Other locations linked to the risk assessment may also be sampled (e.g., occluded/hard to reach surfaces).

Surface samples are taken at the end of use (i.e., the batch or campaign). The surfaces sampled should be returned to a state suitable for aseptic manufacturing before the parts are reused. Typically, sterilized surfaces will be removed, subjected to a qualified cleaning process, and then resterilized. Other surfaces, including barrier gloves, should be cleaned and returned to a state suitable for aseptic manufacture.

Rationale

Surface monitoring is typically aimed at surfaces that have the greatest potential impact on product sterility because they directly or indirectly contact the product, or they are permitted to breach First Air (e.g., forceps).

A risk assessment is required to select and scientifically justify both the surfaces to be sampled and locations on those surfaces that are most representative of the risk. Sampling the entire product-contact surface is not necessary; instead, risk-based sampling should occur on the parts most likely to have been put at risk during installation or use.

RABS gloves are used for interventions; therefore, they are required to be sterile before installation and bio-decontaminated in situ thereafter. During use, RABS gloves are likely to contact surfaces inside the RABS that have been disinfected, not sterilized. Where open-door interventions are permitted, a barrier glove may well reside in a lower grade for the duration of the intervention and then be disinfected as the door is shut. Also, RABS gloves are made of flexible materials prone to puncture. RABS gloves that may have come into proximity with or breach First Air over sterile primary packaging, open product, or product-contact parts potentially puts the product at risk and should be sampled; such events should be avoided whenever possible.

Tools used for critical interventions should be sampled. Consideration should be given to the use of dedicated sterile tools that are used exclusively for conducting certain types of interventions (e.g., involving direct or indirect product-contact parts). Sterile tools should be kept in Grade A condition and maintained in a manner that prevents contamination (i.e., proper aseptic technique).

RTP doors are not sterile; they are bio-decontaminated inside and out in situ.

Surface samples are taken at the end of use because the surfaces being sampled need to be maintained both sterile and clean until the end of use; the samples allow this to be assessed as part of batch release. Care must be taken to ensure that the act of sampling does not put the surfaces being sampled at risk of contamination due to either improper sampling techniques, or the use of wrong or incorrectly prepared sampling equipment or consumables.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Direct-Contact Parts: parts that directly contact the product or product-contact surfaces, such as filling needles and stopper tracks.

Indirect-Contact Parts: parts that do not directly touch the product but may affect the sterile environment.

Contact Plates vs. Swabs: contact plates are used to sample flat surfaces, and swabs are used for surfaces with complex geometry. Choosing the right sampling tool is key to ensuring the data are representative.

Representative Sampling: it is not possible to sample every surface, so the most representative sampling locations must be selected through risk assessment.

Analogy

Imagine you are a food safety auditor inspecting a restaurant's hygiene. You cannot swab every inch of the kitchen, but you would surely choose the most critical spots: the cutting board (direct food contact), the knives (tools) and the refrigerator handle (indirect contact). RABS surface monitoring follows the same logic: risk assessment determines the "focus areas" for sampling.

Key Notes

  • Sampling timing: surface sampling is done at the end of use (at the end of the batch or production activity), because the sterile state of the surface must be confirmed over the whole period of use.
  • Sampling both sides of gloves: if RABS gloves are used ambidextrously, sampling of multiple surfaces should be considered.
  • RTP doors are not sterile: RTP doors are bio-decontaminated, not sterilized. This distinction is important in the risk assessment.
  • Sampling itself is a risk: the act of sampling should not increase contamination risk, which includes the correctness of technique, equipment and consumables.

Practical Application

When a CDMO builds an EM program for different customers' products, the list of surface monitoring locations should be defined individually for each filling line based on its risk assessment. For example, on a cRABS filling line that uses RTP transfer, the RTP door surface should be included in the sampling list, whereas a traditional manually opened oRABS should focus more on the intervention gloves and the surfaces around the door frame. Surface monitoring data are part of the batch release documentation, and without a sound risk assessment they will not pass regulatory review.

  1. Why is it unnecessary to sample the surface of every filling needle? What is the scientific basis for "representative sampling"?
  2. After an open-door intervention, RABS gloves may be briefly in a lower-grade environment. How does this affect the glove sampling strategy?
  3. Compare the situations in which contact plates and swabs are suitable. What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?
Q 5.3 — Transferring EM Plates Into/Out of RABS

Original Text

Q 5.3. What factors should be taken into consideration when transferring environmental monitoring (EM) plates into and out of a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Plates should be brought into the surrounding cleanroom sterile and suitably packaged for transfer into the RABS.

Plates should be transferred into and out of the RABS following QRM principles and good aseptic working practices. Suitable methods include, but are not limited to:

  • Plates sterilized in beta bags or containers that are transferred via alpha RTP ports in the RABS.
  • Plates sterilized in impermeable bags that enter and exit the RABS via an integral bio-decontamination (e.g., VPHP), rapid transfer airlock, or transfer chamber.
  • Transfer into the RABS occurs prior to line setup, with the outer bag being removed or disinfected at the Grade transition point. Exit occurs after the batch is complete. Transport to the testing laboratory should be done in a manner that prevents contamination.
  • Using plate sliders, with the outer surfaces of the plate being disinfected at the Grade transition point. Care must be taken to ensure that no disinfectant goes inside the plate. Design should ensure that no airflow enters the RABS from the cleanroom. An example of a plate slider can be seen in Figure 5.3-1.

It is not recommended to open RABS doors to transfer EM plates in or out of the RABS after line setup is complete or when critical surfaces are exposed.

Rationale

To provide reliable results, plates need to be sterile at the point of exposure.

RABS doors should not be opened after line setup; this includes the transfer of EM plates.

RTPs provide a method for safe entry of materials, including EM plates.

Transfer chambers provide a method whereby the risk posed by the entry of material previously exposed to Grade B conditions can be controlled by a combination of outer bag removal and sporicidal disinfection.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Beta Bags / Alpha RTP Ports: an RTP (rapid transfer port) system consists of an alpha part (fixed to the RABS) and a beta part (a movable container/bag). EM plates can be sterilized in a beta bag and then safely passed into the RABS through the alpha port, without being exposed to the background environment at any point.

Plate Sliders: a dedicated device that allows plates to be slid into or out of the RABS without opening the door. The key design point is to prevent outside airflow from entering the RABS.

VPHP (Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide): a commonly used bio-decontamination method that can be used for surface decontamination of items in a pass-through chamber.

Analogy

Think of the RABS as an operating room, and the EM plates as sampling tools to be used during surgery. You cannot open the operating room door in the middle of surgery to carry tools in, because that would break the sterile environment. The correct approach is to prepare all the instruments and place them in the room before surgery, or to pass them in safely through a dedicated transfer window (like an RTP). A plate slider is like a drive-through window: items can be passed safely without opening a whole wall.

Key Notes

  • Never open the door for EM: the document clearly states that, once line setup is complete or when critical surfaces are exposed, opening the RABS door to transfer EM plates is not recommended. EM should not become a source of contamination.
  • Four transfer methods: RTP, pass-through chamber (with VPHP decontamination), pre-loading and plate slider. Each method has its own advantages and disadvantages and should be chosen according to the equipment configuration.
  • Disinfectant must not get inside the plate: when using a plate slider, take special care that external disinfection does not contaminate the media surface, otherwise false negative results will occur.

Practical Application

When designing a new RABS filling line, the path for EM plates in and out must be considered at the design stage. Relying only on manual door opening for transfer not only goes against the guidance, but also creates a "mini open-door intervention" every time plates are changed, accumulating a considerable contamination risk. It is recommended to include a plate slider or RTP configuration in the user requirements specification (URS) when purchasing RABS equipment.

  1. If your RABS has neither an RTP nor a plate slider, how would you design the in-and-out flow of EM plates to avoid opening the door?
  2. Why must plates be "sterile" at the point of exposure? If the plate itself is contaminated, what effect will that have on the EM data?
Q 5.4 — Media Plate Storage Inside RABS

Original Text

Q 5.4. How should media plates be stored inside a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Plate storage locations (i.e., either within the last layer bag or unbagged) should be risk-assessed and well-defined. Considerations include, but are not limited to, the following:

  • Plates should be stored away from product and product-contact parts in a location close to the sampling location.
  • Plates should not be stored in the same flow of First Air that is protecting a critical surface.
  • The operator should not need to reach over or pass close to a critical surface to interact with the EM plates.
  • Plates should not be stored close to air returns within a cRABS.
  • The use of plate stands is recommended (See Figure 5.4-1).

Rationale

Care must be taken to ensure that EM does not add contamination risk to the process. Risk-assessing the storage location and the linked activities (e.g., retrieval of a plate from pre-use storage, labeling, positioning, retrieval of the plate from the sampling location, relocation to post-use storage location) should be undertaken to define the correct storage location.

The storage location must ensure that the handling of the plates does not require the plate or the operator to breach First Air or come into proximity with sterile primary packaging, open product, or product-contact surfaces.

The defined storage location must also be selected with respect to the airflow within the RABS. The plates, and the packaging they arrive in, should not be allowed to block air exits or returns. Storage of the plates should be considered as part of the AVS (Air Visualization Study).

The storage of exposed and unexposed plates needs to be considered to prevent mix-ups and the potential reuse of a previously exposed plate, which may be dried out if used beyond allowed exposure time. Plate stands can aid in this, for example, by stacking plates such that fresh plates are removed from the bottom of the stack and used plates are returned to the top of the stack.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

First Air: the clean air that first reaches the critical surface after leaving the HEPA filter. EM plates must never be placed in the First Air path that protects the critical surface, because the plates themselves would disturb the airflow.

AVS (Air Visualization Study): uses smoke testing to verify the airflow patterns inside the RABS. The storage location of plates must be one of the items considered in the AVS.

Plate Stands: dedicated holders that store unused and used plates in an orderly way, avoiding mix-ups and reuse. A stacking approach is recommended in which new plates are taken from the bottom and used plates are returned to the top.

Analogy

Think of EM plates as the spare filters for a kitchen range hood. You would not put a spare filter next to the burner (equivalent to placing it in the First Air path), nor above the cutting board where you chop vegetables (equivalent to placing it near the critical surface). You would put it on a shelf that does not interfere with your cooking workflow and is easy to reach. That is the idea of a plate stand.

Key Notes

  • EM must not increase contamination risk: this is the core principle running through the whole of Topic 5. Every action in storing, handling and changing plates must go through risk assessment.
  • Separating used and unused plates: there must be a clear method to distinguish exposed from unexposed plates, to avoid confusion. Exposed plates that exceed the allowed exposure time will dry out and become invalid.
  • Do not block return air openings: in a cRABS, plates and their packaging should not block air outlets or return air openings, otherwise the airflow pattern and differential pressure inside the RABS will be affected.

  2. If plates are stored in an improper location, what effect could this have on the AVS results and on the quality of the filled product?
  3. What actions should operators especially avoid when taking or changing plates?
Q 5.5 — Personnel Monitoring During Setup and Open-Door Interventions

Original Text

Q 5.5. When and how should environmental and personnel monitoring be performed during setup and open-door interventions?

Recommendation

All open-door interventions need to be monitored from the point where the first critical surface enters the RABS. The minimum level of monitoring inside a RABS during critical activities is continuous viable and total particle monitoring; continuous viable monitoring is achieved by passive-air and active-air monitoring.

For open-door activities, including line set-up, monitoring within the intervention zone is recommended. This monitoring needs to be based on QRM principles and may need to be changed to ensure monitoring covers the performed activities. Sampling locations, as defined in the EM risk assessment, should be linked to qualified and unqualified interventions.

Additional samples might be taken, based on QRM principles, in the UDAF immediately outside the intervention zone, close to the operator and open-door. This can be achieved with either passive or active monitoring; samples should be taken at the working position level.

Each primary operator involved in open-door interventions, including line setup, should be monitored immediately as they exit the RABS Grade A interior, after having completed all the intervention steps in the critical zone, including any disinfection that may be required. The RABS door disinfection and closure should be done by the secondary operator.

Representative samples should be taken as defined in the risk assessment of the EM program. Sample locations must be selected as per the risk assessment. The risk assessment should consider any body part entering the Grade A environment and any location selected through the risk assessment, and these must meet Grade A limits (e.g., taking samples from the operator's RABS gloves, sleeves, head, and chest, as appropriate to the specific intervention).

It is recommended that a second, fully trained operator should support the monitoring to prevent false positive growth. This sampling should be done in an environment that prevents contamination of the sample (e.g., the UDAF zone of protection or another UDAF zone close to the RABS).

To be representative, samples should be taken as close as possible (i.e., physical and temporal) to the end of the operators' activities inside the RABS. Gowns should be removed and replaced following sampling. Care must be taken to ensure that agar is not transferred to the RABS or surrounding cleanroom where the sampling takes place, during the degowning and gown disposal step.

The individual whose clothing was sampled will then need to leave the cleanroom and replace some or all of their aseptic clothing (i.e., RABS gloves and gown). The second person should also open any doors to allow the person sampled to pass without transferring agar onto cleanroom surfaces.

Rationale

Entry of personnel into the RABS reduces the effectiveness of the barrier and potentially puts the product at risk of contamination. Performing personnel monitoring after, and active air sampling during, the open-door activities provides valuable additional data to support batch disposition.

An approved EM risk assessment should be used to determine the representative locations that require sampling meeting Grade A limits (e.g., body parts entering the Grade A environment). Relying only on glove-sampling data may not be adequate, especially where RABS gloves are disinfected or changed immediately prior to the intervention.

When sampling occurs close to the point where the person exits the RABS, the data collected will most closely reflect the risk posed by the operator to the RABS and will be less influenced by the surrounding lower-grade cleanroom and surfaces outside the RABS that are touched up to the point where the samples are taken. Ideally, a second person would sample the person exiting the RABS at the point they exit, this minimizes any external influences on the data (e.g., handling of plates, pens, documentation in Grade B) and ensures that the samples fully represent the risk posed by the first operator to the critical zone and have not been unnecessarily compromised.

Note: Operators should leave the RABS and change gloves to prevent the spread of media residues after finger dabs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Intervention Zone: the area inside the RABS affected when an operator performs an open-door intervention. Environmental monitoring must be performed in this zone.

UDAF (Unidirectional Airflow): clean air flowing vertically downward from the HEPA filter, providing grade A air quality protection.

Primary Operator vs. Secondary Operator: the primary operator performs the intervention inside the RABS; the secondary operator is responsible for disinfecting and closing the door and for personnel sampling, forming a "two-person cross-check" quality assurance mechanism.

False Positive Growth: non-genuine microbial recovery caused by improper sampling technique or environmental interference. Having a second person assist with sampling reduces this risk.

Analogy

Think of an open-door intervention as a "security checkpoint" for entering a cleanroom. The primary operator is like a surgeon entering the operating room, and after the work must undergo an "infection control check" (personnel sampling) at the exit. The secondary operator is like the infection control nurse at the door, responsible for disinfecting the door and for sampling. This is a "one person works, one person oversees" two-person mechanism, ensuring that every entry and exit has a complete microbiological data record.

Key Notes

  • Immediate sampling principle: personnel sampling must be performed "immediately" when the operator leaves the RABS grade A environment, because a time delay lets the result be influenced by the surrounding lower-grade environment.
  • Sample the whole body, not only gloves: relying on glove sampling alone may not be sufficient, especially when the RABS gloves have just been disinfected or replaced before the intervention. Gloves, sleeves, head, chest and other sites should be considered.
  • Control of media residue: after finger-dab sampling, the operator must leave the cleanroom to change gloves and garments, to prevent agar residue from being transferred to the RABS or to cleanroom surfaces.
  • Secondary operator assists with the door: the person being sampled should not open the door themselves, to avoid transferring agar to cleanroom surfaces such as door handles.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, the personnel monitoring procedure for open-door interventions is one of the focus items in regulatory inspections. It is recommended to establish a standardized "two-person intervention SOP" that clearly specifies: (1) the primary operator's steps and scope of work, (2) the secondary operator's duties for door control and sampling, (3) the list of sampling sites and methods, and (4) the garment change and plate submission process after sampling. This SOP should be included in the CCS documents and verified during APS.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is sampling by a second person more representative than sampling by the operator themselves? What is the difference in terms of data reliability?
  2. If the RABS gloves have just been disinfected before the intervention, why might sampling the gloves alone be insufficient to assess the risk the operator brings?
  3. Why must sterile garments be changed completely after personnel sampling? What consequences could there be if they are not changed?
Q 5.6 — Separating EM Data for Setup vs. Filling

Original Text

Q 5.6. Should environmental monitoring (EM) for line setup and filling be kept separate?

Recommendation

Yes. While not mandated, there are benefits to keeping EM for line setup and filling separate. Keeping the data from open-door line setup separate aids the investigation and process improvement in the event of EM recoveries (1, 7).

Rationale

Line setup activities usually pose a higher risk to the critical zone because they typically include frequent open-door interventions, material movements into the RABS, and the presence of personnel in the critical zone, whereas filling activities are typically conducted via closed-door interventions. Segregating the two activities for EM allows the data from line setup and filling activities to be visualized and trended independently. Where EM recoveries occur, identifying the assignable root cause of the microbiological data deviation is aided when the data for the two activities are separate.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Line Setup vs. Filling: two stages of RABS operation with very different risk levels. Line setup usually involves frequent open-door interventions, material transfer in and personnel entry into the critical area, whereas the filling stage is usually performed with closed-door interventions (using glove ports) and carries relatively lower risk.

EM Recoveries: events in which microorganisms are detected during environmental monitoring. Separating the EM data of setup and filling allows the source of contamination to be traced more precisely.

Analogy

Imagine you run a restaurant. If you combine the food safety test results from the "food preparation period" and the "serving period" in one report, then when a problem is found you cannot tell whether preparation or serving went wrong. Tracking the two separately is like recording the hygiene data of the "kitchen preparation stage" and the "restaurant service stage" separately, so that when an abnormality appears you can quickly pinpoint which stage is responsible.

Key Notes

  • Not mandated, but strongly recommended: the document says "not mandated" but immediately stresses the "benefits". In a regulatory context this means an expectation of best practice, and if it is not done, adequate justification must be provided during inspection.
  • Value for trend analysis: separating the data allows the microbial trends of line setup and filling to be observed independently, which is very valuable for continuous improvement.
  • Efficiency of root cause investigation: when an EM deviation occurs, separated data can immediately show at which stage the problem occurred, speeding up the investigation.

Practical Application

For a CDMO, separating the EM data of setup and filling is an important indicator of quality management maturity. It is recommended to set an "activity phase" tag in the LIMS or EM database, so that every EM result is automatically labeled "Setup" or "Filling". In annual trend reports or customer audits, this clearly shows the level of microbial control in each stage, and also helps to identify opportunities for systemic improvement.

  2. If a site does not separate setup and filling EM data, what challenges might it face during a regulatory inspection?

Q 5.7 — Fixed EM Air-Monitoring Sampling Point Locations

Original Text

Q 5.7. Where should the fixed environmental monitoring (EM) air-monitoring sampling points be located within the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Firms should conduct a risk assessment in the design phase to determine the number and locations of EM samples that should be taken. (See PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2014): Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program.)

The sampling locations should be positioned as closely as possible to the most critical operations, such as the point of fill, sorting bowl, or rotary-table. They should be positioned vertically at or close to the height of the critical surface they are monitoring and ideally in the same plane.

Note: FDA's 2004 Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing—Current Good Manufacturing Practice states:

"Air in the immediate proximity of exposed sterilized containers/closures and filling/closing operations would be of appropriate particle quality when it has a per-cubic-meter particle count of no more than 3520 in a size range of 0.5 μm and larger when counted at representative locations normally not more than 1 foot away from the work site, within the airflow, and during filling/closing operations. This level of air cleanliness is also known as Class 100 (ISO 5)."

Sampling locations should not breach First Air over critical surfaces. They should be positioned so that they capture the risks from any permitted intervention, for example, between the RABS door or glove and the critical surface being monitored, and between the door and critical surfaces where open-door interventions are permitted.

Viable- and total-particle sampling locations should be co-located wherever possible (if supported by risk-assessment) both to allow a point of comparison and for ergonomic reasons. Sampling locations should not cause an interference on the airflow between the sampling points (i.e., ideally isokinetic sampling for viable monitoring). AVS should demonstrate the noninterference during the sampling (e.g., passages of RABS gloves during the plate changing).

Total-particle sampling positions need to be set to allow a tube no longer than one meter (1 m) to connect the isokinetic sampling-head to the particle counter. Where lengths greater than 1 m must be used,* studies are required to assess the particle loss, in particular, the 5.0 μm particle loss, and determine if improvements are required. Ideally, the connecting tube would run straight. Any necessary bends should have smooth bend radii, where a 90° bend is achieved over no less than 15 cm of tube.

*Note: RABS should be designed/installed to avoid use exceeding 1 m.

Rationale

It is not possible to monitor the entire RABS. A risk assessment is required to position EM samples in locations where they are most likely to detect the risks posed from the main contamination risks, that is, conducting both open- and closed-door interventions.

Each unique particle size will have a different deposition profile in tubing based on, for example, airflow velocity and sample volume, tubing diameter, sampling port arrangement, temperature, bend radius, tube length, and tubing material. Because particulate monitoring attempts to assess a range of sizes (e.g., 0.5 μm and greater in optical diameter), no single set of radii or length conditions can describe best practices for each of these particle sizes. Minimizing the number of tubing bends, maximizing radii, and minimizing overall tubing length should be the goal, and it should be done in accordance with the particle-counter equipment manufacturer's recommended best practices.

The particle-counter equipment manufacturer should provide technical justification for its recommendations. Instrument calibration should address sampling configuration.

Note:

  • EU Annex 1 now states that tube length should typically be no longer than 1 m and any bend radii should be avoided.
  • International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14644-1:2015 Cleanrooms and Associated Controlled Environments — Part 1: Classification of Air Cleanliness by Particle Concentration also states that tube length should be no longer than 1 m.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Isokinetic Sampling: the intake velocity of the sampling probe matches the velocity of the surrounding airflow, so that particles are collected in proportion and no sampling bias results from a velocity difference. This is key to the accuracy of particle monitoring.

Co-location: placing the microbial sampling point and the particle sampling point at the same location, so that when a particle excursion occurs somewhere, the microbial data from the same location can be compared directly, which facilitates correlation analysis.

1 m tubing limit: the tubing connecting the isokinetic sampling head to the particle counter should not exceed 1 meter. The longer the tubing, the greater the loss of particles (especially large particles of ≥5.0 μm) through settling and adhesion to the tube wall, giving falsely low measurements.

Bend Radii: if the tubing must bend, a 90° bend should be made gently over a tube length of at least 15 cm, to avoid particle loss from sharp bends.

Analogy

Imagine installing fire detectors in a building. You would not put a smoke detector at the far end of a corridor away from any fire source. You would put it near the kitchen, where a fire is most likely to start, and at key exits. Likewise, the EM sampling points of a RABS should be as close as possible to the most critical operating positions such as the filling point and the stoppering station. The tubing is like the detector's connecting wire: the shorter and straighter it is, the more accurately the signal is transmitted; with too many bends, the "smoke" (particles) is lost on the way.

Key Notes

  • Plan at the design stage: sampling point locations should be determined through risk assessment during RABS design, not by "finding space" after installation.
  • Three-dimensional consideration of location: horizontally it should be close to the critical operation, and vertically it should be at or near the height of the critical surface, ideally in the same plane.
  • Must not disturb First Air: the sampling point itself must not block the First Air protecting the critical surface, otherwise quality is reduced in the name of monitoring quality.
  • Between the door and the critical surface: sampling points should be located between the RABS door or glove port and the critical surface, so that the contamination risk from interventions can be captured.
  • Multiple standards agree on 1 m: both EU Annex 1 and ISO 14644-1:2015 clearly require tubing length of no more than 1 m. This is already an international consensus.

Tubing Design Parameters

Tubing length limit:  ≤ 1 m (EU Annex 1 / ISO 14644-1)
Minimum arc length for a 90° bend: ≥ 15 cm
FDA ISO 5 particle limit: ≤ 3,520 particles/m³ (≥0.5 μm)
Sampling distance:   ≤ 1 foot (~30 cm) from work site

Factors affecting particle loss:
  - Tube length
  - Bend count & radii
  - Tubing diameter
  - Airflow velocity
  - Tubing material

Practical Application

At the RABS equipment purchasing stage, the installation location of the particle counter and the routing of the tubing must be included in the URS and the design qualification (DQ) documents. It is recommended to confirm with the equipment supplier: (1) the recommended tubing configuration for the particle counter, (2) the particle loss validation plan when tubing exceeds 1 m, and (3) the isokinetic sampling design of the sampling head. Determining these at the design stage avoids costly retrofits after installation when the tubing is found to be too long. In addition, AVS should verify that none of the sampling points disturb the airflow during operation (including plate-changing actions).

  2. If, in your RABS design, the particle counter tubing has to exceed 1 m, what study do you need to perform to show that the measurement results are still reliable?

Section Summary

  • Q5.1 Monitoring frequency: the EM frequency requirements for oRABS and cRABS are exactly the same. Continuous viable and particle monitoring must be performed from the start of critical surface exposure until the last container is sealed. The same EM program applies to production and APS.
  • Q5.2 Surface monitoring: the surfaces to monitor should be selected through risk assessment, with emphasis on direct/indirect contact parts, intervention gloves, RTP doors, and non-sterilizable parts above the critical surface. Surface sampling is performed after the end of the batch.
  • Q5.3 Plate transfer: plates should enter and leave the RABS through an RTP, pass-through chamber, pre-loading or plate slider, and the door should never be opened after line setup just to transfer EM plates.
  • Q5.4 Plate storage: the storage location must be away from the First Air path and the critical surface and must not block return air openings; use plate stands to distinguish used from unused plates, and verify the location in the AVS.
  • Q5.5 Personnel monitoring: an open-door intervention requires immediate personnel sampling (gloves, sleeves, head, chest, etc.), performed by a second person; after sampling, garments must be changed and transfer of media residue prevented.
  • Q5.6 Data separation: although not mandatory, it is strongly recommended to track the EM data of line setup and filling separately, to support trend analysis and root cause investigation.
  • Q5.7 Sampling point location: sampling points should be located at the design stage, as close as possible to the critical operation and in the same height plane; tubing length should not exceed 1 m, and microbial and particle sampling points are recommended to be co-located.

Q 6.1 How should indirect product contact surfaces be sterilized or decontaminated?

Original Text

Recommendation

Indirect product-contact parts such as stopper bowls, hoppers, ancillary items (e.g., tweezers, forceps), and guides must be sterilized (1). This is typically done by wrapping the clean items in suitable semi-permeable packaging material and sterilizing them in a moist-heat autoclave, followed by unloading, transportation through the cleanroom environment, and aseptic assembly inside the RABS. Machine items that break First Air (e.g., sensors, holders, brackets at the filling line or rotary table) should be avoided where possible. When machine items do break First Air, they should be sterilized. (See Question 6.5 for more details.)

Based on facility layout and whether Grade A continuity can be applied for the sterilized items (e.g., by using UDAF carts), either single-wrapping or double-wrapping can be applied to ensure transport of the autoclaved, sterilized parts from the Grade B autoclave to the entrance of the RABS. To provide for aseptic removal and transfer of the parts into the RABS, good aseptic technique must be used to ensure that the inner layer or part is only exposed within the Grade A environment. The wrapping materials and removal process should minimize particle generation as much as possible.

When using double wrapping, the outer wrapping is ideally removed at the barrier door, where the contents are still protected by the inner barrier. For large items, such as the stopper bowl, two people may be needed to properly complete the aseptic transfer. That is, the primary and secondary operators are responsible for different steps: typically, one for the transfer activities outside the RABS, and one for the transfer activities inside the RABS. The inner wrapping is then removed toward the end of the assembly process under Grade A conditions, depending on the design of the manufacturing line just before or after the RABS doors are closed. In case parts of the assembly are done as a closed-door intervention, the wrapping could be removed at an earlier stage to enable removal of the wrap from the RABS just before door closure or as a final open-door intervention. Special considerations must be taken in the sequence of removing the wrapping so as to ensure the First Air of already unwrapped parts is not breached during the removal of other wrappers.

Automated surface bio-decontamination processes such as VPHP, are not considered equivalent to sterilization processes, however, they can reduce bioburden risk if applied after the set-up process is complete and the RABS doors are closed.

If items can be directly unloaded into the cRABS (e.g., via RTP) or an aseptic transfer from the autoclave to the RABS is otherwise guaranteed (e.g., via Grade A continuity, typically a process requiring rigorous controls), different means of wrapping and/or dry-heat sterilization of the indirect product-contact items may be applied. The wrapping materials and removal process should minimize particle generation as much as possible.

In addition to open-door installation, an introduction of indirect product-contact parts via RTP or sterile boxes can be considered for the RABS to minimize the risk of potential contamination during installation by open doors. This especially applies for smaller parts such as guiding and stopper guardrails, for example.

Rationale

Indirect product-contact surfaces are critical for the sterility assurance of the product and must be sterilized (1).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Indirect Product-Contact Parts: these parts do not directly touch the drug solution, but they contact items that are in direct contact with the product. For example, the stopper bowl contacts the stoppers, and the hopper guides stoppers or containers. Because they operate inside the sterile critical area, they must still be sterilized.

Single-Wrapping vs. Double-Wrapping: the choice depends on whether the facility layout can maintain grade A continuity. If there is UDAF protection all the way from the autoclave to the RABS entrance (for example, using a UDAF cart), single wrapping is sufficient. If the transport path passes through a grade B area and a continuous grade A environment cannot be guaranteed, double wrapping is needed so that the inner layer stays protected until it is exposed.

VPHP Bio-decontamination: note that the document clearly states VPHP is "not considered equivalent to sterilization". It can reduce bioburden risk but cannot replace moist heat sterilization. This is a common regulatory exam point.

Analogy

Imagine you are preparing surgical instruments in an operating room. The scalpel directly contacts the wound (direct contact), while the surgical tray, although it does not touch the wound, carries the scalpel (indirect contact). You would not skip disinfecting the tray because it is "only indirect contact". In the same way, stopper bowls and guide rails must also go through the full sterilization process.

The idea of double wrapping is like a Russian nesting doll: the outer layer is removed at the grade B door (the first layer of protection is stripped off) while the contents are still protected by the inner layer; the inner layer is opened only inside the grade A environment, ensuring that critical parts are never exposed to a lower-grade environment.

Key Notes

The unwrapping sequence is critical: the document specifically stresses that the order must be considered during unwrapping, to ensure that the First Air of parts already unwrapped is not disrupted by the removal of other packaging materials. This operating detail is often overlooked in practice, but it is a high-risk finding in inspections.

Division of work between primary and secondary operator: aseptic transfer of large parts (such as the stopper bowl) requires two people working together. The primary operator handles activities outside the RABS, and the secondary operator handles activities inside the RABS. This division is designed to reduce the risk of cross-contamination.

Practical Application

In CDMO operations, the material transfer strategy directly affects batch preparation time and contamination risk. If a new facility is designed to use an RTP (rapid transfer port) to transfer indirect-contact parts directly into a cRABS, the number of door-opening operations can be greatly reduced. Although the initial equipment investment for an RTP is higher, in the long run it can lower the cost of deviation investigations, improve batch release efficiency and reduce the risk of APS failure. This is an option worth prioritizing in the design decisions for a new building.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is VPHP bio-decontamination not considered equivalent to sterilization? What is the value of using VPHP after RABS setup is complete?
  2. If your facility layout requires the path between the autoclave and the RABS to cross a grade B corridor (without UDAF protection), would you choose single or double wrapping? Why?
  3. When removing packaging, why must the sequence be considered rather than unwrapping several parts at the same time?
Q 6.2 Can stopper bowls, hoppers, and tracks remain installed between batches?

Original Text

Recommendation

If a campaign manufacture has been justified and there is no risk of contamination during and in between the batches, which has been validated in APS, indirect product-contact parts can remain within the RABS system for several batches of a campaign using the same equipment. Typically, after completion of a campaign, the items are environmentally monitored, cleaned, and sterilized before being reused. In addition to the aspect of sterility assurance that the campaign time does not adversely affect particle generation by abrasion from stoppers also needs to be considered.

Rationale

Specific principles may be defined for campaign manufacture if product sterility assurance is not jeopardized (1).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Campaign Manufacture: producing several consecutive batches of the same product on the same equipment without a full disassembly and re-sterilization in between. This is an important efficiency strategy in a CDMO, but it requires demonstrating that sterility assurance is not affected.

APS Validation: leaving parts in place between batches in a campaign must be validated by APS. The APS needs to simulate the maximum duration of the campaign, showing that the indirect-contact parts remain sterile throughout.

Analogy

Think of the pots in a restaurant: if you cook different batches of the same dish in the same pot one after another, you only need a simple clean in between rather than sending it for high-temperature disinfection every time. But for the pot to stay safe in continuous use, you must first prove that (1) continuous use does not lead to a build-up of food residue, and (2) wear does not generate harmful particles. The logic of campaign manufacturing is exactly the same: validate first, then execute.

Key Notes

The hidden risk of particle generation: the document specifically mentions stopper wear. Stoppers sliding repeatedly in the bowl and tracks generate particles, and the longer the campaign, the worse the wear. This is not only a sterility issue but also a CQA risk for visible particulates and sub-visible particulates, and it must be assessed in the campaign duration validation.

Handling after the campaign: after the campaign ends, all parts left in place must go through environmental monitoring (EM), cleaning and re-sterilization before they can be used again. This ensures that any risk accumulated during the campaign is completely eliminated.

Practice Questions

  1. If your CDMO customer asks for a five-day campaign, what worst-case conditions do you need to simulate in the APS validation?
  2. Particles generated by stopper bowl wear are directly related to which product CQAs? How would you design a validation plan to evaluate the maximum campaign duration?
Q 6.3 What approaches are recommended for transferring stoppers into a RABS?

Original Text

Recommendation

There are different ways of transferring stoppers into RABS. Aseptic transfer systems (e.g., RTP) have a lower-risk profile compared to manual transfer systems. New RABS systems preferably should be designed using aseptic transfer systems.

Aseptic Transfer Systems

For sterile ready-to-use stoppers, the stopper transfer can be done by aseptic connector systems using RTPs. To enable this, the RABS will need to be designed with an applicable transfer system close to the stopper hopper. An example of this can be seen in Figure 6.3-1.

Stoppers can then be directly sterilized in the transfer port containers (or as ready-to-use packaging material that has been presterilized by the supplier) and aseptically transferred into the RABS by a closed-door intervention. (The RABS design should facilitate opening the transfer door without overreaching any indirect product-contact parts with the RABS gloves.) The integrity of those rapid transfer bags (e.g., by visual inspection for breaches or defects) or rigid containers (e.g., by regular integrity-testing of the vent filters) need to be assured.

Figure 6.3-1 Rapid Transfer Port (RTP) for Stopper Loading

For processes or facilities where stopper sterilization is undertaken, for instance, in a stopper processor, where stopper sterilization cycles are qualified in a closed stopper pod, stopper transfer can be achieved via a docking mechanism that involves docking of the stopper pod to a stopper transfer chute. The sterilization of the stopper transfer chute and bottom of the stopper pod must be demonstrated, therein providing a sterile passage of the sterilized stoppers directly to the sterilized stopper hopper located in the RABS.

Manual Transfer Systems

Other more manual surface-disinfection systems may be used for transferring stoppers into RABS as well. For example, where the double-wrapped stopper bag is first transferred from Grade B into a Grade A hatch by removal of the outer wrap, the hatch can be closed and then operated by built-in RABS gloves, loading the stoppers via a second hatch and chute into the stopper hopper. Figure 6.3-2 provides a schematic drawing of such a system, detailing the use of a UDAF transfer box to access the inside of the RABS; Figure 6.3-3 provides an example of this system.

Figure 6.3-3 Example of Manual Stopper Transfer System

Rationale

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

RTP - Rapid Transfer Port: a sealed mechanical docking system that allows material to be passed into the grade A area without breaking the integrity of the RABS barrier. Its core principle is the "alpha-beta door" design: the outer side (alpha door) and the inner side (beta door) each have a sealed door, and on docking they form a closed passage; after the material passes through, the two doors separate and each returns to its sealed state.

Closed-Door Intervention: operations performed through an RTP or the RABS gloves, without opening the RABS door at any point, maintaining barrier integrity. This is the preferred operating mode.

Stopper Processor: equipment that integrates washing, sterilization and drying of stoppers in a closed system. After sterilization, the stopper pod can dock directly to the transfer port on the RABS, achieving fully closed transfer.

Analogy

Think of stopper transfer as docking at a space station. An RTP system is like the docking hatch between a spacecraft and a space station: each side has its own sealed door, and on docking they form a closed passage. After the astronaut (stopper) passes through, both sides reseal, and the environment inside the station is never exposed to outer space (the lower-grade environment). By comparison, a manual transfer system is more like doing a spacewalk in a spacesuit: feasible, but riskier and more complicated.

Key Notes

Design decides the outcome: the document repeatedly stresses the concept of "by design". A new RABS system "preferably should be designed using aseptic transfer systems". This is not merely a suggestion but close to a requirement. If an RTP is not included at the design stage, retrofitting later is very difficult and expensive.

Avoid loading stoppers with the door open: of all stopper loading methods, door-open operation carries the highest risk, and the document clearly says it "preferably would be avoided by design". This means that if a new facility design still plans open-door stopper loading, regulatory inspectors may question the soundness of the design.

Integrity assurance: whichever transfer system is used, the integrity of the transfer container must be ensured. Flexible bags need visual inspection for damage, and rigid containers need regular integrity testing of the vent filter.

Practical Application

As a CDMO, when you evaluate the stopper loading method for a new line, you should build a risk matrix to compare the three options:

  • RTP system: lowest contamination risk, enables closed-door intervention, highest initial investment
  • Manual transfer (grade A hatch): medium risk, requires operator skill qualification, can be handled through the RABS gloves
  • Open-door operation: highest risk, requires the most EM and personnel monitoring, and should be only a last resort

When submitting the design qualification (DQ) documents, the reasons for selecting or excluding each option must be recorded. This is part of the CCS.

Q 6.4 How should bulk containers or ready-to-use containers be transferred into the RABS?

Original Text

Recommendation

Vials are typically washed and introduced into the RABS via a depyrogenation tunnel, which is similar in concept to isolator lines. In contrast to isolator systems, which are placed in a Grade C background where all the equipment might be placed in one room, the washing and tunnel system for RABS are placed in a separate room of a lower classification than the Grade B RABS background and connected through the wall (1, 4).

For ready-to-use packaging material, for example, syringes, vials, and pre-crimped cartridges, there are different technologies that can be used for their introduction into the RABS. Electronic beam (e-beam) technology (Figure 6.4-1) or VPHP (Figure 6.4-2) can be used for a complete-surface decontamination, or a no-touch-transfer (NTT) system (Figure 6.4-3) with multiple overwrap removal, followed by a de-lidding/de-lining process, can be employed.

NTT applies to double-bagged (secondary packaging) removal via successive debagging steps for the transfer of nested tubs of containers in a sterile barrier into Grade A filling lines without any manual disinfection applied in material transfer. "No touch" denotes there is no physical contact with the contents ejected from the packaging through to the next higher-grade zone, with debagging (cutting a bag open and ejecting the contents) applied as a semi-automated or automated process to reduce operator interventions (19).

Rationale

The transfer of bulk containers must ensure that the open containers are rendered sterile (or remain sterile) and that introduction into the RABS does not lead to contamination of the Grade A environment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Depyrogenation Tunnel: uses high-temperature dry heat (usually ≥250°C) to remove endotoxin (pyrogen) from vials and achieve sterilization. This is the standard path for bulk vials entering a RABS. The exit end of the tunnel connects directly to the entry end of the RABS, forming a continuous sterile passage.

E-beam technology: uses a high-energy electron beam to fully decontaminate the outer surface of RTU (ready-to-use) packaging. It is fast and leaves no residue, but the equipment investment is high.

NTT - No-Touch-Transfer: through automated layer-by-layer debagging, nested tubs are taken out of the multi-layer packaging and delivered into the grade A area, with no human contact and no manual disinfection steps. This is the most advanced technology for introducing RTU containers.

Analogy

The different paths for containers entering a RABS are like the different disinfection routes into an operating room:

  • The depyrogenation tunnel is like a "flame disinfection corridor" before the operating room: vials pass through a 250°C high-temperature tunnel, and all microorganisms and pyrogens are thoroughly eliminated
  • E-beam / VPHP is like a "UV disinfection chamber": energy or chemical gas decontaminates the outer surface of the packaging
  • NTT is like peeling an onion layer by layer: each "outer coat" (packaging bag) is removed automatically in an increasingly clean environment, and the contents are never touched

Key Notes

Facility design differences between RABS and isolator: the document points out a key facility design difference. An isolator system can sit in a grade C background, with all equipment in the same room. For a RABS, however, the washing and tunnel systems must be placed in a separate room of a grade lower than grade B, connected through a wall. This directly affects facility layout and construction cost.

The three techniques for introducing RTU packaging each suit different scenarios: E-beam suits high-throughput lines (fast but expensive equipment); VPHP suits cRABS that already have a VPHP system; NTT suits situations that seek minimal operator intervention. The choice must balance throughput, investment budget and operating complexity.

  2. How does the "no touch" design concept of the NTT system reflect the EU Annex 1 requirement to reduce human intervention?
  3. If a CDMO handles both bulk vials and RTU prefilled syringes, what different container introduction paths need to be planned in the facility design?
Q 6.5 How should equipment, accessories, ancillary items and sterile tools be transferred into a RABS?

Original Text

Recommendation

As a general concept, the transfer of all materials and equipment into the Grade B and, finally, the Grade A RABS should be a unidirectional process, separated from the path for removal of those items. Material and equipment introduced, whenever possible, should be wrapped and sterilized, when necessary.

The principles discussed below are based on the criticality of the item regarding product contact.

Direct Product Contact Parts

Direct product-contact parts (e.g., vials, stoppers, piping, single-use systems (SUSs), connectors or components), which have come into direct contact with the product, must be sterilized (1, 4). This is founded on two basic principles:

  • If the filling equipment has CIP and SIP functionality, then the parts can either remain in the RABS or parts of it can be assembled, followed by a validated SIP process, rendering the equipment and filter (where applicable) sterile. (See PDA Technical Report No. 61: Steam in Place for additional information.)
  • If the filling equipment needs to be manually assembled and has no SIP functionality, either presterilized (e.g., gamma-irradiated) single-use assemblies are used, or alternatively, if the filling equipment is sterilized by wrapping the clean items in suitable semipermeable packaging material and sterilized in a moist-heat autoclave, followed by aseptic assembly inside the RABS. If items can be directly unloaded into the cRABS (e.g., via RTP) or an aseptic transfer from the autoclave to the RABS is guaranteed (e.g., via Grade A continuity, typically a process requiring rigorous controls), different means of wrapping and/or dry-heat sterilization of the indirect product-contact items may be applied. Where presterilized, the equipment should be fully assembled (as far as possible) to minimize the need for aseptic connections.

Indirect Product Contact Parts

Indirect product-contact parts (e.g., stopper bowls, hoppers, tweezers), which have come into indirect contact with the product, are to be sterilized and handled in a similar way to direct product-contact parts (1). (See Question 6.1 for more detail.)

Machine Items that Break First Air

Machine items that break First Air (e.g., sensors, holders, brackets at the filling line or rotary table) should be avoided where possible. This must be part of the initial design considerations for a RABS. Where a First Air interruption is not avoidable, machine items should be moist-heat sterilizable. Where this is not technically possible, sporicidal disinfection and a risk assessment of each individual part should be carried out and included in the CCS (1). These parts should have both the highest-possible level of cleanability (preferably with sporicidal agents) and the lowest-possible air interruption (e.g., fewer gaps/holes, be as small as possible, ergonomically shaped). Inclusion in the EM program (e.g., surface sampling at the end of manufacture) needs to be considered.

Miscellaneous Items that Break First Air

Miscellaneous items that potentially break First Air (e.g., forceps for removing stuck stoppers) must be sterile and remain sterile during their intended use inside the RABS. Those items preferably can be sterilized by moist heat and wrapped in semi-permeable packaging, or by dry heat in stainless-steel boxes (care should be applied on appropriate closure of those boxes). Each individual unit should not contain more items than are reasonably needed for the completion of a batch or campaign. To avoid contamination of the sterile items during their intended usage, suitable holders should be designed inside the Grade A space to avoid reaching over the critical parts of the item with the RABS gloves.

Transfer of Items

Methods to transfer wrapped packages into a RABS depends on whether or not the RABS has automated surface-decontamination functionality (e.g., VPHP). If this is the case, the outside surface of packages could be surface-disinfected by the automated process after a transfer through a cleanroom area. If surface-disinfection is purely manual, either an outer-pouch should be removed if the items were sterilized double-wrapped (preferred) or a manual disinfection of the package/wrapping needs to take place when the items enter the Grade A RABS.

Non-Product Contact Equipment and Parts

Non-product-contact equipment/parts (e.g., format parts such as vial guides) are not always suitable for moist- or dry-heat sterilization. Those are parts formally not required to be sterile but must meet Grade A conditions regarding surface cleanliness when inserted into the RABS. Those parts can either be disinfected manually or with the help of automated surface-disinfection processes (e.g., VPHP or similar). When removing the parts from the RABS (if stored outside the RABS without cleaning (i.e., for product dedicated parts)) and before re-usage, the parts should be protected (e.g., by adding a protective pouch) while still within Grade A. If parts are cleaned, they should be protected during storage and before re-usage.

Removal of Items from RABS

Once RABS doors are closed, the removal process of any items from the RABS (e.g., exposed plates or package/wrapping) must not impact the unidirectional airflow protecting direct and indirect product-contact surfaces. In addition, the removal should follow a unidirectional process where the flow of material into the RABS is separated from the flow of items to be removed. For best practice in cRABS, RTPs placed at different and suitable locations may be used to facilitate the transfer of material. Processing and waste-removal flows should be considered during the equipment design and procurement phase, the placement of access ports should be recorded in the equipment User Requirements Specification, and the rationale should be documented in the Design Qualification and/or CCS.

Rationale

Transfer techniques into and out of the RABS are based on the type of material/accessory/equipment, its cleanability and disposal. Either sterilization or surface decontamination is needed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Item classification hierarchy (by product-contact criticality): this question is the core framework of Topic 6. Items are divided into four tiers according to their criticality of contact with the product:

  1. Direct Product-Contact Parts: such as tubing, filling needles and stainless steel (SUS) components -- must be sterilized
  2. Indirect Product-Contact Parts: such as stopper bowls and forceps -- must be sterilized (handled the same as direct-contact parts)
  3. Machine Items Breaking First Air: such as sensors and brackets -- should be avoided by design first; where unavoidable, they must be sterilized or sporicidally disinfected + risk assessment
  4. Non-Product Contact Parts: such as container guide rails -- need not be sterilized but must meet grade A surface cleanliness

Unidirectional Process: materials entering and leaving the RABS must follow different paths, to prevent "clean" materials from crossing with "contaminated" waste. This is a basic principle of GMP facility design.

Analogy

Think of the RABS as a highly controlled airport. Each type of "passenger" (item) must go through a different security procedure depending on its "security level" (product-contact criticality):

  • VIP (direct-contact parts): must pass the strictest security check (sterilization) and carry a special pass (sterile packaging)
  • Business traveler (indirect-contact parts): the security procedure is equally strict (also sterilized)
  • Ordinary passenger (parts that break First Air): best not to enter at all (avoid by design); if they must enter, they need the highest level of security check
  • Airport staff (non-product contact parts): do not need a VIP-level check but must wear a clean uniform (grade A surface cleanliness)

Moreover, entry and exit for everyone must be separate (unidirectional process), just as arrivals and departures at an airport use different routes.

Key Notes

The "By Design Avoid" principle: for mechanical parts that break First Air, the document's position is very clear: the preferred solution is to eliminate them at the design stage. This reflects the "Quality by Design" idea stressed in EU Annex 1: rather than controlling risk afterwards, eliminate it at the source.

The special status of sporicidal disinfection: when parts that break First Air can be neither avoided nor sterilized, a sporicidal disinfectant must be used (not an ordinary disinfectant). The reason is that the microbial control target in the grade A area is zero growth, and ordinary disinfectants may not kill microorganisms in spore form.

Design considerations for waste removal: the document requires that the waste removal process, including the placement of the RTP, be considered at the equipment design and purchasing stage. These must be recorded in the URS (user requirements specification) and the DQ (design qualification). Many problems originate from oversights at the design stage.

Practical Application

In day-to-day CDMO operations, the classification framework described in Q6.5 can be turned directly into a "Material Transfer Matrix" listing, for each class of item:

  • Classification tier (direct/indirect/breaking First Air/non-contact)
  • Sterilization/disinfection method
  • Packaging method (single/double wrapping)
  • Transfer path (RTP/hatch/open door)
  • Removal method

This matrix should become an appendix to the CCS and a core document for training new staff and preparing for inspections.

  2. A sensor bracket on a rotary table breaks First Air but cannot be moist heat sterilized. According to the guidance in this document, what steps do you need to take to control this risk?

Q 6.6 Is having an approved list of material that is permitted to enter the RABS required?

Original Text

Recommendation

An approved list of material and equipment must be established for all items to be transferred into Grade A and Grade B areas. As part of the CCS, all materials allowed to be transferred into the RABS need to be predefined, including a description of how it is transferred (1). During shut-downs or maintenance stops, where the RABS and cleanrooms are not in an operational state, additional tools, equipment, and material might be brought into the RABS, and those must be controlled through other means of the quality system (e.g., change management, maintenance work orders).

Rationale

Control steps are to be implemented for all material that is about to be transferred to the aseptic environment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Approved Material List: a formal controlled document that clearly lists all materials, equipment and tools permitted to enter the RABS. Every item must include a description of how it is transferred. This list is a component of the CCS and is the equivalent of an "admission register" for the RABS.

Change Management: during shutdown or maintenance, tools or equipment that are not on the list may need to be brought into the RABS. These exceptions must be controlled through the quality system's formal change management procedure, and cannot be decided ad hoc or introduced casually.

Analogy

The approved material list is like the "visitor whitelist" of a high-security building. Only people (materials) on the list may enter, and each person's mode of entry (through the front door, the side door, or with an escort) is clearly specified. If a visitor who is not on the list (such as a repair worker) needs to enter, a special approval procedure (change management) must be followed, rather than leaving it to the guard's discretion. This ensures that every entry is recorded and assessed, with no room for "last-minute additions".

Key Notes

"Must" not "Should": note that the document says "must be established", a mandatory requirement and not a recommendation. EU Annex 1 (2022), section 4.3, clearly requires the CCS to cover all factors that may affect sterility, including control of materials entering the sterile area. The absence of an approved material list is a common major finding in inspections.

Controls must not be relaxed during shutdown/maintenance: many sites assume that material control can be relaxed during shutdown, but the document clearly states that even in a non-operating state, additional items entering the RABS must be controlled through the quality system. This is because contamination introduced during maintenance may affect product quality after production resumes.

Section Summary

  • Item classification determines the control strategy: all items entering the RABS are divided into four tiers according to their product-contact criticality (direct contact/indirect contact/breaking First Air/non-contact), and each tier corresponds to different sterilization or disinfection requirements. The control level for indirect-contact parts is the same as for direct-contact parts.
  • Design decides the outcome (By Design): whether it is stopper transfer (RTP preferred) or eliminating parts that break First Air, the best strategy is to make the right decision at the design stage. The cost and risk of later retrofits are far higher than the up-front design effort.
  • Unidirectional flow is a basic principle: materials entering and waste leaving the RABS must follow separate paths to prevent cross-contamination. This must be recorded in the equipment URS and DQ.
  • Conditions for campaign manufacturing: indirect-contact parts may remain in place between batches, but sterility assurance over the whole campaign must be validated by APS, and the particle risk from stopper wear must be assessed.
  • The approved material list is mandatory: as part of the CCS, a formal list of all materials permitted to enter the RABS and their transfer methods must be established. Additional items during shutdown or maintenance must also be controlled through change management.
  • VPHP is not equivalent to sterilization: automated bio-decontamination can serve as a supplementary measure to reduce risk, but cannot replace formal sterilization of product-contact parts.

Q 7.1 Cleaning and Disinfection Program Planning

Original Text

Q 7.1. What are the considerations for planning, designing, and executing a restricted access barrier system (RABS) cleaning and disinfection program?

Recommendation

Before implementing a cleaning and disinfection program, a RABS cleanability assessment should be performed. This assessment should include the suitability of material surfaces to be cleaned as described in Question 1.2. In addition, cleaning accessibility in terms of ergonomics, the extent of reach required into the RABS, and areas that need cleaning tools to reach should be considered. Hard to clean mechanical spaces and surfaces in processing areas should have been eliminated by design as a basic GMP requirement (4). As disinfection programs must include the use of an approved sporicidal, the materials of construction within the RABS should be assessed to be compatible with such disinfectant agents.

*Note: Sensitive equipment (e.g., environmental monitoring equipment) must be considered during cleaning, disinfection and/or decontamination and may need to be covered at a certain cleaning step. This should be documented in the CCS.

Typically, RABS will include installed process equipment (e.g., filling lines) that are also subjected to cleaning and disinfection together with the RABS barrier. Cleanability assessments will therefore include suitability and access of process equipment surfaces for cleaning and disinfection procedures.

Cleaning is a prerequisite to effective bio-decontamination processes. Initially, at start-up and after shutdowns, a cleaning agent with a surfactant will assist with the removal of bonded layers of soiling (e.g., lubricants, grease, fatty acids from human contact). Cleaning agents are likely to leave residues that may require a follow-up wipe removal step with sterile 70% IPA for example, and such actions should be included in SOP control design (1, 6).

Agents will be validated to demonstrate the appropriate log reduction, including bacteria, spores, and viruses (as required), with the target of achieving Grade A conditions in the RABS. The process in which agents are manually applied must be qualified to ensure appropriate environmental conditions are rendered (6). RABS Grade A conditions must be established and under control before sterilized parts, including fluid path or filling needles and indirect product-contact parts (e.g., stopper bowls, guides, chutes), are introduced (1).

Bio-decontamination processes (e.g., VPHP, dry or wet process technologies) should be automated; an emphasis should be on a validated cleaning process prior to initiating the bio-decontamination process.

More automated bio-decontamination processes (e.g., VPHP, dry or wet process technologies) may be applied to oRABS (in combination with cleanroom) and cRABS. If disinfection cannot be implemented manually (i.e., considered in design), and if enhanced sporicidal efficacy and disinfection assurance is required.

In such applications, cleaning is not replaced by automated disinfection alone and remains an essential pre-step. In first application or following shutdowns cleaning, where more surface-soiling may be present, a neutral detergent with a surfactant may be appropriate, but inevitability it will leave residues, so a following residue-removal step would be required, typically with isopropanol.

A cleanability assessment, selection of cleaning and disinfectant agents, and associated methods of application should be defined in a cleaning risk assessment to cover the risk-based decision-making requirement as part of the QRM. Having a rationale in place, a qualification methodology can be developed ahead of qualification protocols and execution.

Manual Cleaning and Disinfections of RABS

RABS require cleaning and disinfection to establish and maintain Grade A conditions. Manual processes for these steps are described below.

Cleaning is the removal of soiling, residual product, and nonviable contaminants. The process is typically manual, completed with wipes that apply cleaning agents and a physical removal (i.e., collection in a wipe) of contaminants (1, 6).

Disinfection is a process of applying, via a qualified procedure, a laboratory-validated disinfectant that provides an appropriate log reduction of bioburden to specified levels. All disinfection programs for RABS should include a sporicidal agent (1, 6). Appropriate residue removal is required after cleaning using purified/sterile water (e.g., WFI) or sterile isopropanol.

There are two stages of RABS disinfection:

  1. Establishing control of the Grade A processing environment on first installation, after shutdown, and between batches and campaigns. This first step of disinfection should include a sporicidal agent, hence, sporicidal disinfection of the complete internal processing area within the RABS.
  2. Routine disinfection in production, which includes after open-barrier interventions and wiping down the barrier door before closing. The selection of the disinfectant should be based on a risk assessment and assurance that Grade A conditions are not compromised.

It is recommended that primary and secondary operators carry out these disinfection tasks related to interventions. Interventions into Grade A processing zones should be undertaken by a primary operator with the necessary localized internal-zone disinfection followed by personnel monitoring, based on an approved risk assessment, prior to closing the operations. The secondary operator should complete the internal RABS door disinfection before closing the RABS barrier.

Rationale

If RABS are cleaned and disinfected by manual application methods, the prerequisite of a cleanability assessment is paramount.

Different combinations of cleaning and disinfectant agents may be applied based on process steps and levels of soiling.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Cleanability Assessment: A systematic cleanability assessment must be performed before any cleaning and disinfection program is implemented. It is not only about whether the surface materials are suitable for cleaning; more importantly, it asks whether operators can ergonomically reach all areas inside the RABS effectively. This step is often overlooked, yet it is the foundation of the entire cleaning validation.

Sporicidal Agent: EU Annex 1 explicitly requires the RABS disinfection program to include a sporicidal agent. Bacterial spores are the hardest microbial form to kill and the greatest threat to sterility assurance. Commonly used sporicidal agents include hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and peracetic acid.

Log Reduction: The standard measure of disinfectant efficacy. For example, a 6-log reduction means reducing the microbial count from 1,000,000 to 1, a kill rate of 99.9999%.

Analogy

Imagine cleaning an operating room. You would not simply spray disinfectant and start surgery. First you do a "cleanability assessment": which corners trap dirt? Can you reach underneath the equipment? Are the cleaning tools long enough? This is the RABS Cleanability Assessment. Next, you remove visible soil with a cleaning agent (cleaning), and only then kill microorganisms with a disinfectant (disinfection). The order must never be reversed, just as you would not spray alcohol directly onto a greasy table: the grease blocks the disinfectant from reaching the surface and greatly reduces its effect.

Key Notes

Cleaning is always a prerequisite to disinfection! This section repeatedly stresses that "Cleaning is a prerequisite to effective bio-decontamination processes". Surface residues (lubricants, grease, fatty acids left by human contact) form a protective layer that reduces disinfectant efficacy.

Two-phase disinfection strategy:

  • Phase 1 (establishing control): At first installation, after shutdown, and at batch/process changeover, the entire RABS interior must be disinfected with a sporicidal agent
  • Phase 2 (routine maintenance): Wiping after open-door interventions during production and before closing the door; disinfectant selection is based on risk assessment

Grade A must be established first: Before sterilized parts (such as filling needles and stopper bowls) are introduced into the RABS, Grade A conditions must already be confirmed as established and under control.

Operator Roles

The document specifically describes the division of roles between the Primary Operator and the Secondary Operator:

  • Primary operator: Responsible for interventions in the Grade A area, and for local disinfection and personnel monitoring after the intervention
  • Secondary operator: Responsible for disinfecting the inside of the RABS door and for closing the door

This two-person, mutual-oversight design ensures the completeness and traceability of the disinfection process.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, when you take over a new RABS line or prepare to introduce a new product, planning the cleaning and disinfection program is a critical step that cannot be skipped. Common failures include:

  • Equipment designed without considering cleaning accessibility, leaving dead corners that cannot be cleaned manually at all
  • A sporicidal agent that is incompatible with RABS interior materials, causing corrosion or surface damage
  • Cleaning agent residues that are not effectively removed, undermining the subsequent disinfection

The Cleanability Assessment should therefore be built into the Design Review at the RABS procurement stage.

  1. Why must cleaning be performed before disinfection? What happens if the order is reversed?
  2. Under what circumstances is automated bio-decontamination (such as VPHP) more suitable for a RABS than manual disinfection?
  3. Why do sensitive instruments (such as EM equipment) need special protection during cleaning and disinfection? In which document should this be recorded?
Q 7.2 Consistent Execution of Cleaning, Bio-Decontamination, and Disinfection

Original Text

Q 7.2. What are the current options for ensuring consistent execution of cleaning, bio-decontamination, disinfection, and sporicidal disinfection of a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Disinfectant agents will require qualification or validation to provide evidence of efficacy on surfaces for a defined contact time, typically referred to as "wet contact time" (2). Different disinfectant qualification methodologies can be applied based on the best combination of international standards and guidance.

Aseptic-process filling would require Grade A particulate and microbial cleanliness levels inside the RABS, with cleaning and disinfection considered as the surface-contamination control measure (1). The outside of the RABS barrier would require following the cleanroom grading level (in filling Grade B).

Disinfectant agents are validated for efficacy in laboratory studies, including material coupon surface-disinfection studies, with subsequent qualification of the application method and procedure. EM is used to assess the effectiveness of the cleaning and disinfection program (1, 6). In-field efficacy challenge studies are not typically implemented for manual disinfection applied to RABS in contrast to automated VPHP process technologies, bio-decontamination, and alternative sporicidal-agent processes that require biological-indicator (BI) inactivation challenge studies (4).

The bio-decontamination processes of VPHP process technologies have the potential of achieving 6-log reduction when combined with the required qualification of the automated gaseous-disinfection system and method of application with associated control parameters (6). Once the bio-decontamination-equipment integration is qualified, a process of cycle development should be applied, followed by an efficacy-performance qualification (PQ) with a BI reference challenge of Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores on stainless coupons. VPHP BI's are described in PDA Technical Report No. 51 Biological Indicators for Gas and Vapor-Phase Decontamination Processes: Specification, Manufacture, Control and Use.

Enzymatic indicators (EIs) are increasingly playing a role in cycle development to improve efficiency (time savings) and assessment of location-to-location efficacy. However, accepting the initial BI/EI correlation studies is required, and the final cycle qualification currently requires BI challenge studies.

The outcome of manual disinfection, following the application of a qualified agent according to a qualified SOP, is assessed by the EM program. The EM program assesses the effectiveness of the qualified disinfection and bio-decontamination process. RABS cleaning and disinfection qualification should align with ISO 14644-1 requirements for cleanrooms and associated controlled environments and with EU Annex 1 and FDA guidances on manufacturing sterile products by aseptic processing for qualification before conducting APS qualification in readiness for production activities.

Open-door interventions into a RABS barrier during aseptic processing, a rare occurrence, requires additional cleaning and disinfection of the open barrier door, including the inside of the barrier door, before it is closed to reestablish Grade A conditions of the complete barrier. Protective airflow and procedure control should result in the sterilized parts that enter during setup remaining sterile without compromise. Disinfection applied to sterile surfaces could potentially compromise sterility and therefore should be avoided, instead focusing on the procedural control and protective airflow that prevents compromise to the sterile surfaces in the first place.

Rationale

Primarily, RABS are manually cleaned and disinfected using qualified agents and a qualified procedure of application of these qualified agents executed by trained operators. EM, in combination with other microbial challenges (e.g., gowning, material transfers, and environmental control systems), is used to monitor the outcome (effectiveness).

Some of the enhanced attributes in contamination control that isolators provide, are sometimes applied to RABS to enhance the assurance of sterility. In particular, cRABS and oRABS (in combination with cleanroom) with VPHP can align elements of isolators and RABS technology.

The combined cleanroom and oRABS bio-decontamination, using an automated process, can support more assured aseptic hold times and fast changeovers to reestablish the required conditions for aseptic manufacturing. Such processes can be applied, with BI results (negative for growth) defined as the marker, to put the line back in operation at risk, rather than waiting for EM results.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Wet Contact Time: The minimum time a disinfectant must remain wet on the surface to achieve its claimed antimicrobial efficacy. This does not mean "spray it on and wait for it to dry"; the surface must stay wet for the entire contact time. When wiping a large RABS interior manually, this is one of the greatest operational challenges.

Biological Indicator (BI): A known quantity of highly resistant microbial spores (usually Geobacillus stearothermophilus) used to challenge the efficacy of a disinfection/decontamination process. The BI is the "gold standard" for VPHP cycle validation.

Enzymatic Indicator (EI): A newer technology that uses changes in enzyme activity to rapidly assess decontamination efficacy. The biggest advantage of the EI is speed (minutes versus the 7-day incubation of a BI), which can greatly accelerate the cycle development phase. However, final validation still requires a BI challenge.

Analogy

Manual disinfection of a RABS is like mopping the floors of a whole house: you can do it very well, but the result depends on whether the mop is clean, whether the floor was swept first, whether you really reached every corner, and whether the floor stayed wet long enough. Automated VPHP decontamination is like setting off a pest-control fogger throughout the house: the gas spreads evenly into every corner, so the result is more consistent and reliable. But even with a fogger, you still have to sweep the floor first (cleaning remains the prerequisite!).

Key Notes

Validation differences, manual vs. automated:

  • Manual disinfection: Laboratory validation (coupon study) + application method validation + evaluation of EM results. No in-field BI challenge is required.
  • Automated VPHP: Laboratory validation + equipment integration validation + cycle development + PQ (including BI challenge). A 6-log reduction must be demonstrated.

Key warning - do not disinfect sterile surfaces! The document states explicitly that "Disinfection applied to sterile surfaces could potentially compromise sterility". The act of wiping itself introduces a contamination risk, so it is safer to rely on airflow protection and procedural controls.

The Trend Toward Convergence of RABS and Isolators

The Rationale mentions an important industry trend: cRABS and oRABS are borrowing VPHP technology from isolators. This "hybrid" approach brings two practical advantages:

  • More reliable aseptic hold time: Automated decontamination provides higher assurance than manual disinfection
  • Faster changeover: BI results (sterile) can serve as the basis for restarting the line, without waiting for EM results (typically 5-7 days)

For a CDMO, this means higher line utilization and faster delivery.

Formula and Calculation

Meaning of a 6-log reduction:

Initial spore count: 10^6 = 1,000,000 CFU
After 6-log reduction: 10^0 = 1 CFU
Kill rate = 99.9999%

Practical meaning:
- If the BI is inoculated with 10^6 G. stearothermophilus spores
- After the VPHP cycle, the BI culture result must be "no growth"
- This is the acceptance criterion for passing PQ

Practical Application

BI vs. EI in practice: When developing a VPHP cycle at a CDMO, the EI can greatly shorten development time. A typical cycle development flow is:

  • Use the EI to rapidly screen different combinations of VPHP parameters (H2O2 concentration, temperature, exposure time)
  • Once the best parameters are found, run a BI/EI correlation study
  • The final PQ is still judged by a BI challenge (G. stearothermophilus, 10^6 spores)

The document also notes that a BI result (negative) can be used as the basis for an "at risk" restart of the line, without waiting for EM results. In a multi-product CDMO this is an important capacity optimization strategy.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does manual disinfection not need an in-field BI challenge, while VPHP does? What is the fundamental difference in validation logic between the two?
  2. Why should sterile surfaces not be disinfected by wiping? Which core principle would this violate?
  3. In what type of CDMO scenario is a "hybrid" approach of cRABS with VPHP most valuable? Analyze from the perspectives of capacity and sterility assurance.
  4. Can EI results replace the BI as the final acceptance criterion for PQ? Why or why not?
Q 7.3 Qualification and Validation of Cleaning, Disinfection, and Decontamination

Original Text

Q 7.3. What considerations are required for qualification of the restricted access barrier system (RABS) cleaning, bio-decontamination, disinfection, and sporicidal disinfection process, and validation of the bio-decontamination and decontamination process?

Recommendation

The qualification of the RABS cleaning and disinfection process is critical to ensuring a Grade A environment within the RABS. The planning of the qualification should begin as early as possible prior to implementation. A key output of the design phase of a RABS installation is a quality risk assessment detailing the RABS physical attributes and usage scenarios that may have an impact on product quality. This risk assessment then feeds into a validation plan of which cleaning and disinfection validation is a major component.

There are different facets to the RABS cleaning and disinfection (bio-decontamination) and decontamination validation process:

  • Establishing Grade A conditions for aseptic manufacturing
  • Applying cleaning validation for drug-product residue removal as a cross-contamination control measure

Decontamination may be applied as a cross-contamination control measure and/or a measure to manage operator exposure to toxic or biologically hazardous products. Where aseptic-containment is required to process toxic or biohazard products, it may be more appropriate to apply isolator barrier technology because of the inherent limitations of containment measures (e.g., air-overspill to the surrounding environment) in RABS.

RABS Cleaning Process

The qualification of the RABS cleaning process should provide confidence of the:

  • Removal of any residue (chemical, including lubricants) or debris that would detrimentally impact the effectiveness of the disinfecting agent to be used. The target should be visibly clean (1).
  • Reduction of bioburden before the disinfection process step, as determined by the end-user.

RABS Disinfection Process

The qualification and validation of the RABS disinfection process should provide confidence that the disinfection procedure demonstrates its effectiveness in reducing any microbiological contamination within the RABS to an acceptable level. The disinfection process should include:

  1. Use of a sporicidal agent, possibly in rotation with other disinfectant agents that together provide broad spectrum efficacy (7). The selection and frequency of use should be based on EM data (quantitative/qualitative), trends, cleanroom materials, and the efficacy of the disinfectant procedures (2).
Note: Rotation of the disinfectants is not considered due to increasing microbial resistance but because different disinfectants have different modes of action, and their combination can increase the spectrum of efficacy. Rotation may also reduce occurrence of material damage.
  1. Use of sterile disinfectant agents for Grade A and Grade B areas.
  2. A target log reduction for bacteria, spores, and viruses (if applicable) (6, 20).
  3. A method of application that assures surface coverage and wet contact time.
  4. A disinfection waste management procedure dealing with wipes, residual agents, and disinfection tools.
  5. Any associated aseptic RABS hold-time qualification.
  6. Environmental control/EMPQ should be qualified and established before implementation of an APS; confidence that the cleaning and disinfection process meets this objective of environmental control is critical to GMP compliance (4).

RABS Decontamination Process

The qualification and validation of a RABS decontamination process applied post-production for product-residue removal as a cross-contamination control measure requires cleaning-validation studies. The following are points to consider:

  • Cleaning methods may be manual (wiping) or more automated CIP systems (e.g., spray balls, spray wands). In both cases, the target is to remove residual products from surfaces.
  • Cleaning validation includes surface-swabbing studies and chemical analysis to confirm removal of product residuals to target levels considered as safe exposure to operators and/or not at risk of cross-contaminating other products or subsequent batches.
  • Where more automated CIP systems apply, control system development will be required with subsequent qualification.

WFI may be used as an agent for product-residual removal for treatment off-line:

  • Chemical agents that denature products in RABS may need a follow-up residue removal step.
  • If CIP systems are applied, sloping surfaces and "zero" dead-leg valves may be required to mitigate collected water that may exacerbate microbial contamination or proliferation.
Note: Disinfectant efficacy studies or validation studies should be conducted as well to demonstrate that the disinfectants used on surfaces in manufacturing areas, laboratories, and other facility areas are effective in the inactivation or removal of such microorganisms as bacteria, fungi (yeast and molds), and bacterial spores. (See PDA Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities for further details.)

Rationale

The primary purpose of a RABS is to provide people-operator and process-product-barrier separation so that the risk of contaminating sterile product is mitigated to acceptable levels. As such, it is important to determine the most appropriate bio-decontamination method for the RABS. The qualification and validation of the bio-decontamination methods should consider the cleaning agents, the cleaning application processes, and the associated processes and equipment risks. These requirements and processes should be documented and proceduralized for repeatability and monitored to ensure continuous compliance.

Strong process understanding is the best basis for determining RABS validation requirements. Robust monitoring and aseptic manufacturing processes will add to the confidence that the manufacturing process is under a state of control. Decisions should be made based on the facilities process, control structure, and process understanding. Based on those decisions, validation programs should be initiated and followed. Decisions on the frequency of revalidation should be included in the bio-decontamination validation program.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Validation Plan: RABS cleaning and disinfection validation is not a standalone activity; it is an important part of overall RABS installation qualification. It derives from the quality risk assessment (QRA) performed at the design stage and is closely interlinked with equipment qualification, environmental monitoring qualification and APS qualification.

Cross-contamination Control: Beyond establishing a sterile environment, RABS cleaning validation has a second important purpose: removing residues of the previous product to prevent cross-contamination. This is especially critical in CDMO multi-product, shared-line manufacturing.

Visibly Clean: The minimum standard for cleaning validation. It sounds simple, but when working through gloves in a RABS, confirming that all surfaces are visibly clean is a real practical challenge.

Analogy

The three-tier RABS validation framework is like maintaining a building:

  • Cleaning = daily sweeping: Removes dust, rubbish and visible soil. The standard is "looks clean"
  • Disinfection = periodic sanitizing: Uses a biocide to kill microorganisms. The goal is a specified level of microbial reduction
  • Decontamination = deep cleaning/chemical removal: When tenants change (product changeover), ensures every trace of the previous tenant is removed so the new tenant is not exposed to residues

The three have different purposes, different methods and different validation requirements, but all must be done properly.

Key Notes

The real reason for rotating disinfectants: Many people wrongly assume that disinfectants are rotated to prevent microbial resistance. The document clarifies this common misunderstanding. The reasons for rotation are:

  • Different disinfectants have different modes of action, and using them in combination broadens the antimicrobial spectrum
  • Rotation can also reduce damage to RABS materials (long-term use of a single strong disinfectant may corrode some materials)

Seven Elements of Disinfection Validation

This section lists seven aspects that RABS disinfection validation must cover, which makes a very practical checklist:

  1. Use of sporicidal agents (including the rotation strategy)
  2. Sterile disinfectants in Grade A/B areas
  3. Target log reduction (bacteria, spores, viruses)
  4. Methods to ensure surface coverage and wet contact time
  5. Procedures for managing disinfection waste
  6. Aseptic hold time validation
  7. EM/EMPQ must be completed before the APS

Limitations for toxic/biohazardous products

The document specifically points out that when highly toxic or biohazardous products are handled, a RABS has inherent containment limitations (such as air spillage to the surrounding environment), and an isolator is the more suitable technology. This has important implications for a CDMO's equipment selection strategy: if the customer portfolio includes ADCs, highly potent drugs or active biologics, this factor must be considered when choosing between a RABS and an isolator.

Key Points of Decontamination Validation

The purpose of decontamination validation differs from that of disinfection validation: it targets the removal of product residues and is the core of cross-contamination control:

  • Methods: Manual wiping or automated CIP systems (spray balls, spray wands)
  • Validation approach: Surface swab sampling (swab study) + chemical analysis to confirm that residue levels are below the safe exposure limit
  • CIP design note: Sloped surfaces and zero dead-leg valves, to prevent standing water from supporting microbial growth

WFI can be used to remove product residues. If a chemical that denatures proteins is used, an additional residue removal step is required.

Practical Application

CDMO multi-product changeover scenario: Suppose your RABS line has just completed filling a monoclonal antibody product, and the next batch is insulin. The validation data you need include:

  • Cleaning validation: After surface swabbing, residues of the previous product are below the PDE/ADE limit
  • Disinfection validation: Confirmation that the Grade A environment has been re-established
  • Visual equipment inspection: Confirmation that all surfaces meet the "visibly clean" standard

If your RABS is paired with VPHP, you can run an automated decontamination cycle after cleaning and use the BI result as the release basis, greatly shortening changeover time.

Practice Questions

  1. What is the main purpose of rotating disinfectants? How does it relate to "resistance"?
  2. Why is the applicability of a RABS to highly toxic/biohazardous products limited? What is the advantage of an isolator in this respect?
  3. In RABS decontamination validation, what is the purpose of the swab study? Which risk does it verify?
  4. Why do CIP systems need sloped surfaces and zero dead-leg valves? What risk does standing water bring?

Section Summary

  • Cleaning is the prerequisite for everything: Whether disinfection is manual or automated VPHP, cleaning is always the required first step before disinfection/decontamination and cannot be replaced or skipped
  • Two-phase disinfection strategy: Phase 1 establishes Grade A control (full disinfection including a sporicidal agent); Phase 2 is routine maintenance (local disinfection after interventions)
  • Manual and VPHP have different validation paths: Manual relies on laboratory validation + EM monitoring; VPHP requires full cycle development + a BI-challenge PQ (6-log reduction)
  • The EI speeds up development but cannot replace the BI: Enzymatic indicators can make cycle development more efficient, but the final validation decision still requires a biological indicator challenge
  • Validation is a systems effort: Cleaning/disinfection validation derives from the QRA at the design stage and forms a complete chain with EM validation and APS validation; all of it must be in place before the APS
  • Decontamination targets cross-contamination: Unlike disinfection (which targets microorganisms), decontamination aims to remove residues of the previous batch and is a key control measure for multi-product shared lines
  • RABS containment limitation: For highly toxic or biohazardous products, isolator technology should be considered because of the risk of air spillage from a RABS

Q 8.1 - APS in RABS: Special Considerations

Original Text

Q 8.1. What are the special considerations for aseptic process simulation (APS) in a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

A risk-based analysis and approach taken for APS are recommended for all aseptic processing technologies, including RABS systems. By design, the APS should imitate routine manufacturing conditions and should ensure that all critical steps are challenged. A RABS relies on the surrounding cleanroom environment to maintain Grade A conditions for sterile processing. To maintain Grade A, the RABS routinely includes cleaning and disinfection processes, in addition to sterilization processes for processing accessories and equipment. By design, the RABS should only provide for open-door intervention during setup and, by exception, during operations. The cleanroom qualification, operator training, cleaning, and disinfection programs should all be qualified.

There are several key considerations that need to be established in support of APS, and they should include, but are not limited to:

  • Cleanroom and clean air equipment, such as UDAF units, must be qualified according to the required characteristics of the environment.
  • The RABS must be qualified to meet all requirements for aseptic processing including HEPA-filter integrity-testing, pressure differential across the filters, air velocity testing, and AVS. AVS should confirm unidirectional airflow that sweeps over and away from exposed products during processing, without increased risk of contamination through turbulent airflow. Airflow visualization qualification and monitoring should be applied at open-door interventions as a prerequisite to all APS designs to ensure environmental-control conditions are qualified in readiness for an APS.
    Note: APS cannot be used to justify poor design of equipment and processes.
  • Operator training and qualification extending to Grade A and Grade B aseptic gowning and aseptic behavior training must be complete. Operators undertaking RABS cleaning and disinfection programs should be qualified and operations should be implemented procedurally.
  • A list of authorized inherent and corrective interventions should be designed, defined, and appropriately assessed and documented in the CCS to minimize the risk of contamination. Open-door interventions should be a rare and exceptional occurrence. When required, the need to take representative sterility samples should be considered, and the intervention should be effectively demonstrated in an APS.
  • Qualified methods are in place for the transfer of materials that enter the cleanroom, and subsequently the RABS, that do not compromise the cleanroom-surrounding environment or the RABS Grade A processing zone.
  • Risk assessments support the establishment of a comprehensive EM program, which should be based on detailed knowledge of the process, facility, and equipment. Sampling locations, sampling frequency and type of EM monitoring (e.g., active air, passive, contact plate) should be defined based on risk assessment output.

The use of a "piggyback" APS can be considered as part of the overall APS design. A piggyback APS is where an APS run is undertaken at the end of a routine single or campaign filling process and can be used in support of validating an extended run or campaign length/duration. There are specific limitations associated with a piggyback APS which, by design, does not provide for an appropriate simulation of activities and variables that are only performed for the first lot of a campaign, for example, setup activities. Consideration also needs to be given to potential interferences of residual product with the growth-promoting properties of the media used in the APS. Consequently, a piggyback APS alone will not fulfill the validation requirements to demonstrate an effective APS program. Rather, its use can be considered as part of an overall effective APS program, which also must include an APS strategy that covers setup and other potential worst-case scenarios.

The scheduling of piggyback APS in conjunction with traditional APS should be supported by a formal risk assessment, for which the limitations of the piggyback approach and its use are considered.

Rationale

The planning of open- and closed-door interventions and simulation of worst-case criteria should be based on risk management principles. The same criteria for the design of an APS as stated in PDA Technical Report No. 22 (Revised 2011): Process Simulation for Aseptically Filled Products are also appropriate for all RABS.

After establishing Grade A conditions inside a RABS, the barrier technology provides a physical and aerodynamic barrier to contamination ingress to Grade A when the barrier is closed. The barrier is open at setup where presterilized materials are transferred and are installed. At this time, both Grade A conditions and the sterilized parts are at an increased risk for contamination and, thus, can compromise sterility.

If rare open-door interventions take place during aseptic manufacturing, there is also a risk escalation that needs mitigation by design, qualifications, and procedural controls, and is subject to additional qualification in an APS. Such in-process significant interventions require consideration for sterility samples to be taken and should be included in the APS.

Simulation of the production activities is a necessity for designing the APS studies.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Aseptic Process Simulation (APS): Replaces the actual drug product with growth medium to simulate the entire aseptic filling process, verifying that the system can maintain sterility assurance under both normal and worst-case conditions. In essence, an APS is a comprehensive capability test of the whole aseptic manufacturing system.

Risk-Based Analysis: The APS design is not arbitrary; it is driven by quality risk management (QRM). The steps with the highest contamination risk are identified first, and the simulation program is designed accordingly. This also echoes the core requirement of EU Annex 1 for a Contamination Control Strategy (CCS).

Piggyback APS: An APS performed immediately after a routine production batch. Its advantage is that it can verify the ability to support extended production time or campaign length. Its major limitation is that it cannot simulate setup activities (because setup occurs only at the start of a campaign), so it cannot be used alone and must be combined with a traditional APS to form a complete validation program.

Analogy

Imagine you are a pilot and the APS is a flight simulator test. In the simulator you must reproduce every situation you might meet: normal takeoff and landing, bad weather, engine failure and so on. The same is true of a RABS APS: you must simulate not only normal filling but also open-door interventions (the equivalent of emergency landing procedures), setup (the equivalent of pre-flight checks) and every possible worst-case scenario.

A piggyback APS is like running extra simulator tests after a normal flight has ended. You can test the endurance of a "long flight", but you cannot verify that the "complete pre-flight check procedure" is correct, because you are already in the air.

Key Notes

Six prerequisites for an APS: Before an APS is performed, all of the following must already be validated/qualified:

  1. Cleanroom qualification - the UDAF equipment meets the environmental requirements
  2. RABS qualification - HEPA integrity, differential pressure, air velocity and AVS all pass
  3. Personnel training - Grade A/B aseptic gowning and aseptic behavior training completed
  4. Intervention list - inherent/corrective interventions defined and incorporated into the CCS
  5. Material transfer methods - validated and not compromising the Grade A environment
  6. EM program - a complete environmental monitoring program established on the basis of risk assessment

Note the warning in the source text: "APS cannot be used to justify poor design of equipment and processes." The APS verifies the effectiveness of a good design; it is not a way to excuse a poor one.

Regulatory Link

This section refers to PDA TR No. 22 (Revised 2011): Process Simulation for Aseptically Filled Products, the core guidance document for APS design. In addition, Chapter 9 of EU Annex 1 (2022) sets out detailed APS requirements, including minimum fill volume, frequency, and the requirement to simulate worst-case conditions.

Practical Application

Suppose you are the validation manager at a CDMO, and a customer asks for a 3-day campaign on a RABS filling line. Your APS program design must consider:

  • At least one traditional APS, covering the full setup and simulation of closed-door interventions
  • Consider adding a piggyback APS, run after actual production ends, to verify the ability to support extended production time
  • The combined use of the two types of APS must be supported by a formal risk assessment document

  1. Why does a RABS need more attention to open-door intervention simulation in the APS than an isolator does?
  2. If a company performs only a piggyback APS and no traditional APS, what deficiency might be raised during an audit?
  3. Why is AVS listed as one of the prerequisites for the APS? Explain from the perspective of airflow and contamination control.
Q 8.2 - APS for Multiple Shifts and Campaign Production

Original Text

Q 8.2. What are the special considerations of the aseptic process simulation (APS) for multiple shifts and multiple-day campaign production in the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

The duration of the APS should be based on risk assessment and sufficient to adequately challenge the aseptic process. The duration should be long enough to allow simulation of the predetermined interventions and activities and to fill the required units.

Where there are no risk-based time-related effects and where longer duration does not pose a risk to the routine production process, there is no requirement that the APS should be equal to or longer than the maximum production duration.

The following points need to be considered when creating an APS for campaign manufacturing:

  • Setup
  • Number and duration of interventions based on risk management principles to ensure that the APS is representative of the routine manufacturing process and qualified interventions (type, frequency, and duration)
  • Indirect product-contact parts remain installed for the duration for requalification of the campaign and are subject to an aseptic hold time (the duration may need to be shortened based on the risk assessment evaluation)
  • Replacement or leaving in place of direct product-contact parts (i.e., product filling needles), should be representative of the routine manufacturing process exposure time
  • RABS gloves remain installed for the duration of the campaign
  • Cleaning/disinfection practices occurring inside the RABS during routine campaign manufacturing should be simulated
  • Certain circumstances when performing an APS related to a production hold and/or interruptions
  • Taking of sterility samples after significant critical interventions, if justified

The approach to APS with a "piggyback" qualification is not considered a best practice as setup is missing in the final APS and not representative of the full campaign.

Rationale

Contamination of an aseptic process is primarily an outcome of events rather than time. Therefore, the duration of the APS should be sufficient to assess the performance of those activities identified in the risk assessment as having the potential to introduce contamination.

The APS is not sensitive enough to uncover the flaws, weaknesses, or failures in the integrity of the RABS, the environmental quality, or the ability of the personnel to perform activities over time.

The APS should challenge and confirm the effectiveness of these contamination control measures. This means that the maintenance of environmental conditions should be assured and assessed by the RABS system design and EM. Good process design and well-controlled activities required between the batches within a campaign (e.g., fill needles or glove replacement), RABS gloves ergonomics, training and qualification of personnel, assessment of human factors, and scheduling of breaks and rest periods for operators are better tools for controlling the complexity of RABS campaign operations than extended APS runs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Campaign Manufacturing: A mode in which multiple batches are produced consecutively without full cleaning and re-setup. In a RABS, this means that some parts (such as parts with indirect product contact) remain installed throughout the campaign.

"Events Rather Than Time": This is the most important idea in this section. Contamination is primarily an outcome of "events" (such as interventions, material transfers and personnel activities), not simply of a long duration. The APS duration therefore does not need to equal or exceed the longest production time; the key is to adequately simulate all the "events" that could introduce contamination.

Direct vs. Indirect Product-Contact Parts：

  • Direct (direct product contact parts): Such as filling needles - these may need to be changed during a campaign, and the APS should simulate the change
  • Indirect (indirect product contact parts): Such as guide rails and the stopper bowl - these remain installed throughout the campaign and are subject to the aseptic hold time limit

Analogy

Designing an APS for campaign production is like planning a safety test for a long road trip. You do not need to drive the full 3,000 km to prove the car is safe, but you do need to simulate all the key events on the journey: refueling stops (the equivalent of operations between batches), changing a tire (the equivalent of changing filling needles), and managing fatigue after long driving (the equivalent of operator shift and break arrangements). What matters is not how many kilometers you drive, but whether you have tested every "event" that could go wrong.

Key Notes

Checklist of items a campaign APS must simulate:

  1. Setup phase - including the initial open-door activities
  2. Interventions - type, frequency and duration must all be representative
  3. Indirect contact parts - simulate the installed state and hold time over the full campaign period
  4. Direct contact parts - simulate whether they are changed or retained (such as filling needles)
  5. RABS gloves - remain installed throughout the campaign
  6. Cleaning/disinfection - simulate routine cleaning and disinfection between batches
  7. Production pauses/interruptions - simulate planned or unplanned stoppages
  8. Aseptic sampling - the sampling procedure after major interventions

Common Misconception

Many companies mistakenly believe that the APS must run as long as actual production. The source text states explicitly: "there is no requirement that the APS should be equal to or longer than the maximum production duration". The purpose of the APS is to verify "control of events", not "endurance over time". Time-related controls (such as environmental maintenance, glove integrity and operator fatigue) should be assured by other tools (such as EM, equipment qualification and human factors assessment).

Practical Application

A CDMO's RABS filling line runs a 5-day campaign (2 shifts per day), producing 10 batches in total. APS design considerations:

  • The APS does not need to last 5 days, but it must simulate all critical events
  • Include the full setup plus at least a simulation of the cleaning and disinfection changeover "between batches"
  • If filling needles are changed every 2 days, the APS must include this change
  • Simulate the operator shift handover procedure
  • Take a sterility sample at appropriate points (such as after major interventions)

Practice Questions

  1. The source text says "contamination is primarily an outcome of events rather than time". What does this tell you about designing the APS duration?
  2. Why is a piggyback APS "not considered best practice" in campaign mode? What exactly does it lack?
  3. If a RABS glove tears and has to be replaced during a campaign, how should this event be handled in the APS?
Q 8.3 - Worst-Case Conditions in RABS APS

Original Text

Q 8.3. What are the special considerations for determining worst-case conditions in a restricted access barrier system (RABS) aseptic process simulation (APS)?

Recommendation

Based on the features of the operation, the worst-case scenarios chosen for inclusion in an APS should be predefined. The best way to determine the suitable worst-case scenarios is to evaluate the APS, taking into account the pertinent elements of the process and the aseptic process risk assessment. The worst-case condition should be considered within the expected operational boundary. Worst-case does not mean creation of artificial conditions or environments that exceed allowed operating conditions and can force a system failure.

"Note: APS is not used to validate bad practices."

A few examples of worst-case conditions include, but are not limited to:

  • Open-door interventions (if required) in addition to the setup
  • Closed-door interventions (inherent/corrective)

The following parameters generally have a lower impact on the product-contamination risk, since the Grade A RABS interior environment with exposed product/critical surfaces are separated from the lower-classified environment and operators:

  • Hold time of sterile materials (e.g., filtration and filling equipment, filtered media solution, sterile garments/goggles)
  • Filling duration
  • Simulation of the maximum or minimum number of personnel
  • Speed of the filling machine (i.e., slowest speed for largest container considering the maximum-duration vial remains open online and the highest speed for the smallest container considering difficulties in handling)

Any intervention required via opening the door shall be considered along with the frequency and duration for the simulation in the APS.

Interventions that do not require handling via an open door but need manual adjustment via RABS gloves and tools shall be simulated in a similar way.

A risk-based approach shall be followed in order to determine the frequency, duration, and criticality of the intervention.

Rationale

Open-door interventions represent the highest risk for product contamination and therefore should be the focus of the predefined worst-case conditions in the APS. In addition, the design, barrier properties, and the closed-door interventions of RABS might provide other worst-case conditions, which should be considered in the design of APS. Companies should conduct thoughtful risk assessments to help determine which conditions and configurations represent the most useful challenge to the process and how to most effectively include them in the APS.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Defining worst case is like designing the scenario for a fire drill. You simulate the "most difficult but reasonable escape situation", for example a broken elevator or a corridor blocked by smoke. But you do not simulate "the whole building collapsing instantly", because that is beyond reasonable expectation.

Likewise, the worst case in a RABS APS should simulate an "open-door intervention" (the highest-risk event), but should not artificially create an extreme condition that could never occur in normal operation (for example, opening all doors and shutting off all airflow at the same time).

Key Notes

A unique advantage of RABS - some traditional worst-case parameters have less impact:

Unlike conventional open aseptic filling, the physical barrier of a RABS separates the product/critical surfaces from operators and the lower-grade environment. As a result, the impact of the following parameters is markedly reduced in a RABS:

  • Hold time - sterilized materials are protected inside the barrier
  • Filling duration - environmental control is stable while the barrier is closed
  • Number of personnel - operators are outside the barrier and not directly exposed to the product
  • Filling speed - still to be considered (slowest/fastest), but with less impact than in conventional systems

This is an important risk-reduction advantage of a RABS over conventional aseptic filling, and it is the commercial value of choosing RABS technology.

Two Warnings

The source text repeatedly emphasizes two key principles:

  1. "APS is not used to validate bad practices" - the APS is not a tool for endorsing poor practice
  2. "Worst-case does not mean creation of artificial conditions...that can force a system failure" - worst case does not mean deliberately causing the system to collapse

These two statements set clear boundaries for APS design: the APS verifies whether, "within a reasonable operating range, the system can maintain sterility assurance".

  1. Why is open-door intervention the "highest-risk" worst-case condition in a RABS APS? Explain from the perspective of barrier integrity.
  2. A company simulates "slowest filling speed + maximum number of personnel + longest hold time" together in its APS design. Based on this section, is this design reasonable? Why?
  3. How is "APS is not used to validate bad practices" applied in practice? Give a specific example.

Q 8.4 - Setup and Assembly Best Practices

Original Text

Q 8.4. What is the best practice when performing the setup and assembly of a restricted access barrier system (RABS) filling line?

Recommendation

There are two setup conditions for RABS: Initially, with an open barrier door, and then, readiness for production operations with the barrier door closed where full EM is implemented and critical tasks involving sterilized parts are completed, for example, positioning of filling needles (if single-use). As much as possible, set-up should be performed with the doors closed so careful design and understanding of the equipment is essential.

The open-door set-up process presents the highest risk of contamination when transferring in or assembling sterilized parts or components.

Based on risk knowledge and the role First Air takes in preventing contamination ingress into the Grade A zone, operators need to position themselves so protective airflow passes over body parts inserted into the RABS (such as hands and sleeves) and into the surrounding cleanroom. Positioning of materials ready for transfer should also consider protective airflow exiting the RABS Grade A zone and from the overhead or over-open-door UDAF. AVS should demonstrate the appropriate airflow from the interior of the RABS to the surrounding area.

Materials and parts should be presented for transfer at working position, typically on carts.

A secondary operator could support positioning parts and materials provided they do not compromise the protective airflow.

Good aseptic technique (e.g., transfer of inner bag at the RABS interface by double-bag methods) is required on open-barrier-door entry, and the operator's reach should be limited to the required task without touching other surfaces, critical or otherwise. Practices of hand disinfection prior to open-door interventions should be based on the contact time recommended and qualified practices. (See PDA TR-62 for additional information.)

During the intervention, EM sampling locations in the proximity of the intervention should be subject to continuous EM (i.e., viable and nonviable). The surrounding cleanroom should also be monitored at the door opening/operator interface. (See Topic 5.)

Installation of sterilized RABS gloves after open-door setup is commonly done as the final procedure. (See Topic 9.)

Before and at the time barrier doors are closed, the inside of the barrier door should be disinfected. The RABS gloves should be drawn back so only the RABS glove port entry interface should require disinfection (in some cases, covers may be inserted to prevent RABS glove contamination at open-door interventions).

With the barrier door closed, full EM should be implemented and should be ready for monitoring throughout the production-readiness setup.

Installation setup of all critical parts requires good aseptic technique, possibly with the use of tools (e.g., tweezers, tongs) and throughout the procedure. First Air protection should not be compromised.

Rationale

As interventions are such a high-risk activity, best practice is needed in all cases to mitigate risks to an acceptable level.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

RABS setup is like pre-operative preparation in an operating room:

  • Phase 1 (door open) = moving sterilized instruments from the transport cart into the operating room. The room door is open and the risk is highest, so the work must be done quickly and with discipline. Nurses must not stand where they block the laminar airflow.
  • Phase 2 (door closed) = once the operating room door is closed, the instruments are carefully arranged and finally checked. The environment is now controlled, but sterile gloves are still required for the work.
  • First Air = like the laminar flow hood on the operating room ceiling: clean air must flow from above, down over the instrument table and then out, and personnel must not stand "upstream" of the airflow.

Key Notes

Best-practice checklist for open-door setup:

  1. Operator positioning - protective airflow passes over the hands/sleeves and then flows to the cleanroom (First Air must not be blocked)
  2. Material placement - materials to be transferred are placed on a cart at working height
  3. Secondary operator - may assist with positioning but must not disturb the protective airflow
  4. Aseptic technique - use the double-bag method for transfer; limit actions to necessary tasks
  5. Hand disinfection - before an open-door intervention, disinfect hands according to a qualified procedure (see PDA TR-62)
  6. Continuous EM - continuous environmental monitoring (viable + nonviable) at adjacent locations during the intervention
  7. Glove installation - sterilized RABS gloves are installed as the last step of the open-door setup
  8. Door interior disinfection - disinfect the inside of the door before/while closing it

Design Principle

The source text stresses: "As much as possible, set-up should be performed with the doors closed". This means the RABS design should let operators complete most of the setup through the glove ports or RTPs, minimizing operations that require opening the door. This design philosophy should be stated explicitly in the equipment purchase specification (URS).

For a CDMO, when purchasing a RABS, "how much of the setup can be done with the doors closed" should be an important selection criterion. Equipment that allows more setup with the doors closed means lower contamination risk and a higher level of sterility assurance.

Practical Application

You are writing the setup SOP for a new RABS filling line. The key items to define are:

  • Open-door operations list: list all operations that must be done with the door open, and define the maximum allowed time for each
  • Operator position diagram: mark the positions of the primary and secondary operators, ensuring they do not block the direction of the protective airflow
  • EM sampling points: place continuous monitoring points around the open-door intervention area
  • Door-closing checklist: door interior disinfection completed, glove installation confirmed, full EM started
  • AVS reference: attach the airflow direction diagrams from the AVS qualification report as operator training material

Practice Questions

  1. Why is the installation of RABS gloves the "last step" of open-door setup? What problems arise if gloves are installed first and other materials are transferred in afterwards?
  2. The source text says the AVS should demonstrate "appropriate airflow from inside the RABS to the surrounding area". What should the ideal airflow direction be during open-door setup, and why?
  3. If you were an equipment supplier, how would you design a RABS to maximize the proportion of setup operations that can be completed with the doors closed?

Key Concepts

Two Setup Conditions: RABS setup has two distinct phases with completely different risk levels:

First Air: Clean air that has left the HEPA filter and has not yet contacted any object or person. During open-door setup, operators must ensure First Air flows from inside the RABS outward, passing over the operator's hands and sleeves before leaving. This is the first line of defense against external contamination entering the Grade A area.

Double-Bag Method: A technique in which the outer packaging of a sterilized item is removed at the RABS interface and only the inner (sterile) packaging is passed into the Grade A area. This is the standard practice for maintaining aseptic transfer.

Section Summary

  • APS prerequisites: Cleanroom qualification, RABS qualification (HEPA/differential pressure/AVS), personnel training, intervention list, material transfer methods and EM program - all six are indispensable
  • Event-driven principle: Contamination is the result of "events", not of "time" - the APS duration need not equal the longest production time; the key is to simulate all events that could introduce contamination
  • Worst-case boundary: Worst-case conditions must lie within a reasonable operating range; the APS cannot be used to validate poor design or poor operating practices
  • Unique RABS advantage: Because of the physical barrier, traditional worst-case parameters such as hold time, filling duration, number of personnel and filling speed have less impact on a RABS
  • Open-door is the highest risk: Open-door interventions (including setup) are the highest-risk operations in a RABS and should be the focus of APS worst-case design
  • Two-phase setup: The open-door phase (highest risk) and the closed-door phase (controlled environment) have very different operating requirements; equipment design should allow as much setup as possible to be completed with the doors closed
  • Limitation of piggyback APS: It can supplement the overall APS program but cannot be used alone, because it cannot simulate the setup phase

Q 9.1 Aseptic Techniques for Closed- and Open-Door Interventions

Original Text

Q 9.1. Is it necessary to use the same aseptic techniques and practices when performing closed- and open-door interventions in the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

To ensure product sterility, operators performing aseptic operations must adhere to aseptic techniques for both closed- and open-door interventions. As part of a company's comprehensive CCS, a risk assessment must be developed to ensure all intervention steps are appropriately defined, including required mitigation actions, for qualified interventions. All open-door interventions are to be minimized and should have a qualified maximum time limit.

When assessing the risk of an intervention, the following should be considered:

  • Overall contamination risk to product (e.g., open units, exposed components), based on personnel location, movement, and/or RABS glove proximity
  • Maintenance of First Air
  • Including the accessibility of the area to perform the intervention
  • Supported with AVS
  • Consideration of line clearance (e.g., removal of compromised units)
  • Disinfection
  • Area where the intervention takes place needs to be accessible for disinfection, if required (Disinfection prior to EM should be avoided.)
  • Monitoring the intervention
  • Viable (e.g., surface(s), operator(s),) and total particulates
  • Start and end time of intervention (i.e., duration)
  • Before resuming operation, appropriate evaluation and line clearances performed (e.g., disinfection, verified removal of compromised units)

Note: See Topic 4 for clarification on recommended aseptic techniques.

Furthermore, while a RABS with closed doors provides an improved separation between the operator and the product, it decreases the likelihood of nonsterile product; therefore, the risk is still present and should be appropriately controlled.

Rationale

While a RABS with closed doors provides enhanced protection of the product between the operator and extraneous contamination sources (e.g., operator, environment), proper aseptic technique is still required to aid in the overall control of the product. A risk assessment should be documented outlining the necessary controls required to maintain product sterility.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Closed-door vs. Open-door Intervention: In a RABS, even with the door closed, interventions performed through the gloves still carry a contamination risk. PDA's position is very clear: the same aseptic technique must be followed whether the door is open or closed.

Qualified Maximum Time Limit: Every open-door intervention must have a validated maximum allowable time. This is not an arbitrary number; it is supported by risk assessment, AVS and EM data.

Analogy

Think of aseptic operations in an operating room: even when a surgeon wears gloves (similar to a closed-door intervention), strict aseptic technique must still be followed, and vigilance must not relax because "the gloves protect me". When an instrument pack has to be opened (similar to an open-door intervention), the risk is higher and more controls are needed. The logic of a RABS is exactly the same - the barrier reduces risk but does not eliminate it.

Key Notes

Alignment with EU Annex 1: This recommendation directly echoes the core spirit of EU Annex 1, that the CCS must cover all interventions. The document specifically stresses that "Disinfection prior to EM should be avoided", because if you disinfect before EM sampling, you can no longer genuinely assess the contamination risk introduced by the intervention.

Importance of time records: The start and end times of every intervention must be recorded. This is not only a GDocP requirement but also a key data source when investigating deviations afterwards.

Practice Questions

Q 9.2 Qualification and Approval of Interventions

Original Text

Q 9.2. How should interventions be qualified and approved for use in routine production?

Recommendation

All interventions should be included in a documented risk assessment associated with a comprehensive CCS. This documented risk assessment should consider the following risk factors:

  • Duration of the intervention (in minutes)
  • Complexity of the intervention (number of steps)
  • Proximity of the intervention to sterile components (equipment, product)
  • Exposure of personnel

Additional factors may be considered when assessing the risk associated with execution of an intervention. The assessment must consider the use of sterile equipment and tools (e.g., forceps) to minimize impact on the RABS environment when performing an intervention.

All qualified interventions should have the airflow visualization recorded and documented to ensure that the impact of performing the interventions are thoroughly understood. This includes inherent and corrective interventions that can be performed through RABS gloves and open-door activities. First Air principles shall be included in this airflow visualization and must not be ignored during execution of interventions. Based on mechanical or physical limitations, if proper First Air principles cannot be followed, it must be documented within the risk assessment, and proper mitigations (e.g., material discard) must be established. It is recommended that, as much as possible, mitigations be automated, ensuring proper execution each time the intervention is performed. The inclusion of qualified interventions to be performed during routine production should be included in the firm's APS program.

Rationale

Following the process recommended above would ensure the inclusion of QRM principles through a documented risk assessment to be included in a comprehensive CCS. The proper design, followed by risk analysis and airflow visualization, will provide the appropriate understanding of the intervention to approve its use, during routine production, and for execution in a firm's APS program. The execution of interventions, to be performed during routine production, are to be included in a firm's APS program.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Intervention Qualification: Every intervention must go through a complete qualification process before it is permitted in routine production. PDA identifies four major risk factors:

  • Duration: The longer, the higher the risk
  • Complexity: The more steps, the higher the chance of error
  • Proximity: The closer to sterile components, the greater the contamination risk
  • Exposure: The degree of operator involvement in the intervention

AVS (Airflow Visualization Study): Every qualified intervention must have an AVS record. This is not a "do it once" activity; the effect of each intervention on First Air must be fully understood and documented.

Analogy

Think of intervention qualification as a pilot's preflight checklist. A pilot does not skip the checks because "I have flown many times". Likewise, every intervention needs a complete "flight plan": you must know which risk areas it passes through (proximity), how long it takes (duration), how many steps it has (complexity), and who takes part (exposure). Only interventions that pass this review are "cleared for takeoff" in routine production.

Key Notes

Automated mitigations: PDA specifically recommends that mitigations be automated wherever possible. For example, when the First Air principle cannot be followed because of physical constraints, automatically discarding the affected product is more reliable than relying on manual judgment. This fits the spirit of "reducing the human factor" in EU Annex 1.

Inclusion in the APS: All qualified interventions must be included in the APS plan and simulated. This means the APS is not just "filling media"; it must also include realistic simulation of all approved interventions.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Suppose your RABS filling line needs to change the filling needle at product changeover (a common inherent intervention). Your qualification file should include: (1) AVS video showing the airflow pattern during the needle change; (2) a risk assessment matrix recording the maximum duration (for example, 3 minutes); (3) a distance analysis to the nearest sterile surface; (4) the operator SOP and training records. Only when these documents are complete can the intervention be added to the "approved intervention list".

  2. Why does PDA stress that "mitigations be automated"? What are the risks of manual mitigation?
Q 9.3 Interventions that Disrupt First Air

Original Text

Q 9.3. Should interventions that disrupt First Air, as defined in EU Annex 1, be permitted?

Recommendation

The placement of RABS glove positions and the aseptic operation should be designed to avoid the disruption of unidirectional airflow above or in the proximity of exposed sterile product or critical surfaces. Interventions should be designed in a manner that prevents the disruption of First Air to critical surfaces or exposed product. If the intervention is occurring above a critical surface, sterile tools and/or equipment must be utilized for execution of the intervention.

All interventions should be designed following appropriate aseptic techniques as recommended in EU Annex I: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products, FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing-Current Good Manufacturing Practice, or PDA PTC-1. Intervention design must consider equipment limitations and the placement of required equipment (e.g., EM) within the RABS. The use of sterile equipment and tools (e.g., forceps) must be well thought out to not add further risks to intervention execution.

Rationale

The process described above would ensure proper design of aseptic interventions and inclusion in the QRM program through a documented risk assessment to be included in a comprehensive CCS. All interventions shall be designed to meet First Air principles and include proper mitigation if those principles are impacted due to mechanical or physical limitations. The proper design followed by risk analysis and airflow visualization will provide the appropriate understanding of the intervention to approve for use, during routine production, and for execution in a firm's APS program.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

First Air: As defined in EU Annex 1, First Air is unidirectional air that comes directly from the HEPA filter and has not yet contacted any surface. It is the cleanest air in the Grade A environment. No intervention should block the path of First Air to exposed product or critical surfaces.

Design-First Principle: The core of PDA's recommendation is "design": glove port locations, operator movement paths and tool placement should all be designed to preserve First Air at the design stage. Remediation after the fact is never as good as design up front.

Analogy

First Air is like an "air curtain" cascading down from above like a waterfall. The exposed sterile product sits under the protection of this curtain. When you reach in (through a RABS glove), it is like placing a rock in the waterfall: you create a turbulent zone below the rock. You must therefore make sure your "rock" (the hand) never ends up directly above the items that need protection, or use sterilized tools to extend your reach.

Key Notes

Alignment with three regulatory frameworks: PDA lists three documents as the basis for intervention design: EU Annex 1, the FDA Guidance and PDA PTC-1. This means that, wherever your product is marketed, intervention design must meet the requirements of all three. For a CDMO this is essential, because the same line may serve customers in different regulatory markets.

Dual considerations for sterile tools: Sterile tools (such as forceps) can prevent gloves from touching critical surfaces directly, but the design, placement and use of the tools themselves must not add risk. The more tools, the more complex the operation, and the higher the chance of dropping or knocking something.

Q 9.4 RABS Glove Contact with Critical Surfaces

Original Text

Q 9.4. Should contact between restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves and critical surfaces during setup or operations be permitted?

Recommendation

RABS gloves should not be permitted to contact critical surfaces during setup or during operation.

Portions of equipment would only be considered as a "critical surface" when coming into direct contact with product or components. The handle and exterior surface of the stopper bowl are not "critical surfaces" and may be contacted by RABS or operator gloves during setup.

Aseptic technique should be applied throughout the manufacturing process by trained and qualified operators. (See Question 9.7 for more information on glove installation.) Written procedures are to be established to avoid the risks of RABS gloves contacting direct or indirect surfaces and, if contact occurs, what action(s) are to be taken.

Rationale

While the CCS for RABS gloves should maintain proper aseptic conditions throughout the campaign, there is an inherent risk to the product when touching critical surfaces, as the environment and glove materials are not sterile. Proper controls (e.g., process flow, equipment design) and use of sterile tools should be in place to mitigate the need to touch critical surfaces.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Critical Surface: PDA offers a very pragmatic definition: only the parts of the equipment that directly contact the product or components are considered critical surfaces, for example the inner wall of a filling needle or the contact surfaces of stoppers. The handle and outer surfaces of the stopper bowl are not critical surfaces and may be touched by gloves.

A clear "no contact" position: PDA is firm here: RABS gloves should not be allowed to contact critical surfaces. The reason is clear: RABS gloves and the environment they are in are not sterile (unlike the inside of an isolator).

Analogy

This is like food safety rules in a restaurant: a server may touch the rim of a plate (non-critical surface) but must never touch the food itself or the plate surface in direct contact with the food (critical surface). If accidental contact occurs, that dish must be discarded or reprocessed. RABS gloves follow exactly the same logic: you may handle the outside of the equipment but must not touch surfaces in direct contact with the product.

Key Notes

Key difference between RABS and isolators: In an isolator, the gloves are in a Grade A environment and have been bio-decontaminated with VPHP, so the glove surface is regarded as "controlled". RABS gloves, although sterilized before installation, are exposed to risk from the Grade B environment during use. This is one of the most fundamental operational differences between a RABS and an isolator.

Written procedures required: The document explicitly requires written procedures covering: (1) how to avoid contact; (2) what action to take if contact occurs inadvertently. This means training alone is not enough; an SOP is required.

Q 9.5 Post Open-Door Intervention Actions

Original Text

Q 9.5. What should be performed after open-door interventions?

Recommendation

RABS should be designed and operated with doors closed during aseptic processing; however, the process specificities may require identification, qualification, and approval of interventions performed with the doors open. Interventions performed with open RABS doors (e.g., manual setup) are to be minimized, and mitigation controls must be identified and implemented.

When a RABS door must be opened, those interventions need to be assessed for risk of contamination ingress. These interventions must be part of the approved list and must incorporate additional control measures based on the risk of microbiological contamination that the intervention may produce.

Such additional control measures may include, but are not limited to:

  • Additional disinfection and/or sporicidal disinfection of nonproduct-contact surfaces, including the interior of doors and glove ports exposed or touched during the activity.
    Note: The firm is responsible for documenting and defining a validated agent as part of its CCS.
  • Identification of the appropriate units that are to be manually removed (i.e., discarded) as part of the intervention.
    Note: The firm is responsible for validating all automatic unit removal performed by the filling machine.
  • Collection of sterility samples may be taken, if warranted in the CCS
  • Door access with recorded intervention alarms and an effective means of documenting the following:
    • Start and end time of intervention
    • Description of the qualified intervention
    • Participants
    • Documentation of units removed (i.e., discarded)
    • Documentation of required EM
    Note: The firm is responsible for determining the appropriate risk-based monitoring related to the open-door intervention.
  • Monitoring of operator gloves and additional risk-based locations (e.g., forearms, chest, head, etc.), immediately after completion of the open-door intervention. (See Topic 2 for additional EM recommendations.)

Rationale

Personnel are a significant source of microbiological contamination and, therefore, pose a risk to product sterility. Efforts to separate personnel from sterile products and product-contact surfaces reduce the risk of microbiological contamination. Barrier systems, such as RABS, provide a physical separation between personnel and the external environment and exposed product or product-contact surfaces, which helps prevent microbiological contamination of the sterile product by personnel and the environment.

In the case of open-door interventions, there is no longer a physical separation; therefore, additional control measures are required, as described in this section.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Approved Intervention List: This is one of the core documents of RABS operation. Every operation that might require opening the door must be identified, assessed and qualified in advance, and placed on this list. No open-door intervention that is not on the list should be performed.

Five additional controls:

  • Disinfection/sporicidal treatment: For non-product-contact surfaces
  • Product discard: Identify and remove units that may have been affected
  • Aseptic sample collection: Decided according to CCS requirements
  • Documentation: A complete intervention record (time, description, participants, number discarded, EM)
  • Operator monitoring: Real-time EM of gloves and other at-risk body sites

Analogy

An open-door intervention is like opening an "emergency exit door" in a cleanroom. Each time the door is opened, you need to carry out a complete set of "aftermath procedures": check whether anything foreign has entered (EM monitoring), clean the affected area (disinfection), remove items that may have been compromised (discard product), record the event (documentation), and confirm that the "main door" has been closed safely. This process cannot be omitted, just as when an airport security checkpoint is breached, the area must be cleared and re-screened.

Key Notes

Intervention Alarm System: PDA recommends that door access be equipped with "recorded intervention alarms". This means the opening and closing of the RABS door should be linked to an automated system that triggers an alarm and records automatically as soon as the door is opened. This is a key design feature for reducing human error.

Scope of operator EM monitoring: Not only the gloves, but also the forearms, chest, head and other sites. This reflects the strict EU Annex 1 requirements for EM monitoring of Grade A/B operators.

Practical Application

CDMO discard strategy: For a CDMO, the amount of product discarded after each open-door intervention directly affects batch yield and cost. It is advisable to discuss an automatic discard function (auto-reject) with the equipment supplier at the equipment design stage and to verify its accuracy during validation. Automatic discard is more reliable than manual judgment and is also easier to defend in audits.

Q 9.6 How Should Interventions Be Performed?

Original Text

Q 9.6. How should interventions be performed?

Recommendation

Prior to performing an intervention, all product and empty containers are to be cleared, as applicable, then the filling process is stopped. The operator(s) will then don the RABS glove in a slow and deliberate manner, which should immediately be followed by a qualified visual inspection process. The operator(s) then executes the intervention, as described in an approved procedure, following good aseptic practices and the use of a sterilized tool, where appropriate, based upon the intervention.

Upon completion of the intervention, the operator(s) returns the sterile tool, where appropriate, to its designated stand or location within the Grade A RABS. Prior to retracting the hands from the RABS glove, the operator(s) will perform a second qualified visual inspection of the glove. The operator(s) then ensures the glove is correctly placed to minimize stress and ensures the glove does not activate any light curtain, present. The filler can then be reactivated to continue the manufacturing process. When open-door RABS intervention is required, operators should work as a team, with a primary operator performing the task under Grade A environment and a secondary operator assisting the primary operator in the intervention and to ensure proper segregation.

This collaboration between primary and secondary operator(s) during Grade A open-door intervention aims to prevent any contact between the primary operator's gloves and any surfaces exposed to the surrounding environment (i.e., RABS door opening, material transfer). Intrusion of the primary operator and the duration of the intervention into the Grade A RABS should be minimized.

Rationale

Each intervention needs to be performed using a sterile tool to minimize the likelihood of the RABS glove contacting critical equipment and/or damaging the glove. Minimizing the impact to sterile components, equipment, and sterile product, along with the RABS environment, is critical when executing an intervention. Each firm needs to have a robust discard strategy, based on approved airflow visualization, to discard product and open primary packaging components that have potentially been impacted when an intervention is required. It is important to ensure the operator(s) understands and follows good aseptic practices, as they can be a significant source of contamination. The integrity of the RABS gloves is an important part of the comprehensive CCS to minimize hazard ingress; therefore, the RABS gloves need to be inspected prior to and after every use. This process must be proceduralized, and each operator is to be qualified to perform the inspection process. Each intervention needs to be performed using a sterile tool to minimize the likelihood of the RABS glove contacting critical equipment and/or damage the glove.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Standard Intervention Workflow: PDA describes a very clear 7-step workflow:

  1. Line Clearance: Remove all product and empty containers
  2. Stop Filler: Stop the filling process
  3. Don Glove + Visual Inspection: Don slowly and deliberately, and inspect integrity immediately
  4. Execute Intervention: Follow the SOP, using sterile tools
  5. Return Tool: Return it to its designated location
  6. 2nd Visual Inspection: Inspect again before withdrawing from the glove
  7. Position Glove + Restart: Ensure the light curtain is not triggered

Key Notes

Light Curtain: A safety/monitoring device commonly used in RABS to detect whether a glove or object has entered a defined area. After the operation, the glove must be positioned correctly to avoid falsely triggering the light curtain, which would cause unnecessary alarms or stoppages.

Two-person operation (Primary + Secondary Operator): Open-door interventions must be carried out as a team. The primary operator performs the work inside the Grade A area; the secondary operator assists from outside and ensures that the primary operator's gloves do not touch surfaces exposed to the external environment. This is similar to the "surgeon + scrub nurse" teamwork in an operating room.

Analogy

The intervention workflow is like the "scrub-in" procedure before surgery. You do not rush into the operating room and start cutting. You must first scrub (don gloves + inspect), confirm the surgical field has been cleared (line clearance), operate with sterilized instruments (sterile tools), check everything one by one when finished (second inspection), and finally withdraw safely. Every step exists for a reason, and skipping any one of them increases infection risk.

  2. Why does PDA require a visual glove inspection both before and after the intervention? What might be missed if the inspection is done only after the intervention?
  3. On your line, are the roles and responsibilities of the primary operator and secondary operator clearly defined in writing?
Q 9.7 RABS Glove Installation Methods

Original Text

Q 9.7. How and when should restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves be installed?

Recommendation

RABS gloves are sterilized before installation in the RABS barrier wall (glove port), and subsequently, they must be wrapped and sterilized within an integral bag for protection against contamination through transfer within the Grade B area. Prior to installation, RABS gloves are required to be sterile. Typically, this is accomplished via autoclave or gamma-irradiation. There are multiple ways to install RABS gloves and, while this document outlines three industry approaches, other options may be considered based on the company's equipment and risk assessment.

Note: In all the approaches listed below, there should be no contact of the RABS glove to any Grade A surface with the exception of sterile tools.

Approach 1: RABS Door Closed

  1. RABS door is closed.
  2. Glove port inside is decontaminated by spraying and/or wiping with validated agents (e.g., sporicidals).
  3. Wrapped glove is introduced through the glove port; the glove remains covered.
    Note: The wrapped RABS glove does not leave the UDAF (e.g., within cart) or UDAF zone of protection using a UDAF cart, and it is transferred into the RABS under the UDAF zone of protection.
  4. Upper part of the glove bag is opened using sterile tools (outside of RABS).
  5. RABS glove is connected by the attachment of the o-ring on the outside of the RABS door.
  6. Glove wrapping is aseptically removed inside the RABS.
  7. Glove remains inverted (i.e., turned "inside-out").
  8. Glove wrapping is then taken out of the RABS by using the opening below the RABS doors for oRABS. For cRABS another method must be used.

The choice should be based on production scale, replacement frequency and total cost of ownership (TCO).

Q 9.8 Disinfection of RABS Gloves During Operations

Original Text

Q 9.8. Are restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves treated with sporicidal agents and/or otherwise disinfected during operations?

Recommendation

The use of disinfection, including sporicidal disinfection, should be defined within a CCS. In general, there is no need to conduct a sporicidal disinfection of RABS glove(s) during aseptic operations.

Note: Disinfection is only required if the RABS gloves come in contact with nonsterilized surfaces.

RABS gloves should be installed, operated, and maintained in a manner in which they will not become contaminated by exposure to the zone of protection outside the RABS (Grade B environment), by contact with personnel during door opening, or by contact with nonsterilized surfaces within the RABS.

This requires that the RABS gloves be inverted (turned "inside out") to the RABS outside when leaving the RABS gloves with the operator hands (See Figure 1.4-1 for visual example of inversion). Leaving the RABS gloves inserted and hanging down inside the RABS should be prevented as they may be contaminated by direct contact with nonsterilized surfaces.

When there are door openings as part of setup or as a qualified intervention during a filling operation, the RABS gloves should also be inverted and should not be directly exposed to the zone of protection outside the RABS (Grade B environment).

RABS glove disinfection or sporicidal disinfection is only required if the RABS glove has come in contact with potentially contaminated surfaces, which are locations below the working level, for example, for interventions where an equipment-part must be fixed. The disinfection or sporicidal disinfection should be performed by wiping; spraying disinfectants inside the Grade A RABS during filling should be prevented.

Rationale

RABS gloves are sterilized before installation and should be installed in a manner that prevents contamination. (See Question 9.7.) Proper aseptic techniques should always be used when working with RABS gloves during closed-door conditions. If the door is not opened, the RABS glove is not exposed to the Grade B environment, and there is no contact with nonsterilized surfaces. There is no requirement for an additional wiping, via disinfection or sporicidal disinfection, during operations. If a contamination risk results as described above (e.g., RABS glove exposure in Grade B, contact with non-sterilized surfaces, etc.) mitigation should follow the recommendations from Questions 9.4 and 9.5.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Core principle of the glove disinfection strategy: PDA's position is very clear: under normal operation, RABS gloves do not need sporicidal disinfection. Disinfection is needed only when the glove has touched a non-sterile surface.

The key role of inversion: When not in use, gloves must be inverted (turned out to the outside of the RABS) so that the sterile surface faces inward and is protected. If a glove is left hanging naturally inside the RABS, its sterile surface may touch non-sterile equipment surfaces and become contaminated.

Wiping vs. Spraying: Even when disinfection is needed, it must be done by wiping, not spraying. Spraying disinfectant in a Grade A environment generates droplets and particles, which increases contamination risk.

Analogy

The principle of glove inversion is like turning a rubber glove inside out as you take it off. As you pull your hand out, the glove naturally rolls back from the wrist, and the side that was outside (the side that touched things) ends up inside, enclosed. RABS gloves follow the same logic: when not in use, invert them so the "clean side" is protected inside and the "possibly contaminated side" is exposed outside. When they are needed again, turn them back, and the clean side faces outward once more.

Key Notes

The risk of "Below Working Level": PDA specifically points out that when an intervention involves equipment repair below the working surface (for example, repairing parts at the bottom of the filling equipment), the risk of the glove touching non-sterile surfaces is higher. This is because the area below the working surface is generally not directly protected by First Air, and may have contaminants such as lubricant and dust.

Cross-references to Q9.4 and Q9.5: If a glove contamination risk event does occur, PDA directs operators to follow the recommendations in Q9.4 (rules on contact with critical surfaces) and Q9.5 (actions after open-door interventions) to carry out mitigation. This reflects the internal logical consistency of the PDA document.

Practical Application

Glove management strategy during a campaign: In a long campaign (possibly lasting several days), glove management is an ongoing challenge. It is advisable to set up a glove status tracking system that records, for each pair of gloves, the installation time, the number of uses, whether it has ever touched a non-sterile surface, and whether it has ever been disinfected. These data not only support decisions during the campaign (for example, when to replace gloves) but are also a key input to batch release review.

Section Summary

  • Consistency of aseptic technique (Q9.1): The same aseptic technique must be followed for both closed-door and open-door interventions. The RABS barrier reduces risk but does not eliminate it. All open-door interventions need a qualified maximum time limit.
  • Intervention qualification (Q9.2): Every intervention must undergo risk assessment (duration, complexity, proximity, personnel exposure) and be documented with AVS records. All qualified interventions must be included in the APS plan. Mitigations should be automated wherever possible.
  • First Air protection (Q9.3): Intervention design must avoid interrupting First Air over exposed product or critical surfaces. Where physical constraints make this unavoidable, a documented risk assessment and mitigations are required. The design must meet EU Annex 1, the FDA Guidance and PDA PTC-1 at the same time.
  • Critical surface contact restriction (Q9.4): RABS gloves must not contact critical surfaces (surfaces in direct contact with product or components). Written procedures must define how to avoid contact and what to do after contact. Using sterile tools is a key mitigation strategy.
  • Post open-door controls (Q9.5): After an open-door intervention, five additional controls apply: disinfection, product discard, aseptic sampling, complete documentation (including the alarm system) and operator EM monitoring. All open-door interventions must be on the approved list.
  • Intervention workflow (Q9.6): A standardized seven-step workflow: line clearance → stop filler → don glove + visual inspection → execute intervention → return tool → second visual inspection → position glove + restart. Open-door interventions must use a primary + secondary operator two-person system.
  • Three glove installation methods (Q9.7): Approach 1 (closed door, introduced through the glove port), Approach 2 (open door, installed under UDAF protection), Approach 3 (closed door, inverted glove introduced directly). The shared goal of all methods: the glove is never exposed to Grade B and never touches Grade A surfaces. Installation after the last open-door step of setup is recommended.
  • Glove disinfection strategy (Q9.8): Sporicidal disinfection is not required under normal operation. Disinfection is needed only when the glove touches a non-sterile surface. Gloves must be inverted when not in use. Disinfection is by wiping; spraying disinfectant inside Grade A is prohibited.
