1.0 Introduction

Environmental monitoring (EM) is used to evaluate the effectiveness of contamination controls used in the manufacture of pharmaceutical products. EM programs need to be designed and implemented based on the needs of the specific facility and processes involved. PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2014): Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program (TR 13) focuses on the environmental requirements for sterile product manufacturing. This addendum will provide elements to consider when designing a risk-based EM program to support the manufacture of low bioburden products using low bioburden processes.

Regulations and guidance are readily available for EM requirements supporting sterile, nonsterile, and advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMP) manufacturing processes. Guidance for EM supporting the manufacture of low bioburden products is very limited. Where it is, the documents often cite resources intended for aseptic processes and then recommend establishing a risk-based approach to designing an EM program to support low bioburden processes. This is largely due to the fact that most manufacturing operations where low bioburden control is required combine elements of both sterile and nonsterile processing. However, no specific guidance identifies the elements to which these sterile and nonsterile guidelines should be applied.

This document provides a means of determining the critical aspects, assessing the associated risks, and designing the appropriate EM program for areas where bioburden control of manufacturing processes is required. It can be applied across many types of manufacturing operations used to produce pharmaceutical products including biologics, small molecules, ATMPs, and others. This document should be viewed as technical guidance and is not intended to establish any voluntary or mandatory standards.

1.1 Purpose

This addendum complements the existing PDA TR-13 and is not meant to change or modify its content. The addendum provides recommendations for the development of an environmental control and monitoring program for classified areas and the associated GMP processes used for manufacturing operations requiring bioburden control in the biotechnology and pharmaceutical industries. The content includes a review of regulatory requirements and the development of risk assessments based on the criticality and complexity of processes, as well as industry examples of these risk-based approaches.

Risk assessment tools such as Failure Mode Effects Analysis (FMEA), Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP), or hybrid approaches can be used for these evaluations. This addendum presents a hybrid approach that is intended to help analyze operational risk in order to develop appropriate levels of EM required to monitor operations. When implementing these tools in the design of any EM program, the resulting program should fit the intended purpose. It should not unnecessarily impede the operation or introduce additional risk to the process.

1.2 Scope

TR-13-2 focuses on environmental controls and monitoring programs for classified areas where manufacturing processes are required to ensure a low bioburden level of the material being produced. Although a comprehensive EM program addresses both viable and nonviable aspects of the facility, this addendum concentrates primarily on the viable aspects as these parameters have a greater potential to impact product and process quality related to bioburden. This document is not intended to address EM programs for traditional aseptic or sterile manufacturing, which are covered in TR-13, nor does it address traditional nonsterile dosage forms.

Interpretation and Application

💡 Concept | TR13 vs. TR13-2: clearly positioned, no overlap

TR13-2 is a supplementary addendum to TR13. The relationship between the two is as follows:

  • TR13 (2014/2022): addresses sterile pharmaceutical products, such as injectables, lyophilized products and ophthalmic preparations
  • TR13-2 (2020): addresses processes that require bioburden control but are not sterile, such as biological drug substance (mAb drug substance), cell therapy intermediates and upstream culture fluids for recombinant proteins

TR13-2 does not replace TR13. When designing an EM program, you may need to refer to both documents, depending on the nature of the process.

⚠️ Key point | The gap in regulatory guidance

Current regulations give clear guidance for three categories of process:

  • Sterile products: EU GMP Annex 1 and FDA aseptic processing guidance
  • Non-sterile dosage forms: ICH Q6A and national pharmacopoeias
  • ATMPs: EU ATMP Regulation (1394/2007)

However, low bioburden products have no dedicated guidance. Existing documents either apply the standards for sterile products (too stringent) or merely recommend "performing a risk assessment" without a concrete operating framework. This is exactly why TR13-2 exists.

🔄 Analogy | The regulatory dilemma of the in-between zone

Imagine a highway (the rules for sterile processes) and a country lane (the rules for non-sterile processes). A low bioburden process is like a provincial road in between. The existing map (the regulations) shows only the highway and the country lane, so drivers must assess road conditions themselves (risk assessment) to decide which speed limit (monitoring requirements) applies.

The purpose of TR13-2 is to draw the first systematic map of this provincial road.

💡 Concept | 1.1 Purpose: FMEA vs. HACCP vs. a hybrid approach

TR13-2 recommends a hybrid risk assessment approach:

  • FMEA: starts from the process steps and evaluates the failure modes, effects and likelihood of occurrence of each step in turn; suited to equipment and operations analysis
  • HACCP: identifies critical control points (CCPs), sets limits and establishes monitoring procedures; it originated in the food industry and has a solid track record in biological product processes
  • Hybrid approach: combines the strengths of both and is adapted flexibly to the characteristics of the process

Important reminder: once designed, the EM program should not hinder normal operations or introduce additional risk. This is the core design principle of TR13-2.

⚠️ Key point | 1.2 Scope: centered on viable monitoring

TR13-2 explicitly focuses on viable (microbial) monitoring rather than comprehensive environmental monitoring:

  • Microbial contamination has a far greater impact on the quality of biological drug substance than nonviable particles do
  • The process itself often supports microbial growth (culture media, cell culture fluids), which makes microbial control more challenging
  • Nonviable particle monitoring must still be performed, but it is not the focus of this document

2.0 Glossary

The terms and definitions contained in this glossary are specific to the content of this technical report. Although some of these definitions will match those in PDA TR-13 and/or other PDA technical reports, others have been intentionally altered or developed to address the specific content and intent of this addendum and the attributes unique to facilities used to manufacture low bioburden products.

Adverse Trend

EM data (including microbial and particulate) that is consistently increasing in one direction. Adverse trends are system- or area-specific, should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, and should consider factors such as contamination recovery rate, excursion rate, and individual results.

Airborne Particulate Count
(Total Particulate Count)

The total number of particles of a specific size per unit volume of air.

Airborne Viable Particulate Count
(Total Airborne Microbial Count)

The recovered number of colony-forming units per unit volume of air.

Bioburden

The total number of microorganisms per unit of material prior to sterilization.

Bioburden Reduction Step

A process utilizing a method that has been demonstrated to lower the bioburden level in a given sample.

Classified Area

An area typically classified according to ISO standards.

Cleaning

Chemical or physical means used to remove soil and/or microorganisms from surfaces.

Cleanroom

A room designed, maintained, and controlled to prevent particle and microbiological contamination of a drug product. A cleanroom is assigned and reproducibly meets an appropriate air cleanliness classification.

Critical Operations Monitoring

Monitoring performed throughout the duration of a process using methods designed to monitor the environmental quality of the process and to capture all interventions, transient events, and any system deterioration. The design and degree of the monitoring performed should be based on the risk to the process or product being monitored (e.g., closed vs. open systems, use of biological safety cabinet, unidirectional flow hood, or other containment systems).

Disinfection

The chemical or physical inactivation of a bioburden on inanimate surfaces. Typically, this requires a minimum three-log (3-log) reduction of vegetative microorganisms and a two-log (2-log) reduction of bacterial spores to be achieved in validation.

Environmental Control Parameters

Conditions and corresponding measurements as associated with facilities and equipment used in the control of a manufacturing area that may impact the identity, strength, quality, or purity of a product. Among such parameters are airflow rates and patterns, pressure differentials, materials and personnel flow, temperature, and relative humidity, as well as nonviable and viable particulates.

Environmental Monitoring — Four Subtypes
Dynamic (Operational) Monitoring

The condition where production operations are actively occurring as defined by the individual firm. Personnel may or may not be present at this time.

Routine Monitoring — Frequency-Based / Predetermined / Nonbatch-related

Monitoring of a facility at a predetermined frequency, as defined by an approved procedure, to provide an overall assessment of the state of control of the facility.

Batch-related — In-Process or During-Process Monitoring

Monitoring performed during predetermined steps of the production process, as defined by an approved batch record and/or approved procedures or protocols. This can be performed either in the presence or absence of the product.

Continuous

Batch-related monitoring, as defined by an approved batch record, to ensure that a state of control is maintained during processing and that any aberrant events are detected. Frequency of monitoring is determined by a risk assessment.

Closed System

A process system with equipment designed and operated such that the product is not exposed to the room environment at any point in the process. For most biological operations, the system or fluid pathway must remain free of extraneous contamination. Examples include, but are not limited to, a cell culture bioreactor or cell therapy bag assembly.

Functionally Closed System

A process system that may be routinely opened (e.g., to install a filter or make a connection), but is returned to a closed state during processing. In biological operations, for example, a functionally closed system is often employed when a low level of extraneous contamination may be permissible (such as purification skids used in cell-culture operations).

Open System

A process system that exposes the product to the room environment. In this type of system, the room environment is controlled to minimize the risk of extraneous product contamination. Examples include, but are not limited to, processing steps, such as media preparation or supplementation, or inoculum preparation.

Low Bioburden Product

Product manufactured within a controlled and monitored environment in which the final drug product or process intermediate, as applicable, requires bioburden control, but is not required to be sterile (e.g., biological drug substance produced by mammalian cell culture).

Nonviable

A term used in reference to particulates that are not capable of living, growing, or developing and functioning successfully ("unable to divide" or "not capable of reproducing").

Process Control Parameters

Conditions and corresponding measurements associated with the manufacturing process that may affect the identity, strength, quality, potency, and purity of a product. Examples of parameters of concern include bioburden, process rate, weight, volume, temperature, and pressure.

Risk Analysis

The estimation of the risk associated with the identified hazards. The following are commonly used tools that can be used to perform risk analysis:

  • FMEA — Failure Mode Effects Analysis
  • HACCP — Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points
Sanitization

Reduction of microbial contaminants to safe levels as judged by public health requirements for the specific country.

Sterilization

Validated process used to render product free from viable microorganisms.

Processing Area

The area in which processing occurs, with the appropriate environmental (e.g., gowning, cleaning/disinfection, training) and engineering (e.g., HVAC, facility design, material/personnel flows, differential pressures) controls in place to reduce the risk of microbial or particulate contamination.

Glossary Analysis and Practical Comparison

💡 Concept | How the TR13-2 terminology differs from TR13

The TR13-2 glossary is not a copy of the TR13 glossary. Some definitions were deliberately modified or newly created to reflect the particular nature of low bioburden processes. For example:

  • Bioburden (TR13): refers broadly to all microorganisms present in the environment, personnel, equipment and raw materials
  • Bioburden (TR13-2): specifically "the total number of microorganisms per unit of material before sterilization", a definition more focused on critical process attributes
  • Disinfection (TR13-2): builds on the TR13 definition by adding specific log reduction requirements (3-log/2-log), making it more operationally useful

When reading, always rely on the definitions in this document; do not apply your understanding of the TR13 terms directly.

💡 Concept | When to use each of the four EM monitoring types

TR13-2 divides environmental monitoring into four types, each with a clearly defined application:

Dynamic

Performed while production operations are in progress, with or without personnel present. Used to evaluate environmental quality in the "operational" state.

Routine

Performed at a fixed frequency (for example weekly or monthly) to assess the overall control status of the facility; not batch-related.

Batch-related

Performed at predetermined process steps, with or without product present.

Continuous

Performed throughout the batch to confirm that the state of control is maintained and to capture abnormal events; the frequency is determined by risk assessment.

Low bioburden processes usually use a mix of these types rather than a single one.

⚠️ Key point | Critical Operations Monitoring

Critical Operations Monitoring is an important new term in TR13-2. It is conceptually close to the "dynamic monitoring" in TR13, but places more emphasis on capturing, throughout the operation, all interventions, transient events and system deterioration.

The key difference is that the design and intensity of monitoring must be determined by process risk:

  • Closed system → monitoring density can be reduced
  • Open steps protected by a BSC (biological safety cabinet) → moderate density
  • Open operations without a barrier → highest density, with monitoring throughout

🏭 Practice | Typical examples of low bioburden products

"Low bioburden product" sounds abstract, but in the biopharmaceutical industry it is very concrete:

  • Monoclonal antibody drug substance (mAb drug substance): protein bulk after CHO cell culture, which requires bioburden control but has not yet undergone sterilizing filtration
  • Recombinant protein intermediates: protein solutions after chromatography purification, which carry a limited level of bioburden
  • Cell therapy intermediates: cell suspensions of CAR-T cells during the expansion stage (bioreactor)
  • Viral vector drug substance: manufacturing intermediates of gene therapy vectors such as adenovirus and AAV

The shared characteristics of these products: sterility is not required, but excessive microbial levels can affect product quality, safety or the performance of the downstream sterilizing step.

🔄 Analogy | Disinfection vs. Sanitization vs. Sterilization

Three terms for "killing microbes" are often confused. The TR13-2 definitions provide a clear hierarchy:

  • Sanitization: reduction to a level considered safe for public health; relatively mild and the standard in the food industry
  • Disinfection: at least a 3-log kill of vegetative microorganisms and at least a 2-log kill of bacterial spores; the standard for pharmaceutical cleanrooms
  • Sterilization: destruction of all viable microorganisms; the standard for sterile processes (SAL ≤ 10⁻⁶)

The cleaning validation target for a low bioburden process is usually disinfection, not sterilization.

3.0 Low Bioburden Products and Processes versus Traditional Sterile and Nonsterile Products and Processes

Sterile drug manufacturing is designed to produce a final sterile product through sterilizing process steps and subsequent manufacturing processes and controls. In contrast, manufacturing processes that require bioburden control are designed to limit the introduction of microorganisms into the process. Based on the physical properties of the materials being manufactured or, in some cases, regulatory requirements, mitigative steps designed as bioburden control mechanisms, such as the use of antibiotics, preservatives, solvents, low or high pH, sterile filtration and heat, may not be possible. In many cases, the process streams for these processes support the proliferation of any microorganisms present, often requiring control mechanisms during manufacturing operations to prevent or reduce microbial growth. Specific process steps taken if microorganisms are present, such as purification and ultrafiltration, often reduce bioburden levels although, in general, they are not specifically designed to do so. Due to the inherent susceptibility of the manufacturing process in these cases, an added focus must be placed on minimizing the ingress and proliferation of microorganisms and their metabolites that can affect product quality, safety, and stability.

Control of the process and processing environment is a major component of reducing the potential for product stream bioburden. Elements of control include the design of the facilities, utilities, and processing equipment (closed/open), as well as personnel and procedural measures. EM is used as a tool to measure the efficacy of these controls in a manufacturing facility.

The use of a local unidirectional airflow hood (UDF) for specific open manufacturing steps is a common practice where bioburden control is desired. These units are designed to produce a localized EU Grade A / ISO 5 environment. In this application, the units are not being used to provide protection for a sterile manufacturing-related operation but rather a nonsterile manufacturing operation as part of a bioburden control program. In such cases, based on their use and a thorough risk assessment, the placement of the UDF in Grade C environments is often acceptable. The level of gowning associated with their use should also be evaluated and requirements set based on the operation being performed and the level of control required.

The implementation and utilization of a quality risk management program provides scientific rationale for the manufacturing controls and EM program developed for these types of manufacturing processes. Quality risk management is predicated on understanding the specific risk factors of the environment and the process used to manufacture the product.

Once the manufacturing process and the potential contributors to the level of bioburden (e.g., environmental factors, facility and/or equipment design) have been identified and characterized, a thorough and well-documented risk assessment should be conducted to identify the points of potential microbial ingress. The manufacturer may incorporate specific components of a regulation or guidance document into the risk assessment as it applies to a particular process or step; this addition may vary depending on the intent of the risk assessment. Upon completion of the risk assessment, supporting programs can be developed to mitigate the risks identified. While all risks may need to be monitored for a period of time, any risk that cannot be mitigated should continue to be monitored.

When a manufacturing area has been classified in line with a regulatory or industry classification scheme, the EM program is often expected to meet the applicable requirements regardless of how the area is used. For example, if an area is classified as EU Grade A, the EM program and other supporting programs (e.g., gowning, cleaning, and sanitization) must reflect the requirements contained in the corresponding regulatory document. In many cases, these guidance documents are designed specifically to address the manufacture of sterile medicinal products, not nonsterile manufacturing operations designed to control but not eliminate the bioburden load present.

Monitoring performed during the manufacturing process should be aligned with the risk assessment and reflect the monitoring needed to support the process.

Consider the scenario of an aseptic transfer of materials in the UDF to minimize the introduction of additional bioburden. This is a nonsterile manufacturing step. A risk assessment may demonstrate that only certain aspects of the EU Grade A classification requirements are pertinent to the process. For example, in a situation where the concern is focused on the addition of bioburden (microorganisms) to the manufacturing operation, the risk assessment may conclude that the need to perform total particulate monitoring during the manufacturing operation should be reduced or eliminated. In that case, the total particulate monitoring would still be performed at a set frequency to ensure the UDF is continuing to operate appropriately but not during the actual manufacturing step. Similarly, the surrounding room environment should be determined based on the assessment of the manufacturing process and the control needed. For traditional nonsterile operations, for example, the EU Grade C requirements may be more appropriate based on the operation being performed. An alternative could be to classify the area according to ISO 14644 (e.g., ISO 5, 7, 8), as appropriate for the specific process and area. This approach must be documented in the site risk assessment.

An additional example where the use of a risk assessment may be of value is in the use of a UDF where preparation of the inoculum used for seeding of a bioreactor is being performed. In this example, during mammalian cell culture preparation, a designated room is used to prepare the inoculum for seeding the bioreactor. In the scale-up process, aseptic techniques are used to transfer the contents of a thawed cryovial to a sterile T-flask (or similar container). This activity is performed in a UDF to protect the process from the introduction of microorganisms, as microbial contamination would be the largest concern. The UDF is classified as EU Grade A because an aseptic technique must be performed in order to exclude the introduction of extraneous contaminants. This process takes less than two minutes to execute.

Based on the air quality criteria described in EudraLex, Volume 4, Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products, both continuous nonviable particulate counts and active airborne viable monitoring would be required during the manufacturing process based on the UDF classification as Grade A. This would present some challenges if applied. Annex 1 has generally been interpreted to expect a sample size of one cubic meter of air for both types of monitoring, which takes between 5 and 35 minutes to collect depending on the type of equipment used. Considering that the scale-up process takes only two minutes to execute, the requirements for the sample volumes would be difficult to satisfy; however, if the process only takes two minutes, the monitoring would encompass the entire process and meet the intent of the regulation.

In this case, a risk assessment should be completed to evaluate the process and monitoring requirements. If the primary concern is the introduction of viable organisms, the risk assessment may conclude that performing in-process total particulate counts not only provides little value but increases the risk to the operation based on the distraction it creates for the operator (assuming the use of manually operated particulate counters) and should not be conducted during the process while viable monitoring is of greater importance. As presented in the aseptic transfer example above, total particulate monitoring would be expected at an established frequency to ensure the biological safety cabinet is performing properly.

A number of well-known risk assessment tools are available, such as flow charts, check sheets, FMEA, and HACCP. In order to perform a successful risk assessment, the following must be included to ensure that all aspects of the process are appropriately addressed:

  • Knowledgeable representation from the functional areas involved in the process (e.g., validation, manufacturing, quality, facilities, microbiology)
  • Defined risk questions
  • Detailed process map
  • Facility map, including personnel and material movements
  • Production batch records and standard operating procedures

Analysis of Low Bioburden Process Design Principles

⚠️ Key point | Bioburden reduction steps: a passive side effect

TR13-2 specifically points out that process steps such as purification and ultrafiltration do reduce bioburden, but this is not their design purpose. They are protein purification tools, and bioburden reduction is only a side effect.

This point is very important: a manufacturer cannot claim "we have a purification step, so bioburden will naturally be controlled." The risk assessment must clearly identify the actual bioburden control mechanisms and their demonstrated capability.

🔄 Analogy | The role of UDF in non-sterile processes

In a traditional aseptic process, a Grade A UDF (or LAF) sits in a Grade B background environment and acts as a "sterile protective hood." In a low bioburden process, however, the UDF plays the role of a "local cleanliness enhancer":

  • The operating step itself is not an aseptic operation
  • The purpose of Grade A is to reduce, not eliminate, the opportunity for microorganisms to enter
  • The background environment can be Grade C rather than the Grade B required for aseptic processes
  • Gowning requirements should also be reassessed according to the nature of the operation; full Grade B sterile gowning is not necessarily required

This flexibility must be supported by a documented risk assessment; it cannot rest on a verbal "I think Grade C is enough."

🏭 Practice | In-depth analysis of the UDF inoculum preparation case

Section 3 of TR13-2 provides a very specific case: inoculum preparation for mammalian cell culture performed in a Grade A UDF.

Process description: the contents of a thawed cryovial are transferred to a sterile T-flask; the operation takes about 2 minutes.

Conflict with the literal requirements of Annex 1:

  • Grade A requires continuous nonviable particle counting plus active viable air sampling
  • The usual interpretation: the sample volume must reach 1 m³ (which takes 5 to 35 minutes)
  • But the process itself takes only 2 minutes, which is a complete contradiction

Example risk assessment conclusions:

  • The main risk = ingress of viable microorganisms (not particles)
  • Performing nonviable particle counts during the process would not help control the risk and would in fact introduce additional risk by distracting the operator
  • Conclusion: no particle counting during the process; particle counts are performed at a fixed frequency to confirm that the UDF is functioning properly
  • Viable air sampling is performed during the process (although the process lasts only 2 minutes, the sampling time can cover the entire process, which is consistent with the intent of the regulation)

This case illustrates the core principle that "monitoring design must be consistent with the risk assessment."

📋 Framework | The five essential elements of a successful risk assessment

TR13-2 explicitly lists the components that a risk assessment must include; none can be omitted:

  1. Cross-functional representatives: validation, manufacturing, quality assurance, facilities and microbiology; multiple perspectives are needed to avoid the blind spots of a single department
  2. A clear risk question: the core question of the risk assessment must be defined in advance, for example "What is the greatest microbial contamination risk in the inoculum preparation step?"
  3. A detailed process map: every operating step, equipment connection and material movement needs to be visualized
  4. A facility floor plan: including personnel flow and material flow, to identify potential cross-contamination paths
  5. Batch records and SOPs: to ensure that the risk assessment is based on the actual operating procedure rather than idealized assumptions

⚠️ Key point | The "classified means all requirements apply" trap

TR13-2 raises a dilemma common in industry: once an area is classified as EU Grade A, managers often assume that all Annex 1 requirements for Grade A must be fully complied with.

But Annex 1 was designed for sterile product manufacturing, not for non-sterile, low bioburden operations. The TR13-2 solution:

  • Use a documented risk assessment to justify which Grade A requirements apply to the process
  • Determine which requirements can be adjusted or waived (with sufficient scientific justification)
  • Or consider using ISO 14644 classification (ISO 5/7/8) in place of EU grades, which better fits the context of low bioburden processes

Key principle: the monitoring program serves the needs of the process; it does not simply comply with a classification label.

💡 Concept | The core statement of TR13-2

"Monitoring performed during the manufacturing process should be aligned with the risk assessment and reflect the monitoring needed to support the process."

In plain terms: "The design of process monitoring must be consistent with the conclusions of the risk assessment and must reflect the level of monitoring that the process actually requires."

What this means: more monitoring is not necessarily better, and less is not better either; the goal is just enough to support process quality assurance. Excessive monitoring (such as forcing meaningless particle counts) can introduce operational risk, while insufficient monitoring cannot detect contamination trends.

💡 Concept | Sterile vs. low bioburden: fundamentally different goals

This is the most important conceptual framework in this section:

The difficulty with biological processes (such as cell culture) is that the culture medium is itself an ideal growth environment for microorganisms, and any contaminant that enters will multiply quickly, so process design and environmental control must block contamination at the source.

4.0 Environmental Risk Assessment

Several risk assessments need to be performed as part of an overall facility risk assessment; these can include a facility (also called an environmental) risk assessment, a process risk assessment, and an EM risk assessment. The environment (or facility) risk assessment should determine the suitability of the facility or environment in which the process is conducted. A process risk assessment should include identification of potential sources of contamination and associated inherent risks to the process from an operational perspective, including points of potential contamination. The EM risk assessment determines how the contamination risks should be addressed within an effective EM control program. All are part of the overall contamination control strategy for a facility.

This technical report focuses on the EM risk assessment that may be performed to:

  • Develop or expand programs used to identify, control, and/or monitor contamination risks to determine:
    • Appropriate level of facility control for the process being performed
    • EM locations, sampling frequency, and sampling methodology
    • Appropriate environmental controls to be employed in manufacturing areas based on the type of processing performed
    • Develop or revise area and material transfer or cleaning and/or disinfection practices
  • Determine gowning strategy and controls required to prevent contamination by personnel
  • Determine appropriate personnel, material, and waste flows
  • Develop or expand a bioburden and endotoxin-burden monitoring program

EM risk assessments can also be used to investigate a contamination event or trend, such as:

  • Microbiological contamination in a process train or critical utility system
  • Adverse data trend in a microbial measurement system (i.e., bioburden/endotoxin, EM, or critical utility system monitoring)
  • Identification and evaluation of a potential source of contamination or atypical recovery

Analysis: The Three-Tier Risk Assessment Structure

How the Three Risk Assessments Divide the Work

TR13-2 clearly distinguishes three types of RA right from the start, each asking a different question:

  • Facility (environmental) risk assessment: "Is this facility suitable for this process?" Evaluates whether building structure, airflow design and room classification match the process
  • Process risk assessment: "Which steps of the process carry contamination risk?" Takes an operational view to identify the potential contamination points in each process step
  • EM risk assessment: "Given the risks above, how do we track and control them with the EM system?" Determines monitoring locations, frequency and methods

Positioning of TR13-2

The core subject of this technical report is the third tier, the EM risk assessment. The facility RA and process RA are prerequisites; building on them, the EM RA designs a monitoring program that can effectively detect and control contamination risk.

Note in particular that the EM RA is not only for designing the initial EM program. When a contamination event or adverse trend occurs (for example, microbial contamination in a process line, or an upward trend in EM data), it should also trigger a re-examination or investigational assessment of the EM RA.

Analogy: the design logic of a security system

Facility RA = assessing the security of the building itself (are doors and windows intact, is the entry/exit design reasonable)
Process RA = assessing the risk of daily activities (which activities are likely to bring in outsiders or suspicious items)
EM RA = deciding where to install cameras, how often to review the footage, and whether high-risk areas need 24-hour monitoring

4.1

General Guidelines

Process flow map - Multifunctional team - Facility tours

4.1 General Guidelines

Risk assessments highlight manufacturing process steps or room locations that exhibit the greatest opportunity for introduction of potential contamination to the product or process stream, a critical utility system, or the environment. A process flow map based on production records, applicable standard operating procedures (SOPs), personnel/equipment/material flow diagrams, and piping and instrumentation diagrams is used to identify individual process steps. Using a process flow map helps ensure that each risk assessment is performed systematically and that all key aspects of the operations or systems are considered.

As a best practice, the system-owner maps out the process or systems prior to development of the risk assessment by the multifunctional team to ensure that the scope of the risk assessment is clearly identified.

Tours of the facility and manufacturing operations using the process flow map are also recommended to collect information and determine the criticality factors relevant to the risk assessment being performed (see Table 4.3.1-1🔍 and Table 4.3.1-2🔍). Only the criticality factors that apply to the scope of the risk assessment should be selected.

Analysis: Foundational Tools for Performing the RA

Core function of the process flow map

Before an EM risk assessment is performed, the process flow map must be completed. This is not an optional step; it is the fundamental safeguard that the RA does not miss any critical step. A complete process flow map should integrate:

  • Batch records: record the sequence and details of each operating step
  • Standard operating procedures (SOPs): confirm how each step is actually performed
  • Personnel/equipment/material flow diagrams: show the possible pathways of contamination spread
  • Piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&ID): clarify whether the system is open or closed

Multifunctional team

TR13-2 stresses that the EM RA should be performed by a multifunctional team. Typical members include:

  • Validation: provides the qualification/validation perspective
  • Manufacturing: knows the actual operating details
  • Quality Assurance: ensures regulatory compliance
  • Facilities/Engineering: understands facility design and HVAC
  • Microbiology: provides expert judgment on contamination sources

Best practice: the system owner completes the flow map first, then hands it to the multifunctional team for the RA, so that the scope of the RA is clearly defined.

The importance of facility tours

An RA based on documents alone easily becomes a "paper RA": the flow map looks reasonable, but the actual facility has risks that the documents do not record (for example, exposed piping, cracks in walls, poorly placed drains). TR13-2 clearly recommends facility tours together with the flow map in order to collect the complete information used to determine criticality factors.

4.2

Criticality Factors

Eight sources of contamination - Personnel / Materials / Facility Design

4.2 Criticality Factors

Criticality factors assist in identifying contamination hazards by determining how each factor might impact the potential for product, process, or environmental contamination. The criticality factors are based on the various sources of contamination.

Eight major potential sources of adventitious microbial contamination sources and their relevance to the risk assessment are discussed in Sections 4.2.1–4.2.3.5, topics that should be considered applicable to any facility and addressed through the risk assessment.

4.2.1 Personnel

The human body is the source of greatest risk of contamination in the manufacturing environment. The design of the EM program should consider the risk to the various environments based on the engineering and procedural controls designed to minimize the impact of personnel on the environment. Some potential controls are:

  • Establishing maximum personnel load for the room or area
  • Facility designs that eliminate the need for entrance into manufacturing areas (e.g., viewing galleries)
  • Use of facility-dedicated shoes and clothing
  • Gowning and/or personal protective equipment requirements and glove/sleeve sanitization procedures, as dictated by the specific process or process step
  • Unidirectional vs. bidirectional personnel flows
  • Use of good aseptic working practices
  • Personnel training and qualification

4.2.2 Materials/Waste

The second major source of potential contamination is the transfer of materials into and waste out of the manufacturing and support areas and, potentially, into exposed product. All ingredients, processing aids, filters, components, labels, packaging materials, etc., must be disinfected (or sterilized if determined appropriate) and managed in a manner that reduces the risk of transferring contamination to the manufacturing and support areas. Waste should be managed in a manner that ensures a low risk of microbial and/or product cross-contamination. A comprehensive EM program must include sampling locations and frequencies to provide timely indications that materials and waste are being transferred and handled appropriately.

Analysis: Criticality Factors and Sources of Contamination

Definition of criticality factors

"Criticality factors" are the indicators used to assess the risk that various sources of contamination pose to the process, product or environment. TR13-2 divides the main sources of contamination into three major categories, corresponding to eight subsections from 4.2.1 to 4.2.3.5.

Key premise: these factors apply to any facility; when using them, the RA team should select only the factors relevant to the scope of the current RA.

4.2.1 Personnel: the largest source of risk

TR13-2 states clearly: people are the largest source of contamination risk in a manufacturing environment. The human body sheds tens of thousands of skin flakes per minute, and every movement can stir up particles. The EM program design must therefore reflect "how well personnel controls are implemented".

Gradient of key controls (from higher to lower risk):

  • Maximum personnel load (Maximum Personnel Load): the fewer the people, the lower the contamination potential
  • Personnel flow design: unidirectional flow is better than bidirectional flow
  • Gowning/aseptic technique: full sterile gowning + frequency of sterile glove changes
  • Observation corridor design: if the engineering design allows operators to avoid entering the manufacturing area, the EM needs are correspondingly reduced

The intensity of the EM program should be inversely proportional to "how well personnel controls are implemented": the tighter the controls, the lower the weight that EM can reasonably give to personnel-related risk.

4.2.2 Materials and waste

Every material entering the manufacturing area (raw materials, supplementary materials, filters, parts, labels, packaging materials) is a potential carrier of contamination. Key decision points:

  • Disinfection or sterilization? Decided by the intended use of the material and the level of contact risk
  • Waste flow: the waste exit should be separate from the material entrance to avoid cross-contamination
  • EM sampling locations: should include the transfer points for materials and waste; the contamination risk at these locations is highest at the moment of transfer

4.2.3 Facility Design and Construction

The design of the manufacturing and support areas and the implementation of the following minimum requirements are needed to facilitate cleaning and disinfection activities, as well as minimize the potential for contamination of those areas and the product (this list is not exhaustive):

  • All surfaces should be smooth, impervious, nonabsorbent, unbroken, and compatible with appropriate agents
  • The amount of exposed surface area should be minimized to the extent possible (e.g., pipes should be contained in interstitial spaces wherever possible)
  • Construction materials should resist chipping, flaking, oxidizing, or other deterioration
  • Wall and floor junctions should be curved, rather than meeting at sharp angles
  • All flooring materials should be seamless
  • Any recesses should be reachable and cleanable
  • Ledges, shelves, cupboards, and equipment where particles or microbes can settle and accumulate should be minimized
The facility design and construction should be appropriate for the proposed manufacturing process and product. If during the execution of the risk assessment, the facility is found to be inappropriate in any way, the deficiency should be addressed through appropriate mitigation and the efficacy verified through the EM program.

4.2.3.1 Facility Cleaning and Disinfection

The types of cleaning and disinfection agents (i.e., sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides), methodology, and frequency should be considered when designing an EM program, and the frequency of cleaning and disinfection activities should be justified based on historical EM data for existing facilities. For new facilities, this frequency should be determined through appropriate in-situ qualification as defined in PDA Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities.

4.2.3.2 Process Design

The design of the process should be considered when establishing the EM program. The degree of manual manipulations, number and complexity of required steps, and the process duration all affect the potential for microbial contamination to enter the system. Therefore, each step of the process should be considered individually in the risk assessment. For example, open operations may provide more opportunity for product contamination, whereas closed or hard-piped systems and isolators can significantly reduce the risk of contamination as they prevent exposure of the process stream to the environment and personnel. From an operational standpoint, additional bioburden reduction steps, such as filtration, should be considered.

4.2.3.3 Nature of the Product

The nature of in-process intermediates and final product should also be considered, such as the potential for growth-promoting characteristics. For example, upstream cell culture media would be highly sensitive to microbial proliferation whereas potential downstream purification materials with high salt buffers may prohibit microbial proliferation.

4.2.3.4 Environmental Air

Controlling air as a source of contamination is an important factor to consider when designing an EM program. Points to consider include, but are not limited to:

  • Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning design
  • Number of air changes per hour
  • Use of localized filtered air (e.g., UDFs)
  • Use of pressurized airflow cascades
  • Use of transitional spaces or rooms (e.g., airlocks and gowning rooms)
  • Results of airflow visualization studies (e.g., smoke studies)

4.2.3.5 Utilities

The main types of utilities used in controlled environments are water, compressed air or gases, vacuums, and drains. Risk of potential contamination from exposure to these utilities should be considered when determining sampling locations and frequencies for an EM program. How these utilities impact the process and product should also be considered, as they impact the quality and design of the resulting EM program.

Analysis: The Five Sub-Factors of Facility Design

4.2.3 Basic facility design requirements

Facility design is the physical foundation of the entire contamination control strategy. The minimum requirements listed in TR13-2 are intended to support effective cleaning and disinfection:

  • Surface properties: smooth, impervious and non-absorbent, to keep microorganisms from harboring
  • Coved junctions (Coved Junctions): where walls meet floors, junctions should be coved rather than right-angled, eliminating corners that are hard to clean
  • Seamless flooring: eliminates seams where dirt could collect
  • Minimized exposed area: route piping into interstitial spaces where possible, reducing the surfaces that need cleaning

If the RA finds a deficiency in facility design, it must be resolved through mitigation, and the effectiveness of the mitigation verified with EM data.

4.2.3.1 Cleaning and disinfection (C&D)

The type of C&D (disinfectant/sporicide), its frequency and method directly affect EM program design:

  • Existing facilities: C&D frequency should be justified by historical EM data
  • New facilities: in-situ qualification should be performed in accordance with TR70; where no historical data exist, a more frequent monitoring program should be used until sufficient data have been accumulated

4.2.3.2 Process design: open vs. closed systems

This is one of the most critical single factors affecting EM design:

  • Open system: the product is directly exposed to the environment and requires the highest level of EM coverage
  • Functionally closed system: open connections under local protection; moderate EM needs
  • Fully closed system: isolator or hard-piped systems; EM needs are significantly lower

The higher the degree of automation and the less manual intervention, the lower the contamination potential.

4.2.3.3 Product characteristics

A factor that is often overlooked but is critically important:

  • Growth-promoting: cell culture media, serum-containing media -> microorganisms multiply easily -> tighter EM is needed
  • Growth-inhibiting: high-salt buffers, some purification materials -> microorganisms have difficulty proliferating -> EM intensity can be adjusted accordingly

4.2.3.4 Environmental air

Air is an important vehicle for microbial spread. When designing the EM program, consider:

  • HVAC design: whether the arrangement of supply/return/exhaust could cause air from contaminated areas to flow into clean areas
  • Air changes per hour (ACH): determines the rate of air dilution; the higher the ACH, the faster particles are removed
  • Unidirectional airflow (UDF/LAF): provides local ISO 5 protection at critical operating points
  • Smoke studies: visually verify airflow direction and confirm there are no eddies or dead zones

4.2.3.5 Utility systems

WFI (water for injection), compressed air, vacuum and drain systems can all be sources of contamination:

  • A poorly designed drain line can become a back-contamination pathway
  • Compressed air that is not properly filtered will carry microorganisms directly into the process
  • EM sampling locations should cover the points of contact or high-risk locations of utility systems
4.3

Risk Assessment Design

Three criticality factor groups - Risk ranking methodology

4.3 Risk Assessment Design

Many methods are available for designing and implementing a risk assessment. The selection of the method should be based on the process in question and the relevant criticality factors selected. For the purposes of illustration, Sections 4.4 and 4.5 present examples of hypothetical risk assessment designs for a typical mammalian cell culture process (upstream).

Each risk-ranking is performed in accordance with the risk tool selected. The risk control activities are performed according to the criteria specified in the applicable risk tool methodology. If a location or process step is not well controlled and/or monitored and considered a high risk, then that location or process step automatically becomes a potential source of microbial ingress, and tightening contamination controls should be considered.

4.3.1 Criticality Factors

Criticality factors are criteria that are divided into three main groups: process step, potential contributors to contamination, and unusual/unexpected sources of contamination. Tables 4.3.1-1 and 4.3.1-2 provide examples of elements that aid in determining the level of risk—High, Medium, or Low—and the criticality factors of each. These elements may change based on the manufacturing operations, equipment, facility design, or other components. The criteria may be numbers or values but need to be able to allow the user to evaluate the risk on a scaled basis. The criticality and weighting for each criterion may vary depending on the area and process or other factors.

4.3.1.1 Group 1 – Process Step

Each stage of the manufacturing process is considered a process step. The process steps shown in Tables 4.3.1-1 and 4.3.1-2 are examples of those that would be used by the risk assessment team. These may differ based on the products being manufactured, manufacturing process, stage of the process, equipment set-up, facility age, and controls, among other elements.

4.3.1.2 Group 2 – Potential Contributors to Contamination

These criticality factors include the manufacturing process, environmental factors, facility or equipment design, and contamination control programs implemented, any of which may contribute to the potential for contamination. For the factors in this group, enhanced contamination controls may be identified during risk mitigation. In this mammalian cell culture example, the potential sources of contamination are shown in Tables 4.3.1-1 and 4.3.1-2.

4.3.1.3 Group 3 – Unusual/Unexpected Sources of Contamination

Group 3 evaluates sources of contamination that are extraneous to the design intent of the environment, equipment, or process. Any unusual or unexpected source of contamination is considered a high risk that must be mitigated. This may include faulty or leaking equipment or utility connections or breaches in the integrity of the manufacturing environment that are detected during a facility walk-through. Since the source of this hazard is unexpected and must be mitigated, controls should be implemented, and it should be monitored until the unexpected source of contamination has been eliminated. Examples include, but are not limited to, a reverse in pressure differentials due to a power failure, presence of contractors and their equipment, damage to the facility surfaces, or condensation on piping.

Analysis: The Structural Logic of the Three Groups of Criticality Factors

Core principle of RA design

TR13-2 stresses that if a location or process step is inadequately controlled and rated as high risk, that location automatically becomes a potential source of microbial ingress, and stronger controls must be considered. This principle ensures that the RA is not merely a theoretical scoring exercise but is tied to actual control actions.

Group 1: Process steps

Group 1: What are you doing?

The process step itself determines the baseline risk level. For example:

  • Final formulation = High Risk (product directly exposed)
  • Seed inoculation = High Risk (open system operation)
  • Equipment assemblies = Low Risk (no direct product exposure)
  • Tubing welds = Low Risk (closed operation)

These classifications change with the process, equipment, facility age and controls, and must not be applied rigidly.

Group 2: Potential contributors to contamination

Group 2: How do the environment and system design affect risk?

This is the most complex group and covers multiple dimensions:

  • System openness: Open > Functionally Closed > Fully Closed
  • Equipment sterility: cleaned and disinfected only vs. partially sterilized vs. fully sterilized
  • Degree of automation: fully manual vs. semi-automated vs. fully automated
  • Duration of operation: 4–24 hours vs. <4 hours vs. <30 minutes
  • Surrounding room classification: ISO 8/Grade C vs. ISO 7/Grade B
  • Temperature: >25°C (promotes microbial growth) vs. 20–25°C vs. <20°C
  • Number of personnel and intensity of activity: multiple operations/many personnel vs. a single operation/single operator
  • Gowning requirements: ordinary lab coat vs. full sterile gowning
Group 3: Abnormal/unexpected contamination sources

Group 3: Risks beyond design expectations are always High Risk

Group 3 is the strictest group in TR13-2: any abnormal or unexpected source of contamination is always treated as High Risk and must be mitigated. There is no middle value; it cannot be rated Medium or Low.

Typical examples:

  • Reverse pressure differential: a power outage or HVAC failure reverses airflow, so air from lower-cleanliness areas flows into higher-cleanliness areas
  • Entry of contractors and their equipment: outside personnel may bring in microbial species not normally present in the environment
  • Damaged facility surfaces: cracks in walls or broken flooring give microorganisms places to harbor
  • Condensation on piping: damp surfaces promote biofilm formation

After a Group 3 problem is found, controls should be implemented immediately and monitoring continued until the problem is eliminated.

Analysis: Table 4.3.1-2🔍 Differences for Supporting Areas

Supporting areas vs. immediate process areas

Table 4.3.1-2🔍 is the version for "supporting areas" (Supporting Area). Its main differences from Table 4.3.1-1 are:

  • Different classification of process steps: in a supporting area, "High Risk" refers to a critical process taking place next to it (such as seed inoculation), not to the activity of the supporting area itself
  • Unique factors added：
    • Humidity control: no humidity control = High Risk
    • Presence of growth-promoting materials: open water sources, drains = High Risk
    • Traffic routes: corridors/airlocks = High Risk, dedicated rooms = Low Risk
  • Stricter Low Risk criterion for temperature: Table 4.3.1-2🔍 lists refrigerated temperature (2–8°C) as Low Risk, rather than just <20°C

The importance of growth-promoting materials

Risk factors specific to supporting areas: open water sources, drains, CIP/SIP stations and safety showers are all High Risk. This is because:

  • Water is one of the most effective microbial growth media
  • Drains commonly harbor waterborne organisms such as Pseudomonas and Burkholderia cepacia
  • These organisms can be carried by personnel movement or airflow into the manufacturing area

The EM program should place sampling locations near supporting areas that contain drains, as early-warning indicators.

What the traffic route factor really means

Hallways and airlocks are rated High Risk, which is slightly counterintuitive. These areas themselves have no product exposure, so why High Risk?

The reason is that these areas act as vectors for spreading contamination. High-traffic hallways and airlocks are the transition zones between different cleanliness grades, where personnel, materials and equipment frequently cross, and they are the main pathway by which contamination moves from lower-cleanliness to higher-cleanliness areas. EM monitoring must therefore cover these "traffic nodes".

Analysis: The Logic for Selecting EM Sampling Locations

Mapping from risk level to sampling locations

Table 4.3.2-1🔍 establishes a logical framework in which the risk level determines the range of sampling locations to consider:

  • Low Risk: not in the product pathway, but traffic patterns (Traffic Patterns) and vectors (Vectors) must be monitored, because even without direct product contact these places reflect the overall level of environmental hygiene
  • Medium Risk: external surfaces of equipment close to open operations; not directly exposed, but may be affected by splashes or airflow
  • High Risk: every critical process operation, including traffic patterns, vectors and, most importantly, equipment surfaces close to open operations and in the product pathway

Definitions of two core concepts

Traffic Patterns: the movement paths of personnel in the manufacturing area. Every movement disturbs the airflow and may raise particles from the floor. EM sampling locations should be placed at key nodes along personnel walking paths.

Vectors of Contamination: surfaces that personnel touch with their hands, such as door handles, equipment switches, work-table edges and keyboards. These surfaces are the bridge by which microorganisms transfer from personnel to the environment, and they must be monitored even in Low Risk areas.

Documentation requirements

The scientific rationale for selecting sampling locations must be documented and incorporated into:

  • The EM performance qualification (EMPQ) protocol: establishes the initial scientific rationale for the sampling locations in each manufacturing room
  • Change control documents: when the routine EM program is adjusted (sampling locations added or removed), the rationale for the adjustment must be recorded through change control

This ensures that every decision in the EM program is supported by a traceable risk assessment, meeting the documentation expectations of regulators.

Analysis: The Three-Tier Logic of Monitoring Frequency

High Risk = dynamic monitoring (Dynamic/Batch-linked)

"At every critical operation" means that EM is linked to the batch process rather than being routine monitoring on fixed dates. If there are three seed inoculations this week, that High Risk location needs to be sampled three times; if there is no such operation in a given week, no sampling is needed.

The benefit of this design: when a problem occurs, the EM data can be compared directly with the operating records of the specific batch, allowing the source of contamination to be traced quickly.

Frequency adjustment driven by historical data

TR13-2 points out that the frequencies in the table are a reference baseline, and the actual frequency should be adjusted based on historical EM data:

  • If a Medium Risk location has shown very low colony counts over a long period -> the frequency may be reduced based on the risk assessment (with documented support)
  • If a Low Risk location shows an upward trend -> the frequency should be temporarily increased for investigational monitoring
  • New facilities: with no historical data, start with more frequent monitoring and adjust once data have accumulated

This reflects the lifecycle approach (Lifecycle Approach) of TR13-2: the EM program should be continually optimized as process knowledge accumulates.

How Risk Level Maps to Frequency

This table converts a risk rating directly into an actionable monitoring frequency, and it is the "basis for frequency" in an EM program. The key points are:

The assessment of the critical factors identified in this technical report and presented methodology for executing a structured and robust risk assessment provide a scientifically sound approach to developing a risk-based EM program.

Commentary: Summary of the Risk Assessment Design Framework

"a scientifically sound approach to developing a risk-based EM program"

This statement is the core claim of the entire Section 4 framework. TR13-2 stresses that although regulations impose no mandatory sampling locations or frequencies for EM in low bioburden environments, a structured risk assessment still makes it possible to build an EM program with a scientific rationale that will withstand scrutiny in a GMP audit.

The Verification Role of EM

In this framework, EM serves as a "verification tool for contamination controls", not as the first line of defense against contamination. The real lines of defense are HVAC design, disinfection procedures and control of personnel behavior; EM provides a periodic "status check" to confirm that these defenses continue to work.

This example summarizes results of a risk analysis conducted to evaluate the EM sample plans for a drug substance facility cell-culture area (not all details of the complete risk analysis process are included). A facilitator coordinated with participants from the manufacturing, quality assurance, validation engineering, and quality control groups and included a microbiology subject matter expert to conduct the risk analysis.

4.4.1 Executive Summary

This EM program is designed to monitor manufacturing areas and ensure that the levels of environmental controls are maintained in line with site SOPs. An EM risk analysis was conducted using a risk tool to identify and quantify risks in an effort to optimize the EM sample plans in the cell-culture area.

Commentary: Example 1 Background: Mammalian Cell Culture Warm Room

What Is a "Warm Room"?

A warm room is a controlled-environment room maintained above room temperature (typically >25°C; 36°C ± 1°C in this example) and dedicated to the scale-up of mammalian cell cultures, such as roller bottle and T-flask culture.

Why does temperature add risk? Microbial growth rates increase markedly above 25°C, and many environmental microorganisms (such as Staphylococcus and Bacillus species) reach their fastest growth rates close to human body temperature (37°C). The temperature of the warm room itself is therefore an additional contamination risk factor.

Cross-Functional Risk Assessment Team

TR13-2 explicitly lists participants from Manufacturing, QA, Validation Engineering, QC and a Microbiology SME. This reflects the cross-functional spirit of ICH Q9 risk management: the quality of a risk assessment comes from integrating diverse perspectives, not from the unilateral judgment of a single department.

4.4.2 Discussion

A risk analysis team used a risk tool to evaluate contamination risks in all ISO-graded areas, choosing the EM sample site locations by the nature of operations to be carried out in the rooms. Sample site selection considered the various sanitized or sterilized surfaces, personnel, equipment, and material flow, as well as points of human activity that may represent potential risks to the state of control to the product and in the manufacturing area.

The risk analysis included the evaluation of each EM sample location for hazards. The hazards included the various physical conditions, operational activities, and EM-specific indicators that could contribute to or detect contamination. The identified hazards, factor descriptions, rationale, and weighting scores established for this analysis are provided in Table 4.4.2-1.

Each factor was assigned a weighting score of 0, 1, 3, or 5, based on its perceived contribution to contamination risk. Zero (0) equates to extremely low or no impact on contamination and 5 equates to the highest potential impact on contamination. The values were added to allow for a means to generate a risk priority.

Note: the scale is deliberately non-continuous (0, 1, 3, 5) rather than evenly spaced 1-5. This design makes a high-risk factor (5 points) weigh far more on the total than a low-risk factor (1 point), which naturally widens the gap between high-risk and low-risk locations.

What Group 1 and Group 2 Mean

Group 1 (process steps) evaluates "what operation is performed in this space". Final Formulation scores highest because the product is exposed to the environment without protection; corridors and anterooms score lowest because they serve only support functions.

Group 2 (potential contamination contributors) evaluates "the physical conditions and system design of this space". An open system is the most hazardous (5 points) and a fully closed system the safest (1 point); a warm room temperature of >25°C scores 5 points because microorganisms multiply rapidly at this temperature.

Scoring Profile of the Warm Room in This Matrix

Warm Room X (see 4.4.3) meets the following high-scoring factors:

  • Temperature: >25°C → 5 points
  • Process step: upstream operation (roller bottle culture) → 3 points
  • Personnel activity: multiple operations co-existing → 5 points
  • Process duration: multiple days → 5 points

Temperature is often an overlooked item when assessing low bioburden environments. TR13-2 lists it explicitly as a scoring factor, reminding assessors not to focus only on "open vs. closed system" while ignoring environmental conditions.

The total hazard score for each of the EM sample locations was ranked and then filtered by the severity score for the room (Table 4.4.2-2🔍). Severity is the measure of the possible consequences of a hazard potentially resulting in product contamination. Low severity was assigned if product contamination was unlikely as the product was never present in the room. Medium severity was assigned if the product was present but was never exposed to the room environment. High severity was assigned if the product was present and could be exposed to the environment.

Table 4.4.2-2🔍 — Risk Class Ranking Scheme

The resulting risk class was then filtered by the detection controls (Table 4.4.2-3🔍). Low detection is the use of the building monitoring system (e.g., HVAC, temperature monitoring systems) with no other means of detection. Medium detection includes the building monitoring system plus EM. High detection includes in-process product monitoring for contamination in addition to the building monitoring system and EM.

Table 4.4.2-3🔍 — Sampling Priority Designation

The output of the EM risk assessment is the level of risk at the EM sampling location expressed as a high, medium, or low EM sampling priority. The EM sampling priority provides information used to assess the EM sampling plan for each room by EM sample location. This ensures the identification of EM sample locations within the rooms that provide meaningful EM data and those sample locations that may be redundant or do not significantly contribute to the overall picture of environmental control.

High EM sampling priority locations indicate room locations with the highest contamination risk or low detection capability. These EM sampling locations were assessed to determine if additional remediation should be implemented to increase their environmental control. Medium EM sampling priority locations presented an acceptable level of risk and provided meaningful EM data. Low EM sampling priority locations were assigned in areas that either posed a very low risk of product contamination or sites that may have had higher risk classes, but the risk was mitigated by high-detection capabilities.

Three Steps to Determine Sampling Priority

Example 1 of TR13-2 uses a three-tier filtering system, with each tier reducing uncertainty:

↓
Tier 2: Severity

↓
Tier 3: Detection
Whether the existing monitoring system is adequate → final sampling priority

Analogy: The ICH Q9 RPN Calculation

This three-tier framework is essentially an adaptation of the RPN (Risk Priority Number) calculation used in FMEA:

  • Severity: degree of product exposure
  • Occurrence: hazard probability (sum of the factors)
  • Detection: extent of coverage by the existing monitoring system

The difference is that this framework uses matrix classification (Risk Class 1/2/3) rather than a multiplied product, which avoids a known flaw of the RPN product method (for example, high O × low S × low D and low O × high S × low D can yield the same RPN even though the actual risks are completely different).

The Protective Role of Detection Controls

Table 4.4.2-3🔍 illustrates an important concept: strong detection capability can lower the sampling priority. For example, for a sampling site in Risk Class 1 (high hazard, high severity), if "in-process product contamination monitoring" provides additional detection, the sampling priority can drop from "high" to "medium". This means that investing in online monitoring systems (such as rapid microbiological methods, RMM) can justify reducing the frequency of conventional culture-media-based EM.

Commentary: Practical Significance of the Warm Room X Sampling Design

Why Choose "Weekly" Monitoring?

According to Table 4.3.3-1🔍, the monitoring frequency for a medium-risk environment is once a week. The overall risk assessment result for Warm Room X falls in the "medium to high risk" range, and together with the 36°C environment (high hazard score), weekly monitoring was adopted.

In addition, the strategic objective of monitoring is to "protect the cleanliness of the roller bottles": although roller bottles are a closed system, frequent personnel entry and exit (moving bottle racks, daily visual inspection) makes the room a potential route for introducing contamination.

Annual Review of the Sampling Plan

TR13-2 specifically stresses that the sampling plan should be evaluated "once a year", with the following triggers:

  • A change in the activities carried out in the room
  • Adverse bioburden events
  • A change in the trend shown by historical data

This is consistent with the continual improvement spirit of the original PDA TR13 (Ref 1): an EM program is not a static document but a dynamic tool that evolves as process knowledge accumulates.

4.5 Risk Assessment Design: Example 2

This report summarizes results of the risk analysis conducted to evaluate the EM sample plan for a media supplementation (via aliquoting) process for a cell therapy manufacturing process. Knowledgeable personnel from the microbiology, manufacturing, quality assurance, compliance, and manufacturing science and technology departments participated in the risk analysis.

In cell therapy manufacturing, media supplementation is performed by adding sterile supplements into sterile media using a sterile pipette or other sterile transfer method. The process occurs inside an ISO 5-classified UDF hood. Routine EM is performed based on the results of a risk assessment. Personnel are appropriately trained on the process and on the proper aseptic techniques. Personnel also wear sterile gloves and sterile sleeves while performing the process.

The media, supplements, and containers used during the process are sterile. In addition, an absence-of-contamination test is performed post-process using a rapid microbiology method.

4.5.1 Executive Summary

The EM program is designed to monitor manufacturing areas and ensure that the levels of environmental controls are maintained as specified in site SOPs and in the laboratory information management system. Using FMEA and following the risk assessment guidance methodology, an EM risk analysis was conducted to identify and quantify risks. The effort was intended to optimize the EM sample plans for media supplementation as well as to determine the appropriate ISO/Grade level needed. (See Table 4.3.1-1🔍 for recommended classifications and types of activities.) Other risk tool methodology may be used depending on the specific process.

Commentary: Example 2 Background: Media Supplementation Process

What Is "Media Supplementation"?

In cell therapy manufacturing, the culture media used for cell culture usually need specific supplements added before or during use, such as cytokines, growth factors, antibiotics or serum replacements. This addition step is called media supplementation and is usually performed by aliquoting: a sterile pipette is used to draw the supplement from its container and add it to the media container.

Why is this a specific risk scenario? Although the operation is performed inside an ISO 5 UDF hood, pipetting is itself a moment of open-system handling: the operator's gloves and sleeves come into direct contact with the pipette, and each time the pipette enters or leaves a container is a window of opportunity for introducing contamination.

Choice of the FMEA Method

Unlike Example 1 (which uses a general risk tool), Example 2 explicitly uses FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis). The advantages of FMEA are that it:

  • Systematically lists the possible failure modes of each process step
  • Evaluates the effect and detectability of each failure mode
  • Is particularly suitable for manual processes with many operating steps and frequent personnel contact

TR13-2 notes that "other risk tools may also be used", reflecting the ICH Q9 emphasis on methodological flexibility.

The Role of Post-Process Rapid Microbiological Testing

The "absence-of-contamination test" is performed after the process using a rapid microbiological method (RMM). This provides a third layer of detection and corresponds directly to "High Detection" in Table 4.4.2-3🔍. It means that even if a given EM sampling site has a higher risk level, its sampling priority can be lowered accordingly because direct product testing takes place after the process.

A risk tool was executed using appropriate methodology to evaluate contamination risks during media supplementation. EM sample site locations, including personnel monitoring, and the types of monitoring were determined based on the nature of the process. The nature of the materials (i.e., sterile), duration of the process, personnel, material flow, and post-processing testing were considered during the analysis. The risk analysis evaluated each EM sample location for hazards including the various physical conditions, operational activities, and EM-specific indicators that could contribute to or detect contamination.

"Step-Specific" vs. "Time-Specific" Monitoring

An important feature of this EM program is that monitoring frequency is defined per process step rather than per unit of time. For example, personnel monitoring is performed "at defined time points in the media supplementation process", not "once a week".

This step-specific design is better suited to batch-related operations, because the frequency of batch execution itself determines the monitoring frequency, which avoids the dilemma of "low batch frequency but mandatory weekly monitoring" or "high batch frequency but insufficient weekly monitoring".

Detecting Color Change in the Culture Medium

TR13-2 mentions that media contamination can be detected directly by turbidity and by a color change from red to yellow, because most cell culture media contain phenol red as a pH indicator. When microorganisms grow and metabolize the medium, they produce acidic products, the pH falls, and the medium changes from red to yellow. This is an immediate, visible indicator of contamination and a natural "rapid contamination detection" mechanism in cell culture processes, filling the monitoring gap during the incubation wait of conventional EM.
