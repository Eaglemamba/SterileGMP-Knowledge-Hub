Learning Objectives

  • Understand why raw materials for ATMPs (advanced therapy medicinal products) pose more complex quality management challenges than those for conventional drugs, including diverse sourcing, inconsistent regulations and variable supplier maturity.
  • Grasp the scope of this document: all materials that contact the process stream in clinical and commercial manufacturing, including cell therapy, gene therapy, mRNA and tissue-engineered products.
  • Distinguish the definitions of primary, secondary and tertiary animal-derived and human-derived materials, and apply them correctly in real-world sourcing and risk assessment.
  • Become familiar with the core abbreviations used in this document (AAV, CAR, RUO, TSE, iPSC, etc.) as the language foundation for the sections that follow.
Introduction

Original Text

This document provides key "Points to Consider" for navigating the complexities of raw materials in the manufacturing of advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMP), aiming to guide stakeholders in establishing robust strategies for raw material management, promoting best practices, and supporting the safe and effective development and manufacturing of ATMPs.

The unique nature of ATMPs presents distinct challenges compared to traditional pharmaceuticals, demanding a focused approach to material selection, qualification, and supplier management.

Specifically addressed in this document are the intricacies arising from the fact that ATMP raw materials are often:

  • Animal- or human-derived
  • Not monographed (no pharmacopoeial standard)
  • Not manufactured for GMP applications (e.g., research-only grade / RUO)
  • Single-sourced and/or required to be used for aseptic manufacturing
"Very often, ATMP material suppliers and vendors are small, specialized companies with roots in academia that do not have a mature GMP quality system."

These characteristics contribute to a complex regulatory landscape, as regulations for ATMP materials are not globally harmonized. Variations in regulations exist among the:

  • U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)
  • European Medicines Agency (EMA)
  • Japan Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA)
  • Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S)
  • Individual country-specific laws to which companies must adhere

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is Materials Management So Complex for ATMPs?

Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) fall into four categories:

  • Gene therapy products: for example, AAV vector gene therapies and mRNA therapies
  • Somatic cell therapy products: for example, CAR-T and TIL therapies
  • Tissue-engineered products: for example, artificial skin and cartilage repair
  • Combined ATMPs: medical devices combined with cell or gene therapy

Compared with small-molecule drugs or conventional biologics, ATMP raw materials often come directly from living organisms (human or animal), lack pharmacopoeial monographs as an anchor for quality standards, and are in many cases available only to research use only (RUO) specifications. As a result, quality assurance is already difficult at the starting point.

Analogy: A CDMO Sourcing Perspective

Imagine you are buying ingredients for a three-star Michelin restaurant. A conventional pharmaceutical plant is like buying supermarket vegetables with certified origin and pesticide residue standards. Sourcing materials for ATMPs is more like ordering rare wild mushrooms directly from an individual mountain farmer: the supplier may be a retired professor growing the product in a home laboratory, with no ISO certification and no GMP facility, yet your patients depend entirely on the quality of that bottle of culture medium.

This is the fundamental reason PtC-13 exists: to give CDMOs and MAHs a systematic framework for thinking, so that patient safety can still be assured when the "farm-direct wild mushrooms" cannot be avoided.

Key Point: The Practical Impact of Regulatory Inconsistency

Today, regulatory requirements for ATMP materials differ significantly across four regulatory systems:

  • FDA (United States): relies mainly on 21 CFR Parts 210/211 and Part 1271, with the IND/BLA application as the core control framework
  • EMA (European Union): ATMPs are governed by Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007, with a dedicated Committee for Advanced Therapies (CAT)
  • PMDA (Japan): the JSBRM (Japanese Standards for Biological Raw Materials) is a uniquely Japanese requirement, with extremely strict documentation requirements for animal-derived materials
  • PIC/S: provides GMP guidance, but member authorities still have room for their own interpretation

A CDMO serving clients under FDA, EMA and PMDA oversight at the same time has to maintain several sets of compliance documents for the same batch of material. This is one of the core reasons PtC-13 stresses strategic materials management.

  3. PtC-13 notes that regulations are not harmonized globally. If your ATMP product needs to be filed for marketing in both the US (FDA) and the EU (EMA), which region-specific documents should your materials management strategy prepare in advance?
Scope

Original Text

This document applies to all process-stream-contacting raw materials, including:

  • Starting and ancillary materials
  • Excipients
  • Consumables
  • Container–closure systems

…for ATMPs both in clinical stage and commercial manufacture.

This includes but is not limited to:

  • Cell-free mRNA therapeutics
  • Viral vector-based in-vivo gene therapies
  • Human cells for autologous or allogeneic cell therapies
  • Tissue-engineered medicinal products

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Scope of "Process-Stream-Contacting" Materials

Process-stream-contacting raw materials are the control focus of PtC-13. The concept covers any material that may contact the product directly during the process, or affect product quality indirectly, including:

  • Starting materials: for example, patient or donor cells and virus seed stocks
  • Ancillary materials: for example, culture medium components, growth factors and transduction reagents
  • Excipients: components of the formulation
  • Consumables: for example, filters, single-use bags and tubing
  • Container-closure systems: the final storage container

Note that equipment that does not directly contact the process stream (such as building structure and HVAC) is outside the main scope of this document.

Analogy: The Spectrum of ATMP Product Types

The scope of PtC-13 covers the full spectrum of ATMP products. In order of increasing manufacturing complexity, they can be arranged as follows:

mRNA therapies (such as mRNA vaccine types) → AAV viral vector gene therapies → allogeneic cell therapies → autologous cell therapies (such as CAR-T)

The further to the right, the more biologically derived materials are used in the process, the more complex the process, and the greater the materials management challenge. Understanding where your product sits on this spectrum directly determines the depth of your materials risk assessment.

Key Point: Differences in Materials Strategy Between Clinical and Commercial Manufacturing

PtC-13 explicitly covers both the clinical stage and commercial manufacture, but in practice the materials strategies differ between the two:

  • Clinical stage: higher-risk RUO materials are accepted, but they must be supported by a documented risk assessment; supplier audits may use questionnaires in place of on-site audits
  • Commercial manufacture: full vendor qualification must be completed, and material specifications must be locked in with the regulatory filing documents

This phase-appropriate approach is the core philosophy of ATMP materials management and recurs throughout the later sections of PtC-13.

Practical Application: Inventorying the CDMO Materials Scope

As a QA head taking on an AAV gene therapy CDMO contract, you should inventory process materials in the following tiers when launching a materials management program:

  1. Tier 1: Starting materials - Do plasmid DNA and cell seed banks (such as HEK293 cells) have GMP-grade CoAs and CoOs?
  2. Tier 2: Ancillary materials for the culture process - Is the analysis of animal-derived components in the serum-free medium (Animal-Derived Component Free, ADCF statement) complete?
  3. Tier 3: Downstream purification consumables - Does the affinity chromatography resin contain animal-derived proteins (such as the Protein A/G ligand)?
  4. Tier 4: Formulation excipients and container closure - Are USP/EP pharmacopoeial-grade materials used? Is the container material compatibility testing documentation complete?

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Primary / Secondary / Tertiary Classification System

PtC-13 establishes a "three-tier contamination traceability classification system" for animal-derived and human-derived materials, one of the most important conceptual frameworks in this document. Understanding the differences among the three tiers is the basis for the risk assessments that follow:

  • Primary materials: materials taken directly from animals or the human body.
    Animal-derived examples: bovine serum albumin (BSA), porcine insulin, fetal bovine serum (FBS)
    Human-derived examples: human plasma albumin, human platelet lysate (hPL), patient PBMC
  • Secondary materials: animal- or human-derived materials were used in the manufacturing process, but the final product itself does not necessarily contain animal- or human-derived components.
    Examples: a recombinant protein produced in BSA-containing medium; an enzyme produced in medium containing animal components
  • Tertiary materials: the final product itself contains no animal- or human-derived components, but its manufacturing process used secondary materials.
    Examples: a chemical raw material synthesized with the aid of an animal-derived enzyme; an animal-free reagent produced using secondary materials

Analogy: The Coffee "Origin Traceability" Analogy

Consider the coffee supply chain as an analogy for the three-tier classification system:

  • Primary material = the coffee bean itself (taken directly from the plant). If you replace "animal" with "plant", green coffee beans are the direct source.
  • Secondary material = a latte brewed with animal milk (animal-derived raw material used in the process). The final liquid contains milk components.
  • Tertiary material = coffee candy made from the latte (after processing, the final product may no longer contain milk, but the process did use a milk-containing intermediate).

In ATMP manufacturing, a tertiary material may "look clean", but its process traceability must still be documented to assess the potential TSE/BSE or viral contamination risk. This is why suppliers must provide a complete CoO.

Key Point: The Regulatory Significance of CJD and TSE/BSE

Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD) belongs to the family of Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSE) and, like bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, commonly called "mad cow disease"), is a prion disease.

Why does PtC-13 define CJD as the first entry in the glossary? Because animal-derived materials (especially bovine-derived materials such as FBS) are the main potential route of TSE/BSE contamination. Regulators impose strict geographic-origin requirements on bovine-derived materials:

  • FDA: based on BSE risk categories, bovine-derived materials from high-risk countries require additional supporting documentation
  • EMA：Note for Guidance on Minimising the Risk of Transmitting Animal Spongiform Encephalopathy Agents via Human and Veterinary Medicinal Products
  • PMDA / JSBRM: the strictest requirements on the animal origin and geographic origin of biological raw materials

Therefore, if any bovine-derived material is used in your ATMP process, you must obtain the CoO (Certificate of Origin) for that material and assess the TSE risk. This is not only a GMP requirement but also a prerequisite for market access in many countries.

Classification Framework: Decision Tree for the Three-Tier Material Risk Classification

Practical Application: Material Classification Exercise

Classify the following materials commonly used in ATMP manufacturing as primary, secondary or tertiary animal-derived materials, and explain your reasoning:

  1. Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS): isolated directly from the blood of bovine fetuses → primary animal-derived material

Core Concept: Understanding ATMP Product-Type Abbreviations

The abbreviation list in PtC-13 reveals the main technology directions in the ATMP field. They are grouped below by therapeutic modality:

Gene therapy vectors:

  • AAV (adeno-associated virus): currently the most widely used in vivo gene therapy vector, as in Hemgenix for hemophilia and Zolgensma for spinal muscular atrophy
  • gRNA (guide RNA): a core component of CRISPR gene editing; the gRNA guides the Cas9 protein to cut at a specific DNA site

Cell therapy starting materials:

  • PBMC (peripheral blood mononuclear cells): collected from the blood of patients or donors; the cell starting material for CAR-T and TIL therapies
  • TIL (tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes): immune cells isolated from tumor tissue and used in cell therapy for solid tumors (such as melanoma)

Stem cell sources:

  • hESC (human embryonic stem cells): pluripotent stem cells; ethically controversial, and their use is tightly regulated
  • iPSC (induced pluripotent stem cells): reprogrammed from adult cells; a key platform technology for next-generation allogeneic cell therapy

Core Concept: Regulatory and Quality Document Abbreviations

CoA vs. CoO: the two most critical material documents:

  • CoA (Certificate of Analysis): records the measured quality data of a material (such as purity, potency and appearance) and is the quality basis for material release
  • CoO (Certificate of Origin): records the geographic origin of the animal- or human-derived components of a material; it is the regulatory basis for TSE/BSE risk assessment and customs declaration

In ATMP manufacturing, the CoO is just as important as the CoA. A batch of animal-derived medium without a CoO may be unusable for GMP manufacturing in some jurisdictions, even if its quality data are perfect.

IND vs. BLA：

  • IND (Investigational New Drug): the US FDA application for clinical trials; material compliance requirements are relatively lenient (phase-appropriate)
  • BLA (Biologics License Application): the US FDA marketing application for biological products; materials must complete full qualification

Key Point: What Is JSBRM and Why Does It Matter?

JSBRM (Japanese Standards for Biological Raw Materials) are the specific requirements of Japan's PMDA for biological raw materials used in biopharmaceutical manufacturing, issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW).

The core requirements of JSBRM include:

  • Complete documentation for all animal-derived and human-derived materials
  • Manufacturers must establish and maintain documentation of the "origin and history of manufacture" of biological raw materials
  • TSE risk assessment requirements stricter than those of the EMA or FDA

For ATMP CDMOs planning to enter the Japanese market, JSBRM compliance is often the most time-consuming up-front preparation work. It should be built into the materials sourcing strategy from the start, rather than remedied only when the Japanese application is filed.

Key Point: The Compliance Boundary of RUO Materials

RUO (Research Use Only) is the most common compliance challenge in ATMP materials management. The main problems with RUO-grade materials are:

  • Production environment not under GMP oversight: the supplier may have no GMP facility certification
  • Looser specifications: the CoA may lack some critical tests required for GMP manufacturing
  • Greater lot-to-lot variability: lack of strict process control
  • Regulatory filing risk: the FDA and EMA have very low acceptance of RUO materials in commercial applications

However, in early clinical development of ATMPs, many critical materials (such as specific cytokines and rare growth factors) are currently available only as RUO grade. Later sections of PtC-13 provide risk mitigation strategies for when RUO materials cannot be avoided (such as enhanced incoming testing and supplier audits).

Analogy: ATMP Materials as the "Bricks of the Last Line of Defense"

In conventional oral drug manufacturing, small quality deviations in raw materials may be absorbed by later process steps (such as recrystallization and sterilization). ATMP manufacturing is more like a building with no chance of repair: if the bricks at the very bottom (raw materials) are already cracked (for example, TSE contamination, or lot-to-lot variability that lowers cell viability), the whole building (final efficacy and safety) cannot be saved.

This is the starting point of the entire PtC-13 document: raw materials management is not the last step of ATMP manufacturing but the first and most critical line of defense.

Section Summary

Animal/human derivation, lack of pharmacopoeial standards, RUO grade, single sourcing, insufficient supplier GMP maturity and non-harmonized global regulations combine into six overlapping factors that create a unique risk environment.
  • Scope: PtC-13 covers all materials that contact the process stream (starting materials, ancillary materials, excipients, consumables, container-closure systems), applies to both clinical and commercial manufacturing, and covers mRNA, AAV gene therapy, cell therapy and tissue-engineered products.

  • Decision hierarchy: patient safety (TSE risk, sterility assurance) always takes priority over commercial considerations, and materials management is the first line of defense in ATMP manufacturing, not the last step.

Topic 1. ATMP Material Categories

Q 1.1. Which ATMP material categories exist and how are they defined?

There is no health authority guideline that clearly defines ATMP material categories nor an aligned definition across countries or regions. The material categories listed in Table 1.1-1🔍 reflect existing regulatory guidance relevant to ATMPs and common industry vernacular.

The term critical raw material is used by European Union (EU) health authorities to describe materials critical for product quality in their Guidelines on Good Manufacturing Practice Specific to Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products. This guideline does not provide a clear definition of a "critical raw material," however, but lists a few examples such as sera, growth factors, enzymes (such as trypsin), cytokines, and gene-editing reagents. ATMP sponsors may define the criticality of raw materials based on their specific products and internal procedures.

The term material is a general term used to describe manufacturing ingredients, raw materials (e.g., starting and ancillary materials, excipients, primary packaging) consumed in the process that may or may not be present in the final drug product. The material categories given in Table 1.1-1🔍, as well as "critical raw materials," fall under "materials."

The term animal-derived raw material or "animal-derived material" is widely used, but its definition has not yet been globally aligned by health authorities. PDA Technical Report No. 83 and European Pharmacopoeia, Chapter 5.2.12 provide definitions, which the authors consolidated into the following:

Primary Animal-Derived Materials

Any material derived directly from animal sources

  • Bovine serum
  • Porcine-derived trypsin
  • Animal-tissue-derived hydrolysates

Secondary Animal-Derived Materials

Any material manufactured using processes that include materials derived from animal sources

  • Recombinant protein produced in an E. coli fermentation that uses a medium containing bovine tryptone
  • Recombinant protein expressed in plants that are exposed to bovine manure fertilizer
  • Recombinant CHO-cell culture products

Tertiary Animal-Derived Materials

Any material free of animal-derived components that has been manufactured using secondary animal-derived materials

  • Tubing sets or other polymeric components that were manufactured using slip agents derived from animal fat (stearates)
  • Solvent that has been purified using bone-derived, activated charcoal

The definitions provided above for primary and secondary animal-derived materials are widely applicable to human-derived (raw) materials as well.

Primary Human-Derived Materials

Any material derived directly from human sources

  • Human donor cells
  • Human serum
  • Human Serum Albumin

Secondary Human-Derived Materials

Any material manufactured using processes that include materials derived from human sources

  • Recombinant protein produced in a human cell line

Tertiary Human-Derived Materials

Any material free of human-derived components that has been manufactured using secondary human-derived materials

Any of the material categories presented in Table 1.1-1🔍 could also be subclassified as primary, secondary, or tertiary animal-derived or primary, secondary, or tertiary human-derived materials.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The ATMP Material Classification System

The first major insight of PtC-13 is that there is currently no globally harmonized classification standard for ATMP materials. The ten categories listed in Table 1.1-1🔍 are a framework compiled by PDA from existing regulatory guidance (EMA, FDA, USP, BioPhorum) and common industry terminology, not taken from any single official guideline.

For a CDMO, this means you must proactively establish and defend your own material classification definitions in the client's IND/CTA documents, and cannot assume that regulators already share a consensus.

Quick reference to the ten categories:

  • Starting Materials - materials used to manufacture the active substance (such as plasmid DNA and patient leukapheresis material)
  • Ancillary Raw Materials - used in the process but not part of the final product (such as feeder cells and growth factors)
  • Excipients - inactive ingredients that are deliberately added (such as DMSO as a cryoprotectant)
  • Novel Excipients - used for the first time in a given route of administration (additional safety studies required)
  • Single-Use Components - contact the process stream directly and are discarded after use
  • GMP Support Materials - do not contact the product directly (such as cleaning agents)
  • Laboratory Reagents - used for analytical testing; do not contact the process stream
  • Primary Packaging / CCS - contacts the final drug product directly (such as cryobags)
  • Process Aids - not consumed and reusable (such as chromatography columns)
  • Process Gasses - contact the product or primary container surfaces directly

Key Terminology: Differing Definitions of Critical Raw Material (CRM)

EU (EMA) view: "Critical Raw Material" is a formal term in the EU GMP ATMP guideline, referring to raw materials that are critical to product quality. However, the EMA documents themselves give no precise definition and only list examples (serum, growth factors, trypsin, cytokines, gene-editing reagents). Manufacturers must define for themselves which materials are critical.

US (FDA) view: the FDA does not use the specific term "critical raw material" but a broader "materials" framework that emphasizes a risk-based approach.

Practical impact: in a CDMO environment filing in both regions (BLA + MAA), your CMC documents must satisfy both the EU requirements for CRMs and the FDA's expectations for risk-based materials control. The two frameworks need to be presented in a coordinated way within the same Quality Agreement.

Analogy: Ingredient Classification in a Restaurant

Imagine the kitchen of a Michelin-starred restaurant:

  • Starting Materials = the main ingredients (steak, lobster) - they directly determine the quality of the dish and are controlled most strictly
  • Ancillary Materials = lubricant for cooking tools - used during cooking but never reach the final plate
  • Excipients = seasonings (salt, sauces) - deliberately added to the finished dish
  • GMP Support Materials = cleaning agents and disinfectants - they never touch the food but keep the kitchen hygienic
  • Process Aids = sieves and strainers - they help the process but are not consumed

Just as a restaurant traces its main ingredients (origin, farm certification) more strictly than its cleaning agents, the classification of ATMP materials determines how strict a quality control you apply to each material.

Key Point: The Regulatory Significance of the Animal-Derived / Human-Derived Three-Tier Grading

The Primary / Secondary / Tertiary three-tier grading is a risk framework specific to ATMPs. The key points are:

  • Primary (such as bovine serum and porcine trypsin): taken directly from animals; the highest TSE/viral safety risk; requires a full supplier audit + viral safety data
  • Secondary (such as a recombinant protein produced in medium containing bovine tryptone): indirect animal components; lower risk, but traceability and documentation are still required
  • Tertiary (such as tubing made with an animal-fat stearate as a slip agent): contains no animal components itself, but the manufacturing process contacted secondary materials. Many suppliers overlook this category, but regulators (especially the EMA) require it to be included in the risk assessment

Human-derived materials (such as patient autologous cells and human serum albumin) follow the same three-tier framework and also involve Chain of Identity (source traceability) requirements; see Topic 4 for details.

Practical Application: A CDMO Material Classification Decision Tree

When a CDMO receives a new AAV gene therapy project, the first step in reviewing the bill of materials is to label each material according to Table 1.1-1🔍:

  • Plasmid DNA → Starting Material (explicitly defined by the EMA)
  • HEK293 cell bank → Starting Material (host cells for AAV manufacture)
  • Serum (FBS/FCS) → Ancillary Raw Material + Primary Animal-Derived (highest risk level)
  • PF68 (Pluronic F-68, used in AAV formulation) → classified as a Novel Excipient if the route of administration is entirely new
  • Bioprocess bag → Single-Use Component
  • Chromatography column → Process Aid
  • Endotoxin test kit → Laboratory Reagent

Once classified correctly, map each category to the supplier requirement level in the quality agreement (CoA specifications, audit frequency, backup supplier strategy).

Topic 2. Defining an ATMP Material Strategy

Q 2.1. What are key considerations when defining an ATMP material strategy?

Defining an ATMP material strategy means to constantly maintain a balance between imperfections regarding material quality and supply standards and potential fast-track commercialization (e.g., clinical Phase 2 as a pivotal trial). This requires a risk-based approach that is both comprehensive and phase-appropriate and considers all aspects of material quality, including but not limited to manufacturer controls, contamination risk, and potential for process-related impurities. The objective is to always strive to maximize knowledge about the materials used at all stages. Focusing on the following key considerations has shown to be helpful.

Human- and Animal-Derived Materials

For many ATMPs (e.g., cell-based therapies), the use of animal-/human-derived materials is unavoidable during early clinical trials, and health authorities accept these materials if safety requirements for adventitious agents (e.g., microorganisms, viruses, prions) are met. However, sponsors are advised to work on replacing these materials in parallel with the product development, as they often pose a risk to eventual commercial manufacturing in terms of supply chain, batch-to-batch variability, and transmissible agents. It is important to note though that for some applications, no chemically defined replacements for animal-/human-derived materials have yet been found (e.g., human serum used for stem cell differentiation) as the development of these replacements requires extensive development activities.

Research Use Only Materials

Non-GMP material grades, such as research use only (RUO), can be acceptable for early clinical trials when higher-grade material is not openly available, but RUO material should be replaced with higher grades for later stages if possible. A potential replacement strategy could be to evaluate higher grades, once available, for their compatibility with the manufacturing process in parallel with the ongoing clinical manufacturing. Health authorities, such as EMA, may require companies to follow cGMP or GMP principles for starting materials and/or critical materials, and excipients for clinical phases and commercial manufacturing.

"Defining an ATMP material strategy means to constantly maintain a balance between imperfections regarding material quality and supply standards and potential fast-track commercialization."

Proprietary Materials

Certain key ATMP materials can be proprietary (e.g., cryoprotectants used as excipients, cell culture media), which complicates regulatory filing since sponsors do not have access to critical information associated with the intellectual property for the material (e.g., manufacturing method, full ingredients list). However, these materials are often not replaceable, and sponsors must find ways to mitigate the risk, for example, by submitting a Letter of Authorization to the FDA to access a supplier Master File.

Chain of Identity / Chain of Custody

Manufacturers should have traceability systems in place to avoid material mix-ups and cross-contamination, as these could have severe consequences for patient safety. Especially for cell-based therapies, manufacturers must work with suppliers to ensure the chain of identity for human donor material in the entire supply chain (see Topic 4).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Four Pillars of an ATMP Materials Strategy

The central question of Q2.1 is that ATMP development is a continuous tug-of-war between "perfect quality" and "clinical speed". The answer PtC-13 gives is a risk-based, phase-appropriate framework, embodied in four pillars:

  1. Replacement pathway for human/animal-derived materials - accept reality and plan the exit
  2. RUO material upgrade strategy - qualify GMP-grade alternatives in parallel manufacturing
  3. Risk mitigation for proprietary materials - Letter of Authorization + backup suppliers
  4. Chain of Identity / Chain of Custody - especially critical in autologous therapies

Adventitious agents: include microorganisms, viruses and prions, and are the core safety risk of animal/human-derived materials. Regulators accept the use of such materials on the condition that the manufacturer has fully assessed and controlled these risks.

Analogy: A Student Growing into a Working Professional

The phase-appropriate approach is like a student growing into a working professional:

  • University years (early clinical, Phase 1): you may enter with a student discount ticket (RUO materials) as long as you can demonstrate safety. Mistakes in graduate school are cheap, so trial and error is allowed.
  • Graduating from graduate school (Phase 2 pivotal trial): professional credentials (GMP-grade materials) start to be required. You can no longer buy software licenses with a student ID.
  • Full employment (commercial manufacture): the full set of professional standards (thorough characterization, backup suppliers, supplier audits) is indispensable.

The key insight is that the upgrade is foreseeable and should be prepared in parallel early, rather than scrambling to replace materials just before the Phase 3 filing, when a process change may require repeating bridging studies at very high cost.

Key Point: Single-Source Materials Are the Biggest Landmine for a CDMO

ATMP manufacturing involves many irreplaceable single-source materials (for example, a specific cGMP-grade IL-2 cytokine or a specific brand of CliniMACS magnetic bead system), which pose multiple risks for a CDMO:

  • Supply interruption risk: pandemics, raw material shortages and supplier bankruptcy can all interrupt supply. During COVID-19, several ATMP programs were delayed by breaks in the upstream biological raw material chain.
  • Lot-to-lot variability risk: animal-derived materials (such as FBS) vary widely in quality between lots, which can affect cell expansion efficiency.
  • Regulatory filing risk: if a material is a proprietary formulation, the supplier cannot disclose the full composition to regulators, and access to the supplier's master file (Master File / DMF) depends on a Letter of Authorization mechanism.
  • Intellectual property dispute risk: some materials used in ATMP processes are patent-protected (such as Miltenyi magnetic beads); if the supplier changes the license terms, the CDMO may be unable to continue using them.

Best practice: before signing a long-term service agreement with a sponsor, the CDMO should complete a dual-sourcing feasibility assessment for critical materials, or at least obtain an adequate safety stock strategy.

Key Point: The Regulatory Harmonization Gap

PtC-13 states explicitly that significant gaps remain among the major regulators, and CDMOs must understand these differences:

  • EMA: the strictest; explicitly requires GMP principles to apply to starting materials and critical materials (even at the clinical stage)
  • FDA: relatively flexible and risk-based; allows non-GMP materials in early clinical stages, but with adequate justification
  • PMDA (Japan): follows the ICH Q7/Q10 framework; the GMP requirement pathway for starting materials is rather unique
  • PIC/S: closely aligned with EU GMP; many Asian countries (including Taiwan's TFDA) adopt the PIC/S framework

For a Taiwan CDMO serving both European and US clients, the material quality agreement must be designed to be "compatible with the strictest regulator", that is, designed on the basis of EMA requirements while also ensuring compliance with FDA expectations.

Practical Application: A Parallel Qualification Strategy for CDMO RUO Material Upgrades

Scenario: a CDMO is manufacturing for a Phase 1 CAR-T cell therapy program and currently uses RUO-grade anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies for T cell activation. The EMA has raised a question and asked for a plan to upgrade to GMP grade.

Recommended strategy (Phase-Appropriate Parallel Validation):

  1. Identify one or two suppliers with GMP-grade antibodies of the same type (such as Miltenyi and Thermo Fisher GMP grade)
  2. While Phase 1 manufacturing continues, start a parallel comparability study (small-scale comparability run) to assess the effect of the GMP-grade material on T cell fold expansion, activation markers and CAR transduction efficiency
  3. If the GMP-grade material passes comparability, complete the material change filing (Type II variation or Prior Approval Supplement) before entering Phase 2
  4. Include the parallel qualification results in the Phase 2 IND/CTA submission to show the regulators the upgrade roadmap proactively

This strategy avoids the risk of "waiting until Phase 3 to change materials" without affecting the current clinical manufacturing schedule.

  1. An iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte therapy used medium containing human serum for stem cell differentiation in Phase 1. The investigators say there is currently no chemically defined alternative. As CDMO QA head, how would you justify the use of this material in the EMA IMP Dossier?
  2. A supplier's proprietary cryopreservation medium is the only formulation usable for an ATMP, but the supplier refuses to disclose the full composition. Besides using a Letter of Authorization (LOA) to access the DMF with the FDA, what other risk mitigation measures can be adopted?
  3. In autologous CAR-T cell manufacturing, what consequences could result if the Chain of Identity is broken (for example, the label falls off a patient's leukapheresis material)? Analyze from three angles: patient safety, regulatory compliance and the CDMO's commercial liability.

  • No globally harmonized material classification standard: Table 1.1-1🔍 provides an industry-consensus framework of ten ATMP material categories, but regulators in different countries still interpret the definitions differently. The CDMO must establish and justify the classification basis in the quality agreement.
  • The EU "Critical Raw Material" definition is vague but legally forceful: the EU GMP ATMP guideline uses this term without a precise definition, so manufacturers must determine criticality proactively based on their own product risk. The US FDA uses a risk-based materials framework; the wording differs but the direction of expectation is the same.
  • The animal/human-derived three-tier grading is the cornerstone of TSE/viral safety assessment: Primary (direct source) > Secondary (process contact) > Tertiary (manufacturing aid). Risk decreases with each tier, but all tiers must be included in the risk assessment documentation.
  • The core of the phase-appropriate strategy is "parallel preparation": RUO materials are acceptable in early clinical stages, but comparability studies of GMP-grade alternatives should be carried out at the same time, to avoid bridging studies and filing delays caused by late material changes.
  • Single-source materials are the biggest hidden risk in the CDMO supply chain: proprietary materials (cryoprotectants, special cytokines) are protected by intellectual property, so complete composition information is hard to obtain and must be managed through the DMF/LOA mechanism. Topic 3 — Risk-Based Material Qualification: Overview & Q3.1

Original Text

Topic 3. Risk-Based Material Qualification

ATMP material qualification follows a risk-based approach (see PDA Technical Report No. 81: Cell-Based Therapy Control Strategy for risk assessment examples). Besides the general requirements, which apply to all clinical stages and commercial manufacturing, several requirements differentiate between early- and late-stage clinical to commercial transition. These differences must be considered when developing a comprehensive strategy that is also phase appropriate. Further, the sponsor of a clinical trial (manufacturer) is responsible for managing critical material information, like the Certificate of Analysis (CoA), Certificate of Origin, and material numbers, from material suppliers because health authorities require this information in regulatory filings to ensure traceability and consistent quality of material. Appropriate supplier management, along with phase-appropriate material testing, where required, are the key elements of qualification.

Q 3.1. What are key considerations for ATMP material qualification and specification setting?

Critical material attributes should be established based on the intended material use and final product quality. Material specifications are intended to ensure that materials meet the compliance requirements of the appropriate market and to ensure materials consistently meet the expected quality. Further, technical information regarding the composition, formulation, and characterization of critical materials is crucial for the assessment of suitability and safety. The level of risk related to the presence of organic and other impurities (e.g., nitrosamine, melamine, heavy metals, human allergens) must also be identified and evaluated.

For ATMPs that fall under PIC/S Guide to Good Manufacturing Practice for Medicinal Products regulation, Annex 2A, Part A, Section 5.23 provides additional guidance. For more information on the qualification and specification of plasmids and vectors, see PDA Points to Consider No. 11: Development, Classification, Manufacture, Control, and Testing of Plasmids and Vectors Used in ATMP Production.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Risk-Based Qualification Framework

Risk-Based Material Qualification: Instead of applying the same level of rigor to every material, testing resources are allocated in tiers according to the impact of the material on patient safety and product quality.

Phase-Appropriate: Material compliance expectations for a Phase I clinical study can be considerably more relaxed than for commercial manufacturing, and they step up as the program advances. This is how the lifecycle management concept of ICH Q8/Q9/Q10 is put into practice at the material level.

Critical Material Attributes (CMA): The counterpart of the product CQA (critical quality attribute). A CMA is a characteristic the material itself must meet, for example the biological activity of a specific cytokine or the purity and sequence integrity of a plasmid.

Analogy — Tiered Ingredient Purchasing in a Restaurant

Picture the ingredient purchasing strategy of a high-end restaurant. Table salt (not of human/animal origin, low risk) only needs the supplier CoA. Beef steak (animal origin, medium risk) calls for a certificate of origin and animal health inspection. The chef's special raw fish (high risk, eaten as is) is tested for microbes by the restaurant itself on every delivery.

The logic of ATMP material qualification is exactly the same: the higher the risk (the closer to the patient and the harder to process afterwards), the stricter the qualification and the more testing is done. That is the essence of "risk-based": spend limited testing resources where they matter most.

Key Point — The CDMO's Supplier Management Obligations

PtC-13 states clearly that the clinical trial sponsor (that is, the manufacturer) is responsible for obtaining and managing the CoA, Certificate of Origin and material lot numbers from suppliers, because health authorities will always require traceability documentation when reviewing an IND/IMPD or BLA/MAA.

CDMO practice recommendation: when accepting a new ATMP client, specify in the Quality Agreement when the client must provide these documents, so that missing documentation does not delay batch release or regulatory submissions.

Practical Application — A Tiered Material Qualification Strategy for a CDMO

A CDMO handles several ATMP programs at the same time. How should material qualification be planned? We suggest the following three tiers:

  • Tier 1 (highest risk): Materials of human/animal origin (FBS, human serum) → full testing of every lot; the supplier must provide a TSE/BSE certificate.
  • Tier 2 (medium risk): Reagents in direct contact with the process (cytokines, plasmids) → ID testing on receipt, with other parameters accepted on the basis of the CoA.
  • Tier 3 (low risk): Chemically defined medium components (no animal origin) → accept on the basis of the supplier CoA and audit the supplier periodically.

This strategy mirrors the tiering concept (Tiers 1-3) of USP <1043> Ancillary Materials.

Q3.2 General Requirements — Critical Material Attribute: Biological Safety

Original Text

Q 3.2. What are the General Requirements?

Prior to setting up the manufacturing process and whenever a change of the respective material is implemented, a quality risk management (QRM) process should assess the overall risk to the entire manufacturing process and the resulting product.

Only materials that have been released by the Quality Unit that are within their expiration or retest date should be used.

Critical Material Attribute: Biological Safety

Ensuring patient safety and preventing transmission of infectious diseases is a general requirement for ATMP materials that applies to all clinical phases and commercial manufacturing.

Human- or animal-derived materials (see Section 3.1 for a detailed definition) are tightly controlled by health authorities, as they pose the highest risk of transmitting pathogens to patients of all ATMP materials. The agreement between the ATMP manufacturer (or the Marketing Authorization Holder) and the supplier, including blood and tissue establishments, should contain clear provisions about the transfer of information. This should include the results of tests performed by the supplier, traceability data, and transmission of healthy donor information.

Controls can include, but are not limited to:

  • Testing for human and animal viruses and other adventitious agents like mycoplasma and parasites.
  • Virus inactivation (e.g., heat inactivation, low pH treatment, filtration, gamma-irradiation), if possible.
  • Controlled sourcing and screening of human and animal materials to reduce the risk of CJD/TSE/BSE transmission.
Note: Based on their inherent risk profile, autologous cell therapies are usually exempted from donor (patient) testing, per 21 CFR 1271.

Many suppliers of ATMP materials cannot guarantee sterility assurance compliant with Eur. Ph. 5.1.1, EU Annex 1, EMA Guideline on the sterilization of the medicinal product, active substance, excipient and primary container, or similar regulations. Consequently, these raw materials should be considered as nonsterile even if they have been 0.2 µm-filtered. Sponsors may mitigate the risk associated with these nonsterile raw materials for aseptic ATMP manufacturing by considering the following aspects:

  • Nonsterile materials should be added to the aseptic process via validated 0.2 µm filtration steps followed by a filter-integrity test. A generic filter validation per the ASTM F838-20 standard or equivalent is acceptable.
  • Nonsterile raw materials should be tested for sterility (e.g., USP <71>) or compendial microbial enumeration testing (TAMC/TYMC) (e.g., USP <61>) with tight limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Dual Safety Requirement in Q3.2

The "general requirements" of Q3.2 actually cover two quite different aspects of safety:

  • Biological Safety: Preventing the transmission of human/animal pathogens (viruses, TSE/BSE, mycoplasma) to the patient. This is a biological risk.
  • Sterility Assurance: Preventing microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) from contaminating the product during manufacture. This is a process risk.

The two must not be confused: a "sterile" material can still carry a TSE agent (prion), and a material with a TSE certificate does not necessarily meet the sterility assurance expectations of Eur. Ph. 5.1.1.

Analogy — TSE/BSE Is Like the "Mad Cow Problem" in Food Safety

TSE (Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies) and BSE (Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy, commonly known as "mad cow disease") are caused by prions, misfolded proteins that cannot be eliminated by ordinary sterilization methods (autoclaving, gamma irradiation).

By analogy: even if cooking a piece of beef at high temperature kills all the bacteria, eating it can still cause disease if the animal carried prions. That is why animal-derived materials require "controlled sourcing", ensuring from the start that the raw material comes from herds free of BSE risk. For ATMPs, FBS (fetal bovine serum) is the most common source of TSE/BSE risk, and the supplier must provide a TSE/BSE certificate stating the country of origin (for example, New Zealand and Australia are recognized as low-risk regions).

Key Point — The Limits of Sterility Assurance under Eur. Ph. 5.1.1

This is a very important practical reminder from PtC-13: most ATMP material suppliers cannot guarantee compliance with the sterility assurance standard of Eur. Ph. 5.1.1, even when they provide a 0.2 µm filtration certificate (the standard requires validation with a specific biological indicator (BI), not filtration alone).

These materials should therefore be treated as "nonsterile raw materials". This has a major impact on GMP operations at a CDMO:

  • Before these materials enter the sterile process, they must pass through a validated 0.2 µm filtration step plus a filter integrity test.
  • Alternatively, perform USP <71> sterility testing or USP <61> microbial enumeration testing (TAMC/TYMC) on these materials and set strict limits.

Note: ASTM F838-20 is the standard for validating bacterial retention of filters. It replaces the traditional pyrogen validation (the Eur. Ph. 5.1.1 model) and is an accepted, generally applicable filter validation method for ATMP materials.

Caveats on the Autologous Exemption

The rule that "autologous cell therapies are generally exempt from donor (patient) testing" comes from 21 CFR 1271. The logic is that patients receive their own cells, so they already bear the risk of disease transmission and infectious disease screening of the donor (the patient) is unnecessary.

However, this exemption applies only to donor (patient) testing. It does not waive the safety requirements for other materials (such as medium components and cytokines). A CDMO running an autologous program should pay close attention to the boundary of this exemption.

Original Text

Specific Safety Considerations for Allogeneic Therapies

For allogeneic therapies, testing at various levels (e.g., donor cells, cell banks, during drug substance/drug product manufacture) can address additional safety concerns from donor cells not covered by donor testing at the first timing of donation. Ensuring donor consent for commercial usage is recommended; otherwise, it may become an issue at the timing of commercialization if it is not fully agreed upon. For allogeneic ATMP manufacturing, which falls under the PIC/S GMP regulation, detailed guidance on seed lot and cell banking systems is given in Annex 2a, Sections 5.30 to 5.38.

ID Testing

Verification of identity is required for all raw materials that will be used to manufacture drug products; the requirements of global health authorities range from testing to the full specifications of the manufacturer to accepting on physical inspection and/or documentation. U.S. regulation 21 CFR 211.84(d)(2) requires a specific identity test, where available, to be performed for each lot of raw material received. In instances where specific tests are not available, surrogate testing to evaluate relevant characteristics (e.g. size, purity, spectral profile) may be combined to serve as verification of identity.

EU regulation EudraLex - Vol. 4 allows acceptance of materials based on the manufacturer's CoA where all risks are understood or adequately mitigated by other means; however, it does recommend identity-testing for critical raw materials (e.g., excipients), see Table 3.2-2🔍 and Figure 3.2-1🔍 for an overview. Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Material Testing Differences between Autologous and Allogeneic Therapies

Autologous therapy: The patient is the donor and the cell source is known. The risk of disease transmission "returns to the patient", so under 21 CFR 1271 donor testing is generally exempt. Manufacturing materials (medium and so on) still require full qualification.

Allogeneic therapy: Cells from one donor treat multiple patients, which greatly increases the risk of disease transmission. In addition to donor screening at the initial donation, multi-layered safety testing is required during cell bank establishment and during drug manufacturing.

This is why the material testing requirements for allogeneic therapies are far stricter than for autologous therapies: a single contaminated Master Cell Bank could affect thousands of future patients.

Analogy — The "One-to-Many" Risk Multiplier of Allogeneic Therapy

Imagine a blood bank. If one bag of blood is contaminated with HIV, only the one patient who receives that bag is harmed (similar to autologous). But if an allogeneic CAR-T cell bank is contaminated and the cells it produces are distributed to 500 patients, 500 patients may be harmed.

This is the fundamental reason PtC-13 stresses that allogeneic therapies need "multi-layered testing (donor cells → cell banks → DS/DP manufacturing)": the earlier a problem is found, the fewer patients are affected and the lower the cost.

For a CDMO, taking on an allogeneic ATMP program means building a more complete cell bank quality system (in line with PIC/S Annex 2A §5.30-5.38).

Key Point — The Commercialization Trap of Donor Consent

PtC-13 specifically reminds that donor consent for commercial usage must be ensured. This is often overlooked in early clinical trials but can become an obstacle at the time of a commercial application (BLA/MAA).

CDMO practice recommendation: the Donation Agreement for an allogeneic ATMP program should explicitly include a consent clause for commercial manufacturing use. If cells donated during Phase I lack this clause, obtaining consent again later can be very difficult in practice (donor deceased, lost to contact, and so on).

Core Concept — The Philosophical Difference in ID Testing between the US and the EU

US 21 CFR 211.84(d)(2) — Mandatory Specific Identity Test:

Every lot of raw material received must undergo at least one specific identity test. If no specific test is available, a combination of substitute tests (size, purity, spectral profile) may be used. This is a mandatory requirement and cannot be replaced by a CoA.

EU EudraLex Vol. 4 — Risk-Based CoA Acceptance:

Materials may be accepted on the basis of the supplier CoA (provided the risks are adequately assessed and appropriate mitigations are in place), but ID testing of critical raw materials (such as excipients) is recommended. This is a recommendation, which leaves more room for flexibility.

Key Point — The Special Exemption for USP <1043> Tier 1

Under USP <1043> (Ancillary Materials), a Tier 1 ancillary material is a drug or medical device already approved by FDA for another use (for example, an FDA-approved injectable excipient). When used as a raw material in ATMP manufacturing, it may be accepted directly on the basis of the supplier CoA, without confirmatory testing of the results.

This is one of the few exceptions in the US system that allows the mandatory ID test requirement to be skipped. The logic is that FDA has already reviewed these materials (for their original approved use), so the manufacturer can reasonably rely on their quality system. The exemption has a strict precondition, however: the material must be of FDA approved drug or medical device grade, not merely backed by a supplier statement.

  1. Confirm the target market of the program (US / EU / both)
  2. Confirm the clinical phase (Phase I → US exempt; Phase II+ → US mandatory ID test)
  3. Classify the material (non-human/non-animal vs human blood vs animal origin)
  4. Is it USP <1043> Tier 1? If yes → may be accepted on the CoA (US only)
  5. Is there a specific identity test method? If yes → perform it; if no → design a combination of substitute tests
  6. Document the basis for the decision (regulatory citation + risk assessment) in the material specification or the QRM documentation

Core Concept — The Particular ATMP Challenges of Sampling and Retention Strategy

In traditional drug manufacturing, retaining samples (retention samples) is routine and presents no special difficulty. ATMP materials, however, pose two unique challenges:

1. Very limited quantity of starting material (such as autologous cells): For one autologous CAR-T treatment, the patient's leukapheresis collection may be only enough to make a single batch, leaving no surplus for retention. 2. Limits on freeze-thaw cycles for frozen materials: Many ATMP materials (such as cytokines and viral vectors) are stored frozen, and every freeze-thaw cycle reduces quality. We recommend dedicated QC vials so that QC sampling does not cause additional freeze-thaw damage to the main batch.

Early clinical (Phase I) is like a new graduate just entering the workplace: the company allows a learning curve and does not expect the full standard of a mature employee from the start (GMP exemption).

RUO (Research Use Only) materials are one of the most common sources of risk in early ATMP development. The position of PtC-13 is: avoid them where possible, but if unavoidable, a formal risk assessment must be performed.

Key Point — Commercial Material Specifications Must Not Simply Copy the Supplier's

PtC-13 specifically stresses that at the commercial stage the internal material specification should not be a copy of the supplier specification unless justified.

The reason: supplier specifications are designed for the supplier's general customer base. For example, a cytokine supplier's specification may only require "biological activity ≥ 80%", while your particular ATMP manufacturing process may need "biological activity ≥ 95%" to ensure the product CQAs are met. Accepting the supplier specification as is amounts to giving up process-specific quality control.

CDMO recommendation: during process development (PD), systematically collect material CoA data (especially lot-to-lot variation in critical parameters) as the scientific basis for setting internal material specifications.

Core Concept — The Importance of Leachables and Extractables

Extractables (E): Chemicals extracted from contact materials under extreme conditions (such as strong solvents or high temperature). They are used to understand the potential chemical risks in the worst case.

Leachables (L): Chemicals that actually migrate from contact materials into the drug product under normal process conditions. They directly affect patient safety.

For ATMPs, cryopreservation solutions (cryoprotectants) containing DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) are a scenario that calls for particular attention to E&L, because DMSO is a strong solvent that can markedly increase the migration of chemicals from plastic containers. Regulators may require these study data in late-stage clinical/commercial applications.

  • Risk-based framework: ATMP material qualification assigns testing requirements of differing rigor according to the biological risk tier of the material source (non-human/non-animal → human blood → human tissue → animal), with reference to the risk assessment examples in PDA TR81.
  • Two dimensions of biological safety: TSE/BSE/CJD risk must be managed through "controlled sourcing" (prions cannot be eliminated by ordinary sterilization); for sterility assurance, note that ATMP materials generally do not meet Eur. Ph. 5.1.1, and the risk must be mitigated through validated 0.2 µm filtration plus the ASTM F838-20 standard.
  • The four material categories of Table 3.2-1🔍: non-human/non-animal origin (lowest risk, general GMP), human blood (21 CFR 610/640, Ph. Eur. 5.2.12), human tissue/cells (most complex; donor eligibility + multi-layered testing), animal origin (9 CFR 113, ICH Q5A, TSE control).
  • Key US vs EU differences in ID testing: US 21 CFR 211.84(d)(2) mandates at least 1 specific ID test per lot (exempt in Phase I, applicable from Phase II); EU EudraLex Vol. 4 §7.15 allows CoA acceptance after risk assessment, with ID testing recommended.   • Autologous vs allogeneic differences: Autologous therapies are exempt from donor testing under 21 CFR 1271; allogeneic therapies need multi-layered testing (donor cells → cell banks → DS/DP manufacturing), and donor consent for commercial use must be obtained.
  • Phase-appropriate escalation path: In Phase I, RUO materials may be used (with a risk assessment) and supplier specifications relied on; from Phase II, ID testing is mandatory and materials move to GMP grade; before commercialization, qualification of at least 3 lots of material (or concurrent qualification meeting the 3 conditions) must be completed, independent internal specifications established and E&L studies completed.

Container-Closure Systems for ATMPs

ATMP final containers span a range of formats, each with distinct considerations:

Sterility Assurance for Single-Use Components

Many suppliers of ATMP materials cannot guarantee sterility assurance compliant with Eur. Ph. 5.1.1, EU Annex 1, EMA Guideline on the sterilization of the medicinal product, active substance, excipient and primary container, or similar regulations. Consequently, these raw materials should be considered as nonsterile even if they have been 0.2 µm-filtered.

  • Nonsterile materials should be added to the aseptic process via validated 0.2 µm filtration steps followed by a filter-integrity test.
  • Primary container closure systems should be tested, at a minimum, for sterility, integrity, and bacterial endotoxin, in addition to visual examination, prior to usage.

Container Integrity Under Cryogenic Conditions

For ATMPs stored at cryogenic temperatures (e.g., −80°C freezer or liquid nitrogen vapor phase), container closure integrity (CCI) under thermal stress is a critical qualification parameter. Freeze-thaw cycling can cause:

  • Seal failures at bag weld points or vial crimp closures
  • Micro-crack propagation in glass vials under rapid temperature change
  • Stopper retraction in prefilled syringes at ultra-low temperatures

Risk assessments should evaluate temperature range, thermal cycling frequency, and storage duration against component material specifications.

PIC/S Annex 2A, Section 5.27 — Individualized Therapies

For individualized therapies (e.g., autologous cell therapies), since each batch of product is manufactured for a specific patient, an error-free system for patient-derived materials (e.g., tumor samples or leukocytes) must be in place to maintain the chain of identity and chain of custody.

PIC/S Annex 2A, Section 5.27 guidance states: the agreement between the ATMP manufacturer and the supplier should contain clear provisions about the transfer of information, including traceability data and transmission of health donor information that may become available after the supply that may have an impact on the quality or safety of the ATMPs manufactured.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — What Makes Single-Use Components Special in ATMPs

Single-Use Component: In ATMP manufacturing, single-use components are not merely a convenience; they are a direct barrier for product quality and patient safety. Unlike traditional small-molecule drugs, the final container of an ATMP (especially the cryopreservation bag of a cell therapy) is in direct contact with live cells throughout its entire lifecycle.

Container-Closure System: The choice of an ATMP container-closure system must take into account all of the following:

  • Physical integrity: Maintaining the seal through the extreme temperature swing from −196°C liquid nitrogen to 37°C thawing
  • Chemical compatibility: Prolonged contact with DMSO (a common cryoprotectant, at concentrations up to 5–10%)
  • Biocompatibility: No release of leachables that affect cell viability

Traceability: For autologous cell therapy, every container corresponds to one specific patient, so complete traceability of the supply chain is a "non-negotiable baseline", not just a general GMP good practice.

Analogy — Choosing between a Cell Therapy Bag and a Glass Vial

Imagine shipping live tropical fish (live cells) across continents and oceans. Your choice of container decides whether the fish survive to the destination:

  • A cryopreservation bag is like a specially insulated fish transport bag: soft and flexible, able to be compressed at very low temperatures without cracking, but you need to confirm the bag material does not release toxins into the water (the E&L issue).
  • A glass vial is like a sturdy glass aquarium: chemically stable, but under rapid freezing and thawing it may develop microcracks from thermal expansion and contraction (the CCI issue).
  • A prefilled syringe is like a precision syringe-style dosing tool: well suited to direct delivery to the patient, but the silicone oil in the barrel may affect cell function.

The key question: "Will this container keep my cells viable throughout the entire cold chain journey (from the manufacturing site to the patient's operating room)?"

Key Point — The Unique Threat of E&L to Live Cells

Traditional E&L studies (for example under USP <661> and ISO 10993) mainly assess chemical toxicity to patient safety. For cell therapy products, however, E&L has an additional dimension: the direct effect on cell viability.

Even if the concentration of a leachable is low enough to be non-toxic to humans, it can still cause the following during manufacturing:

  • Increased cell death (viability < 70% may lead to batch rejection)
  • Reduced function of T cells or NK cells (lower CAR-T expansion efficiency)
  • Altered stem cell differentiation (abnormal differentiation of induced pluripotent stem cells, iPSC)

In practice: a Cell Compatibility Study should be performed at the very start of container material selection, rather than waiting for the E&L chemical analysis to finish before checking the effect on cells. The two should run in parallel.

Practical Application — A Container Selection Decision Framework for a CDMO

When a client comes with a new ATMP cell therapy product and asks for advice on container selection, we recommend the following decision flow:

  1. Product form: Is the final product a cell suspension? A lyophilized powder? A liquid formulation? → determines the container format (bag/vial/syringe)
  2. Storage conditions: Does it need to be frozen at −80°C or in liquid nitrogen (−196°C), or refrigerated at 2–8°C? → determines the temperature resistance specification of the material
  3. Formulation characteristics: Does it contain DMSO? Special buffers or proteins? → determines the priority solvents for E&L testing
  4. Route of administration: Intravenous infusion? Direct injection into the tumor site? → determines the required sterility assurance level
  5. Regulatory market: EU (EMA), US (FDA), Japan (PMDA)? → determines the applicable CCI test standards

Only by mapping the answers above onto a "container material requirements specification" can supplier inquiries and qualification be carried out meaningfully.

Topic 4. Risk-Based Supplier Qualification

ATMP material-supplier qualification is a risk-based approach. The level of oversight should be proportionate to the risks posed by the individual materials, taking into account their source, manufacturing process, supply chain complexity, and final use to which the material will be put in the ATMP.

General Considerations — Material under GMP Regulation

  • Paper audit (e.g., questionnaire) may be acceptable for lower-risk raw material, such as off-the-shelf material used in the upstream process of cell and gene therapies (early stage only).
  • Whether an on-site audit needs to be performed at a manufacturer's or distributor's premises should be defined based on QRM principles. Generally, audits need to be performed at vendors of all materials defined as critical for the manufacturing process according to its product risk profile.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Supplier Risk Tiering Model

The core philosophy of supplier qualification in PtC-13 is that "the intensity of oversight is proportional to the risk of the material". In practice this translates into Supplier Tiering:

  • Tier 1 (critical material suppliers): Materials in direct contact with the product that affect the safety of the final product (for example cryopreservation bags, cell expansion media, viral vectors) → an on-site audit plus a written quality agreement are required.
  • Tier 2 (important material suppliers): Materials that affect product quality indirectly but do not contact the final product directly (for example GMP-grade cytokines, key reagents) → a paper audit (questionnaire) plus a change notification agreement is sufficient.
  • Tier 3 (general material suppliers): Low-risk, replaceable, commercially available off-the-shelf items (for example standard laboratory consumables) → a supplier selection record plus the CoA is sufficient.

Basis for tiering: biological source risk of the material, contact time and temperature, irreplaceability and degree of GMP compliance.

Analogy — Managing Suppliers Like a Restaurant Chain

Imagine you are the quality director of a top restaurant chain, and your food suppliers are the ATMP material suppliers:

  • Truffle supplier (Tier 1, critical material): You visit the farm in person every quarter to confirm harvesting, preservation and transport end to end - just like an On-Site Audit.
  • Organic vegetable supplier (Tier 2, important material): You send a questionnaire every year to confirm the organic certification is still valid, with no need to visit in person - just like a Paper Audit.
  • Industrial salt supplier (Tier 3, general material): You only confirm that each lot meets the food safety specification - just like a CoA check.

If the truffle supplier switches farms (the equivalent of a major supplier change, or Change Notification), you will certainly want to be told right away, rather than discovering a quality problem after receiving the goods. That is the core value of a "Written Change Notification Agreement".

Key Point — "GMP Grade" Does Not Mean Real GMP

PtC-13 specifically points out a misconception that is very common in the ATMP industry: a supplier advertising a material as "GMP Grade" does not mean its manufacturing process complies with cGMP regulations.

Common situations in practice:

  • The supplier prints "GMP Grade" in the catalog, but this is only a self-declared claim with no third-party audit verification.
  • True GMP compliance has to be verified through a questionnaire assessment or an on-site audit.
  • If an audit finds the supplier cannot provide Batch Manufacturing Records, this is a major deficiency, regardless of the grade stated in its catalog.

Recommended action: the CDMO's Approved Supplier List (ASL) should record, for each material, the "basis for the GMP grade claim" and the "actual verification method", to avoid assigning a grade on the catalog alone.

Supplier Risk Scoring

Supplier risk score =
  Material criticality score (1–5)
  × Supplier GMP maturity (1–5, 5 = no certification)
  × Supply source risk (1–5, 5 = animal origin)
  ÷ Replaceability (1–5, 1 = easily replaced)

Risk tiers:
  Low risk (≤ 10)     → Tier 3 → CoA + change notification
  Medium risk (11–25) → Tier 2 → paper audit + quality agreement
  High risk (> 25)    → Tier 1 → on-site audit + full SQA

Examples:
  Cryopreservation bag = 5 × 2 × 2 ÷ 1 = 20 → Tier 2/1 boundary
  Human serum albumin = 5 × 3 × 5 ÷ 2 = 37.5 → Tier 1
  Standard culture dish = 1 × 2 × 1 ÷ 5 = 0.4 → Tier 3

Q4.1 (continued) — Early Clinical Stage Requirements (PtC-13 p24)

Original Text

Early Clinical Stage

A quality system should be in place. The level of quality standard should be adequate to the criticality of the raw material. To enable a clinical trial application, the following minimum requirements must be met:

  • Supplier quality system must be capable of providing safety-relevant documentation.
  • Traceability of materials and reagents (e.g., a unique material or catalogue number assigned).
  • Description of the origin and history of the material (e.g., donor tissue, chemically defined or recombinant, based on a cell bank).
  • For EU-regulated starting materials, GMP principles should be applied.

For customized materials (e.g., viral vector, custom-made media and buffers), a quality agreement should be in place.

For off-the-shelf or low-risk materials, it may be sufficient to have a change-notification agreement in place before comprehensive quality agreements are in place.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Minimum Supplier Requirements in the Early Clinical Stage

At the Phase 1 clinical trial application (IND/IMPD) stage, PtC-13 takes a pragmatic "minimum but workable" position. The requirements differ for three categories of material:

  • Custom Materials: For example custom viral vectors or special media → a written Quality Agreement is required, because these materials are unique and cannot simply be replaced by another supplier.
  • Critical Off-the-Shelf: For example a cytokine of a specific brand → at least a "Change Notification Agreement" is needed, so that you know right away when the supplier changes the formulation or manufacturing site.
  • Low-Risk Off-the-Shelf: For example standard laboratory reagents → catalog number plus CoA is enough, with no formal agreement needed for the time being.

Key principle: clinical progression is the trigger for "upgrading qualification". On the road from Phase 1 to Phase 3, the qualification level of each supplier must be reassessed at every milestone.

Analogy — Managing Academic-Origin Suppliers

Many suppliers in the ATMP industry are academic spin-offs. Their technology is often the most advanced in the world, but their GMP system may only be starting out, like a brilliant engineer fresh out of school: head full of the latest knowledge, but not yet familiar with factory SOPs.

The role of the CDMO here is like a patient senior colleague:

  • Step 1: Help them build a basic documentation system (material traceability, lot number management)
  • Step 2: Ask them to sign a change notification agreement (so they form the habit of "report any change")
  • Step 3: Provide training on GMP requirements, and upgrade to a formal quality agreement once their capability matures

This strategy is called "phase-appropriate supplier development" in PtC-13: you do not need to wait until the supplier is fully mature to start working together, but appropriate controls must be in place at every stage.

Key Point — The Change Notification Agreement Is the Most Important First Step

Of all supplier agreements, the Change Notification Agreement is the one to put in place soonest, because its purpose is to "prevent you from unknowingly using a material of different quality".

Common supplier change scenarios and their potential impact on ATMPs:

  • Adjustment of the medium formulation (change in cell expansion efficiency)
  • Relocation of the manufacturing site (environmental monitoring standards may differ)
  • Change in raw material source (animal-origin risk, TSE/BSE issues)
  • Change in the type of preservative (E&L risk changes)

Even in Phase 1, the lack of a change notification agreement can result in using a material that has quietly been revised, causing lot inconsistency between the material used in the toxicology studies and in the Phase 1 clinical trial, and raising questions in FDA/EMA review.

Q4.1 (continued) — Late Clinical Stage & Commercial: Single-Source Risk Mitigation (PtC-13 p24–p25)

Original Text

Late Clinical Stage and Commercial

The quality system standard should be increased to meet GMP regulations or GMP principles defined by EMA whenever possible. The level of GMP adherence should be justified based on the risks of the gaps, with the understanding of material attributes and impact on manufacturing the final drug product.

Note: Material grades are not standardized. The term "GMP grade" does not necessarily mean that material is manufactured and distributed under current GMP regulations. Instead, the supplier's GMP should be confirmed through assessment by questionnaire or audit to verify the material is of GMP grade.

To ensure supply for confirmatory or pivotal clinical trials and the approved product, establishing second suppliers of materials, especially critical raw materials, is essential. Where high-risk materials (e.g., animal-derived, high-risk supplier) are determined to be critical to product availability and cannot be eliminated or substituted, risk assessments should be utilized to identify and evaluate the specific risks posed by the material in relation to the production and availability of the finished product.

Consideration of such factors as the number of suppliers and/or alternatives available, especially where materials are single-sourced, as well as the lead time and shelf life, for example, are crucial to ensuring the control measures applied are commensurate with the level of risk posed. Specific control measures sufficient to eliminate or mitigate to an acceptable level each risk scenario identified should be established, documented, and applied to each receipt of the respective material.

Having a second qualified supplier is preferred to reduce the risk of drug shortages and ensure business continuity.

Summary: Key Control Measures for Single-Source Situations

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Supplier Upgrade Requirements in the Late Clinical and Commercial Stages

Phase 2/3 (Confirmatory/Pivotal Clinical Trial) is a major turning point in supplier management. At this point:

  • A lot failure is not just a loss of research material; it directly affects patient enrollment progress, the trial timeline and a clinical investment of hundreds of millions of dollars.
  • After commercialization, a material shortage (Drug Shortage) can directly affect patients waiting for treatment and trigger an emergency investigation by the regulator.
  • Regulatory filings (BLA/MAA) require greater transparency and consistency of supplier information, including material specifications, the content of quality agreements and audit frequency.

This is why PtC-13 particularly emphasizes a "second supplier" at this stage: not because regulations mandate it, but because the consequences of having no backup are simply too serious.

Analogy — Single-Supplier Risk Is Like Having No Spare Tire

In a Formula 1 race, if your only tire supplier cannot ship the day before the final, you have to withdraw, no matter how fast your car is. For a commercial ATMP product, a single critical material supplier is exactly this single point of failure.

Analogy for the mitigation strategies:

  • Spare tires (Safety Stock): Stock up in advance and calculate a reasonable safety stock level (considering material shelf life vs. lead time for receipt).
  • A backup fleet (Second Qualified Supplier): Qualify a second supplier in advance, so that even if it is not used day to day, you can switch immediately in a crisis.
  • Fleet insurance (Business Continuity Plan): Document in writing the response process for when the main supplier stops production, including the process for notifying regulators.

CDMO commercial consideration: setting up a second supplier takes extra time (3–12 months) and cost for qualification, but compared with the loss from a material stock-out after commercialization, it is a worthwhile investment.

Key Point — Priority Order at the Decision Level

1. Sterility Assurance
▼
2. Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) of the product (patient safety)
▼
3. Business Continuity

This hierarchy is critical when assessing whether to accept a single-source supplier with GMP gaps: if accepting the supplier's gaps would endanger sterility assurance (Level 1), it should not be accepted, however great the business pressure, until the gaps are corrected.

Practical scenario: when your only cryopreservation bag supplier cannot provide sterility test records (a Level 1 risk), the CDMO must suspend receipt of the material and notify the client, even if this may delay the patient's treatment schedule. Patient safety always takes priority over schedule pressure.

Practical Application — Core Clauses of a Supplier Quality Agreement (SQA)

A complete SQA for an ATMP critical material supplier should cover the following core clauses:

  • Material specification: A technical specification agreed by both parties
  • Testing responsibilities: The split between testing done by the supplier and incoming testing on site
  • Change management: The notice period before the supplier initiates a change (recommended: at least 6 months' notice for major changes)
  • Batch records: The documents to be provided with each lot (CoA, CoC, batch manufacturing record summary)
  • Audit rights: The frequency of on-site audits by the purchaser and the advance notice time
  • Nonconformance management: The handling process for OOS lots and the CAPA notification obligation
  • Supply chain information: (special clause for human-derived materials) The mechanism for passing on donor health information required by PIC/S 5.27

Section Summary — PtC-13 Section 3b & Topic 4 Q4.1

  • Q3.3 Single-use components: Selection of an ATMP container-closure system must evaluate physical integrity (cryogenic CCI), chemical compatibility (cryoprotectants such as DMSO) and biocompatibility (the effect of E&L on live cells). All three are indispensable, and the E&L assessment must include cell compatibility testing, not only chemical safety analysis.

  • Single-source supplier risk: In the late clinical and commercial stages, qualifying a second supplier for critical materials is a necessary risk management measure, not an option, because a material stock-out affects ATMP patients far more than patients on ordinary drugs.

Topic 5 — Lifecycle Management Considerations for Commercial Manufacturing

Original Text

Ideally, lifecycle management of raw materials starts early in the development of an ATMP product. It evolves as the product moves through the early clinical phases, through commercialization, and continues throughout the lifecycle of the product as more data and understanding are gained.

When an ATMP product is commercialized, the raw materials should be reviewed from the perspective of lifecycle management and potential new industry guidance. New information may be available that will change the risk-rating for raw materials and, therefore, testing and/or mitigation requirements. Topics to consider include:

  • Raw material that was single-sourced may now have additional suppliers that can be considered as a secondary or tertiary source.
  • Suppliers may now offer a raw material as GMP grade that was previously labeled for RUO (Research Use Only).
  • Historical data will now be available on raw materials that may allow for updates to the incoming testing requirements and reassessment of testing specifications, including the widening of specifications.
  • Historical data will now be available for reassessment of a supplier's qualification that may allow for reduced testing on the receipt of raw materials.

Product regulatory filings are important to consider with any raw material reassessment, as it may require updates in multiple markets. The timing of these regulatory submissions should also be considered from the perspective of how it will impact the manufacturing and planning schedule and, by extension, patient supply.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Lifecycle Management

Lifecycle management is not a one-time exercise but a continuous activity that runs through the product's entire path from development to commercialization. For ATMP raw material management, this means:

  • Early clinical stage: establish the initial supplier assessment and risk assessment baseline
  • Late clinical/BLA preparation: strengthen GMP compliance requirements and fill in historical data
  • After commercialization: keep optimizing as the market matures, new suppliers emerge and regulatory guidance is updated

PDA PtC-13 specifically emphasizes that raw material risk levels must be reviewed periodically after commercialization, because both the market environment (for example, a new supplier offering a GMP-grade version of the same material) and the regulatory environment keep evolving.

Analogy — A Student Growing into a Professional

Think of ATMP raw material management as a student's journey to becoming a professional:

  • University years (early IND clinical stage): get the job done with the most basic tools and buy the cheapest second-hand textbooks (RUO-grade materials). What matters is finishing the coursework, not owning perfect equipment.
  • First job after graduation (Phase II/III clinical): start upgrading your kit, evaluate whether better sources of supply exist, and set up working procedures (SOPs).
  • Seasoned professional (commercial launch): use tools that meet professional standards (GMP-grade materials), build a complete supply chain management system, and periodically check whether better options are available.

Key point: the standard differs at each stage, but each stage needs deliberate management, rather than scrambling to "catch up" only when commercialization arrives.

Key Point — The Importance of Regulatory Filing Timing

Any significant change to a raw material (such as changing the supplier, upgrading the grade or adjusting the specification) can trigger regulatory filing obligations in multiple markets. When helping clients manage such changes, a CDMO must assess in advance:

  • Which markets require a supplemental filing (the EU, US FDA and Japan PMDA each have different requirements)
  • Whether the review period affects the commercial production schedule and patient supply
  • Whether a pre-approved change management plan can reduce the risk of supply interruption

Q5.1 — Managing Suppliers Without GMP Service (RUO Grade Only)

Original Text

Q 5.1. How do you manage the situation when the supplier does not provide GMP service for the material, for example, if only Research Use Only grade is available?

The sponsor (i.e., cell-therapy drug manufacturer) can identify the risks on-site and figure out the pathway for enabling the material to be fit for purpose. The following case study will provide an example of quality risk management.

Case Study: Example of Implementation of GMP Principles for ATMP Raw Materials

This fictional case study is about raw materials used in genome-editing for induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which is an upstream cell-engineering process followed by single-cell cloning and, finally, differentiation to a CAR-T cell drug product. Health authorities give allowance for manufacturing materials, such as genome-editing materials, generation of iPSCs, and the subsequent selection of process cells, under the principles of GMP operation rather than full GMP compliance (5, 6, 15). In Japan, PMDA does not specify GMP or non-GMP adherence during the clinical phase, and the clinical-study sponsor owns the responsibility to secure the product quality for patients.

This case study is an example of the implementation of "Principles of GMP" for gRNA and nuclease used in the genome-editing of iPSCs. In this context, principles of GMP refer to the application of specific GMP elements, such as robust documentation practices and contamination-control measures, even when a fully GMP-compliant environment is not available.

Statement: Certified GMP facility and its established GMP-compliant quality management system is not available for a kind of custom-made small-volume production at the supplier. The only existing development laboratories can be used for manufacturing a product of appropriate quality for fit-for-purpose.

Approach: Instead of performing a normal GMP audit, strict sponsor's Quality team oversight is required on gap identification and definition of control measures. After those measures are completed, the sponsor's Quality confirms readiness on site prior to initiation of the contract manufacturing work. Agreed measures are spelled out in a Scope of Work (or ideally, a Quality Agreement) with the signatures of both parties.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Fundamental Difference Between RUO and GMP Grade

RUO (Research Use Only): the supplier does not guarantee that manufacture complies with GMP, and there is no batch release testing, no complete manufacturing record and no formal obligation to undergo supplier qualification. When such a material is used in drug manufacture, the buyer bears the compliance risk.

GMP-grade materials: the supplier manufactures in a GMP-certified facility, with complete batch records, a CoA (Certificate of Analysis) and a change control system, and accepts periodic audits by the drug manufacturer.

In early ATMP development, many critical reagents (gRNA, nucleases, Cas9 protein) are available only in RUO grade. This is the reality of the industry, and it is not a violation, but the risk must be managed through compensating measures.

Analogy — A Fine-Dining Restaurant Using Farmers' Market Ingredients

Imagine a Michelin-starred restaurant (the drug manufacturer) that has to use ingredients from a farmers' market (the RUO supplier), because certain rare ingredients simply do not exist in a "supermarket standard pack". What should it do?

  • The head chef (the Quality team) visits the farm in person to check that the production environment is hygienic
  • Draw up a written purchasing agreement that sets out the farmer's production standards and pesticide-use rules
  • After each batch of ingredients arrives, the kitchen runs its own safety tests before use
  • Record in detail which batch of ingredients went into each dish, so that everything is traceable

This is the core logic of PDA PtC-13 for RUO materials: if you cannot change the supplier's situation, strengthen your own monitoring and documentation capability.

Key Point — GMP Principles vs. Full GMP Compliance

The key phrase in this case study is "Principles of GMP", not "full GMP compliance". The distinction is critical:

  • Full GMP compliance: requires an ISO-classified facility, a complete QMS and eligibility for regulatory inspection
  • Principles of GMP: under the conditions available, achieve at least sound documentation, contamination control measures and batch traceability

The EMA (Reference 5) and PIC/S Annex 2A (Reference 6) explicitly allow certain raw materials in ATMP manufacturing (such as gene-editing materials) to follow the principles of GMP rather than full GMP compliance, which gives companies legitimate room to operate.

A Quality Agreement is the legal backbone of this strategy: without a signed written agreement, no verbal commitment counts in an audit.

Practical Application — RUO Material Management at a CDMO

Take a real-world scenario from a CDMO client (an iPSC-based CAR-T developer):

  • The client uses Cas9/gRNA for gene editing, and the supplier offers only RUO grade
  • The CDMO's Quality team signs a Quality Agreement with the supplier covering the format of production documentation, batch sampling requirements and change notification obligations
  • After each batch of material arrives, the CDMO performs supplementary testing itself (endotoxin, sterility, identity confirmation)
  • In the CMC section of the IND submission, the compensating controls are described in detail so that the FDA can see that the risk has been adequately managed

This approach is consistent with the PDA PtC-13 recommendations and has also passed FDA OTAT review (Reference 15).

Q5.2 — How to Implement GMP Principles and Enable IND Filing

Original Text

Q 5.2. How to implement principles of GMP and enable IND filing?

  • Identify gaps on site and address them by measures for upgrade to principles of GMP. See contamination control strategy below as an example.
  • Provide detailed and complete production records with sufficient information for enabling an IND/BLA filing, which is traceable for all aspects of the manufacturing process and any incidents.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — CMC Requirements for an IND Submission

The CMC (Chemistry, Manufacturing and Controls) section of an IND (Investigational New Drug Application) requires the applicant to describe:

  • The source, specifications and quality control methods of raw materials
  • A detailed description of the manufacturing process and process controls
  • For ATMPs, the quality assurance strategy for critical raw materials such as gene-editing materials

The key message of Q5.2 is: even when RUO-grade materials are used, detailed and traceable manufacturing records can still support an IND submission. Completeness of documentation is the key to clearing the regulatory hurdle.

Documentation Framework for Materials in an IND Submission

Documentation requirements for raw materials in an IND/BLA CMC section (minimum standard)
─────────────────────────────────────
1. Material identity (Identity)
   - Chemical name, CAS number, molecular formula
   - Supplier information (even if RUO)

2. Specification (Specification)
   - Appearance, purity, potency (bioactivity)
   - Safety indicators (endotoxin, sterility/bioburden)

3. Analytical methods (Analytical Methods)
   - Level of validation: Phase I → appropriate; Phase III → full validation

4. Batch records (Batch Records)
   - Complete records of all manufacturing steps
   - Records and investigation of any deviation (Deviation)

5. Stability data (Stability Data)
   - At least enough to support the shelf life through the clinical trial period
─────────────────────────────────────
Principle: the later the phase → the stricter the documentation requirements

Key Point — The Practical Significance of Process Traceability

Traceability is the soul of IND/BLA documentation: for every batch of drug product, it must be possible to trace from the records which batch of raw material was used, under what conditions it was manufactured and whether any abnormal events occurred.

For a CDMO, this means the integrity of the electronic batch record (eBR) system is critical: in the event of an adverse event, regulators may require tracing back through all related batches, and even to the supplier's own production records.

Analogy — The Food Recall in the Taiwan Tainted Oil Scandal

Lesson from the 2014 tainted oil scandal in Taiwan: the problem grew partly because downstream manufacturers could not quickly trace which products used the contaminated raw material.

The same logic applies to an ATMP IND submission: if a serious adverse event (SAE) occurs in a future clinical trial, the FDA/EMA will immediately require tracing of all related material batches. Only companies that have built a complete traceability system in advance can deliver a clear root cause analysis within 24-48 hours.

Q5.3 — Mitigating Cross-Contamination and Microbial Contamination Risks

Original Text

Q 5.3. How to mitigate risks for cross-contamination and microbial contamination?

  • As a minimum requirement, use a non-ISO-grade certified facility (i.e., controlled research and development (R&D) laboratory) but dedicate its use for one product at a time. Replace all tubing of instruments with brand new tubing dedicated for the contract work.
  • Make the bulk product in the controlled R&D lab and transfer it into a qualified isolator for sterile filtration and fill–finishing. The adequacy of the aseptic operation and environmental control is confirmed through aseptic process simulation before actual production.
Note: The supplier will not commit to producing "sterile" product; however, the sponsor is confident with the patient safety-based assurance on the material control, downstream manufacturing control, and overall quality control strategy of the final drug product.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Two Layers of Contamination Risk

In ATMP manufacturing, contamination risk comes mainly from two sources:

  • Cross-contamination: from other products or materials in the same facility (especially dangerous with allogeneic cell therapies, where cells from different patients must never be mixed in a batch)
  • Microbial contamination: contamination by bacteria, fungi, molds and other microorganisms, which poses a direct threat to patient safety

The core of the Q5.3 strategy is: facility segregation + aseptic protection at the final step. Even if the raw material manufacturing environment is not ideal, as long as the final fill-finish step is carried out in a qualified isolator, the material the patient receives can be assured to be safe.

Analogy — A Hospital's Infection Control Program

Compare it with a hospital's infection control strategy:

  • An ordinary ward (the R&D laboratory) can handle general patients, but it is thoroughly cleaned after each use and treats one patient at a time (one product at a time)
  • The operating theater (the qualified isolator) handles high-risk procedures: a strictly aseptic environment, with an Aseptic Process Simulation performed beforehand to confirm the reliability of the operation
  • All tubing is single-use: brand new tubing for each patient/batch eliminates the risk of residual contamination

This strategy matches the risk-tiering logic of PDA PtC-13: when resources are limited, concentrate the strictest controls on the highest-risk steps.

Key Point — Autologous vs. Allogeneic ATMPs

Cross-contamination risk management differs significantly between types of ATMP:

  • Autologous therapy: the patient's own cells; the greatest risk is microbial contamination of the same patient's cells during manufacture
  • Allogeneic therapy: uses cells from healthy donors; the greatest risk is cross-contamination between batches (cells from patient A contaminating the batch for patient B, with serious consequences)

The Q5.3 requirement to "dedicate its use for one product at a time" is especially critical for allogeneic ATMPs. A CDMO handling allogeneic ATMPs usually needs to design stricter physical barriers against cross-contamination (dedicated rooms or closed systems).

Practical Application — Contamination Control for iPSC-CAR-T Gene-Editing Materials

Using the Q5.3 case scenario as an example: the supplier produces gRNA and Cas9 nuclease (RUO grade) in an R&D laboratory:

  • Dedicated facility: during the contract manufacturing period, the R&D laboratory handles no other products and all tubing on every instrument is replaced
  • Upgraded final step: once the bulk product is complete, it is transferred to a qualified isolator for sterile filtration and filling
  • APS verification: before the filling operation, an Aseptic Process Simulation is performed to confirm that operators and equipment can achieve sterility assurance
  • Risk communication: the supplier explicitly states that sterility is "not guaranteed", but the sponsor's overall quality strategy already covers this risk

Q5.4 — Assuring Quality of Critical Raw Materials (gRNA, Nuclease)

Original Text

Q 5.4. How to assure the quality of the product (i.e., gRNA, nuclease)?

  • Define the material specifications and perform quality control release-testing at a GMP-compliant laboratory.
  • Store the product in a GMP-controlled warehouse once it is manufactured.
  • Conduct a stability study to ensure the quality of the raw material during storage when no stability data is available from the supplier.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Critical Quality Attributes of gRNA and Nuclease

gRNA (guide RNA) and nuclease (such as Cas9) are the core tools of CRISPR gene editing, and their quality directly determines the precision and safety of the editing. The main CQAs include:

  • Identity: sequence confirmation (the gRNA must match the target exactly)
  • Purity: residual protein, residual nucleic acid, endotoxin
  • Potency/Activity: in vitro gene-editing efficiency (cleavage activity)
  • Safety indicators: sterility (or bioburden), viral safety

The strategy of Q5.4 is: even if the manufacturing environment is not ideal (RUO), release testing must be performed in a GMP-compliant laboratory. This is the last line of defense for quality assurance.

Analogy — Third-Party Testing Before Food Leaves the Factory

Even if the food factory (the RUO supplier) does not meet the highest production standards, a responsible importer (the drug manufacturer/CDMO) will still commission a qualified third-party laboratory (a GMP-compliant laboratory) to run complete safety and quality tests before the product goes into stock and onto the shelves.

This is exactly the logic of the first point of Q5.4: the manufacturing method may fall short, but the release standard must be met. This release testing is done in a GMP-compliant laboratory, so the results have regulatory credibility and can be used in IND/BLA submission documents.

Key Point — Why Stability Studies Are Especially Important

gRNA and Cas9 protein are both biological macromolecules and are extremely sensitive to storage conditions (temperature, pH, number of freeze-thaw cycles):

  • gRNA is easily degraded by RNase and must be stored under specific frozen conditions (usually -80°C)
  • Cas9 protein loses activity significantly after repeated freeze-thaw cycles

When the supplier provides no stability data (the norm for RUO suppliers), the drug manufacturer must carry out its own stability studies to establish:

  • Recommended storage conditions (temperature, light, container material)
  • Expiry (shelf life / retest date)
  • Degradation pattern and key degradation indicators

This stability data is used not only for routine management but is also a required element of the CMC section in an IND/BLA submission.

Core Concept — Quick Overview of the 15 References by Category

The 15 references of PDA PtC-13 cover four regulatory systems, and understanding the position of each helps in quickly locating the applicable regulatory basis:

Section Summary — Topic 5 & References

  • Lifecycle management: ATMP raw material management is not a one-time exercise; after commercialization, risk levels must still be reviewed periodically, testing requirements updated, and new suppliers and regulatory updates monitored.
  • RUO material management (Q5.1): when GMP-grade material is not available, establish a written control framework through a Quality Agreement; rigorous sponsor Quality oversight in place of a formal GMP audit is a legitimate and workable strategy.
  • IND submission preparation (Q5.2): even when manufacturing follows the principles of GMP (not full GMP compliance), detailed and traceable manufacturing records can still support an IND/BLA submission. Completeness of documentation is the core.
  • Contamination control strategy (Q5.3): facility segregation (one product at a time) plus final filling in a qualified isolator is a practical strategy for protecting patient safety in an RUO environment. Cross-contamination risk needs particular attention in allogeneic ATMPs.
  • gRNA/nuclease quality assurance (Q5.4): release testing must be performed in a GMP-compliant laboratory; product is stored in a GMP warehouse; if there is no supplier stability data, the drug manufacturer must carry out its own stability studies.
  • Core regulatory references: the EMA Q&A (Ref. 5) and PIC/S Annex 2A (Ref. 6) provide the regulatory basis for the "principles of GMP" strategy; USP <1043>, <1046>, <1047> provide specific testing frameworks; the FDA OTAT Town Hall (Ref. 15) reflects the direction of US CMC regulation.

Solution Hints Commentary

Question 1 Solution Framework — Risk Classification Decision

To assess whether Cas9/gRNA are Critical Raw Materials, consider:

  • Direct impact on product quality: gRNA determines the precision of the editing target and Cas9 determines cleavage efficiency → directly affects CQAs (editing efficiency, off-target rate) → Critical Raw Materials
  • Impact on patient safety: if the gRNA sequence is wrong or Cas9 activity is insufficient, the result may be an ineffective treatment or even carcinogenic off-target effects → high patient safety risk
  • Supply chain vulnerability: RUO grade from a sole source → also a single-source risk

Conclusion: both Cas9 and gRNA should be classified as Critical Raw Materials with High Risk and require the strictest control strategy.

Question 3 Key Points — gRNA/Cas9 Incoming Test List

Minimum incoming test program (to support IND Phase I):

  • gRNA: sequence confirmation (mass spectrometry or sequencing), purity (PAGE or HPLC), endotoxin, bioburden/sterility
  • Cas9 protein: purity (SDS-PAGE), identity confirmation (Western blot or mass spectrometry), potency (in vitro cleavage assay), endotoxin, residual host cell protein
  • Common items: appearance, concentration, pH (if applicable)

Before Phase III, the above methods must undergo full method validation, particularly for the accuracy and repeatability of the potency test.

Question 5 Approach — Managing Filing Timing Across Markets

Filing raw material changes across markets (US + EU) is like waiting for clearance at intersections in different countries:

  • US (FDA): generally requires a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) or CBE-30/CBE-0, depending on the significance of the change
  • EU (EMA/national competent authorities): Type IA/IB/II variation applications, with markedly different review times (Type II can take 12-18 months)

Strategic recommendation: once the supplier upgrade plan is confirmed, start preparing the filing documents 12-18 months in advance, and evaluate whether a dual-supplier arrangement (old and new in parallel) is needed to safeguard patient supply during the transition.

Chapter Summary — Core Learning from PDA PtC-13

After completing the five topics of PDA PtC-13, here are the three most important core messages:

  1. Risk-driven, not one-size-fits-all: the essence of ATMP raw material management is the Risk-Based Approach. The risk level of a material determines the intensity of control, rather than always pursuing the highest GMP standard.
  2. Documentation is compliance: in early ATMP development, even when manufacturing conditions are not ideal, complete and traceable documentation is the key to passing regulatory review. "If it's not written down, it didn't happen."
  3. Supply chain resilience: moving from a single-supplier strategy to a diversified supply chain at commercialization is the path every successful ATMP commercialization must take. Lifecycle management is not a one-time exercise but a continuously evolving process.
