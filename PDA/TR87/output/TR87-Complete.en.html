1.0 Introduction

1.0 Introduction

Glass has long been one of the first materials fabricated by humankind to contain valuables—from perfumes, oils, and potions to the most technologically advanced. Glass has many properties that make it an appropriate packaging material for parenteral drugs, including:

  • Rigidity and durability
  • Stability and compatibility with filling and shipping processes
  • Ease of cleaning and sterilization
  • Gas impermeability and maintenance of container-closure integrity
  • Compatibility with a wide range of drug products
  • Optical transparency and clean appearance
  • Ease of drug product inspection

Like other packaging materials, glass is not always the appropriate packaging material. Not all glass types are the same in composition and properties. Based on the glass type, some drug formulations can react with glass, particularly those with a high pH or certain buffers.

Beyond this consideration is the fact that glass can break. Glass breakage can result in processing downtime, an increased number of production interventions, injury, increased particulate load, and loss of product. Ultimately, patient safety can be put at risk due to a loss of container-closure integrity (possibly including sterility breaches), lost or improper doses, or injury from glass shards and particulates.

The resistance of glass to breakage is less a property of the material, as glass is inherently strong, but more about the condition of its surface. For glass to break, both a stress and a flaw must be present at the same place and at the same moment. As will be explained in this technical report, stress, whether from an applied load, impact, or thermal shock, is generally temporary and the glass container will retain its initial integrity and strength. Flaws, however, are permanent once induced.

As glass is handled through its manufacturing processes and subsequent pharmaceutical processes, it is vulnerable to surface damage. Best handling practices can preserve the inherent strength of the glass, whereas poor glass handling practices can significantly reduce the practical strength of glass containers. Surface damage induced early in the manufacturing process may manifest itself as failure later in the process or in the field as the container is subjected to subsequent stresses.

Commentary

Core concept: why use glass?

Glass has been used for parenteral drugs for a century because it meets four needs at the same time: chemical inertness, physical strength, visual inspectability, and sterilization compatibility.

On a CDMO fill-finish floor, nearly every aseptic process is built around glass vials. Understanding the strengths and limitations of glass is the most basic knowledge foundation for operators and engineers.

  • Container-closure integrity (CCI): ensures the sterile barrier is not compromised, which bears directly on patient safety.
  • Visual inspection: the optical transparency of glass is the precondition for manual or automated visual inspection for foreign particles.

Note: glass is not a universal material

High-pH formulations or certain buffers may react with glass, leading to delamination or ion leaching. This is one reason a compatibility assessment must be performed during material selection.

At the CDMO order-intake stage, ask the client for the formulation pH and buffer information, and include them in the packaging-material compatibility study.

Core formula: the necessary conditions for breakage

Glass breakage = Stress + Flaw
          same location, same moment

Remove either condition → no breakage

This "dual-condition" logic is the core of the whole of TR87. What engineers can mainly control is: (1) reducing the flaws generated in the process; (2) reducing unnecessary stress applied to the glass.

Analogy: glass is like car paint that is already scratched

Imagine a car with a fine scratch on its paint. Normally nothing happens, but once a stone strikes it at high speed (stress), the paint around the scratch flakes off over a large area. Glass vials work the same way: a surface scratch (flaw) caused early in the process may only turn into breakage later, during sterilization, lyophilization, or transport.

Operational implication: glass breakage often does not occur at the "point of damage" but at the "point of stress". Root cause analysis (RCA) must therefore look upstream.

Floor application: the knock-on effects of breakage

Glass breakage on a production line is not just the loss of one vial. It brings:

  • Line downtime → lost capacity
  • More interventions → higher contamination risk
  • Glass particulates contaminating surrounding batches
  • Risk of operator injury
  • Worst case: CCI failure → batch rejection or a patient safety event

For this reason, TR87 emphasizes "prevention over remediation": good handling practices reduce these risks at the source.

1.1 Purpose and Scope

1.1 Purpose and Scope

The purpose of this technical report is to provide a technical understanding of glass, its strength, and its limitations, and to provide guidance in best handling practices for glass vials throughout the pharmaceutical process—receipt, storage, preparation, filling, stoppering, freeze-drying, and capping. This document focuses on glass vials, where surface damage may occur, and where excess stresses may be applied during processing and handling. Many of the principles discussed here can be applied to the handling of glass syringes, cartridges, ampoules, and other glass container types.

This report also reviews typical pharmaceutical glass handling processes and identifies areas of concern. Points to consider are presented for line, material, and equipment enhancements as well as guidance and practical approaches for using procedural controls to better employ existing equipment.

Commentary

What TR87 covers

This technical report covers the complete process chain for glass vials, from receipt → storage → preparation → filling → stoppering → lyophilization → capping. Every step is a potential source of surface damage.

Although the main focus is vials, the principles also apply to:

  • Glass syringes
  • Cartridges
  • Ampoules

CDMO application perspective

TR87 is not just a theoretical document; it provides practical tools at three levels:

  • Equipment level: identify risk points in equipment design
  • Material level: evaluate glass specifications and packaging-material choices
  • Procedural controls level: use SOPs and operating practices to make up for the shortcomings of existing equipment

This three-level structure is also the standard entry framework when an FMEA is used to assess glass breakage risk.

Analogy: TR87 is like a "user manual" for glass vials

Most plants know how to use glass vials but not "why we do it this way". The value of TR87 is that it supplies the underlying principles, moving engineers and operators from "following the rules" to "acting with understanding". This is a prerequisite for effective continual improvement (CI) and CAPA.

2.0 Glossary and Abbreviations

2.0 Glossary and Abbreviations

2.1 Abbreviations

Glossary Commentary

The breakage triad: the hierarchy among Breakage, Crack, and Check

These three terms are often used interchangeably, but TR87 defines them strictly and separately:

  • Check (surface discontinuity): does not penetrate the glass wall; it is a surface defect that can still become the starting point of later breakage.
  • Crack: has penetrated the glass wall; CCI is lost and the vial must be rejected.
  • Breakage (breakage event): a general term for the result of failure once practical strength is exceeded; it is the end point of the whole breakage mechanism.

Writing only "glass broke" in floor documents is imprecise. Specify whether it was a check, a crack, or complete breakage, because the root cause and the disposition differ.

Key distinction: Eject Bin vs. Reject Bin

These are the two terms most easily confused on the floor, and they are critical for GMP compliance:

  • Eject Bin (temporary holding bin): vials are removed temporarily and may be re-inspected and returned to the line. Used for situations such as downed vials.
  • Reject Bin: vials are removed for good and must not return to the line; they must be segregated and destroyed or scrapped.

If the floor SOP does not clearly distinguish these two bins, a quality event may result: vials already judged defective could re-enter the production flow by mistake. It is recommended to use the TR87 definitions consistently in equipment labeling, SOPs, and training materials.

Practical Strength vs. Glass Strength

Glass Strength (intrinsic material strength)
  → theoretically very high (strong Si-O bonding)

Practical Strength (actual resistance to breakage)
  = f(geometry, surface flaws, residual stress, mechanical load)
  → far lower than Glass Strength

Operating goal: keep Practical Strength close to Glass Strength
         = reduce flaws + control stress

This explains why good handling practices "preserve" the natural strength of glass, while rough handling "destroys" it.

Analogy: brittle vs. ductile - glass vs. aluminum foil

Glass is a brittle material: once stress exceeds its limit it fractures immediately, with no "deformation warning". Aluminum, by contrast, is ductile: it deforms plastically before reaching its limit, giving operators time to notice.

This is why glass breakage often occurs "without warning". Because of the nature of brittle materials, prevention is ten times more important than remediation.

Process equipment terms: Dead Plate, Screw Feed, Starwheel

These three equipment terms are very common on fill-finish lines, but each carries a specific risk:

  • Dead Plate (stationary transfer plate): where vials transition from a moving conveyor to the next station. If it is not flat enough or has burrs, it will scratch the vial base (flaw).
  • Screw Feed (feed screw): forces vials into a single lane; if the speed is poorly matched, it creates compressive stress on the vial side wall.
  • Starwheel: positions individual vials precisely; if the pocket size does not match the vial diameter, it causes pinch stress.

Using these standard terms when discussing equipment improvements with equipment suppliers ensures precise communication.

Molded Vial vs. Tubular Vial: selection logic

The two manufacturing processes lead to different vial characteristics:

  • Molded Vial: more uniform wall thickness and higher mechanical strength; suited to large volumes or environments with repeated vibration. Higher cost.
  • Tubular Vial: thinner wall, lighter weight, and high optical clarity; suited to small-volume, high-value products. However, the tube drawing and cutting steps in manufacturing introduce a greater risk of surface flaws.

When assessing a CDMO order, the vial type affects the entire handling-equipment setup, the breakage-rate estimate, and the packaging-material validation strategy.

Lyophilization and the special risk of thermal stress

Lyophilization (freeze-drying) involves large temperature swings (cooling to below −40°C, then sublimation back to ambient temperature), which generate thermal stress in the glass.

If a vial already has a surface flaw before entering the lyophilizer, the thermal cycle may supply just enough stress to trigger breakage. Such breakage is often found inside the lyophilizer chamber, and the root cause is hard to trace. Regular incoming inspection and protective handling practices are the key to prevention.

Floor application: abbreviations in their working context

How the four abbreviations used in TR87 apply in day-to-day CDMO work:

  • COF (coefficient of friction): used to evaluate the effect of conveyor materials and surface coatings on glass scratching. Too high a COF = higher scratch risk.
  • CTE (coefficient of thermal expansion): used to evaluate the compatibility of glass with metal fixtures/equipment contact surfaces under temperature differences, preventing thermal stress concentration.
  • FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis): a systematic risk assessment tool; later sections of TR87 guide how to use FMEA to identify high-risk points for glass breakage.
  • RPN (risk priority number): the quantitative output of an FMEA (severity × occurrence × detectability), used to prioritize improvements.

Section 1 Summary

  • Glass is an ideal container for injectables, but not a universal one: its strengths are chemical inertness, visual inspectability, gas impermeability, and ease of sterilization; its limitations are compatibility problems with high-pH formulations and the risk of brittle breakage.
  • The dual necessary conditions for breakage: glass breakage requires stress and flaw to be present at the same location at the same time. Stress is transient, whereas a flaw, once created, is permanent. This means prevention should focus on reducing flaws.
  • Delayed manifestation of damage: surface flaws caused by early process steps (such as tube drawing, washing, and depyrogenation) may only cause breakage in later steps (such as lyophilization and transport), which is why RCA must trace back across processes.
  • Scope of TR87: it covers the complete process from receipt to capping, provides improvement guidance at three levels (equipment, material, and procedure), and also applies to other glass container types such as syringes and ampoules.
  • Terminological precision is the basis of compliance: the distinction between Eject Bin and Reject Bin, the hierarchy between Check and Crack, and the characteristics of Molded versus Tubular Vials are all language that floor documents and communication must use precisely.
  • Combining FMEA with TR87: the four abbreviations COF, CTE, FMEA, and RPN foreshadow that later sections of TR87 will systematically introduce risk assessment methodology, providing a quantitative basis for process improvement.

3.0 Best Practices for Handling Glass Vials

3.0 Best Practices for Handling Glass Vials

Processing of glass containers on equipment and lines always presents some level of risk for container damage. To reduce the risk for creating flaws (e.g., scratches, scuffs, checks, bruises) on glass containers and generating glass particles, abrasions, cracks, and breakage, friction and forces exerted upon the containers should be minimized throughout the entire process. To this end, the principles in Table 3.0-1🔍 should be applied to both existing and new equipment. New equipment design and installation should include additional considerations that are outlined in Section 3.1.

Commentary: core principles of glass container handling

Core concept: where does damage risk come from?

As glass vials move along the line, every contact point is a potential source of damage. The core logic of PDA TR87 is simple: reduce friction and contact force, and the incidence of scratches, bruises, checks, and breakage falls.

This damage affects more than appearance: it can generate glass particles, directly endangering injectable product quality and patient safety.

Analogy: handle them like eggs, not bricks

Imagine carrying eggs in a supermarket: you lift and set them down gently, avoid stacking them, and do not let them knock together. The logic for glass vials is exactly the same, only scaled up to line speeds of hundreds of vials per minute.

Table 3.0-1🔍 interpreted: nine handling considerations

  • Limit glass-to-glass contact: gravity-fed ramps are a high-risk area, and vials colliding with each other is one of the most common sources of damage. The best solution is an equipment design that avoids glass-to-glass contact altogether.
  • Limit glass-to-metal contact: stainless steel is much harder than glass and leaves scratches on the vial. Switching to polymer materials such as PTFE, PEEK, and UHMWPE is standard industry practice.
  • Reduce friction: UHMWPE has a very low coefficient of friction and suits guide-rail materials. Another strategy is to have containers "carried along" rather than "pushed and slid".
  • Process management: frequent stop-starts are a main cause of back pressure and collisions. "Funneling" (a sudden narrowing of the flow path) must also be minimized.
  • Minimize transitions & accumulation: alignment and synchronization among the star wheel, screw feed, and dead plate are critical.
  • Limit back pressure: a pressureless loop accumulator or a bi-flow reversing lane accumulator are advanced solutions.
  • Evaluate thermal stressors: thermal shock can create stress cracks inside the glass; pay particular attention around sterilizing tunnels and before and after freeze-drying.
  • Mitigate the impact of glass breakage events: shielding, line clearance procedures, and frequency tracking; all three are indispensable.
  • Investigate breakage events: every vial breakage event should undergo root cause analysis and result in a CAPA (corrective and preventive action).

CDMO practical note

In a CDMO environment, the same line may serve multiple clients and multiple vial types. The principles in Table 3.0-1🔍 must be re-verified for alignment after every changeover; it cannot be assumed that the previous batch's settings are still valid.

The use of the eject bin in particular: to save labor, many CDMOs return vials from the eject bin to the line, and this action is itself a damage risk. TR87 explicitly recommends using an eject lane instead.

3.1 New Equipment Design, Installation, and Qualification

3.1 New Equipment Design, Installation, and Qualification

In order to optimize the glass handling process for a specific application, equipment design, installation, and qualification should be conducted as a joint activity of the pharmaceutical manufacturer, machine suppliers, and manufacturers of the product's primary and secondary packaging. The full range of dimensional variability and consideration of critical dimensions required for optimal container handling must be accounted for in equipment design.

Factory and site acceptance testing should be conducted to evaluate all vial sizes in order to demonstrate suitability. Process parameters, such as line speed, dwell times, and change parts, should also be evaluated. Both the maximum and minimum speeds should be evaluated to assess the impact on glass breakage, pinch points, torque limits on star wheels and screw feeds, and pressure measurements.

Commentary: three-party collaboration in qualifying new equipment

The importance of joint activity

TR87 stresses that the design and qualification of new equipment must be an activity in which all three parties participate:

  • Pharmaceutical company / CDMO: provides process requirements and product specifications
  • Machine supplier: provides equipment design capability and its limitations
  • Container/closure manufacturer: provides vial dimensional specifications and tolerance ranges

If the three parties do not collaborate, the most common result is that the equipment is designed around "ideal dimensions", but dimensional variability in actual lots of vials causes jams or breakage.

Analogy: FAT / SAT are like a test drive when accepting a new car

The Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) is performed at the equipment manufacturer's site to confirm that the equipment runs correctly under ideal conditions. The Site Acceptance Test (SAT) is performed at the pharmaceutical site to confirm that the equipment meets requirements in the actual environment.

It is like buying a new car: you first drive it on the test track, then verify its performance on mountain roads and highways. Neither scenario can be skipped.

Why test at both maximum AND minimum speed?

Maximum speed: confirms that at high speed the equipment does not generate excessive impact force, back pressure, or pinch point stress.

Minimum speed: when running too slowly, some mechanisms (such as the screw feed) may jam vials because of insufficient torque or loss of synchronization.

In other words, both the upper and lower limits of the operating window must be verified; testing only the midpoint is not enough.

Practical application: key points for an FAT/SAT checklist

  • Prepare representative vial samples covering the upper limit, lower limit, and midpoint of the dimensional tolerances (worst case)
  • Test all vial sizes planned for use (not only the main format)
  • At FAT, require the equipment supplier to demonstrate a pinch point analysis
  • Record the line speed, stoppage events, and number of broken vials during all tests as baseline data

3.2 Considerations of Component Design and Variation

3.2 Considerations of Component Design and Variation

During the qualification process, several aspects of the components should be considered:

  • Container dimensional variability (lot-to-lot or vial-to-vial)
  • Component stack-up tolerances (vial + stopper + seal)
  • Container design appropriate for the equipment to be used
  • Technical drawings, appropriately detailed with all specifications that could impact performance of the vial in the equipment (e.g., contact points on the rails, star wheels, screw feeds, grippers), including specifications on shoulder angles, radii, concentricity, and roundness

Aligning measurement techniques among and within the pharmaceutical company, equipment suppliers, and component suppliers is very important.

Commentary: dimensional variation and tolerance stack-up

Two kinds of variation need attention

Lot-to-lot variability: even from the same supplier, vials from different lots may differ in outer diameter, shoulder angle, and base flatness. If the equipment is tuned only to one lot of samples, problems may appear immediately after a lot change.

Vial-to-vial variability: within the same lot, individual vials also differ in size. The equipment design must tolerate this natural variation, rather than being able to handle only "perfectly sized" vials.

The concept of stack-up tolerance

When the vial, stopper, and seal are assembled together, the tolerance of each component accumulates:

Total height variation = vial height tolerance
           + stopper thickness tolerance
           + dimensional variation after seal crimping

Example:
  Vial height: ±0.3 mm
  Stopper thickness: ±0.2 mm
  Seal deformation: ±0.1 mm
  ─────────────────
  Worst-case total variation: ±0.6 mm

If this 0.6 mm of variation is not taken into account in the equipment design, it may lead to incomplete capping or pinched vials.

Details in the technical drawing matter

TR87 specifically names several specifications that are easily overlooked on technical drawings:

  • Shoulder angle: affects how the star wheel grips the vial
  • Radius: the corner radius affects the contact area with the guide rail
  • Concentricity: whether the vial body is truly centered affects the stability of rotary equipment
  • Roundness: how close the cross-section is to a perfect circle affects how the screw feed engages

If the drawings provided by the supplier lack these specifications, it is difficult to define acceptance criteria during qualification.

Practical application: aligning measurement techniques

TR87 stresses "aligning measurement techniques": if the pharmaceutical company, the equipment supplier, and the packaging supplier each measure the same vial using different gauges or methods, they may obtain different numbers, leading to disputes.

Recommended practice: at project kickoff, hold a three-party measurement system analysis (MSA) meeting to confirm that measurement definitions and methods are consistent.

3.3 Real-Time Stress Monitoring

3.3 Real-Time Stress Monitoring

New technologies, such as vial diagnostic drones, are available that can measure line pressure and shock events and transmit these data live during fill and packaging operations or in transit. These real-time data acquired for line and vial sizes can diagnose problem areas and enable corrective actions to be taken.

The data can be used to adjust and optimize conveyor speeds, format part tolerances, and improve robotic pickups to reduce the mechanical stress that leads to glass damage events. These technologies can also be used in machine design and acceptance testing.

[Figure 3.3-1🔍: Example of a Vial Diagnostic Drone and Associated Data Visualization Technology — shows a sensor device placed among vials on a production line to measure forces, pressure, and shock events during processing; associated software displays real-time data visualization for line optimization]

Commentary: Vial Diagnostic Drone technology

What is a Vial Diagnostic Drone?

It is a smart device that looks like an ordinary vial but has sensors embedded inside. It is placed in the normal vial flow and moves along with the line, recording and transmitting the following data in real time:

  • Line pressure: the lateral pressure vials experience in accumulation zones or at transition points
  • Shock events: the instantaneous acceleration when vials collide or fall
  • Temperature changes (some models): used to evaluate thermal stress

With these data, problems no longer have to wait for vial breakage or visual inspection to be found; high-risk areas are diagnosed before damage occurs.

Analogy: an "ECG" for the production line

The traditional approach is a post-mortem: the cause is investigated only after vials break. The Vial Diagnostic Drone is more like putting a continuous ECG monitor on the line: you see each "abnormal heartbeat" (shock or overpressure event) in real time and act before the "heart attack" (mass vial breakage).

Application scenarios: three main uses

  • Optimizing existing lines: for processes with a known high breakage rate, use the drone to find the specific high-risk points (which star wheel? which turn?), then quantify them and make targeted improvements.
  • Acceptance of new equipment (FAT/SAT): during factory acceptance testing, use the drone to verify objectively whether the equipment design meets low-stress standards, rather than relying on visual observation alone.
  • Transport validation (in transit): vials can also be damaged by vibration during shipment. The drone can be placed in the shipping case to record shock data throughout transport.

The data-driven improvement cycle

TR87 stresses that these real-time data can be used to optimize:

  • Conveyor speed: find the balance point between speed and pressure
  • Format part tolerances: adjust the dimensions of guide rails and star wheels based on measured data
  • Robotic pickup: optimize the gripping force and position of robotic arms

This marks a new era in which glass vial handling moves from "adjusting by experience" to "data-driven optimization". For a CDMO, it is also a powerful tool for demonstrating process capability to clients.

  • Core logic: reducing friction and contact force is the fundamental principle for protecting glass vials, and it runs through every handling step.
  • The nine principles of Table 3.0-1🔍: limit glass-to-glass contact, limit glass-to-metal contact, reduce friction, process management, minimize transitions, limit back pressure, evaluate thermal stressors, mitigate the impact of breakage, and investigate breakage events. These nine principles apply to both existing and new equipment.
  • Qualification of new equipment: must be a three-party collaboration among the pharmaceutical company, equipment supplier, and packaging supplier; both FAT and SAT must cover all vial formats and maximum/minimum speeds.
  • Dimensional variation: lot-to-lot and vial-to-vial variation and stack-up tolerance (vial + stopper + seal) must all be built into the equipment design; technical drawings need to include detailed specifications such as shoulder angle, concentricity, and roundness.
  • Real-time monitoring technology: the Vial Diagnostic Drone measures line pressure and shock events in real time, enabling data-driven process optimization, and can be applied to FAT/SAT and transport validation.

4.1 Risk Management Overview

4.0 Risk Assessments

An effective risk management process can reduce glass handling hazards during initial process development and design and also when evaluating process changes. Further, risk management can be used to discover the root cause of glass breakage events and make informed decisions during investigations. A risk management program can support regulatory discussions and inquiries, ultimately providing regulators with higher confidence in the pharmaceutical manufacturer's capabilities to address these types of events.

The scope of the risk management program, specific to glass handling, should include the full lifecycle of the glass container including the manufacture and shipment from the supplier to filling, packaging, and distribution to the customer. There are several helpful documents available to support risk assessment activities, such as ISO documents, ICH guidelines, and PDA technical reports.

Risk assessment comprises identification of potential risks, analysis of the probability of occurrence, evaluation of potential impact of the risk, and prioritization for action. Risk assessments can be quantitative or qualitative in nature. A quantitative assessment, in which a numerical value or range of values describing the level of risk, may be used. Or, a qualitative assessment, using relative categories such as "high," "medium," and "low," may be used to characterize the risks. In either case, the definition and rationale for each category should be clearly defined.

A risk assessment is performed by a cross-functional team that includes the appropriate SMEs and/or responsible persons to assure accurate analysis. Multiple data sources are used to support the assessment including, among other material, process maps, procedures, manufacturing floor experience, frequency data, deviation and complaint history, and detection capability. These inputs ensure the accuracy of the risk analysis, so they must be robust.

Commentary and Application

Core concept: why is risk management needed?

TR87 positions risk management as a tool with three functions:

  • Preventive: identify potential glass breakage risks early in process design
  • Investigative: help find the root cause of glass breakage events
  • Regulatory communication: raise regulators' confidence in the manufacturer's capability

These three functions are closely tied to day-to-day CDMO operations; client audits, FDA inspections, and CAPA execution all need this way of thinking.

Full lifecycle scope

The scope of risk management is not just your filling line, but every step from the supplier manufacturing the glass vials → transport → receipt at your site → filling and packaging → outbound distribution.

This matters especially for a CDMO: fluctuations in supplier delivery quality directly affect your process risk and must be included in the assessment.

Analogy: quantitative vs. qualitative assessment

Quantitative: like a thermometer, giving a specific number such as 38.5°C. Suited to situations with reliable data that can be measured precisely.

Qualitative: like a doctor saying "you have a fever, a moderate one". It classifies as high/medium/low, and suits situations with insufficient data or complex circumstances.

TR87 allows the two approaches to be used together, but stresses that whichever is chosen, the definition of each category and its basis must be clearly documented.

Cross-functional team

A risk assessment is not a document QA writes alone behind closed doors! TR87 explicitly requires relevant SMEs (subject matter experts) to be involved. In a CDMO environment this usually includes process engineers, QA, equipment maintenance, line supervisors, and even client representatives.

The more diverse and solid the data sources, the more credible the analysis; this is where regulatory reviews most often push back.

4.2 Risk Controls and Communication

Risk Controls and Communication

Subsequent to risk assessment, risk controls are considered and applied where appropriate to eliminate or reduce risks identified by the risk assessment. The amount of risk control (i.e., risk reduction or elimination) should be proportional to the significance of the risk and should be assessed based on a benefit-to-risk analysis.

The resulting risks, mitigating activities, and level of risk acceptance must be effectively communicated. Risk communication and review requires appropriate documentation as well as communication of the risk decisions to the persons authorized to accept the risk and direct response strategies for risk mitigation and follow-up (e.g., functional experts and area or operations heads). Risk response activities need to be reviewed periodically to confirm ongoing compliance effectiveness and proactively reevaluated when new process, equipment, or industry changes influence or alter the underlying risk profile.

Commentary and Application

Principle of proportionality: risk control effort ∝ significance of the risk

TR87 states clearly that the strength of risk control should be commensurate with the severity of the risk. This avoids two extremes:

  • Over-controlling low risks (wasting resources and adding operating burden)
  • Understating high risks (regulatory compliance gaps and product safety concerns)

The basis for the decision is benefit-to-risk analysis, which is fully consistent with the spirit of ICH Q9.

Easily overlooked details of risk communication

Many companies complete an FMEA and then just "file it away", but TR87 stresses:

  • Risk decisions must be communicated to authorized personnel with the authority to accept the risk (not only recorded by QA)
  • Risk responses need periodic review to confirm they remain effective
  • When there are new changes in the process, equipment, or industry, the risk profile must be proactively re-evaluated

In a CDMO, a client's formulation change or a parameter shift after equipment maintenance can trigger this re-evaluation mechanism.

Practical application: risk communication checklist

  • Have the FMEA results been formally presented to the process manager and the QA manager?
  • Do high-risk items have a designated owner and a follow-up due date?
  • When the process changes, is the update process for the existing FMEA triggered?
  • Is the frequency of periodic review explicitly stated in writing?

4.3 Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) Methodology

FMEA — Overview and Method

One effective method used to assess the risk inherent in a process specific to glass handling is the Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA). This method is widely used by many industries, including the pharmaceutical industry. Although its original and intended use had been to proactively identify unacceptable risks prior to failures occurring, the FMEA has also been used successfully in a reactive fashion to evaluate processes for most likely root causes of failures after they occur.

The FMEA method is based on the theory that the risk of any particular failure is a composite of the severity, frequency, and probability of the detection of that failure. Cross-functional team participants, with a diverse knowledge of the process, compile an exhaustive list of potential failures and, taking into account the known attributes of the process, thoroughly analyze each one. This output is then evaluated to ascertain if the risk is acceptable or unacceptable. Mitigation activities are identified and completed for those risks deemed unacceptable.

Commentary and Application

The dual use of FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis)

TR87 specifically stresses two modes of applying FMEA, a point that is often overlooked:

  • Proactive: identify potential failures during process design and prevent them before problems occur
  • Reactive: after a glass breakage event, use the FMEA framework to systematically assess the most likely root causes

This makes FMEA a valuable tool at any stage; it is not just a "document to complete before opening the plant".

The core RPN (risk priority number) formula

RPN (risk priority number) = Severity × Occurrence × Detection

RPN = S × O × D

S (Severity):
  How severe is the effect of the failure on the product?
  Range usually 1–10, 10 = most severe

O (Occurrence):
  How likely is this failure cause to occur?
  Range usually 1–10, 10 = occurs most often

D (Detection):
  How likely are the existing controls to detect/prevent it?
  Range usually 1–10, 10 = cannot be detected at all

→ Higher RPN = greater risk = higher priority for action

Note: the scoring logic for D is inverted: the poorer the detection capability, the higher the score (indicating greater risk). This is what FMEA beginners most often get confused.

Analogy: FMEA is like a pre-flight safety check

The aviation preflight checklist embodies the spirit of FMEA: before the aircraft takes off, the pilot systematically confirms every element that could fail: engines, hydraulics, communications. The same goes for a pharmaceutical glass vial filling line; you cannot wait until the product has a problem to look for the cause.

4.4 When and What to Assess in an FMEA

FMEA — Timing, Scope, and Data Sources

An FMEA should be performed during the initial design and development of the product. Thus, critical elements of the container design can be identified to ensure product quality including, but not limited to, considerations for processing steps such as filling, blast freezing, lyophilization, and final storage conditions. The glass breakage FMEA may be performed in conjunction with a FMEA for other types of potential failures. This can confirm the original FMEA and provide a benchmark risk assessment for production. An FMEA should be performed during evaluations of changes to the process as a specific step of process change control. The existing FMEA should be modified and used to ascertain if risk will change due to the planned process change. Any increase in risk due to the planned change should be mitigated prior to implementation. A periodic review should be completed to confirm ongoing effectiveness of controlling risk levels and identify opportunities for further mitigation through development of new technology or advanced procedures.

The specific glass-handling hazards to address as part of an FMEA should include glass particles or fragments that could enter a container from a glass breakage event, and/or events or risks resulting in breakage or a cracked container. The scope of the FMEA should cover as much of the lifecycle of the glass container as possible, including supplier manufacturing and shipment as well as receipt, filling and packaging, and final distribution.

During the risk analysis, potential failure modes specific to the glass-handling hazards are identified and the potential causes and current controls are captured. The risk associated with specific failure scenarios is estimated using a risk priority number (RPN). An RPN is determined for each scenario based on the severity of the failure effect, the frequency of occurrence of the potential cause, and the detection and mitigation (prevention) capability that exists based on the controls currently in place. The definition and rationale for rankings used to complete each element of the RPN should be clearly outlined and agreed upon by the FMEA team.

Multiple sources of information are employed to determine the RPN. Process maps and procedures that outline the relevant process steps, and the equipment associated with them, are used to ensure that all relevant areas are captured in the evaluation. In addition, and importantly, observations on the manufacturing line are conducted as part of a complete process review of the manufacturing floor or location of the process and equipment. Pictures and videos gathered, either during the process review or from previous efforts, are useful for evaluating the severity, frequency, and detection of each failure mode. Specific glass-breakage frequencies, locations, and root causes, as well as investigation history, are used to further inform the FMEA. Visual inspection capabilities throughout the process should be outlined to provide information relative to detection.

Finally, the definition and rationale for rankings used to assess each criterion of the RPN should be clearly outlined and documented.

Commentary and Application

The three main times to perform an FMEA

TR87 explicitly lists three key points at which an FMEA should be performed:

  • 1. Initial design and development stage: systematically identify risk points before problems occur. This particularly involves filling, blast freezing, lyophilization, final storage conditions, and similar steps.
  • 2. When assessing process changes: part of change control; any planned change should first be assessed for whether it increases the RPN, and if so, mitigation must be completed before implementation.
  • 3. Periodic review: confirm that risk controls remain effective, and identify further mitigation opportunities offered by new technologies or procedures.

Scope of glass-specific hazards

The glass hazard scope of an FMEA should cover two main types:

  • Glass fragments/particles entering the container (contamination): directly affects product safety
  • Events that cause breakage or cracks (breakage/crack): affect container integrity

Lifecycle coverage: supplier manufacture and transport → receipt at the site → filling and packaging → final distribution.

RPN data sources: diverse inputs ensure accuracy

TR87 stresses that RPN scores cannot be imagined out of thin air; they must have an evidence base:

  • Process flow diagrams and SOPs (to ensure all steps are included)
  • Actual observation of the production line (most important! details that documents cannot show are visible only on the floor)
  • Photo and video records (to assess severity, frequency, and detectability)
  • Historical data on glass breakage frequency, location, and root cause
  • Deviation and complaint history
  • Visual inspection capability analysis

Practical application: key points for performing an FMEA in a CDMO environment

  • Prepare the flow diagram before the floor observation, so the observation has a more systematic direction
  • Use a phone or industrial camera to record key segments of vial movement on the filling line (especially the transition points where vials contact equipment)
  • Include every glass-related entry in the historical deviation records as a basis for the FMEA occurrence score
  • When a client requests a process change, immediately trigger a gap assessment against the existing FMEA

Scoring logic of the three dimensions

  • Severity: focuses on the "consequence of the impact"; glass fragments entering a sterile product is the highest severity (10), and no possibility of causing breakage is 1

Important: the inverted logic of Detection

The Detection score is the opposite of general intuition:

  • Detection capability 100% (certain to catch it) → D = 1 (low risk)
  • Detection capability <50% (almost never caught) → D = 10 (high risk)

This reflects a core idea: even if a failure is severe and frequent, as long as your system can detect and stop it 100% before it reaches the customer, the RPN will not be too high. Confirming the capability of visual inspection therefore becomes a key input.

Analogy: RPN is like a car insurance rate

When an insurer calculates a rate, it considers three factors: how severe an accident is (a wrecked car and injuries vs. a scrape), how often you have accidents (your past record), and whether you have protections such as ABS and airbags (detection/control capability).

The better the protection, the lower the premium; the logic for a lower RPN is the same: good detection and control reduce overall risk.

4.5 Post-Assessment Actions and Documentation

Risk Acceptance, Mitigation, and Documentation

After all risks are identified and assigned an RPN, each risk is evaluated to determine the appropriate level of mitigation or acceptance of risk and prioritized as to which potential failure modes should be addressed first. The level of mitigation should be proportionate to the level of risk. The rationale for the level of mitigation and/or acceptance of risk should be clearly documented and agreed upon by all FMEA participants and stakeholders.

The FMEA results, participants, ranking rationale, and supporting data should be appropriately documented to support future investigations, process change or periodic review, and regulatory discussions. In addition, the results of the FMEA should be communicated to the appropriate stakeholders to ensure mitigation actions are taken and tracked to closure.

Commentary and Application

After the RPN: prioritization and mitigation

Completing the RPN scoring is only a midpoint in the FMEA; the follow-up steps are equally important:

  • Prioritization: the failure modes with the highest RPN are addressed first (a decision tool when resources are limited)
  • Proportionality: the strength of mitigation should match the risk level; over-mitigating low risks wastes resources
  • Risk acceptance: if the decision is to accept a risk (take no further action), the rationale must be clearly documented and agreed by all FMEA participants

Documentation: the regulatory value of an FMEA

TR87 stresses the importance of FMEA documentation because it is used directly in three scenarios:

  • Future deviation investigations: allows quick look-back at whether this risk was foreseen and how it was scored
  • Process change review: use the existing FMEA as the baseline to assess the impact of a change
  • Regulatory discussions (FDA inspections, client audits): demonstrate that you have a systematic risk management capability

The document must include: FMEA results, the list of participants, the scoring rationale, and supporting data.

Practical application: FMEA closure checklist

  • Does every high-RPN item have a designated owner and a target completion date?
  • After mitigation is completed, has the RPN been re-evaluated (post-mitigation RPN)?
  • Is the FMEA document stored in the quality management system (QMS) and linked to the relevant SOP version numbers?
  • Is there a mechanism to pull up this FMEA for reconfirmation at the next periodic review?
  • Have the FMEA results been formally communicated to the process manager, the QA manager, and the client (if applicable)?

Analogy: the FMEA document is your "pre-game playbook"

Like a team's pre-game analysis report: you spent time studying the opponent's weaknesses and set a response strategy. If you do not write it down, the previous wisdom is lost the next time a similar game comes along. In the pharmaceutical industry, this "playbook" is your most powerful piece of evidence during a regulatory inspection.

Section 4 Summary

  • Three functions of risk management: prevention (identify risks during process design), investigation (root cause analysis), and regulatory communication (build confidence in audits). The scope covers the full lifecycle of glass containers, from supplier to customer.
  • Two approaches to assessment: quantitative (numerical) vs. qualitative (high/medium/low classification); both can be used, but the definition of each category and its basis must be clearly documented.
  • The need for a cross-functional team: a risk assessment cannot be done alone; SMEs and relevant responsible persons must participate together, and data sources must be diverse and solid.
  • Six risk tools: basic tools, Risk Ranking, statistical tools, FTA, HAZOP, and FMEA; each has its own applications, and FMEA is the tool of first choice for glass vial handling risk assessment.
  • Core RPN formula: RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection. Detection uses inverted logic: the stronger the detection capability, the lower the D score and the lower the overall RPN.
  • Three main times to perform an FMEA: ① initial design and development ② process change assessment ③ periodic review. The glass-vial-specific hazard scope covers two main categories: fragment contamination and container breakage.
  • Post-RPN actions: prioritize mitigation by RPN, with effort proportional to risk; the decision rationale must be documented, and the results communicated to authorized personnel and tracked through to closure.

5.0 Glass Vial Handling Process - Overview

5.0 Glass Vial Handling Process

The glass-handling process begins at the glass vial manufacturer and continues through the final packaging at the pharmaceutical company and shipment to their customer. Numerous transfers take place within that process. Considerations for glass handling must be taken into account for the transfers between process steps.

Some points to consider in transfers to and from the warehouse include ensuring the hand truck or lift wheels are in good condition, the operators are properly trained, the facility floor is in good condition with no potholes or deep cracks, and the floor of the transition from room to room is level.

Once vials are in the filling process, many types of transfers occur such as tray loading (flipping) or unloading onto the washer loader. Ensuring the proper fit for off-loading trays and the proper alignment of the transfer trays will mitigate the risk of damage to the vials. During transfer, "pushing" on glassware during the transfer can create excessive back pressure, which should be avoided. Any damaged vials should be discarded promptly throughout the process.

Caution should be taken to ensure suitable materials of construction are used for transfers on the filling line for belts, guide rails, dead plates, screw feeds, and star wheels.

5.0 Commentary: Process Overview and Transfer Risks

Core Concept: "Handling Is Risk"

From the moment vials leave the glass manufacturer until the pharmaceutical company completes secondary packaging and shipment, every transfer along the chain is a potential point of damage. The key message of TR87 is: damage often does not occur at filling, but accumulates at some unremarkable handling step upstream.

Analogy: Moving a Carton of Eggs

You would not push eggs quickly over a bumpy, uneven cart, nor let them press hard against each other. The logic for glass vials is exactly the same: floors, wheels and operator training are all risk factors. A vial with a micro-crack is a time bomb for future breakage when it is pressurized at filling or subjected to thermal expansion and contraction in lyophilization.

Key Equipment Terms (first appearance)

  • Back pressure: the pressure that vials at the rear exert on the vials ahead when a group of vials is pushed; excessive back pressure easily causes stress concentration in the glass.
  • Dead plate: the stationary transfer plate between two conveyor sections; a common abrasion point during vial transfer.
  • Star wheel: an intermittently rotating vial-pickup wheel used for positioning and spacing control; one of the components with the highest impact risk.
  • Screw feed: an infeed device that sets vial spacing by means of a helical groove; improper spacing easily pinches the vial body.

5.1 Packaging for Transport and Storage

5.1 Packaging for Transport and Storage

The glass-handling process begins at the glass vial manufacturer, where the pack-out configuration must be specified to minimize the risk of damage or breakage during transit or shipment from a supplier to a pharmaceutical firm. These configurations should be standardized for each container type and size: shrink-wrapped module type with glass-to-glass contact or a tray with or without cell partitions or separators. The number of containers that are held in a module or tray and their arrangement is typically driven by the infeed of the pharmaceutical processing equipment. The packaging format is also sized to maximize pallet space. Where modules are used, ensure proper packing and shrink-wrapping of the module (e.g., vial alignment, tightness of shrink-wrap) and avoid flexing and module-to-module impact during handling which may cause glass-to-glass abrasive damage.

The remaining aspect of the pack-out configuration should include a review of such factors as: the set-back of the load from the edge of the pallet; module-stacking configuration; need for corner protection and straps; need for slip sheets between layers; method of securing the load to the pallet; and total height of the pallet. Double-stacking of pallets, especially during transit, should be avoided.

5.1 Commentary: The Science of Packaging Design

Two Main Forms of Pack-out Configuration

  • Shrink-wrapped module: vials are in close contact and held together by shrink film; the advantage is high space utilization, the disadvantage is that once the shrink film loosens, glass-to-glass friction increases immediately.
  • Tray with/without cell partitions: a design with partitions prevents direct glass contact; the choice depends on whether the vial size and the equipment feed format match.

Analogy: Shipping Precision Instruments

When shipping a high-end camera lens, the manufacturer specifies the foam material, the fixing position and the pallet height. Vials may be consumables, but if hundreds of thousands of vials in one batch are crushed at sea because pallets were double-stacked, the loss is not just the glass but the credibility of the entire batch process.

Key Points of Pallet Design

  • Load must not overhang the pallet edge (set-back from edge)
  • A slip sheet is required between layers
  • Corner protection and straps are required
  • Total pallet height must comply with storage and transport limits
  • Double-stacking of pallets is strictly prohibited, especially during transport

In Practice: QA Audit Checklist

When requesting from the glass supplier, confirm the following documents:

  • Pack-out specification, stating module/tray dimensions and arrangement
  • Transport test report (vibration / drop test), verifying that the packaging can withstand actual transport stresses
  • Pallet layout drawing, confirming compatibility with storage equipment
5.2 Integrated Line with Multiple Transfer Points

5.2 Integrated Line with Multiple Transfer Points

To ensure the pharmaceutical processing equipment is designed appropriately, collaboration with machine manufacturers is recommended. Where equipment from different machine manufacturers is used, ensure that each machine is properly integrated into the line. An appropriate risk assessment should be conducted during the machine design and line integration. Typical transfer points at the pharmaceutical company that should be evaluated include belt transfers, guide rails, dead plates, screw feeds, star wheels, and pick-and-place devices.

Vials can be automatically or manually pushed from a tray onto an accumulation table, where they are transferred to an infeed screw. To ensure the proper feeding of vials into the screw, proper spacing between vials, which is determined by belt speed before the screw, is important. The number of vials that back up prior to the screw should be kept to a minimum to reduce glass-to-glass contact between vials that could potentially cause damage.

Machine designers endeavor to remove potential negative impacts to the vial; however, not all risks can be removed. With the transport of vials, abrasion to the body is always a risk; therefore, proper materials should be used to reduce friction on the bottom, heel, and body of the vial. The greatest risk in vial damage comes during the transition from infeed screw to star wheel or from star wheel to star wheel (or inspection turret). Proper timing of the star wheels is critical, as is precision machining of size-change parts and low-backlash gearing or clutches. If the vial is not handled smoothly, with care taken to reduce impact forces, the vial body may experience excessive stress, which can cause breakage, cracks, or other damage.

A risk assessment should be conducted to properly design the process and materials for collecting downed vials that are ejected from the line if they may be reintroduced into the line. The assessment should determine the appropriate drop height, the amount of cushion or padding required, the frequency of emptying the eject bin, and the level of inspection required prior to reintroducing the vials to the line. The edges of the eject bin should be rounded to avoid sharp edges that could damage the vials.

5.2 Commentary: Risk Management of Integrated Lines

The Challenge of Multiple Suppliers

In reality, the washer, depyrogenation tunnel, filler and lyophilizer of one filling line may come from 3 to 4 different equipment suppliers. For each interface, speed matching, height differences and material compatibility must be risk-assessed before equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ). TR87 explicitly requires a risk assessment during machine design and line integration, rather than waiting until validation to discover problems.

Highest-Risk Transfer Point: Screw to Star Wheel

The infeed screw feeds vials at a set spacing, while the star wheel receives them with an intermittent motion. If there is an error in the timing of the two, the vial is "pinched" at the handoff point and the resulting impact force far exceeds the allowable stress of the glass. This is the primary source of vial breakage incidents on the line.

Corrective direction: precision-machined size-change parts + low-backlash gearing/clutches + periodic timing calibration.

Analogy: Merging onto a Highway

When a car merges from an on-ramp onto the main road at the wrong moment, it causes a side collision. The star wheel picking up vials works on the same principle: when speed difference, angle difference and timing difference all go wrong at once, vials break.

Risk Assessment Elements for Re-introducing Vials from the Eject Bin

The risk assessment should cover:
1. Drop height
2. Cushion/padding specification
3. Frequency of emptying
4. Inspection level before re-introduction
5. Eject bin edge design (rounded edges, to avoid secondary cuts from sharp edges)
5.2.1 Accumulators

5.2.1 Accumulators

Accumulators are used between segments of a filling line where the throughput speeds of the specific units of equipment may vary or be intermittent, or where one unit may be more likely to experience periods of down time (e.g., after depyrogenation and before filling, or after stoppering but before capping). Accumulators are also used for loading and off-loading trays at the beginning, during transfers, or at the end of line.

Accumulators may be of several types, including rotary tables, push (back pressure), and bi-flow.

5.2.1.1 Key Parts of the Process and Potential Impact on the Containers

The accumulation of vials often leads to abrasion damage to the glass surface. This happens especially where large numbers of vials are tightly gathered and the vials continue to revolve and rotate against one another, or where one or more vials become trapped in an eddy of the flow and remain for a long period of time. This type of damage is typically evidenced by circumferential scuff or scratch rings. These rings are usually observed at contact areas just below the shoulder and above the heel of the vial. While every surface discontinuity affects glass strength, the significance of these, relative to the potential for container failure, should be evaluated through the process described in Section 5.4.

The type of damage may occur wherever rotary tables are used, but it is most prevalent when these tables are used immediately after depyrogenation. Depyrogenation dehydrates the glass surface, eliminating its natural lubricity and exacerbating friction damage. In addition to damaging the glass surface, visible and subvisible glass particulates may be generated in the process.

5.2.1.2 Points to Consider

As good general practice, the table should be properly sized for the planned line speeds and large enough so that it is not loaded to more than one-third of its capacity. Additionally, directional flow control devices should be engineered to prevent eddies (opposite circular flows) in the flow of vials.

Guides and rails should be constructed of suitable, nonabrasive plastic materials. Transfers onto the table should prevent any impact, either glass-to-glass or glass-to-metal, especially at the heel of the vial. To facilitate this, the turntable itself should be slightly lower than the dead plate or belt from which the vials are being received. This difference, however, must be small enough that it does not cause vials to tip over while still allowing for a smooth transfer.

Monitoring and controlling the vial accumulators can mitigate the risk of damage to the glass surface, and limiting the number of vials allowed to accumulate on the table to a practical minimum is recommended.

Throughput speeds should be as consistent as possible for each segment of the line, reducing the need for accumulation. When this is not possible, the line should be regulated to minimize accumulation. Accordingly, the movement of vials on the table should also stop if the line itself is stopped. This will preclude excessive accumulation of vials onto the table.

If turntables or accumulation tables must be used, they should be designed to minimize the momentum of the containers (e.g., low speed settings) in order to reduce the level of forces between containers, thus reducing the occurrence of abrasions in the glass and generation of the glass particles.

5.2.1 Commentary: Damage Mechanisms of Accumulators

Why Is an Accumulator Needed?

The equipment sections of a filling line run at different speeds (for example, the depyrogenation tunnel delivers vials continuously, but the filler may stop briefly for a format change or a fault), and the accumulator acts as a buffer in between. Common types:

  • Rotary table: the most common; vials queue up moving in a circle on the rotating table
  • Push / back pressure: relies on continuous thrust from behind to keep vials moving forward; prone to excessive back pressure
  • Bi-flow: can convey in both directions; highly flexible

The Most Overlooked Damage: Circumferential Scuff Rings

On a rotary table, vials continuously rotate against each other. If a vial is trapped in an eddy and cannot move forward, it is repeatedly rubbed at the same height, forming a ring-shaped scuff.

Location: below the shoulder and above the heel, which are exactly the two geometric transition zones where glass stress is most concentrated.

Consequence: although the damage looks "not serious", these micro-cracks can become crack initiation points under the thermal stress of lyophilization or autoclaving, causing container failure.

Analogy: The Principle of Sanding

Two glass vials grinding against each other on a rotary table is the same physical principle as sanding with sandpaper. Glass after depyrogenation is more like "dry sandstone": without the lubricating water film, COF rises, and the same force causes abrasion that is twice as deep.

Key Rules for Rotary Table Design

Best practice rules:

  Loading ≤ table capacity × 1/3

Table height:
  Table surface < height of the infeed dead plate
  The height difference must be small enough not to topple vials

Line speed control:
  Keep the speed of all sections as consistent as possible → reduces the need for accumulation
  Line stops → the table should also stop immediately
  Table speed setting → as low as possible, to reduce container momentum

In Practice: Rotary Table Operation Checklist

  • Confirm that the guide rail material is non-abrasive plastic (non-metal)
  • Confirm that the table has a flow control device (to prevent eddy formation)
  • Set a maximum accumulation alarm, not exceeding 1/3 of capacity
  • When the line stops, confirm that the table interlock stop is effective
  • Monitor the first rotary table after depyrogenation with special care, as glass COF is highest at this point
5.3 Vial Washing

5.3 Vial Washing

In parenteral packaging, glass vials are typically washed prior to being sterilized for filling. Vial washing is conducted in order to ensure removal of such materials as bacterial toxins, chemical substances, and particulates so that the product meets expectations of identity, purity, quality, and safety.

5.3.1 Washing Machines

Washing machines are available in various designs, the most common being linear tunnel and rotary. Vials are washed with either a diving spray nozzle or a stationary, nonpenetrating spray nozzle. When using diving spray nozzles, where a needle enters the vial and sprays the vial from the bottom up, there is a risk of needle strikes on the bottom of the vial, the inside wall, or the finish of the vial that could result in a bent needle, glass damage, and particulate generation (glass chips). The risk of vial strikes increases when multiple vials must be aligned with an array of needles for simultaneous washing. The process parameters, including needle depth and centering, must be adapted for varying vial sizes or designs. Needles should be routinely inspected for straightness to prevent needle strikes. Some newer washing machines can be equipped with needle strike alarms to aid in risk management.

For washers that use buckets to hold the vials, inverted or double-stacked vials (two vials within the same bucket) are vulnerable to breakage due to needle strikes. Automated systems that detect and eject upside-down vials or detect double-stacked vials can be incorporated to mitigate this risk. Manual intervention procedures to address vial orientation issues may be needed for some washing systems.

For rotary washers, water jet pressure should be moderated to ensure vials are not forcibly ejected from the grippers by an excessive jet stream.

In limited applications, batch washers are used to wash vials one tray or module at a time. Loading plates and corresponding washing needles must be matched to the respective tray configuration and vial size to avoid glass damage or breakage during loading. A visual check and preventive maintenance plan should be in place to identify bent or damaged needles. Care should be taken during loading and unloading to prevent glass damage or breakage.

5.3 Commentary: Damage Risks of Vial Washers

The Purpose of Washing

The goal of washing injectable packaging components is to remove: bacterial toxins, chemical substances and particulates. This bears directly on the safety of the final drug product and on FDA/EMA compliance requirements.

Needle Strike: The Biggest Risk in Washing

Cause: when a diving spray nozzle is used, the needle must be inserted accurately into the vial mouth. With misalignment, the needle strikes the vial bottom, the inner wall or the finish, resulting in:

  • A bent needle, causing continued needle strikes in subsequent batches until it is detected
  • Glass chips, causing invisible particulate contamination
  • Finish damage, reducing stopper seal integrity

Prevention: periodic visual or automated checks of needle straightness; re-confirm needle depth and alignment at format change; newer washers can be fitted with a needle strike alarm.

Inverted / Double-stacked Vials

In a bucket-type washer, if a vial is inverted or two vials are stuck in the same bucket, the risk of needle strike rises sharply, and because an inverted vial blocks the spray, cleaning effectiveness is also greatly reduced. An automated detection and eject system is the best solution; equipment without this function needs a manual procedure.

Analogy: Aiming an Injection Needle at a Vein

When a nurse gives an injection and the needle is not aligned, it damages the vessel wall. The same applies to the washing needle of a vial washer: alignment accuracy determines whether you get "clean washing" or "new particulate contamination".

In Practice: Key Points for Washer PM

  • Before a format change: confirm that the needle depth and centering settings have been adjusted
  • Periodically: 100% visual check of needle straightness (recommended after each batch format change)
  • Rotary washer: confirm that the water pressure setting does not exceed the gripper clamping force
  • Batch washer: the loading plate and tray specification must correspond one to one, with documented control
5.4 Dry Heat Sterilization and Depyrogenation

5.4 Dry Heat Sterilization and Depyrogenation

Dry heat sterilization and depyrogenation of the vials occurs immediately after washing and is usually accomplished using dry heat. Dry heat ovens may take the form of tunnels or batch ovens. The ovens often serve as the transfer portals from the preparation area into the aseptic area. Vials are usually sterilized at ≥ 160 °C for up to two hours and depyrogenated at approximately 325 °C for at least thirty minutes, but the times and temperatures can vary depending on the specific process requirements.

These two processes may increase the coefficient of friction (COF) of the glass surface and make the vials more susceptible to abrasion. This is of particular concern if the vials will be subjected to glass-to-glass or glass-to-metal contact, such as on a rotary accumulation table. This type of friction damage is typically evidenced by scuff rings or scratches on the vials. This damage is usually observed just below the shoulder and above the heel of the vials. Though small and relatively minor in appearance, scratches and scuffs can potentially contribute to breakage under sufficient mechanical or thermal stresses further downstream. In addition to damaging the glass surface, visible and subvisible glass particulates may also be generated in the process. The longer the time the vials are subjected to high temperatures, the more profound the increase of the COF. In a case where vials are in contact with one another, one vial may bond with the surface of another vial, causing them to stick together. When these vials come apart, distinct vertical marks (stuck marks) will be apparent. These marks may act as stress concentrators and fracture origins as the vials are subjected to subsequent loads during downstream processing or handling.

5.4.1 Continuous Convection Tunnel

After the air-drying process, the glass vials (either in single file or in rows) pass onto a conveyor belt over a dead plate. Back pressure from the continuous loading of the belt pushes the material onto the conveyor.

Ideally, the conveyer belt should be operated in a "pull mode" and run at a slightly higher speed than the feed rate from the washer in order to maintain a small space (e.g., ~1 mm) between the rows of vials. This transition is sometimes supported by a pushing device to complete the transfer from the washer to depyrogenation tunnel.

Maintaining a minimal downward transition of the vials is recommended to reduce the probability of damaging the heel region.

Vials are then moved through the convection tunnel on the conveyor at a predetermined rate. Tunnels typically include three heat zones: a preheat zone, a hot zone (where the glassware is exposed to sufficient temperature for sufficient time to effect sterilization/depyrogenation), and a cooling zone. Depyrogenation guidelines are presented in PDA Technical Report No. 3.

The vials are then pushed out of the tunnel, using slight back pressure, across a dead plate, and onto the next stage of the process by an accumulator.

5.4.2 Points to Consider

As best practice, containers should be dry prior to exposure to depyrogenation temperatures (Zone 2), as residual moisture may attack the interior surface of the glass causing etching or glass corrosion.

In the event downstream processes come to a stop, it should also halt additional vials from entering the tunnel to prevent overloading, while allowing the in-process batch to complete the validated depyrogenation cycle.

Viewing windows are recommended so the entire depyrogenation area can be observed for evidence of overcrowding, fallen or broken containers.

Although the glass structure is not impacted directly by the temperatures used for depyrogenation, leaving the containers in the tunnel beyond the validated depyrogenation process time is not recommended.

The temperatures achieved during depyrogenation remove the protective water skin from the containers, leaving them more vulnerable to surface damage, such as scratches.

5.4.3 Batch Depyrogenation Oven

Another method for dry heat sterilization and depyrogenation is the use of a depyrogenation oven, which allows for processing of smaller rather than continuous processing. Trays of vials are loaded into the oven on racks and depyrogenated according to a validated cycle. The trays are typically unloaded under a Grade A (ISO 5) unidirectional airflow and then transported to the vial-filling line. Due to the absence of the protective water skin, consideration should be given during loading, unloading, and transport to limit movement of the glass vials within the stainless-steel trays to prevent damage or breakage.

5.4 Commentary: Fragility of Glass After Depyrogenation

Dry Heat Sterilization vs. Depyrogenation: Comparison of Temperature and Time

Dry heat sterilization:
  Temperature: ≥ 160 °C
  Time: up to 2 hours
  Purpose: kill microorganisms (to SAL 10⁻⁶)

Depyrogenation:
  Temperature: approx. 325 °C
  Time: at least 30 minutes
  Purpose: degrade pyrogens/endotoxins
  Regulatory basis: PDA Technical Report No. 3

Key point to remember:
  Depyrogenation temperature >> sterilization temperature
  → greater effect on the glass surface

Consequences of Increased COF

During depyrogenation, the "protective water skin" on the glass surface is completely evaporated. This water film is a natural lubricant, and once it is lost:

  • Glass surface COF rises significantly
  • Any glass-to-glass or glass-to-metal contact causes abrasion that is multiplied
  • The longer the time at high temperature, the greater the rise in COF

Stuck marks: at high temperature, two vials in contact may bond at the surface; when separated they leave vertical streaks, which become stress concentrators and sites of downstream vial breakage.

Analogy: Ceramics Fresh from the Kiln

When a ceramic piece is taken out of the kiln, its surface is dry and its brittleness is at its highest; if pieces knock into each other at this point, they chip more easily than at room temperature. Vials after depyrogenation are in a similar state: structurally intact, but with the surface protective layer gone, so any mechanical friction leaves a mark.

Three Temperature Zones of a Continuous Tunnel

  • Preheat zone: avoids thermal stress in the glass from rapid heating
  • Hot zone: achieves the effective exposure time and temperature for sterilization/depyrogenation
  • Cooling zone: cools slowly to avoid glass breakage from temperature difference, and ensures vials have fallen to a safe temperature before entering the aseptic area

"Pull mode" conveyor design: the belt speed is slightly faster than the washer output speed, leaving a gap of ~1 mm between vials and avoiding back pressure buildup.

5.4.2 Summary of Operating Precautions

  • Vials must be completely dry before entering the tunnel; residual moisture attacks the inner glass surface at high temperature (etching/corrosion)
  • When the downstream line stops, the infeed must be stopped by interlock, but vials already inside the tunnel should continue to complete the validated cycle
  • Install viewing windows to monitor vial accumulation and fallen vials inside the tunnel
  • Exceeding the validated depyrogenation time is not recommended; although the glass structure is not directly affected, the longer the water film stays absent, the worse it is

Batch Depyrogenation Oven (5.4.3)

Suitable for small batches or the development stage. Key points:

  • After leaving the oven, unloading must take place under Grade A (ISO 5) unidirectional flow to maintain aseptic conditions
  • Vials in stainless steel trays have no water film protection; during handling, minimize shaking to prevent abrasion or breakage
  • Risk of batch mode: compared with a continuous tunnel, more manual transfer operations are needed after the oven, and the chance of exposure to an uncontrolled environment is higher

In Practice: Recommended SOP Controls After Depyrogenation

  • First rotary table after depyrogenation: reduce speed to the minimum (low speed setting)
  • Confirm that all guide rail material is non-abrasive plastic; metal guide rails are prohibited
  • The height difference of the dead plate between the tunnel outlet and the filler must not exceed 1 mm
  • Batch oven unloading: performed by trained personnel under a Grade A hood, avoiding knocks to the trays
5.5 Vial Filling

5.5 Vial Filling

Drug product is added to vials at a filling station. Vials are transferred to the filling station after washing and depyrogenation by a conveyor belt, with a star wheel or by a gripper that picks up the vial by the neck. Vials are filled with a filling needle by either a fixed nozzle or a diving nozzle apparatus. Once filled, the vials move along the line to the stoppering station where they are stoppered either by direct placement (where the stopper is picked up by vacuum) or by a rotating wheel.

5.5.1 Impacts

Vials transported by means of a conveyor belt experience glass-to-glass contact as they move along the line, which can result in abrasions to the glass. Vials that are transported by a star wheel or by gripper have glass-to-glass contact only at the vial gate, where they accumulate, and where damage can occur. For filling lines that use grippers (i.e., pick and place) to move vials, the gripper configuration and the amount of pressure exerted on the vial are critical and should be compatible with the vial neck size. Ideally, grippers should be constructed of plastic rather than metal.

Needle installation and alignment should be verified at set-up. A needle strike may occur when a filling needle is misaligned with the vial being filled; the needle strikes the top of the vial and can chip the vial and/or bend the needle. Needles can also become bent when striking vials that have offset finishes. Damage in the lip region of the vial can go unnoticed through inspection as the stopper and seal can mask the damage. Regular inspection during the production batch should assess misalignment of and damage to filling needles.

During weight checks, vials can be damaged or broken when transferred from the filling line to the weigh station. Vacuum-assisted vial grippers can reduce the incidence of damage but can also be impacted by particulate matter if the vials are not adequately clean.

5.5.2 Points to Consider

The filling process can be improved to further reduce damage to the vials by replacing the metal with plastic throughout the line. Damage to the vial due to needle strikes can be reduced by changing the stainless-steel filling needles to disposable plastic. In contrast to the impact from a metal needle, a plastic filling needle would fail or bend before the vial would break.

5.5 Commentary: Damage Modes at the Filling Station

Filling Needle Strike: The Filling Station Version

Similar to needle strike in the washer, but the filling station version has a special danger: damage to the lip region of the vial is masked by the stopper and aluminum seal, making it nearly impossible to detect by visual inspection.

This means a vial that already has a micro-crack at the lip may pass the release AQL smoothly and only break when the consumer opens the cap, which is a very high safety risk.

Special risk: Offset finish. Vials with geometric deviation of the finish are more likely to deflect the filling needle.

Analogy: The "Sacrificial Design" of a Plastic Needle

The principle of the disposable plastic filling needle is the engineering "safety fuse" concept: it is better for the needle itself to bend (making it easy to detect) than for the glass vial to be damaged. A metal needle is the opposite: it is hard enough that when it hits a vial, the glass breaks first.

Damage Risk During Weight Sampling

When filled vials are periodically sampled for weighing (IPC weight check), improper handling (such as gripping the vial body with a metal clamp) easily causes damage at this step. Recommendations:

  • Use a vacuum-assisted gripper to pick up vials by vacuum suction
  • Ensure vials are clean so that particulates do not block the vacuum suction holes
  • Establish a standardized weight-sampling SOP and include it in personnel training

In Practice: Recommendations for Replacing Filling Line Materials

  • Change the guide rails along the whole line to non-abrasive plastic (replace metal parts)
  • Change grippers to plastic material, and confirm compatibility with the vial neck dimensions
  • Evaluate the use of disposable plastic filling needles (to reduce the risk of glass breakage from needle strike)
  • Visually check filling needle alignment and straightness periodically each shift (recommended to be recorded in the batch record)
5.6 Stopper Placement

5.6 Stopper Placement

At the stoppering station, the stoppers can be placed on the vials either directly (pick-and-place) or by a rotating wheel. The risk of incurring damage during the stoppering process is very low as there is no glass-to-glass contact during stoppering. Any breakage observed during stoppering is most likely due to damage that occurred upstream.

5.6 Commentary: The Quality Significance of Stoppering

The Stoppering Station: A Point Where Damage Is "Revealed", Not "Created"

An important conclusion of TR87: the stoppering process itself involves no glass-to-glass contact, so the risk of glass damage is extremely low. If a vial breaks at this station, it is almost certain that damage has accumulated at some upstream step (most likely the star wheel handoff, rotary table abrasion or filling needle strike).

Therefore, if broken vials or glass fragments are observed at the stoppering station:

  • A root cause investigation should be started immediately, tracing back upstream
  • Evaluate whether vials already stoppered in the same batch may have latent hidden damage
  • Re-examine the operating conditions of the rotary table after depyrogenation

Analogy: The Last Straw

If a stone slab has a crack, a light press will break it; but you would not conclude that "the light press was too strong", you would ask "where did the crack come from?" The logic of vial breakage at the stoppering station is the same: do not just fix the stoppering machine, but find the upstream step that gave the glass its crack in the first place.

Two Stoppering Methods

  • Pick-and-place: the stopper is picked up by vacuum and placed directly; requires high alignment accuracy, but the insertion force can be controlled precisely
  • Rotating wheel: stoppers are pushed into the vial mouths in sequence on a rotating disc; fast, suitable for high-capacity lines

Neither method involves glass contact, so the key control at the stoppering station is stopper integrity and positioning accuracy, not glass damage.

Section 5 (Part A) Summary: Glass Vial Handling Process (5.0-5.6)

  • Overall framework: damage to glass vials is a cumulative process; starting from the supplier's pack-out configuration, every transfer step may leave micro-damage, and the consequences appear downstream (especially in lyophilization or secondary packaging).
  • Packaging and transport (5.1): the pack-out configuration must be standardized; double-stacking of pallets is prohibited; shrink-wrap modules must be kept tight to avoid glass-to-glass abrasion from collisions between modules.
  • Integrated line (5.2): the highest-risk point is the infeed screw to star wheel handoff; rotary accumulator loading should not exceed 1/3; the rotary table after depyrogenation is the node most prone to abrasion.
  • Washing (5.3): needle strike is the primary risk, and needle straightness must be checked periodically; automated detection of inverted/double-stacked vials can effectively reduce the risk.
  • Depyrogenation (5.4): after the water film is removed at 325 °C, glass COF rises and the abrasion effect intensifies; a pull mode conveyor maintains ~1 mm spacing between vials; after batch oven unloading, operate under Grade A and minimize shaking.
  • Filling (5.5): damage from the filling needle striking the vial lip is masked by the stopper and is the type of damage hardest to detect; plastic filling needles are recommended in place of stainless steel, using "bent needle" instead of "broken vial" as the detection mechanism.
  • Stoppering (5.6): the stoppering step itself has the lowest risk of damage; a broken vial at this station is almost certainly the result of upstream damage, and a traceback investigation should be started rather than just adjusting the stoppering machine parameters.

5.7 Lyophilization

5.7 Lyophilization

Lyophilization, or freeze-drying, is a process through which water is removed from the liquid solution by freezing the product, subliming the ice, and desorbing residual water to low levels. Vials are filled with liquid product and stoppers are partially seated into the vials on the filling line prior to accumulation for the lyophilizer-loading operation. The vials are loaded onto the freeze-dryer shelves, either manually using bottomless trays, or via an automated mechanical loading system. Upon reaching the specified end conditions of the process, the stoppers are fully seated into the vials with the hydraulic shelf-stoppering system. Vials are then unloaded from the chamber. Manipulation of the vials during the loading, stoppering, and unloading processes, in addition to the freeze-drying process itself, can all be potential sources of glass vial handling issues.

5.7 Lyophilization: Conceptual Guide

Core Concept: The Three Stages of Lyophilization

  • Freezing: the liquid product turns into solid ice crystals, with volume expansion.
  • Primary drying / sublimation: under low pressure, ice sublimes directly into water vapor, removing most of the water.
  • Secondary drying / desorption: removes residual bound water so that the moisture content falls below specification.

Note: Glass Vials Undergo Multiple Mechanical Operations in Lyophilization

From the partially seated stopper after filling, to entering the lyophilizer, stoppering and leaving the lyophilizer, every step carries a risk of glass damage. This makes lyophilization one of the most complex unit operations for glass integrity management in aseptic fill-finish.

5.7.1 Loading and Unloading

During the traying, transfer, loading, and unloading process, glass-to-glass and glass-to-metal interactions occur similar to other vial manipulation operations, such as filling and capping. Vials are typically conveyed by sliding across stainless-steel surfaces and then nested with other vials in a close-packed configuration, experiencing forces applied from surrounding vials during movement. Vials could be accumulated onto individual trays and transferred manually to the freeze-dryer or transferred by automated loading systems. Typical automated material-handling systems transfer and load the entire lyophilizer shelf, row by row, or by some other indexing method. The tooling used to manipulate these vials should be compatible with the vial size and type. Material-handling methods with significant glass-to-glass and glass-to-metal contact can lead to small scratches, checks, or cracks, creating a weakened region in the glass that can potentially lead to breakage at that location when stresses are exerted.

Over time and repeated use, cleaning, and sterilization, wear on the bottom surface of the tray can become evident and warping of the transfer ring may occur, leading to "pinched" vials when nested. In turn, the prominence of glass defects can increase the risk of container failure due to increased friction and limited mobility.

5.7.1 Loading and Unloading

Analogy: The Sardine Can Effect

Close-packed glass vials are like sardines squeezed together: every surrounding vial exerts force on them. Sliding on stainless steel surfaces and collisions between adjacent vials are sources of scratches, checks and cracks on the glass surface.

Advantages and Disadvantages of an Automated Loading System

  • Advantages: reduces personnel contact, increases speed and lowers contamination risk.
  • Disadvantages: if the tooling does not match the vial type, or the transfer ring is deformed by repeated SIP/WFI cleaning, a "pinched vial" results, causing stress concentration in the glass.

Key Points of Preventive Maintenance

Wear on the underside of trays and warping of transfer rings are cumulative problems, and a scheduled maintenance program should be established. Visual inspection combined with flatness measurement is recommended, with replacement criteria defined.

5.7.2 Lyophilization Process Concerns

The potentially significant stress imposed on the glass from the expansion of product during the freezing and drying steps is a concern. As the product freezes, crystals form and the product can expand, often upward from the bottom of the vial, but also outwardly and down from the product surface toward the bottom. The extent of this effect will vary depending on the specific product formulation. Although this expansion would not initiate glass breakage by itself, the risk to vial breakage increases with the presence of a defect, such as a blemish due to scratches in the vial from glass-to-glass and glass-to-metal abrasion. Vial design, especially in the heel area, may also have an impact on breakage during expansion. With a large fill volume, internal forces are increased significantly. Examples of glass fractures and lensing can occur during the freeze-drying process. Other product effects can also impose stress on the container.

5.7.2 Effect of the Process Itself on Glass

Superposition Model of Glass Breakage Risk

Breakage risk = f (product expansion force × pre-existing glass defects × fill volume × vial bottom design)

- A single factor is usually not enough to cause breakage
- When multiple factors are present at once, risk rises non-linearly
- The heel area (bottom corner of the vial) is a high-risk site for stress concentration

What Is Lensing?

When the product expands during freezing, it creates local stress on the glass surface, causing arc-shaped cracks in the glass that look like a lens, known as lensing. This is a glass defect type specific to lyophilization and must be specifically identified during visual inspection.

Special Considerations for High Fill Volume Products

For products with a large fill volume, the internal forces generated by freezing expansion increase significantly. The maximum fill volume should be evaluated during process development, and the suitability of the vial design confirmed with the glass supplier.

5.7.3 Seating the Stopper

Final seating of the stopper occurs within the lyophilizer chamber at the end of the lyophilization process, when the shelves are either lowered or raised to fully insert the partially seated stoppers. The suspended shelves most often are driven by a hydraulic ram. Pressure exerted by the hydraulic system is set initially for the most demanding need, such as 2 cc or 3 cc vials with a 13 mm finish. Such small vials have greater density across the shelf surface area and require the greatest pressure (force per unit area) to fully seat the stoppers. In order to apply a uniform pressure to each vial across the shelves, the shelves should be level and planar. If there is any significant variation to pressure distribution, the force on the vials can be localized significantly in one area, which could lead to glass breakage.

One area to assess is the container-to-container height variation; stoppering forces will be concentrated to any vials that may be slightly taller than the surrounding vials. The difference in vial-stopper height, in some instances, has been sufficient to result in broken vials. When a partial shelf-load of vials left at the end of the filling operation are being placed on the lowest shelf, there must be a sufficient number of vials, and they must be evenly positioned across the shelf to accommodate the pressure evenly. Failing to evenly distribute the vials across the shelves can lead to cocked shelves and distortion of the shelf suspension hardware. In addition, for container-closure systems with a poor stopper fit or misplaced stopper, cocked closures upon stoppering can lead to excessive force and, potentially, broken vials.

Some breakage observed in a lyophilizer may not be due to improper handling but may be attributed to vial nonconformities such as leaners, rocker or uneven bottoms, or an improperly designed or formed bottom radius.

5.7.3 Stoppering Operation

Principle of the Hydraulic Shelf-Stoppering System

The lyophilizer shelves are driven by hydraulic rods, moving up and down to fully press the partially seated stoppers into the vial mouths. The design pressure is set based on the specification that is hardest to seat (typically 2 cc / 3 cc vials with a 13 mm finish).

Three Main Root Causes of Breakage During Stoppering

  • Shelves not level/planar: uneven pressure distribution causes local overload.
  • Container height variation: taller vials bear a disproportionate force.
  • Partial shelf-load: the last batch has too few vials and an uneven distribution, causing cocked shelves.

Analogy: A Stapler on a Desk

Imagine using a stapler to press a whole row of documents at once: if some documents are stacked thicker, the stapler applies pressure to those first. Even a tiny difference in vial height (even only 0.X mm), under the uniform downward pressure of a hydraulic system, makes the taller vials bear a clearly greater force, exceeding the limit the glass can tolerate.

CDMO Practice Point: Managing Partial Shelf-Load

The "partial batch" at the end of a batch is a high-risk scenario. Recommendations:

  • Establish a written procedure specifying the minimum number of vials and the uniform distribution requirement for a partial batch.
  • Use dummy vials (inert filled vials) to fill out the shelf and ensure uniform distribution.
  • Record the loading diagram of each lyophilization run, as an important basis for investigating glass breakage.

5.7.4 Points to Consider

Once risks for glass handling defects and breakage have been assessed, based on material-handling conditions and variables for the specific product and container configuration, there are systems that can be implemented to reduce the risk. The material of construction on contact surfaces, such as guide rails on the filling line during material-handling operations, has an impact. The condition of the shelves, conveyors, trays, and rings should be flat, level, and smooth, and monitored on a predetermined schedule for adequate maintenance. Vial pushers, indexers, and other vial manipulators should be compatible with the system. Using plastics for vial contact surfaces and eliminating pinch points can help alleviate glass-to-glass or glass-to-metal friction (stickiness). The container-closure industry is making strides in decreasing the risk of vial defects; for example, preloaded vials, already indexed into tubs, can limit glass-to-glass and glass-to-metal interaction.

The height of the container-closure system should have an allowable tolerance range specified to prevent significant variation across a shelf and unequal forces. Thermal stresses should be limited, where possible, during the lyophilization process. Product expansion behavior during the solidification process should be known and potentially limited, by limiting fill height in the container, altering the ratio of crystallizing to noncrystallizing components, or considering alternate freezing methods, such as controlled nucleation, which initiates nucleation of ice from the liquid surface.

5.7.4 Risk Mitigation Measures

Controlled Nucleation Technology

In conventional freezing, ice crystal nucleation is random and may form a non-uniform ice crystal structure at the vial bottom or side wall, aggravating expansion stress. Controlled nucleation technology uses an external trigger (such as a pressure pulse or ultrasound) so that ice crystals in all vials start nucleating simultaneously from the liquid surface, forming more uniform and larger ice crystals, which reduces glass stress and improves drying efficiency.

Lyophilization Glass Risk Control Checklist

  • Contact surface material: prefer plastic guide rails and avoid metal-to-glass contact.
  • Equipment condition: periodically confirm the flatness and smoothness of shelves, trays and transfer rings.
  • Container height specification: establish a height tolerance range for the container-closure system.
  • Fill height limit: evaluate whether fill height can be lowered to reduce the expansion force.
  • Formulation optimization: adjust the ratio of crystalline to amorphous components to control freezing expansion behavior.
  • Preloaded tub procurement: reduces glass-to-glass contact.

5.8 Capping

5.8 Capping

Vial capping is the last step in the aseptic process to fully seal a parenteral vial. The primary seal of a parenteral vial packaging system is the interface of the elastomeric stopper flange and the land sealing surface on the crown of the vial. Capping is the process of sealing the vial by compressing the flange of a rubber stopper against the top surface of the glass vial, forming the primary seal, and then crimping an aluminum ferrule around the stopper and vial finish. Sufficient vertical load force is necessary to optimally compress the stopper, achieve sufficient seal tightness, and assure container-closure integrity. A side load may also be imparted when the metal is crimped.

5.8 Overview of the Capping Process

The Dual Seal Structure of Capping

  • Primary seal: the contact interface between the elastomeric stopper flange and the top surface of the vial mouth (land sealing surface / crown); this is the key barrier preventing external contamination from entering.
  • Aluminum ferrule crimping: the aluminum cap is pressed tightly onto the stopper and the outer edge of the vial mouth, providing mechanical fixation and tamper-evidence.

The Key to CCI: Vertical Load Force

Sufficient vertical compression force (vertical load force) is the core of ensuring Container-Closure Integrity (CCI). Insufficient force leads to insufficient stopper compression and seal failure. Excessive force risks glass breakage. Optimizing the pressure setting is a core element of capping process validation.

5.8.1 Impacts

Regardless of the sealing method, the stress created in the glass from these loads of an optimized capping process (< 130 N force) should be well below that where breakage would be expected. Breakage, however, is sometimes observed at the capper. Glass failure at capping is likely the manifestation of significant surface damage caused to the vial earlier in the process.

Improper set-up of the capper or excessive vial variability (e.g., offset necks, bent necks, large necks, leaners, rocker bottoms, laps (folds) in the neck) could cause the neck to come in contact with the sealing rail or disc, causing surface damage to the neck of the vial that may propagate into fractures. Transfer of vials into and out of the capper usually consists of a combination of belts, screw feeders, and star wheels.

5.8.1 Capping Impact and Breakage Analysis

Root Cause Logic of Vial Breakage at Capping

Glass breakage observed at the capper is
≠ caused by the capping force itself (< 130 N is generally safe)

Instead: the upstream processes (filling, lyophilization, conveying)

High-Risk Vial Defects (to be rejected before infeed)

  • Offset neck / bent neck
  • Large neck
  • Leaners
  • Rocker bottoms
  • Laps (folds) in the neck

Any of the above defects may cause the glass to contact the sealing rail or sealing disc, creating new surface damage on the neck.

5.8.2 Points to Consider

To manage the capping process, an appropriate combination of set-up parameters and change-parts must be determined for each container-closure combination. That the sealing disc or rail will not contact vials within normal variability must be assured, and detailed capping equipment set-up parameters must be established and documented for each package combination. The parameters should be based on achieving sufficient stopper compression to achieve acceptable container-closure integrity without excessive force. Component variation and stack-up tolerances should be considered in establishing the capper parameters.

Offsetting (top and bottom) springs or dampers are employed to compensate for total height variation. Additionally, automated inspection systems are used to remove out-of-specification components, vials missing stoppers, or misplaced stoppers.

5.8.2 Key Points of Capping Process Control

Documented Setup for Each Container-Closure Combination

The setup parameters of the capper must be established and documented separately for each container-closure combination. At product format changeover, a formal changeover verification must be performed to confirm that:

  • The sealing disc/rail does not contact vials within the normal range of variation.
  • Stopper compression meets the CCI requirement.
  • Stack-up tolerances have been taken into account in the parameter settings.

Spring/Damper Compensation Mechanism

The offsetting springs or dampers above and below the capper compensate for batch-to-batch differences in vial height, ensuring the compression force on each vial stays within a reasonable range. This is the engineering control for the natural height variation of glass containers.

5.9 Terminal Sterilization

5.9 Terminal Sterilization

Terminal sterilization is performed once the vial is stoppered and capped to assure the sterility of the product. Moist heat, which is applied in the sterilizer (oven) by spraying hot water on the product, is the method used for parenterals in vials. The process uses pressure, temperature, and time parameters. The loading pattern of the units in the chamber should be uniform to ensure proper heat distribution.

Vials that are in contact with one another in the sterilizer may bond with the surface of another vial, causing them to stick together. When these vials come apart, the stuck marks may act as stress concentrators. Breakage of vials during terminal sterilization is most likely due to an upstream incident. The practical strength of the glass may have been reduced, thus reducing the burst pressure of the vials.

5.9 Terminal Sterilization

Terminal Sterilization vs. Aseptic Manufacturing

Terminal sterilization is a method in which the entire sealed container is sterilized after filling, stoppering and capping are complete. It is suitable for products that can withstand high temperature and pressure. In contrast, in aseptic fill-finish, a sterilized product is filled into sterilized containers in a sterilized environment, and the product itself is not heat-sterilized afterwards.

Glass Bonding and Stress Concentration

In a high-temperature, moist-heat environment, the surfaces of vials in contact may bond through local melting or ion exchange. The tear marks produced on separation form new stress concentrators, reducing the practical strength of the glass and lowering burst pressure.

Control direction: ensure that the loading pattern keeps appropriate spacing between vials and avoids direct contact.

5.10 External Washing

5.10 External Washing

Transitions that convey the glass containers into and out of the external washing unit operation (e.g., star wheels, screw feeds) must be aligned and synchronized. Also, a slight downward slope or short vertical step downward should be employed when transitioning to or from conveyors and dead plates.

When grippers are used to convey the glass container through the external washer, proper alignment of the grippers and the pressure applied must be set to avoid introduction of glass defects or glass breakage. Feedback mechanisms or a sensor to detect proper functionality of the grippers should be used to prevent such glass breakage. The water and air pressure and their direction should be set appropriately to avoid glass-handling issues.

5.10 External Washing

Design Principles for Transitions

The transition between the conveyor and the dead plate should use:

  • A slight downward slope or a short vertical step downward: use gravity to help vials transition smoothly and avoid jamming or impact.
  • Precise alignment and synchronization of star wheels and screw feeds, to prevent impact on vials at the inlet and outlet.

Gripper Pressure Control

The grippers of the external washer apply clamping force to the vials to maintain their conveying path. Excessive clamping force directly causes indentations on the glass surface, which become a source of glass defects in subsequent processes. Sensors or a feedback mechanism should be installed to detect gripper malfunction in real time and interlock a stop. The direction and magnitude of water pressure and air pressure must likewise be adjusted for each vial type.

5.11 Inspection

5.11 Inspection

Filled containers should be inspected individually for contamination or other defects. Inspections can be carried out using three methods: manual, semi-automated, or automated. The sorting of accepted or rejected vials should follow the recommendations outlined in this section.

5.11 Overview of Inspection

5.11.1 Manual Inspection

Manual inspection consists of a human inspector handling the vials directly. Typically, an inspector manipulates a vial under a calibrated light source and in front of both white and black backgrounds to enable proper inspection. During the inspection process (as with all manual processes), proper and careful handling should be employed to avoid damaging the vials. The accept-or-reject decision is based on the inspector's knowledge and training.

5.11.1 Manual Visual Inspection

Glass Risks of Manual Inspection

Manual handling of vials by hand is the step in the whole process with the most intensive human contact with glass. Every "pick up, rotate, put down" is a potential opportunity for impact or scratching.

Control points: a calibrated light source to ensure defect visibility; a white/black dual background to provide contrast for different defect types; personnel training and periodic vision tests.

5.11.2 Semi-Automated Inspection

Semi-automated inspection also uses a human operator to make accept-or-reject decisions; however, the vial handling is done with automation. Typical semi-automated inspection machines include a roller conveyor for vial transportation and spinning of the vial, while also providing proper lighting and mirrors that allow the inspector a full view of the container. Handling the vials within a semi-automated inspection machine should limit glass-to-glass contact and minimize impact during transitions.

5.11.2 Semi-Automated Visual Inspection

The Function of Roller Conveyor Rotation

A semi-automated inspection machine uses a roller conveyor to spin the vials, so that foreign matter in the liquid keeps rotating by inertia and, after the vial stops, shows relative motion, making it easy for the human eye to identify. A mirror system lets the inspector view the vial body through 360° at once, increasing coverage without adding personnel.

Glass handling principle: transition points should minimize glass-to-glass contact and impact.

5.11.3 Automated Inspection

Automated inspection can be performed using both sensor-based and camera-based systems and, depending on the process, can incorporate different handling designs. Appropriate, container-specific change parts and set-up are necessary to ensure proper handling, inspection, and prevention of additional damage. For example, the base attachments and spindle caps on machines using inspection turrets are designed in such a way that the vial is held in place during the spin process to prevent the vial from being thrown from the turret ("spin-out"). Vials are sorted depending on the results of the inspection process. For ejected vials, limit glass-to-glass contact and minimize impacts during transitions as the ejected vials may be reinspected manually or reintroduced into the automated inspection process.

5.11.3 Fully Automated Visual Inspection

Spin-Out Risk

When the inspection turret spins vials at high speed, if the base attachment or the spindle cap is not suited to that vial type, the vial may be "thrown out" of the turret by centrifugal force (spin-out), causing serious glass damage and endangering the equipment.

Prevention: select the correct change parts for each vial type, and verify the fixation before starting the line.

Risk of Secondary Damage to Ejected Vials

Vials automatically ejected may already have defects and reduced glass strength. In the eject lane or during re-inspection, any additional glass-to-glass impact may worsen the defect and affect the accuracy of subsequent manual re-inspection. Controlled eject lanes should be used instead of eject bins.

5.11.4 Inspected Vial Ejects

Vials that fail an initial inspection may be reintroduced into the line. Acceptance criteria should be defined, and a risk assessment should be conducted to determine the level of reinspection required.

Controlled eject lanes are preferred over eject bins to avoid damaging the vials. If eject bins are used, their design and process should be determined as outlined in Section 5.2.

5.11.4 Management of Re-inspection of Ejected Vials

Key Points in Designing the Re-inspection Process

  • Establish clear acceptance criteria for re-inspection: which defects can be re-inspected? How many times?
  • Perform a documented risk assessment to determine the inspection level required for re-inspection.
  • Use controlled eject lanes first: structured and cushioned, avoiding the impact of falling into a bin.
  • If eject bins must be used, design them in accordance with Section 5.2 (including cushioning material, maximum stack height, etc.).

5.11.5 Points to Consider

Two upgrades are recommended to improve the handling of glass vials during the inspection process: puck-handling and direct-spin system. With puck-handling, after the vial is fed through an infeed screw and star wheel, it is placed on a specialized puck. This puck is then transported throughout the machine carrying the vial. The advantage of this system is the reduced interaction between machine and glass. The machine passes the puck from the star wheel to an inspection turret and back to the star wheel without any additional forces exerted on the glass vial.

The direct-spin system provides an individual servo motor for each location on the pre-spin or inspection turret. That allows increased control of the spin speeds of the vial, optimizing the machine for a larger range in containers and reducing the risk of vial "spin-out".

5.11.5 Directions for Upgrading the Inspection Process

Puck-Handling System

After entering from the infeed screw and star wheel, vials are placed on dedicated "pucks", and from then on the whole inspection flow conveys pucks as the unit; the machine directly handles the puck rather than the glass vial.

Advantage: greatly reduces the direct contact force between machine and glass; the vial itself is no longer pushed, pulled or clamped, but simply sits on the puck and is carried along.

Direct-Spin System

Each rotation position is equipped with an individual servo motor that precisely controls the rotation speed of each vial. Regardless of differences in vial size or weight, the speed can be optimized, eliminating the spin-out risk under high-speed rotation and also improving the inspection machine's adaptability to multiple formats.

5.12 Off-Loading

5.12 Off-Loading

Off-loading is the collection of finished product from the line. When transferring the vials into interim containers prior to labeling, materials, and practices similar to those outlined in Table 3.0-1🔍 should be employed.

5.12 Off-Loading Operations

Off-Loading Is a High-Risk Step at the End of the Process

Off-loading is the operation of collecting finished product from the production line into interim containers, usually done manually. Although this step is simple, it easily causes glass impact or scratching through carelessness.

5.13 Vial Labeling

5.13 Vial Labeling

Typically, labeling does not occur in-line following the final container inspection. Vials are often packaged for transport (within a facility or to a remote location) and care should be taken to prevent damage from glass-to-glass contact, vibration, and other shocks. Trays are unloaded manually onto the labeler in-feed; then, a combination of star wheels and/or scroll feeds orient and singulate the vials for labeling. As with other processes, vials downed during labeling should be handled as recommended in Section 3.0. Once the label is applied, the vials are transferred to the label inspection unit.

5.13 Labeling Operations

Conveying Mechanism for Labeling

Labelers usually use star wheels and/or scroll feeds to orient and singulate the vials, ensuring accurate label position on each vial.

In-Plant Transport Risks Before Labeling

Labeling is usually not in-line with the filling line; vials must first be packed and then transported to the labeling area. Glass-to-glass collisions, vibration and impact during in-plant or inter-site transport are the main sources of damage at this stage. Ensure that interim packaging provides adequate protection, and refer to Section 3.0 for the handling of downed vials.

5.14 Secondary Packaging

5.14 Secondary Packaging

Secondary packaging is the final step in the pharmaceutical process, one that provides the glass vial with extra protection from rough handling during distribution and storage. Folding cartons, made of cardboard or coated paperboard, are often used. The carton should be properly sized, designed in a way that prevents glass breakage or damage, and made of materials with a lower friction factor and shock absorption. For multi-vial packs, the inner compartment of the carton should be designed so that the packaging prevents glass-to-glass contact.

5.14 Secondary Packaging

Functional Role of Secondary Packaging

Secondary packaging is the last protective barrier in the whole pharmaceutical process, and the quality of its design directly affects the integrity of glass vials during distribution and storage.

Key design requirements for folding cartons:

  • Dimensional fit: the space inside the carton matches the vial dimensions, avoiding being too loose or too tight.
  • Low coefficient of friction material: reduces surface friction when vials slide inside the carton.
  • Shock absorption: cushions impact energy during transport.
  • Multi-vial packs: internal partition design ensures no direct contact between vials.

5.14.1 Points to Consider

Newer carton designs are available with double-walled construction and cushioning that protect against severe impact and provide crush resistance. Cartons with an integrated partition should be suitable for both manual and automated packaging lines. Pre-glued edges can support the cover, preventing pressure on the vials during stacking. Additionally, labels that add an extra layer of protection are available for certain applications (e.g., cushion or double-thickness labels).

5.14.1 Upgrades in Secondary Packaging Design

Features of Modern Carton Designs

  • Double-walled construction: provides greater compression strength and cushioning, resisting severe impact during distribution.
  • Integrated partition: compatible with manual and automated packaging lines, giving high production flexibility.
  • Pre-glued edges: no pressure is applied to the vials when the carton is closed, preventing force from being transmitted to the glass vials during stacking.
  • Cushioning label / double-layer label: the label itself acts as an additional cushioning layer, suitable for specific high-risk applications.

5.15 Finished Product Storage and Handling

5.15 Finished Product Storage and Handling

The remaining aspect of the pack-out configuration, how the vials will be stored and handled during distribution, presents a number of factors that should be considered, such as:

  • Set-back of the load from the edge of the pallet
  • Carton-stacking configuration
  • Need for corner protection
  • Need for slip sheets between layers
  • Method of securing the load to the pallet
  • Total height of the pallet

Double-stacking of pallets is generally not recommended and should be avoided, especially during transport.

5.15 Finished Product Storage and Handling

Double-Stacking of Pallets Is Prohibited

Double-stacking of pallets is generally not recommended and is especially prohibited during transport. The pallet's own weight adds to the product weight; if the load on the lower pallet exceeds its design limit, the cartons at the bottom will be crushed, directly causing vial breakage. For refrigerated/frozen products, the pallet design needs special evaluation of the loss of carton strength at low temperature.

Analogy: Floor Load Design of a Building

Pallet stacking is like the floor design of a building: each level must bear all the weight above it. The pressure on vials at the bottom = the weight of all upper-layer product + the weight of the pallet material. This pressure must stay within the strength design range of the carton and the glass; otherwise the laws of physics will screen out the unqualified packaging designs for you.

8.0 Introduction to Glass Types

8.0 Appendix I: Fundamentals of Glass Science

To know how to properly handle glass and the various rationales behind it, the fundamental chemical and physical properties of glass need to be understood. The standard types of glass used for medical purposes are soda-lime silicate and borosilicate. Borosilicate glass is a Type I glass recommended for use in parenteral packaging. Soda-lime silicate glass is a Type III glass recommended for packaging nonparenteral drugs.

Typical compositional ranges for pharmaceutical glasses are outlined in various national standards, such as ISO 12775 and ASTM E438. Both these standards describe two types of borosilicate glass based on their coefficients of mean linear thermal expansion.

Key Concepts Explained

Key Differences Between Type I and Type III

Borosilicate glass is Type I glass and the standard choice for parenteral packaging. It is highly chemically inert, has a low coefficient of thermal expansion, is unlikely to react with the drug solution, and tolerates both high and low temperature shocks.

Soda-lime silicate glass is Type III. It has lower chemical stability and is suitable only for non-parenteral packaging, such as containers for oral liquids.

For a CDMO, Type I must never be downgraded. Switching to Type III under pressure not only violates USP/EP requirements but also directly endangers the safety of the injectable product.

Real-Life Analogy

Type I borosilicate glass is like a heat-resistant laboratory beaker (Pyrex), while Type III soda-lime silicate glass is like an ordinary drinking-glass bottle. Both are glass, but the difference is very large when facing high temperature, chemical contact or rapid cooling.

8.1 Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE)

8.1 Coefficient of Thermal Expansion

Most materials expand and contract upon heating and cooling, respectively. The extent of expansion is dependent on the specific glass chemical composition. This expansion can be measured using techniques such as push-rod dilatometry and is expressed as the change in length per temperature unit, otherwise known as the coefficient of linear thermal expansion (CTE). The CTE for a glass is generally reported as mean value over a specific temperature range. The higher the CTE, the more the material will expand when heated and, conversely, the more it will shrink when cooled. ASTM E228 and ISO 7991 provide guidance on measuring the CTE of glass.

The CTE is also a main factor in determining the resistance of glass in a flawless, nonstrengthened state to sudden temperature changes. Increasing resistance to thermal shock is correlated with decreasing CTE.

Soda-lime silicate glass with a wall thickness of 0.5 mm can withstand a maximum rapid temperature difference of approximately 110 °C without cracking. Standard borosilicate glass of the same thickness can endure greater temperature differences. Borosilicate glasses having a CTE of 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ and 3.3 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ with a wall thickness of 0.5 mm can withstand approximately 220 °C and 340 °C rapid temperature differences, respectively, and are generally more resistant to thermal shock than soda-lime silicate glass.

Due to the fact that glass has poor thermal conductivity, a thinner wall thickness is recommended for thermal resistance while a thicker wall will withstand greater mechanical loads. These calculations are theoretical, however, and refer to glass in a flawless state. In practice, glass is never in a flawless state; therefore, the presence of surface flaws may be more significant than the CTE alone in determining thermal shock resistance.

Direct Comparison of CTE and Thermal Shock Resistance

Taking a 0.5 mm wall thickness as the example (theoretical values, flawless condition):

  • Soda-lime silicate (Type III): withstands a temperature difference of ~110°C

This is the physical basis for choosing Type I for injectables: the temperature differences in processes such as lyophilization and depyrogenation (350°C) far exceed the safe range of Type III.

The Dual Effect of Wall Thickness (Important!)

Poor thermal conductivity of glass is the key to understanding the effect of wall thickness:

  • Thin wall: small temperature difference between inside and outside, so low thermal shock risk, but weaker mechanical strength
  • Thick wall: large temperature difference between inside and outside, so high thermal shock risk, but able to bear greater mechanical loads

Design and selection must balance "thermal resistance" against "mechanical strength", which is a core consideration in vial specification design.

Theory vs. Reality

The report states clearly that the above data apply only to "flawless" glass. In reality, glass surfaces inevitably carry microscopic flaws (micro-flaws). The effect of surface flaws is therefore often a better determinant of the actual risk of thermal shock breakage than CTE.

In other words, a badly scratched Borosilicate 3.3 vial may be more fragile than an intact Borosilicate 5.0 vial. This understanding runs through all of the handling recommendations in TR87.

8.2 Stress Inside the Glass

8.2.1 Melting and Solidification Behavior of Glass

The transition of a solid crystal to molten liquid occurs instantaneously at fixed melting temperatures, Tm. In contrast, solid glass does not possess a melting temperature. Instead, the viscosity of glass changes continuously as a function of temperature when going from the solid state to the liquid state. This behavior can be illustrated using the Volume-Temperature (V-T) diagram.

In order to form a typical glass, a molten liquid must be cooled below Tm without crystallizing, thereby becoming a supercooled liquid. The point at which the slope of the V-T curve deviates from the supercooled liquid line marks one boundary of the "glass transformation range." The intersection point of lines extrapolated from the solid and liquid sections of the glass V-T curve is one definition of the glass transition temperature, Tg.

The Tg of a glass is dependent on chemical composition and is of practical importance in glassmaking. The Tg lies between the annealing point and the strain point. The annealing point is approximately located at the upper temperature boundary of the glass transformation range and can be broadly defined as the temperature at which a majority of residual stress within a glass is dissipated within several minutes. The strain point is approximately located at the lower temperature boundary of the glass transformation and can be broadly defined as the temperature at which a majority of residual stress within a glass is dissipated within several hours. In addition, the strain point marks a limit for structural rearrangement of the glass. Knowledge of these temperature points are used to design annealing schedules that remove a majority of forming-induced stress within a glass article.

How Glass Solidifies

Glass Has No Fixed Melting Point

A crystalline solid (such as ice or metal) changes between solid and liquid instantly at a fixed melting point (Tm); glass is different. Its viscosity changes continuously with temperature, and it is a frozen state of a "supercooled liquid".

This property makes glass forming and annealing a "rate-controlled" process rather than a "temperature-switch" process.

Three Key Temperature Points (from High to Low)

  • Annealing point: residual stress dissipates within "minutes". This is the upper temperature limit of the annealing process, about 550–575°C for borosilicate glass.
  • Glass transition temperature (Tg): the midpoint of the transition range in which the structure changes from liquid-like to solid-like behavior, lying between the annealing point and the strain point.
  • Strain point: residual stress dissipates within "hours". This is the lower temperature limit of the annealing process; below this point the structure no longer rearranges.

Analogy: Annealing Is Like "Relaxing Slowly"

Imagine a rubber band twisted too tightly: if you let go quickly, it springs apart (cracks); if you release it slowly and under control, it returns to its natural state without breaking. Annealing is exactly what gives the glass atoms enough time to rearrange and release the stress accumulated during forming.

8.2.2 Physical Definition of Stress

Physical stress (σ) can be defined as the force (F) per unit area of a material element (A). There are two important types of stress in the pharmaceutical packaging handling process: tensile stress and compressive stress. If the atomic positions are changed by an external force (tensile or compressive force), the atoms will attempt to return to their original, optimal distance by counteracting the external force.

Physical Definition of Stress

Two Core Types of Stress

  • Tensile stress: a force that pulls the material apart and increases the distance between atoms. Glass "always breaks at points of tensile stress".
  • Compressive stress: a force that compresses the material and decreases the distance between atoms. Glass tolerates compressive stress much better; this is also the core principle of chemical strengthening.

Important concept: where compressive force is applied on one side, tensile stress is often generated at the same time elsewhere in the container, and it is these tensile stress zones that are the real danger points.

8.2.2.1 Thermal Stress During Container Manufacturing

During the formation of a container, the raw material is melted, and a tube is drawn and cut into lengths. The glass has a low, even stress distribution at this point in the process. Heat must be applied again to the glass tube when forming a container. Forming the container employs temperatures of 1000 °C or greater, which causes stress at the bottom and at the opening of the container while the rest of the tubing remains cool.

While heating up one part of a glass tube, the atoms being heated increase their oscillation. As the heated part is cooled down quickly after the forming process, the atoms do not have sufficient time to rearrange to their former structure. Rather, they are stuck in the position they occupied at that time, and both tensile and compressive stress is created.

In order to restore the glass to a low-stress state, the entire vial is annealed by heating to about 20 °C above Tg for some period of time and then slowly cooled in a strictly controlled process in an annealing lehr (oven). After the annealing process is complete, a majority of forming-induced stress in the glass should be reduced to an acceptable level. In this manner, introducing thermal stress into the glass and releasing it again does not lead to an accumulation of stress and weakening of the glass. However, if this stress is not released properly, the risk of the glass breaking at the zone of tensile stress is greater.

The amount of residual stress can be determined by polariscopic measurement devices.

Except for the sealing of ampoules, residual thermal stress cannot be introduced in the filling process as the temperature never exceeds the Tg of the glass.

Like the tubular process, stress is introduced into molded vials or bottles during the forming process. A gob of molten glass is dropped into a mold and formed via a press-and-blow process or blow-and-blow process. The vials must undergo an annealing process with controlled cooling to relieve the stress.

If the temperature difference at a certain spot on the glass exceeds the particular thermal shock parameter of that glass, however, micro-cracks can result. Thermal shock occurs when a thermal gradient causes different parts of an object to expand by different amounts. This phenomenon can occur where cold metal meets hot glass. For example, residual water from the washing process can cool the bottom of the vial in the depyrogenation oven while the glass is heated from the outside.

During depyrogenation, the containers are typically heated to about 350 °C in order to reduce the risk of possible pyrogen contamination. As this temperature range is below the typical Tg temperatures of glasses used in pharmaceutical packaging, this process poses no risk in terms of thermal stresses. It does, however, facilitate the introduction of scratches that decrease the mechanical strength.

Thermal Stress in Manufacturing and the Annealing Mechanism

Sources of Forming Stress in Tubular Vials

When glass tubing is formed into vials, the bottom and the opening are heated (>1000°C) while the middle of the tube body stays cooler. Localized heating leads to more intense atomic vibration, then rapid cooling, so the atoms have no time to rearrange and are "frozen" in non-ideal positions, producing tensile and compressive stress.

Function of the annealing lehr: the whole vial is heated to Tg + 20°C to give the atoms time to rearrange, and is then cooled slowly at a precisely controlled rate so that the stress dissipates evenly.

Consequences of Incomplete Annealing

If annealing is poorly controlled (temperature too low, cooling too fast, uneven temperature in the lehr):

  • Residual stress remains at the vial base or at the corners of the neck
  • When an external impact occurs during later handling (filling, capping, conveying), the vial is more likely to break in the tensile stress zones
  • The distribution of residual stress can be measured with a polariscope

This also explains why vendor qualification should include residual stress testing, rather than relying only on dimensional specifications.

The Dual Impact of Depyrogenation

Depyrogenation heats the glass to ~350°C, which is below the glass Tg (~550°C), so it does not introduce new thermal stress. However, this process carries another type of risk:

  • Residual moisture after washing may cool the vial base locally, creating a temperature difference with the hot external air, which leads to thermal shock microcracks
  • At high temperature the protective adsorbed water layer on the glass surface evaporates, exposing the surface, so contact with metal guide rails easily causes scratches and a loss of mechanical strength

See the discussion of the adsorption layer in Section 8.4.

8.2.2.1.1 Exposure to Cold Temperatures

Glass will be exposed to temperatures of approximately -40 °C to -80 °C during lyophilization and of -150 °C to -196 °C for cold storage, including cryopreservation. The lower the CTE, the more stable the glass is in terms of thermal shock resistance, but the rate of change must be extremely high for thermal shock breakage.

Use of glass with lower CTEs may reduce the breakage risk due to rapid rate of change; however, surface defects still play the major role in glass breakage. Breakage during exposure to cold temperatures are primarily due to flaws present in the glass and the outward pressure of the internal materials, not thermal stress. The fewer defects there are on the bottom area and area of drug contact, the less likely the glass will break. Vial breakage during the lyophilization process is usually due to the mechanical stress from the expanding solution rather than thermal stress.

Breakage Mechanisms in Low-Temperature Environments

The Real Cause of Breakage in Lyophilization

During lyophilization, vials are exposed to -40 to -80°C, and in long-term storage (cryopreservation) down to -196°C (liquid nitrogen). However, the main cause of breakage is not thermal shock but:

  • Mechanical stress from expansion of the contents: the solution expands in volume when it freezes and exerts outward pressure
  • Pre-existing surface flaws: microcracks in the area of the vial base in contact with the drug solution grow under stress

Conclusion: lowering the CTE (choosing a better glass) helps, but reducing surface flaws is the fundamental strategy for lowering the lyophilization breakage rate.

Practical Implications for a CDMO

Key points for managing lyophilization breakage risk:

  • Incoming inspection includes visual or automated inspection of the vial base for appearance and defects
  • Select vendors with lower defect rates (surface quality requirements should be included in the specification)
  • Minimize vial collisions and scratching during conveying from filling to the lyophilizer
  • When developing the lyophilization formulation, consider the freezing expansion ratio and adjust the fill volume accordingly

8.3 Mechanical Strength of Glass

8.3.1 Ductile versus Brittle Materials

Along with thermal stress, physical load can introduce a great amount of stress onto glass. Breakage will occur if the physical load is greater than the practical strength of the container.

Materials are generally differentiated into two failure mode categories: ductile and brittle. Ductile materials, like metals (and some polymer materials), deform under tensile stress; that is, they undergo a plastic deformation (changing their shape) rather than breaking immediately. During plastic deformation, the molecules in the material are able to move. This provides the material's ability to bend before breaking.

Brittle materials break before the molecules in the material can move sufficiently to support bending. The molecules are literally torn apart in place. Brittle materials, such as glasses, are unable to undergo plastic deformation and therefore, break abruptly.

Ductile vs. Brittle Materials

Glass Is a Brittle Material

Ductile materials such as metals undergo plastic deformation before fracture: they can bend and stretch, giving the operator a "warning sign".

Glass is a brittle material: the atoms have no time to move before the material is torn apart at the tip of a flaw. This means:

  • Breakage is sudden and gives no warning
  • Any flaw is a potential origin of breakage
  • The emphasis is on prevention rather than detection

Analogy: Pencil vs. Rubber Band

A rubber band (ductile) thins out partway through stretching, warning you it is about to break. A pencil (brittle) snaps with a sudden "crack" when bent hard, with no warning. Glass is more like the pencil, and after breakage the fragments may contaminate the sterile drug solution.

8.3.2 Practical Strength

For brittle materials like glass, practical strength depends on such parameters as geometric aspects of the glass piece, flaws inside and on the surface of the glass, residual stress inside the glass, and the interior and exterior mechanical loads on the glass. An equation to determine practical strength for brittle materials, developed by A. A. Griffith:

Griffith Fracture Criterion 
K = σ × Y × √c

Where:
  K  = Stress Intensity Factor
       (amplification of tensile stress at flaw tip)
  σ  = Applied stress
       (from external force or internal pressure)
  Y  = Geometric Factor
       (location and shape of the defect)
  √c = Critical Dimension
       (square root of defect depth/size)

Breakage occurs when K exceeds the fracture toughness (KIC) of the glass.

The stress (σ) applied to the glass can result from an impact with a material harder or equally as hard as glass (e.g., metal). Stress can also come from inside the container, such as when internal pressure is built up during lyophilization, from vertical pressure (e.g., during crimping), or from side pressure (e.g., a jammed screw feed).

The geometric factor (Y) takes into consideration the flaw geometries, including the relative location of the flaw (through thickness crack, edge/surface flaw, corner flaw, surface flaw shapes).

The critical dimension (c) describes the geometric aspects of the defect — the larger the radius of the flaw tip, the more the glass is weakened. A very deep flaw will lower the glass strength much more than a surface scratch.

Ultimately, breakage only occurs if both the stress and the flaw or stress riser are present in tandem, and the combined phenomena exceed the practical strength of the particular piece of glass.

Practical Significance of the Griffith Equation

Interpreting the Equation: Three Variables You Can Act On

  • σ (stress): can be reduced through process design, for example by optimizing conveyor line speed, avoiding vial back-up, controlling capping force, and using flexible guide rails.
  • Y (geometry factor): the position of the flaw affects the degree of stress concentration. A corner flaw at the vial base is more dangerous than a surface flaw on the side wall, because the geometric amplification effect at corners is stronger.
  • c (flaw depth): a deep flaw is far more dangerous than a shallow scratch. This is why chipping affects strength more than a fine-line scratch does.

Core Insight: Stress and a Flaw Must Be Present Together

This is one of the most important conclusions of TR87 Section 8:

「Breakage only occurs if both the stress and the flaw are present in tandem.」

Practical implications:

  • Glass without flaws will not easily break even under considerable stress
  • Glass with flaws but zero stress will not break either
  • When both are present, the risk rises sharply

Risk control strategy therefore needs to address both sides: reduce flaws (surface quality management) + reduce stress (process and equipment design).

Common Stress Sources and the Corresponding TR87 Handling Recommendations

  • Vertical force (capping): produces tensile stress on the outer surface of the neck and at the heel, so the capper torque/force must be controlled
  • Lateral squeezing (screw feeders, conveyor back-up): produces tensile stress on the outer surface opposite the point of force, so vials must be prevented from jamming on the conveyor line
  • Internal pressure (lyophilization): stress applied outward from inside the container; freezing expansion is the main source

8.3.3 Tensile and Compressive Stress

Glass always breaks at the point of tensile stress location. When compressive stress is exerted from one side, it always induces tensile stress in certain other places, but these areas of tensile stress are not always clearly detectable.

(a) A downward force (e.g., capper) creates tensile stress in the outer surface of the neck and heel and compressive stress in the side wall.

(b) A side or squeeze-force (e.g., a backed-up conveyor or screw feed) creates tensile stress on the interior surface at the exterior load location and exterior surface 90 degrees from load.

Actual Patterns of Stress Distribution

Glass Always Breaks at Points of Tensile Stress

This principle is the basis for operational risk assessment. The direction of the applied force is not the direction of breakage:

  • Capper (downward force): compressive force acts on the side wall, but tensile stress arises on the outer surface of the neck and at the heel, so breakage is common at the neck or the base corner
  • Conveyor squeezing (lateral force): tensile stress arises on the inner surface of the container at the point of force and on the outer surface 90° perpendicular to the direction of force, so breakage can occur far from the contact point

This explains why the location of breakage on a production line sometimes looks "illogical" relative to the direction of the applied force.

8.3.4 Surface Condition and its Impact on Practical Strength

During the lifecycle of a glass container, it passes through a number of handling steps. Any of these steps can create varying degrees of surface discontinuities, like scratches or even microfissures in the glass, that may decrease the functional strength of that particular glass container. Only in combination with a specific stress on the glass, however, does breakage occur.

Break force, strength, or failure probability measurements can be used to determine the extent that surface discontinuities and physical impact result in decreased practical strength. In certain cases, the effects of different handling processes can be determined by these measurements.

The Dynamic Relationship Between Surface Condition and Strength

Cumulative Damage over the Container Life Cycle

Every step that touches the vial may accumulate damage: manufacturing → packaging → warehousing → incoming feed → washing → depyrogenation → filling → capping → outfeed → visual inspection.

A single minor injury may not be enough to cause breakage, but cumulative damage combined with process stress may exceed the strength threshold. This is why TR87 emphasizes end-to-end monitoring.

Measurement Methods

Measurement of break force, strength and failure probability can be used to:

  • Compare the relative effect of different handling steps on strength
  • Evaluate the impact of a new process or new equipment on vial integrity
  • Establish a "damage baseline" for each process step

8.4 Adsorption Layer on the Glass

8.4 Adsorption Layer on the Glass

When glass is freshly heated or cleaned, the surface reacts with adsorbates from ambient air (e.g., water, hydrocarbons) that then saturate the surface. Initially, the water is only adsorbed, and a monolayer is developed. After a few weeks, however, the water diffuses deeper into the surface. This adsorption layer acts like a cover on the glass and protects it from scratches, and the more developed layer can better protect the container from scratches. This protective layer can be removed by heating the glass (e.g., depyrogenation oven); a monolayer will evaporate more quickly under dry air/heat conditions. As the kinetics of reestablishing the protective layer are very slow; submerging the glass in water will not accelerate the reaction.

This phenomenon of building up and evaporating the protective layer is equally valid for all glasses. This also infers that scratches and sticking behavior is a matter of glass handling rather than the composition of glass itself.

The water layer not only protects the glass from scratches, but it also plays a significant role as a lubricant, decreasing the friction between glass containers or between glass and metal. This property becomes especially important during the filling process as it can limit the number of scratches that the containers may receive, potentially allowing an increase in the speed of the filling line.

Protective and Lubricating Mechanism of the Adsorbed Water Layer

Three Stages of the Adsorption Layer

  • Stage 1 (initial): after cleaning or heating, the glass surface quickly adsorbs water molecules from the air, forming a monolayer
  • Stage 2 (after several weeks): water molecules gradually diffuse deeper into the surface, forming a thicker protective layer with stronger protection
  • Stage 3 (after heating): depyrogenation or high-temperature treatment evaporates the protective layer, leaving the surface "bare" again, and the layer rebuilds very slowly

Key Insight: Scratching Is a Handling Problem, Not a Material Problem

TR87 states clearly: "Scratches and sticking behavior is a matter of glass handling rather than the composition of glass itself."

Practical implications:

  • When vials come out of depyrogenation, the surface protective layer has evaporated, so this is the most fragile moment for the glass in the whole process
  • High-speed conveying or contact with metal surfaces at this point carries the highest scratch risk
  • Soaking in water cannot speed up rebuilding of the protective layer (the reaction kinetics are too slow)
  • Two glasses of different composition tend to scratch to a similar degree if handled in the same way

Analogy: Sunscreen

The adsorbed water layer is like the natural oil layer on skin: right after a bath (cleaning = depyrogenation), the skin is at its driest and most fragile, and it takes time to secrete enough protective oil again. The adsorption layer mechanism of glass is very similar.

Scientific Basis for Optimizing Filling Line Speed

The adsorbed water layer is also a lubricant, lowering the coefficient of friction (COF) between vials and between vials and metal guide rails.

Where the adsorption layer is intact, filling line speed can be raised moderately without significantly increasing the scratch rate. However, for vials that have just entered the filling line after depyrogenation, conveying speed should be controlled with particular care, or external lubrication measures should be used (such as the chemical strengthening coating in Section 8.5).

8.5 Chemical Strengthening

8.5 Chemical Strengthening

Chemical strengthening by ion exchange creates a residual compression layer on the inner and outer glass vial surfaces that resists the applied tensile stresses required to propagate flaws. It can also hinder minor to moderate surface damage, blocking the routes along which cracks could otherwise propagate and, thereby, prevent breakage. Chemical strengthening can be performed on aluminosilicate, borosilicate, or soda-lime silicate glass containers with sufficient available sodium ions to increase their mechanical strength. Because the glass tubing is heated in the container formation process, any chemical process must be performed on the final container, not the glass tubing.

To avoid thermal shock, the empty glass containers are gradually heated to the bath temperature before immersion in a potassium melt (e.g., KNO₃) where an ion exchange reaction takes place. In this reaction, sodium ions (Na⁺) from the near-surface regions are exchanged for potassium ions (K⁺). Potassium ions are larger in diameter than sodium ions (K⁺'s radius is roughly 0.38 Å larger than that of Na⁺), leading to compressive forces that let the glass withstand higher external loads.

At the end of the designated immersion time, the containers are removed from the bath, allowed to cool, undergo thorough salt-removal process, and are then dried.

This surface compressive stress creates an additional barrier to prevent the formation and propagation of checks as well as suppressing the creation of visible glass particles. Chemical strengthening also improves the overall robustness of the vial, reducing the probability of breakage from high-stress loading and particulate generation from fragmentation.

Applying an additional protective coating to the outer surface of the glass container will lower its COF and minimize surface damage during frictive sliding contact.

The Ion-Exchange Strengthening Mechanism

Core Principle of Chemical Strengthening

The ion exchange process replaces small ions in the glass surface layer with larger ions, forcing the surface into compressive stress:

  • Na⁺ (smaller) → K⁺ (larger, radius difference about 0.38 Å)
  • Once embedded, K⁺ "wedges apart" the surrounding space, so the surface remains under continuous compression
  • For a crack to propagate, the external tensile stress must first overcome this compressive pre-load, which greatly raises the breakage threshold

The principle is similar to tempered glass, but the chemical method can treat complex shapes (such as vials) without the need for rapid air cooling.

Multiple Benefits of Chemical Strengthening

  • Prevents crack propagation: the surface compressive layer physically closes off the crack path
  • Suppresses particulate generation: reduces the risk of fragmentation, with direct significance for injectable safety
  • Raises overall mechanical strength: lowers the probability of breakage under high stress loads (such as capping)
  • Applicable to all glass types: provided they contain enough Na⁺ (aluminosilicate, borosilicate and soda-lime silicate glass can all be used)

Process Limitation: Must Be Done on the Final Container

Because glass tubing is heated during forming, the compressive layer produced by chemical strengthening would be destroyed. Chemical strengthening must therefore be carried out after container forming is complete and cannot be applied to the glass tubing beforehand.

This also means that chemical strengthening is a downstream operation that must be integrated into the container supply chain, and is not a raw material specification issue.

Analogy: Pre-Stressed Concrete

Chemical strengthening is like pre-stressed concrete: a pre-load is first applied to the steel reinforcement so that the concrete must overcome this pre-load before it is subjected to external tension. The compressive layer from chemical strengthening is the "pre-stressed reinforcement" of the glass surface.

Added Benefit of a Protective Coating

On top of chemical strengthening, applying a protective coating (such as SiO₂ or an organic thin film) to the outer surface can further lower the coefficient of friction (COF) and reduce sliding-contact damage on the filling line. The two measures complement each other and can be combined for high-risk or high-value products.

Appendix II — Section 9.0: Investigations

Glass Breakage Investigation: Initial Assessment, Root Cause Analysis, Corrective and Preventive Actions

9.0 Appendix II: Investigations

Production personnel must be aware when a failure occurs. Any failure should be immediately investigated; this may include conducting a risk assessment to determine the cause and the corrective action steps to be taken. These steps should be detailed in a standard operating procedure (SOP) and the corrective actions taken should be documented. Production personnel should be well trained in proper glass handling, conducting initial-failure assessments, sample collection, and sample preparation for fracture analysis.

Certain risks must be well understood when glass damage or breakage is observed. Risks go beyond the immediate failure of a broken or damaged container, potentially including subsequent failure of additional containers and possible glass particulate contamination in adjacent containers on the line. Steps should be taken to mitigate any risk by isolating and evaluating adjacent vials before batch release. If glass fragments are observed on or near the line, it is crucial that all the other remnants be found and collected. The objective is to ensure that no vials—chipped, damaged, or containing glass particles—continue to be processed that pose a potential risk to subsequent steps or harm to the patient.

In order for breakage to occur there must be a:

  • Force applied to the item to generate stress in the glass
  • Flaw to act as the origin of the fracture

The general aim of fracture analysis is to identify the:

  • Type and magnitude of force that was applied to the container
  • Type and severity of the defect at the fracture origin
  • Cause of the breakage, whether due to:
    • Defective container that failed at normally expected levels of applied force
    • Excessive force applied to a commercial-quality container

Commentary: Why Glass Breakage Investigation Is More Complex Than You Think

The Risk of Breakage Is Not Just "That One Vial"

The instinctive reaction of many operators is to remove the broken vial and carry on producing. But TR87 states clearly that the real risk lies in the surrounding vials, which may already be contaminated with glass fragments while looking perfectly intact.

On an aseptic filling line, once glass fragments enter an open container they can cause glass particulate injury to patients. Adjacent vials must therefore be isolated and assessed before batch release, which is a basic regulatory compliance requirement.

Two Necessary Conditions for Breakage

The TR87 framework is very clear: glass breakage = force (stress) + flaw. This formula directly determines the direction of the investigation:

  • Flaw only, without sufficient force → the vial may survive normal handling, and the problem lies in supplier quality
  • Force only, without a flaw → the equipment is applying too much force, and the problem lies in line operation or mechanical settings
  • Both present together → the supplier and the equipment both need to be traced, the most complex scenario

Analogy: Just Like Forensic Science

Fractography is the glass version of "forensic science": the fragments are the evidence, and the markings on the fracture surface record the physical process at the moment of breakage. A qualified fractographer can reconstruct the scene from the fragments and tell you:

  • Where the first break occurred (origin)
  • In which direction the crack propagated (crack propagation)
  • Roughly how much force was applied

This is why fragment collection must be done carefully: fragments from different vials must not be mixed, and secondary damage must not be created on the fragments, otherwise the chain of evidence is compromised.

The core goal of SOP training is not only "how to do the task" but also "why it matters". Only operators who understand the risk of breakage can make the right first-response judgment.

9.1 Initial Investigation

Initial evaluations by production personnel can often help identify the root cause(s) of the failure. Observations can include the breakage pattern, line location, and relative size and number of fragments; these comments can provide important clues as to the cause of the breakage, for example:

  • A chip observed near or around the filler, or on a belt or accumulator just beyond the filler, often indicates a filling needle strike on the mouth of the vial.
  • Numerous small fragments or a complex fracture pattern observed often indicates the breakage was due to unusual excessive forces on the container; small fragments could also occur when a primary fragment (such as a chip) is subsequently crushed by another applied load. The excessive force may be due to misaligned transfer mechanisms, a pinch point, a mispositioned container, or an out-of-spec container.
  • A simple fracture pattern or just a few large fragments observed indicates a low-energy break, possibly a manifestation of a significant flaw or damage that occurred upstream.

Sometimes, the only corrective action needed is a simple line adjustment and a determination that the correct change parts are in place. Other times, more extensive corrective actions may be needed.

Performing a detailed analysis of the breakage using fractography (fracture analysis) may be the best way to determine or confirm the exact cause of a failure. A qualified fractographer can identify the point of failure origin, providing clues to the type of surface defect and the cause, including the material at contact. Fractography can also estimate the force that caused the failure. Fractography requires that all, or as many of the fragments as possible, be collected for evaluation along with the portion of the sample containing the fracture origin. As much as possible, fragments from different individual vials should not be comingled; they should be treated carefully so as not to create secondary defects on the broken glass.

Commentary: On-Site Initial Assessment, with the Operator as the First Detective

When Is Fractography Needed?

It is not always needed. If the cause is very obvious (for example, the filling needle is clearly seen hitting the vial mouth), it is enough to confirm it, adjust the equipment and record it. But a full fractography analysis is strongly recommended in the following situations:

  • The cause is unknown, or multiple possible causes are suspected
  • Breakage recurs at the same location or in the same batch
  • A regulatory audit may request an in-depth investigation record
  • The breakage leads to batch quarantine or a release/reject decision

Adjusting online parameters hastily before the cause is clarified may mask the real problem or even introduce new risks.

Key Points for CDMO Floor Operations

Golden rules of fragment collection:

  • Bag fragments from different vials separately and label the source location
  • Use tweezers or cotton gloves and avoid bare hands, to prevent secondary damage
  • Also collect intact vials from before and after the breakage point as control samples
  • Photograph the scene: fragment distribution, direction of ejection, equipment contact surfaces

Decision Logic of "Quick Fix vs. In-Depth Investigation"

Imagine you are driving and suddenly hear a strange noise. If you see a stone on the tire, you can simply remove it: the cause is clear and the fix is simple. But if you are not sure where the noise comes from, you must never replace parts before opening the hood.

Glass breakage investigation follows the same logic: cause known → quick adjustment + record; cause unknown → analyze first, then act.

Interpreting Fragment Patterns

This three-factor decision framework is the fastest classification tool for floor staff. It needs no special equipment, and visual observation alone gives an initial direction for the investigation.

9.2 Root Cause Analysis

Proper analytics and root-cause determination can help identify and address systemic failures in the glass handling process. If no trained fractographer is onsite, there are fractography services available to outsource analysis of breakage samples.

In cases where the breakage is witnessed or the cause is known, fracture analysis may provide further information regarding the mechanics of the failure. However, if the cause of fracture is not known, before adjustments are made to the line handling, it is very important to perform a thorough fracture analysis to identify the exact cause of failure.

  • Simple breakage patterns with a few large fragments sometimes suggest a significant flaw in the container; the source of the flaw needs to be identified in order to be corrected.
  • Complex breakage patterns with many small fragments suggest a high-energy break due to excessive applied stresses.

To mitigate future incidents of glass breakage, root cause analysis may include:

  • Fracture Analysis – Conducted by a qualified fractographer
  • Supplier Evaluation – Review for atypical levels of incoming defects, dimensional conformity, etc.
  • Incoming Supplies Inspection – Review alignment with supplier testing procedures and criteria
  • Equipment and Handling – Assess areas prone to breakage, including glass-to-glass contact, glass-to-metal contact, high back-pressure zones, transitions, and others; high-speed cameras are often used to assess glass breakage sources on the line
  • Line Clearance Procedures – Remove all open containers that could be impacted during a glass breakage event
  • Training – Assess employee training and ensure that operations staff are trained to understand the criticality of their response to glass breakage
  • Metrics – Review glass breakage frequency and locations of impacted lots
  • Visual Inspection – Review visual inspection process and defect rates
  • Packaging and Distribution – Review downstream processing for potential issues

Commentary: Root Cause Analysis, Nine Dimensions of Systematic Thinking

Why "Outsource" Fractography?

Most CDMO sites do not have a dedicated fractographer. The technique requires microscope equipment and materials science training, and the demand is not frequent enough to justify a full-time position. TR87 explicitly accepts outsourcing the analysis. The key points are:

  • Establish a list of qualified outside testing facilities in advance, rather than looking for one during an emergency
  • Make sure samples are properly preserved before shipment, to avoid secondary damage in transit
  • Keep in touch with the outside laboratory, so they understand the equipment model and the process background

Nine RCA Dimensions: Suggested Practical Priority

TR87 lists nine RCA tools, but in practice not all of them need to be run every time. The suggested priority is as follows:

  1. Fractography: first confirm the nature of the force, so the other analyses have a direction
  2. Equipment and operation: a high-speed camera is the most intuitive tool and is recommended for early deployment
  3. Metrics review: look at the trend in breakage frequency to see whether it is sporadic or a systemic problem
  4. Supplier evaluation: if the pattern shows more problems in a particular lot, start a supplier audit
  5. Visual inspection review: confirm whether the current AQL can intercept this type of defect

The Value of High-Speed Cameras

Glass breakage happens within milliseconds and cannot be captured by the human eye. A high-speed camera can record at thousands of frames per second, letting engineers see clearly:

  • Which point of contact caused the breakage
  • The direction in which fragments are ejected
  • The relationship between equipment motion and vial position

This is one of the most effective non-destructive tools for investigating glass breakage on a modern aseptic filling line.

Systematic Improvement at a CDMO: Build a Breakage Dashboard

The RCA dimension of "metrics" is a low-frequency but valuable tool that many sites overlook. It is recommended to establish:

  • A per-batch or weekly record of breakage events (equipment location + lot number + vial type)
  • Pareto analysis: which equipment or process step accounts for the majority
  • Trend tracking: whether the breakage rate rises after a supplier changes lots

This data is the strongest objective evidence when verifying CAPA effectiveness (CAPA effectiveness check).

9.3 Corrective and Preventive Actions

Corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) should be defined as part of the glass breakage investigation. A list of typical CAPAs would include:

  • Collaborate with supplier to eliminate the source of incoming defects and ensure high-quality supply
  • Remediate sources of glass breakage found on the line, upstream and/or downstream
  • Install active monitoring through a walk-down of the line or continuous operator-monitoring
  • Enhance line-clearance procedures to eliminate the potential impact of glass-breakage events
  • Install shielding to limit the impact of glass-breakage events
  • Train operators, supervisors, and all staff to reinforce the importance of proper glass handling and the appropriate response to glass-breakage events
  • Improve detection methods (visual inspection)

References include published works on fractography, fracture mechanics, and PDA TR43 for nonconformities in glass containers used in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

Commentary: CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action), from Investigation to Action

Three Levels of CAPA

The CAPAs listed in TR87 can be classified by level:

  • Immediate correction (corrective): isolate the affected batch, adjust the equipment, perform line clearance, to solve the problem at hand
  • Short-term prevention: strengthen line patrol monitoring, install shielding, update line clearance procedures, to reduce the impact of the next event
  • Long-term systemic improvement (preventive): work with the supplier to improve quality and raise visual inspection standards, to eliminate the risk at the source

Special Note for CDMOs: CAPA Responsibilities in Customer Contracts

When handling a glass breakage CAPA, a CDMO needs to consider the following in addition:

  • Customer notification obligations: if a batch is affected, the timing and method of notification are usually set out in the Quality Agreement
  • Assignment of supplier responsibility: if the RCA shows a container defect, a supplier deviation report needs to be raised with the vial supplier
  • CAPA effectiveness verification: during a regulatory audit, the inspector will look at whether you followed up on effectiveness after the CAPA was completed, and not only whether the CAPA was "closed"

The Engineering Logic of "Installing Shielding"

Shielding is an engineering control measure. Even if breakage cannot be completely avoided, shielding can limit the spread of fragments and protect adjacent open containers from contamination. This strategy "controls the consequences" rather than "preventing the event", and is a practical risk-reduction measure until process capability has been fully improved.

In terms of the ICH Q9 risk management framework, this is "risk reduction" rather than "risk elimination".

The Key to Training CAPA: "Why" Matters More Than "How"

TR87 stresses that training is not just teaching steps but making everyone understand the seriousness of glass breakage. Practical suggestions:

  • Use real (anonymized) cases as training material
  • Explain the potential harm of glass particulates to patients
  • Make sure both supervisors and operators understand the decision logic of the first response
  • Provide periodic retraining, especially after equipment changes or a change of vial supplier lot

Commentary: Practical Use of the Worksheet and the Meaning of Each Field

The worksheet is not just a form for recording. It is the basis for reconstructing the event and a key input document for starting a CAPA and for the batch release decision. Every field exists for a reason.

Item 1: Immediate Line Stoppage, and Why "Who" Stopped the Line Matters

Recording who initiated the stoppage (Stoppage initiated by) is not only for assigning accountability. It is to confirm whether the operator made the call independently or the machine stopped automatically. The two situations can lead to completely different inferences about the root cause.

The timestamp (date + time) is the key index for later correlation with the batch production record (BPR), equipment logs and monitoring system data.

Items 2-3: Tracing Material Sources, the Starting Point for Supplier Accountability

Recording the vial supplier, lot number and tray ID is the basis for later analysis of whether there is a batch-wise defect. If vials from the same lot appear in multiple events, there is reasonable grounds to start a supplier evaluation or to strengthen incoming inspection.

Closure materials also need to be recorded, because some breakage occurs during stoppering or capping, and the hardness or dimensional deviation of the closure material may also be a source of abnormal force.

Item 4: Recording the Scene, with Photos + Video as the Most Objective Evidence

What regulatory inspectors value most is "objective evidence". Written descriptions vary from person to person, whereas photos and videos reconstruct the scene objectively. Key points to record:

  • Appearance of vials before and after the breakage (any visible cracks or chips)
  • Distribution of fragments (cross-check with the direction/distance record in Item 5)
  • Whether equipment contact surfaces show scratches or metal residue

The storage location of the video must ensure long-term traceability; it is recommended to store it under the same folder structure as the batch record.

Item 5: Fragment Data, the Preliminary Information for Fractography

The direction, distance, size and number of fragments map onto the decision framework in Section 9.1:

  • Few + large + near → low-energy breakage, and the vial may have a significant flaw
  • Many + small + far → high-energy impact, and the equipment may be applying too much force

The noise record (Item 5g) is also valuable: a sudden "crack" and a continuous "grinding sound" correspond to completely different mechanisms. Have the operator record a description of the sound immediately afterwards, because this memory fades quickly.

Item 6: Collecting Surrounding Vials, a Key Step in Protecting Patient Safety

This corresponds to the core risk of Section 9.0: adjacent vials may already be contaminated with glass fragments. Collecting vials from before and after the breakage point is not "wasting" material, but serves to:

  • Provide samples for visual inspection, to confirm whether there is visible fragment contamination
  • Provide control samples for fractography (unbroken vials from the same lot)
  • Provide the basis for deciding the extent of isolation

Item 7: Sample Shipment Record, Chain of Custody

Physical evidence in court requires a "chain of custody", with a record of every handover from collection to analysis. Breakage samples at a pharmaceutical plant are the same: who collected, who packed, who shipped and who received all need to be retained. This is the basis for ensuring the analytical results are credible, and also an important document for later audits.

It is recommended to establish a list of qualified external analysis providers (with contact information) in advance, to avoid being unable to find an analysis provider in an emergency or sending samples to an unqualified organization.

Where the Worksheet Sits in the QMS Process

This worksheet is usually attached to a deviation report or nonconformance report (NCR) as the original record of the preliminary investigation. The suggested workflow is:

  • Complete the paper form on site → scan and archive → enter into the electronic QMS
  • After the worksheet is completed, Quality decides from its content whether to start a formal CAPA
  • The retention period of the worksheet should match that of the batch record (usually product expiry + 1 year, or a minimum of 5 years)

Section Summary: Appendix II & III

Appendix II and Appendix III of PDA TR87 together form a complete framework for responding to glass breakage, from the first response when an event occurs, to systematic root cause analysis, to the development of CAPA (corrective and preventive action), and a complete set of on-site recording tools.

  • Stopping the line is the first step, but not the whole response: isolating the event immediately and collecting samples matters more than resuming production quickly.
  • Fragment pattern is a clue: a small number of large fragments → low energy, vial flaw; a large number of small fragments → high energy, equipment force. This decision framework needs no special equipment, and operators can apply it for an initial assessment.
  • When the cause is unknown, analyze first and adjust afterwards: adjusting online parameters hastily before fractography is complete may mask the root problem.
  • CAPA covers several levels: supplier, equipment, training and inspection: a single CAPA rarely resolves the problem completely, and a targeted combination is needed based on the RCA results.
  • The value of the worksheet lies in "systematic recording": the seven record items ensure no critical information is missed, making it an important document for batch release decisions and audit response.

By mastering this framework, CDMO operators can not only respond correctly to a single event but also build a culture of continuous improvement in glass handling quality.
