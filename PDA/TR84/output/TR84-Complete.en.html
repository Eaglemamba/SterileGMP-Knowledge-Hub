1.0

Introduction and Scope

PDA TR84 — p1

Original Text (English)

Data integrity is the cornerstone of establishing and maintaining confidence in the reliability of the data that assures product quality and patient safety. The reliability of manufacturing production and control data depends on the procedures, systems, processes, and controls in place to ensure data integrity. In production, these controls start at the point of data creation and continue through the entire data lifecycle, including the storage of data and the ability to retrieve it later to support the quality of the products manufactured.

The term "data integrity" has evolved to denote the degree to which data are complete, consistent, enduring, and available (ALCOA+) as well as the degree to which the characteristics of the data are maintained for all operations throughout the data lifecycle. Pharmaceutical manufacturers need to ensure that relevant data are available to document and provide traceability to what occurred, that the data are attributable to the person who performed the activity and entered the data, and that the data cannot be deleted, omitted, or in any way modified to misrepresent what occurred. All breaches of data integrity, whether intentional or unintentional, must be investigated.

Advances in information technology and in-process monitoring sensors, coupled with lower costs of computer memory and better processors, has resulted in explosive growth in electronic data generation, acquisition, and storage. Applying a one-size-fits-all approach to data integrity controls established on principles developed decades ago may be neither valuable nor, in certain instances, feasible. Therefore, the use of a risk-based approach is essential to developing a robust data integrity program, considering the requirements from a data-lifecycle perspective. Risk-based concepts apply even as industry transitions from manual documentation systems to fully electronic or hybrid (manual and electronic) systems.

This technical report addresses data integrity from the perspective of manufacturing operations. It discusses regulatory trends, risk management concepts, and recommendations for implementing appropriate data integrity controls in manufacturing operations applicable to paper-based, electronic-based and hybrid systems. The case studies included in this technical report provide examples of how to assess current data integrity risks and implement the concepts presented here.

Commentary and Application

Core Concept: Data Integrity

TR84 opens by stating that data integrity is the foundation for establishing and maintaining confidence in product quality and patient safety. Put simply, without trustworthy data, GMP compliance is an empty claim.

The modern definition of data integrity has expanded from the early ALCOA to ALCOA+:

  • Attributable: the person who performed the action can be identified
  • Legible: clearly readable
  • Contemporaneous: recorded at the time of the activity
  • Original: the first-hand record
  • Accurate: correct and error-free
  • + Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available

Regulatory Focus: All Violations Must Be Investigated

「All breaches of data integrity, whether intentional or unintentional, must be investigated.」

This statement is critical: whether the cause is human error or deliberate falsification, an investigation must be started. Many companies wrongly assume that an "unintentional mistake" does not need investigating, and that assumption is itself a GMP violation.

Analogy: Data Is the Plant's "DNA"

Picture a sterile filling plant: the production record of each batch is like the DNA of that batch of drug product. If that DNA is defective (incomplete or altered data), you cannot prove the batch is safe, no matter how good the product itself is.

Data integrity controls protect this "production DNA" so that it stays true and trustworthy from the moment it is created until the end of its retention period.

Why a Risk-Based Approach Is Needed

TR84 states clearly that a "one-size-fits-all" approach to data integrity controls is no longer practical, given:

  • Explosive growth in electronic data
  • Sharply lower memory costs
  • The widespread use of hybrid systems

Control must be built on QRM (quality risk management), with the level of control set according to the criticality and vulnerability of the data.

CDMO Practical Scenario

Your site uses paper batch production records (BPR), while some equipment already has electronic recording systems (such as the environmental monitoring BMS). This is a typical "hybrid system". The data integrity control principles of TR84 apply equally; the requirements cannot be lowered just because "this part is on paper".

1.1

Purpose

PDA TR84 — p1–2

Original Text (English)

The continuous and rapid advances in automation and information technology, availability of economical data storage, and superiority of electronic audit trail capabilities over paper records have compelled the pharmaceutical industry to rethink good manufacturing practices (GMP) controls. Among the consequences is a heightened awareness of the need to establish and maintain effective data integrity controls at every stage of the manufacturing process for drug products. While the requirement to maintain accurate and complete data is well recognized by industry, what is not universally understood is the level of data integrity control needed at each step to comply with GMP regulations. Many companies continue to struggle when deciding what controls are appropriate for each manufacturing operation and what levels of review and verification are necessary to ensure reliable manufacturing control data.

This technical report describes an approach using quality risk management (QRM) for establishing and assessing the appropriateness of data integrity controls for each manufacturing operation based on the criticality and vulnerability of the data for its intended use.

Developed by subject matter experts from global industry and regulatory agencies, this technical report summarizes manufacturing data integrity risks and identifies best practices that can be used to develop and sustain robust documentation as well as data integrity management procedures, systems, processes, and controls. Employing these practices will help users achieve compliance with applicable laws, regulations, and directives for pharmaceutical products such as active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), solid oral dosage forms, sterile injectables, biologics, and vaccines.

Assuring data integrity is an organizational responsibility. Employees at any facility that manufactures, processes, packages, or holds a finished pharmaceutical, intermediate ingredient, or API are responsible for ensuring that data collected throughout the manufacturing process are accurate and reliable. Managers, quality assurance personnel, operators, technicians, and support staff alike must remain aware of the significance of maintaining and documenting data integrity for the quality and safety of their products.

Commentary and Application

The Three Purposes of TR84

  1. Diagnose problems: help industry identify the data integrity risks at each manufacturing step
  2. Provide tools: use a QRM (quality risk management) framework to assess the level of control each step needs
  3. Establish best practices: bring together expert input from industry and regulators worldwide to provide best-practice guidance

A Common Source of Confusion in Industry

TR84 admits that "many companies continue to struggle": they know data must be accurate and complete, but are unsure how much control each step needs.

This is the core value of the report: it offers a systematic QRM approach so that decisions rest on evidence rather than on gut feeling.

Analogy: QRM Is Like Insurance Actuarial Work

Applying QRM (quality risk management) to data integrity is like an insurer doing actuarial work: not every risk needs the highest coverage; the amount of cover is decided by "probability of occurrence × severity × detectability".

For example, the critical process parameter records of sterile filling are high risk, so they need an electronic audit trail; a non-critical warehouse handling record is low risk, so paper with two-person verification may be enough.

Data Integrity Is an Organizational Responsibility, Not Just a QA Matter

TR84 states clearly that everyone is responsible:

  • Managers
  • QA personnel
  • Operators
  • Technicians
  • Support staff

This is not the QA department's responsibility alone; data integrity needs the support of a site-wide culture.

Questions to Consider

At your CDMO site, is there currently a document that clearly lists the data integrity control requirements for each manufacturing step (including the type of control and the review frequency)? If not, the QRM approach in TR84 can serve as the starting point for creating one.

CDMO Application Scenario

The scope covers APIs, oral solid dosage forms, sterile injectables, biologics and vaccines. For a sterile filling CDMO, every principle in TR84 applies directly to day-to-day operations, from the process parameter records of the filling machine to the management of electronic audit trails for sterilization records.

Glossary and Abbreviations

PDA TR84 — p2–5

Original Text (English) — Glossary

Audit Trail
FDA A secure, computer-generated, time-stamped electronic record that allows for reconstruction of the course of events relating to the creation, modification, or deletion of an electronic record.
MHRA Metadata containing information associated with actions that relate to the creation, modification or deletion of GXP records. An audit trail provides for secure recording of life-cycle details such as creation, additions, deletions or alterations of information in a record, either paper or electronic, without obscuring or overwriting the original record. An audit trail facilitates the reconstruction of the history of such events relating to the record regardless of its medium, including the "who, what, when and why" of the action.
WHO The audit trail is a form of metadata that contains information associated with actions that relate to the creation, modification or deletion of GXP records. An audit trail provides for secure recording of life-cycle details such as creation, additions, deletions, or alterations of information in a record, either paper or electronic, without obscuring or overwriting the original record.
Audit Trail Review Assessment (ATRA)
A tool that can be used to help determine what elements within the audit trail should be reviewed, and the frequency at which the review should take place for each part of the audit trail where a review is required.
ALCOA+
Attributable — It should be possible to identify the individual or computerized system that performed the recorded task. The need to document who performed the task/function is, in part, to demonstrate that the function was performed by trained and qualified personnel. This applies to changes made to records as well: corrections, deletions, changes, etc.
Legible — All records must be legible – the information must be readable in order for it to be of any use. This applies to all information that would be required to be considered Complete, including all Original records or entries. Where the 'dynamic' nature of electronic data is important to the content and meaning of the record, the ability to interact with the data using a suitable application is important to the 'availability' of the record.
Contemporaneous — The evidence of actions, events or decisions should be recorded as they take place. This documentation should serve as an accurate attestation of what was done, or what was decided and why, i.e., what influenced the decision at that time.
Original — The original record can be described as the first-capture of information, whether recorded on paper (static) or electronically (usually dynamic, depending on the complexity of the system). Information that is originally captured in a dynamic state should remain available in that state.
Accurate — Ensuring results and records are accurate is achieved through many elements of a robust pharmaceutical quality system: equipment-related factors such as qualification, calibration, maintenance and computer validation; policies and procedures; deviation management including root cause analysis, impact assessments and CAPA; trained and qualified personnel.
Complete — All information that would be critical to recreating an event is important when trying to understand the event. The level of detail required depends on the criticality of the information. A complete record of data generated electronically includes relevant metadata.
Consistent — Good Documentation Practices should be applied throughout any process, without exception, including deviations that may occur during the process. This includes capturing all changes made to data.
Enduring — Records must be kept in a manner such that they exist for the entire period during which they might be needed. They need to remain intact and accessible as an indelible/durable record throughout the record retention period.
Available — Records must be available for review at any time during the required retention period, accessible in a readable format to all applicable personnel who are responsible for their review whether for routine release decisions, investigations, trending, annual reports, audits or inspections.
Critical Process (CP)
A process that impacts a critical quality attribute of the intermediate, drug substance or drug product being manufactured and therefore should have established critical process parameters that can be monitored or controlled to ensure that the process produces the desired quality.
Critical Process Parameter (CPP)
A process parameter whose variability has an impact on a critical quality attribute and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces the desired quality.
Critical Quality Attribute (CQA)
A physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality.
Data Integrity
FDA Refers to the completeness, consistency, and accuracy of data. Complete, consistent, and accurate data should be attributable, legible, contemporaneously recorded, original or a true copy, and accurate (ALCOA).
MHRA The degree to which data are complete, consistent, accurate, trustworthy, reliable and that these characteristics of the data are maintained throughout the data life cycle. The data should be collected and maintained in a secure manner, so that they are attributable, legible, contemporaneously recorded, original (or a true copy) and accurate. Assuring data integrity requires appropriate quality and risk management systems, including adherence to sound scientific principles and good documentation practices.
WHO The degree to which data are complete, consistent, accurate, trustworthy and reliable and that these characteristics of the data are maintained throughout the data life cycle. The data should be collected and maintained in a secure manner, such that they are attributable, legible, contemporaneously recorded, original or a true copy and accurate. Assuring data integrity requires appropriate quality and risk management systems, including adherence to sound scientific principles and good documentation practices.
Data Integrity Controls
Controls put in place to either minimize the potential for a data integrity issue to occur or, if an issue does occur, the controls applied to increase the probability of detection.
Data Lake
A storage repository that holds, in a structured way, a vast amount of raw data, including metadata, in its native format until it is needed.
Data Lifecycle
MHRA All phases in the life of the data (including raw data) from initial generation and recording through processing (including transformation or migration), use, data retention, archive/retrieval and destruction.
WHO All phases of the process by which data is created, processed, reviewed, analyzed and reported, transferred, stored and retrieved and monitored until retirement or disposal. There should be a planned approach to assessing, monitoring and managing the data and the risks to those data in a manner commensurate with potential impact on patient safety, product quality and/or the reliability of the decisions made throughout all phases of the data life cycle.
Data Process Flow Map
A flow map that uses a baseline process flow map and overlays the data flow.
Data Vulnerability
An indicator of data's level of exposure to data integrity failures due to intrinsic weaknesses in manufacturing processes, data-capture technology, and human factors or a combination thereof.
Detectability
The ability to discover or determine the existence, presence, or fact of a hazard. For purposes of this technical report, a hazard typically would be a data integrity breach.
Gemba Walk
A method of walking through and personally observing processes.
Historian
A database/software program that archives automation and process data.
Looped Memory
An electronic system with limited storage capacity that overwrites older data when it reaches that capacity.
Mitigation
Systematic steps taken or in place to reduce or limit the identified risk.
Peer Review
A review of data by a colleague who has a similar level of responsibilities as the person performing the activity or capturing the data.
Quality Unit
An independent quality unit/structure with authority to fulfill certain pharmaceutical quality system responsibilities.
Risk Control
Actions implementing risk management decisions.
True Copy
FDA 21 CFR 211.180(d) requires records to be retained "either as original records or true copies such as photocopies, microfilm, microfiche, or other accurate reproductions of the original records." Electronic copies can be used as true copies of paper or electronic records, provided the copies preserve the content and meaning of the original or raw data, which includes metadata required to reconstruct the CGMP activity and the static or dynamic nature of the original records.
MHRA A copy (irrespective of the type of media used) of the original record that has been verified (i.e., by a dated signature or by generation through a validated process) to have the same information, including data that describe the context, content, and structure, as the original.
WHO A copy of an original recording of data that has been verified and certified to confirm it is an exact and complete copy that preserves the entire content and meaning of the original record including, in the case of electronic data, all essential metadata and the original record format as appropriate.

Commentary and Application

Audit Trail: A Regulatory Cornerstone

The three regulators (FDA, MHRA, WHO) agree on the core of the definition:

  • Secure and tamper-resistant
  • Time-stamped
  • Records who, what, when and why
  • Must not overwrite the original record

The MHRA version is the most complete, stressing the four elements "who, what, when and why", and is the standard auditors cite most often.

ATRA: Audit Trail Review Assessment

ATRA (Audit Trail Review Assessment) is a practical tool introduced by TR84 that answers a common question: "What do I need to review in the audit trail, and how often?"

Many companies switch on electronic audit trails but never review them regularly and systematically, which is a common observation in FDA/MHRA inspections.

Analogy: Contemporaneous Is Like Surveillance Video

"Contemporaneous" requires recording at the moment the event happens, not filling in from memory afterwards. Think of a traffic accident: the camera records what really happened, whereas a statement written later from memory, however honest, carries far less legal weight.

In pharmaceutical manufacturing, back-dating batch record entries is one of the most common data integrity violations.

CP / CPP / CQA: The Triangle of Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

Understanding the hierarchy among these three concepts is essential for data integrity risk assessment:

CQA Critical Quality Attribute
(product quality target)
↑ Affects
CP Critical Process
(process that affects a CQA)
↑ Controls
CPP Critical Process Parameter
(parameter to be monitored)

Because CPP data records directly affect CQAs (product quality), they need the strictest data integrity controls.

Data Integrity Controls: A Dual Purpose

TR84 defines two purposes for data integrity controls:

  1. Prevention: minimize the likelihood that a data integrity problem occurs
  2. Detection: if a problem does occur, increase the chance that it is found

This is consistent with the "risk control" thinking in QRM: prevention comes first, but the ability to detect must also be in place.

Data Lifecycle: From Start to Finish

MHRA definition (more concise): from initial generation → recording → processing → use → retention → archive/retrieval → destruction

WHO definition (more complete): emphasizes a "planned approach" to assess, monitor and manage the data and the risks to them, in a way commensurate with the impact on patient safety, product quality and the reliability of decisions.

Looped Memory: A High-Risk Trap

"Looped memory" is the built-in storage method of many older instruments (such as older filling machines and balances): once full, it automatically overwrites the oldest data.

This directly violates the "Enduring" requirement of ALCOA+. Solutions:

  • Download the data regularly and store it in a controlled system
  • Or upgrade to equipment with larger capacity or real-time data transfer

True Copy: Must Preserve Content and Meaning

The three regulators agree that a true copy must preserve the full content and meaning of the original record.

This is especially important for electronic records:

  • It must include all necessary metadata
  • It must preserve the static or dynamic format (for example, a PDF printout is not equivalent to the original electronic file)
  • An electronic copy must be generated through a validated process, or be dated and signed off

Analogy: Gemba Walk Is "Management by Walking Around"

The Gemba Walk comes from the Japanese word for "the actual place" and is a method in which managers go to the production floor in person to observe. In data integrity management, the Gemba Walk is the most effective way to find the gap between the written SOP and actual practice.

Many data integrity problems are not policy-level problems but habits in day-to-day execution, and they can only be seen by going to the floor.

CDMO Application Scenario: Historian Systems

In a CDMO, the historian is usually part of a DCS (such as DeltaV) or SCADA system and automatically records:

  • Fill weight trends of the filling machine
  • Temperature/pressure curves of the autoclave
  • Temperature and humidity data from the environmental control system (BMS)

The historian system itself must also be included in CSV (computerized system validation) to ensure the integrity of the data it records.

Questions to Consider

Which equipment in your site's manufacturing systems may have a "looped memory" problem? How is that data currently kept complete before it is overwritten?

Data Integrity: Comparison of the Three Regulators' Definitions

The MHRA and WHO definitions are broader than the FDA's, emphasizing the "entire data lifecycle" and the "quality risk management system". A global CDMO should work to the most stringent requirement.

Commentary and Application

Abbreviations Every CDMO Employee Must Know

The following abbreviations appear repeatedly throughout TR84 and their meanings must be memorized:

  • ALCOA: the acronym for the core data integrity principles; with a + sign it becomes ALCOA+
  • BPR (batch production record): the core document of manufacturing operations
  • EBR (electronic batch record): the electronic version of the BPR
  • BMS (building management system): the system that monitors the facility environment, such as temperature, humidity and differential pressure
  • MES (manufacturing execution system): the middle-layer system that integrates production scheduling and batch records
  • HMI (human-machine interface): the equipment operating interface, common on filling machines and sterilizers
  • PLC (programmable logic controller): the core of equipment automation control, whose records require CSV
  • QRM (quality risk management): the core assessment methodology of TR84

Regulatory Abbreviations: A Global View

TR84 is an international document, and the regulators it cites include:

  • FDA (United States): 21 CFR Part 11, Part 211, etc.
  • MHRA (United Kingdom): its Data Integrity Guidance is widely regarded as the most detailed
  • WHO (World Health Organization): applicable to markets in developing countries
  • TGA (Australia)
  • NMPA (China): additional attention is needed for compliance in the mainland China market
  • PIC/S: the Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme, the basis of the European GMP framework

As a CDMO, your clients may come from different markets, so you need to meet the requirements of several regulators at the same time.

Analogy: MES vs EBR vs BMS

The hierarchy among these three systems:

  • BMS: records the facility environment (temperature, humidity, differential pressure), like a "weather station"
  • EBR: records the batch production process (charging, operations, checks), like a "ship's log"
  • MES: integrates the two and manages production scheduling, like the "command bridge"

The data integrity control requirements differ for each of the three, and all fall within the scope of CSV.

CDMO Inventory Recommendation

We recommend that your IT/QA team create a "system inventory" listing every system on site that records data (HMI, BMS, historian, EBR, LIMS, etc.) and noting:

  • Whether CSV has been completed
  • Whether the audit trail is enabled and reviewed regularly
  • Data retention period and backup mechanism

This list is the starting point for building data integrity procedures and is also a key document during inspections.

Section Summary

Key Concepts Recap

Key Takeaways of This Section

1. Data integrity is the cornerstone of GMP
The very first statement of TR84 makes the point that data integrity is the foundation for establishing and maintaining confidence in product quality and patient safety. All data integrity violations, intentional or not, must be investigated.

2. ALCOA+ has nine principles, not just five
ALCOA (attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate) is the basis; adding complete, consistent, enduring and available makes ALCOA+. Each one has its own specific control requirements.

3. TR84 covers manufacturing and packaging, not laboratories
Data integrity management in the laboratory is governed by PDA TR80. The two documents must be read together to cover the data integrity needs of the whole pharmaceutical plant.

4. Hybrid systems are the norm and need special management
Most pharmaceutical plants are still in transition between paper and electronic systems, and data integrity control of hybrid systems is one of the focal points of TR84. During the transition, "we are not fully electronic yet" cannot be used as an excuse to lower requirements.

5. QRM (quality risk management) is the assessment framework
The strength of data integrity controls should be commensurate with the criticality and vulnerability of the data. QRM is the core assessment method throughout TR84 and avoids "one-size-fits-all" over-control or under-control.

Early Warning: Frequent Inspection Questions

The data integrity questions FDA/MHRA inspectors ask most often in manufacturing operations:

  • Is your audit trail enabled? How often is it reviewed? (Is there an ATRA assessment?)
  • Do operators share computer login accounts? (Attributable requirement)
  • Is there equipment at risk of data being overwritten because of looped memory?
  • Are corrections to paper batch records made according to the principle of single-line strike-through, signature, date and reason?
  • Has the backup mechanism for electronic records been validated? Do true copies meet the regulatory definition?

Post-Study Actions for This Section: CDMO Practice Recommendations

  1. Carry out a system inventory: list all systems on site that generate GMP records (electronic and paper) and confirm the data integrity control status of each.
  2. Verify audit trail status: confirm that audit trails are enabled on all electronic systems, and establish an ATRA (audit trail review assessment) procedure.
  3. Identify looped memory equipment: find older equipment on site that uses looped memory and set up a regular data backup procedure.
  4. Promote "data integrity is everyone's responsibility": make sure everyone from operators to senior management understands the ALCOA+ requirements, especially "Contemporaneous": records must not be filled in after the fact.

Final Analogy: Data Integrity Is Like a Building's Foundation

No matter how advanced your process technology or how new your equipment, if the "foundation" of data integrity is unstable, the whole GMP building may collapse. Many FDA Warning Letters and import bans were issued not because the product itself had a problem, but because the records could not be trusted: the regulator could not confirm that the batch was really made in accordance with GMP.

The core message of TR84: the credibility of the data determines the credibility of the product.

3.1

Historical Perspective

From the 1963 GMP regulations to the modern era of global DI enforcement

Original Text (English)

In 1963, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued its current good manufacturing practices (GMP) regulations for processing, packing, or holding drugs and its major revision in 1978 under 21 CFR Part 211. Since that time, the FDA has acted upon data integrity violations detected during its GMP inspections of pharmaceutical manufacturers. Unique skill sets are required to detect data integrity problems and collect evidence to establish GMP violations. These skill sets can be acquired through focused training on prevailing good documentation practices, including the technology, or lack thereof, used for documenting GMP activities and the associated data and human behavior.

The assessment of data integrity procedures, processes, systems, and controls using a consistent, structured approach and forensic techniques was not always a primary focus during FDA inspections. Periodically, the detection of egregious and unethical events causes FDA to redouble its efforts and refocus its attention on lapses in data integrity. For example, during the late 1980s and early 1990s, FDA uncovered data integrity violations at a large number of U.S.-based generic drug manufacturers (and some innovator companies) in what was later dubbed the "generic drug scandal."

The FDA discovered widespread falsification of production and control records and found that many abbreviated new drug applications contained falsified data and other serious data integrity violations. Several individuals from the industry and the FDA were criminally prosecuted, and the scandal prompted FDA to establish in 1990 a pre-approval inspection program that remains in effect today. That program requires successful FDA pre-approval inspections as a condition of approval for drugs and biologics; subsequently, other major health authorities established similar pre-approval inspection programs. Then, in 1997, the FDA promulgated a regulation related to computer system electronic records and signatures for digital data in 21 CFR Part 11.

These developments impacted industry practices, too. Data integrity became a focus of companies, industry conferences, and industry best practice documents such as the International Society for Pharmaceutical Engineering (ISPE) good automated manufacturing practice (GAMP) guides and PDA technical reports. Data integrity awareness and expertise increased among pharmaceutical professionals, and the incidence of data integrity problems reported by FDA during its GMP inspections of domestic and international pharmaceutical manufacturers declined. Egregious data integrity violations, however, still resulted in sanctions.

Starting in the late 1990s, the advent of the internet and growth in information technology fueled increased globalization of pharmaceutical supply chains. This rapid expansion roused regulators' concerns about data integrity. In 2007, FDA reported that it had found objectionable data integrity issues at 3 of the 10 companies it had recently inspected on this topic. In the years immediately following, FDA again refocused its attention on data integrity practices. Since then, FDA has included data integrity citations in more than 200 warning letters sent to companies in more than 20 countries.

Since 2012, inspections by the FDA, European Medicines Agency (EMA), Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA), World Health Organization (WHO), and other health authorities have revealed a notable increase in the incidence of data integrity issues at international manufacturers of APIs and finished pharmaceuticals worldwide. The prevalence and significance of the data integrity lapses discovered in recent years has led regulators to make data integrity a primary focus during inspections, and regulators are detecting data integrity lapses with increasing frequency.

Pharmaceutical regulators continue to find conditions and practices that compromise data integrity, among them human errors, inadequate management oversight, insufficient employee training, system failures, inappropriate qualification or configuration of systems, poor procedures or not following procedures, and intentional acts of falsification.

Analysis and Application (Commentary)

Core Concept: The Historical Context of GMP Data Integrity

Data integrity (DI) is not a new issue. Since FDA first established GMP regulations in 1963, accuracy of records has been implicitly required, and 21 CFR Part 211 in 1978 formally set out the regulatory framework for production records.

Key milestones:

  • 1963: FDA GMP regulations first issued
  • 1978: 21 CFR Part 211 (current GMP) revised
  • 1990: Pre-approval inspection program established after the generic drug scandal
  • 1997: 21 CFR Part 11 (electronic records and signatures) issued
  • After 2007: Expansion of the global supply chain drives a surge in DI deficiencies

Analogy: "The Evolution of the Auditor"

Think of how a bank examiner's work has evolved: at first they only checked the figures in the books, then began cross-checking the journals, verifying original vouchers, and reviewing surveillance video. FDA investigators' DI inspection techniques have gone through the same evolution, from simply reading through batch records to using forensic techniques to analyze audit trail timestamps.

Regulatory Alert: The Far-Reaching Impact of the "Generic Drug Scandal"

The U.S. generic drug scandal of the 1980s-1990s was the most significant turning point in DI history. Several firms were found to have falsified batch records on a large scale and submitted false data in abbreviated new drug applications (ANDAs).

The consequences included criminal prosecutions (including FDA personnel), industry reform, and the creation of the Pre-Approval Inspection Program that remains in use today.

Why 21 CFR Part 11 Matters

21 CFR Part 11 is the U.S. regulation specific to electronic records and electronic signatures. Issued in 1997, it governs:

  • Integrity controls for electronic records
  • Audit trail requirements
  • Identity verification for electronic signatures
  • System access control and security

The corresponding EU framework is EU GMP Annex 11.

Trend Alert: DI Problems Surge After 2012

2012 was the turning point for modern DI enforcement. FDA, EMA, MHRA (the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency), and WHO simultaneously stepped up inspections of international manufacturers, with manufacturers in India and China bearing the brunt.

FDA has issued more than 200 Warning Letters citing DI to firms in more than 20 countries.

Quality Risk Management Applied to Data Integrity

ICH Q9 principles applied across the full data lifecycle

Original Text (English)

The principles of QRM apply to the data lifecycle, which includes the capture, recording, transcription, archiving, and other aspects of data management. This section uses examples to describe how certain elements could pose a risk to the integrity of pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical data. Risk should be controlled regardless of whether the data are managed using a manual system or an automated electronic system. Any potential breach related to data integrity—particularly the loss, omission, deletion, or alteration of data—must be investigated, addressed, and accounted for in the quality system and, as necessary, reflected in the risk management documentation. Given that data may be managed and/or transferred between different formats or systems, efforts should be made to mitigate the risk to the integrity of the data during these transfers. Therefore, judicious application of these concepts and targeted application of data integrity principles is warranted as the industry transitions from paper to hybrid (manual and electronic) and, eventually, to fully electronic systems. Data integrity risk also should be addressed during the transfer of data between electronic systems.

The PDA Technical Report No. 54 series describes QRM more broadly, including how to conduct an appropriate risk assessment and formulate a mitigation strategy to reduce the risks identified. For more information on QRM generally and other important elements, such as risk communication, please see the TR 54 series and ICH Quality Guideline Q9: Quality Risk Management.

Analysis and Application (Commentary)

Core Concept: QRM and the Data Lifecycle

Quality Risk Management is not a system separate from data integrity (DI). It is a management framework that runs through the entire data lifecycle. From generation, recording, transcription and transfer to archiving, every step carries potential risk.

Key stages of the data lifecycle:

  • Capture: the point where raw data are generated, such as sensor readings or weighing results
  • Recording: writing the data into an official document or system
  • Transcription: moving data from one system or format to another
  • Archiving: long-term retention and retrievability

Analogy: "The integrity of a courier parcel"

Think of data as a valuable parcel. From sender (data generation) to recipient (final use), it passes through pickup, sorting, transshipment and delivery. If anything goes wrong at any step (the parcel is lost (deletion), the contents are swapped (alteration), or it is sent to the wrong address (transfer error)), the integrity of the parcel is broken. QRM places a quality checkpoint at every transfer point.

Regulatory Focus: Risk During the Transition from Paper to Electronic

TR84 stresses that DI risk is especially high while industry moves from paper to hybrid to fully electronic systems. Many CDMOs are currently at the hybrid stage, with handwritten batch records alongside electronic systems, and errors are most likely when data are transcribed between the two.

Note: ICH Q9 (the quality risk management guideline) and the PDA TR54 series are the higher-level reference documents for this section.

CDMO Practical Scenarios

Typical hybrid data flows in a sterile fill-finish facility include:

  • The filling machine PLC records fill weight automatically, then the operator copies it by hand into the batch record
  • The environmental monitoring (EM) system samples automatically, then the laboratory manually records the results in the LIMS
  • Electronic data from a temperature logger are printed and attached to the batch record

Every "transcription between systems" node is a high-risk area for data integrity.

Considerations in Assessing Risk

Proactive and reactive risk assessments for data management systems

Original Text (English)

Risk assessments should be conducted proactively at the time any new data management system is implemented and should be captured by the overarching change control. At this stage in the process, risk assessments can serve as preventive measures to ensure that data integrity is maintained throughout the system lifecycle. When performed on existing data management systems, the assessments should identify potential data vulnerabilities based on the existing level of controls and the criticality of the data for its intended use.

Organizations also should consider performing a risk assessment after a deviation or incident to identify potential gaps in the data management system. Assessment of data integrity risks also should be done in conjunction with the change management process of a data management system to avoid creating new or additional risks. This will enable informed decision-making regarding the extent of the issue and the potential impact. Key objectives of this assessment are to determine if the root cause is related to a gap in data management and, if so, to identify the extent of the gap and the impact to the data.

Data integrity controls should be considered from both the behavioral and technical perspectives. From a behavioral perspective, the overall quality culture, including communications and training, is intimately related to the effectiveness of the data integrity program.

Analysis and Application (Commentary)

Core Concept: Three Occasions for Risk Assessment

TR84 states clearly that risk assessment is not a one-time activity. It is performed on three key occasions:

  1. Proactive: when a new system is introduced, performed in parallel through change control
  2. Reactive: after a deviation or event, to identify potential gaps in the data management system
  3. Change-linked: when the data management system changes, to avoid introducing new risks

Analogy: "When to inspect a house"

Imagine buying a house. You have a home inspection before handover (proactive), call a plumber after a pipe leaks (reactive), and reassess structural safety when you renovate (change-linked). Risk assessment of a data management system follows exactly the same logic.

Regulatory Focus: Dual-Track Control of Behavioral and Technical Aspects

TR84 emphasizes that data integrity controls must cover two aspects at the same time:

  • Technical: hardware and software controls such as system validation, access control and audit trails
  • Behavioral: human-related controls such as quality culture, communication and training

Relying on technical measures alone while neglecting quality culture will ultimately fail to prevent data integrity problems. This is also why FDA has put growing weight on assessing quality culture during inspections in recent years.

Considering Human Factors in Assessing Risk

Unintentional vs. intentional acts and the Human Factors Matrix

Original Text (English)

In considering the human behavioral influence when examining the elements that pose risk to data integrity, two categories need to be examined: unintentional and intentional acts (see Table 4.1.1-1🔍):

Table 4.1.1-1🔍: Human Factors Matrix

One category is the unintentional act. Risk may be unintentionally introduced in a variety of ways. The unintentional act may take the form of an "action error" caused by a slip in attention or lapse of memory, or a "thinking error" caused by gaps in governing procedures (knowing what or who) or knowledge (knowing how). For instance, numbers might be reversed in an entry or entered in the wrong spot due to a slip in attention. A bar code might have been applied incorrectly to materials that are brought into the processing room, and the operation started before operators realize that the materials were for a different batch. Another example could be that one operator sets up the equipment believing it will be used for one product, but a different product is brought in and processed.

Appropriate procedures should be in place to require a documented investigation in the event of any mishandling or misuse of the data generated through such unintentional acts. The investigation should identify the root cause, determine the impact and criticality of the deviation, and assign appropriate CAPAs. Where human error is suspected or identified as the cause, this should be justified, having taken care to ensure that process, procedural, or system-based errors or problems have not been overlooked, if present.

The other category is the intentional act. Risk may be introduced intentionally through misconduct, by failing to follow established procedures and controls due to misplaced priorities or through malicious acts. For example, to personally benefit or to avoid personal consequences associated with having made the mistake, an employee may cover up a mistake by creating false entries showing that the desired activity or result occurred. In one misconduct scenario, the operator was not paying attention to the mixing or blending operation and allowed it to run for longer than the validated time, but entered a value within the required range. Another example could be an overworked operator who is struggling to keep up with the manufacturing schedule and, in an effort to catch up, reprints the last acceptable in-process test result with the batch number of the new sample. Determining the reason for an intentional or malicious act may not always be possible.

When a data integrity incident is identified, the most important information to learn is the what (event and systems affected), when (timelines), who (persons involved), why (gaps that contributed to the events occurring), how (specifics, step-by-step explanation of occurrence), where (systems affected) and impact (product quality and patient safety). These elements are essential to determine the need for market action and, ultimately, lead to implementing corrective actions, which will assist in mitigating a recurrence of the data integrity issues.

In the case of unintentional risks to data integrity, it will suffice to follow the process described for risk assessment in this section. Provided the "right" people are in the room—whether operators, technicians, mechanics, programmers, and/or supervisors—the risk assessment is likely to identify the areas in which an accident in data capture and recording is possible. The purpose of the risk assessment is to determine the probability of that accidental error occurring.

When the risk to data integrity is the result of an intentional action, a comprehensive investigation is needed. Intentional manipulation of data is hard to detect and may require the use of forensic detection techniques. The risk assessment process may uncover some of the circumstances in which deliberate acts have occurred, but solid assurances that no repercussions will occur for contributing such information during a risk assessment requires a great deal of trust.

Analysis and Application (Commentary)

Analogy: "Classifying traffic accidents"

Unintentional behavior is like a traffic accident:

  • Slip = being distracted by the phone while driving and running a red light by accident
  • Lapse = forgetting to signal before turning
  • Procedural gap = an intersection with no traffic signal, so drivers do not know who yields
  • Knowledge gap = a new driver unfamiliar with the rules of a roundabout

Intentional behavior is like drunk driving (knowing it is illegal but gambling on not being caught) or deliberate ramming (malicious damage).

Regulatory Warning: "Human error" must not become a catch-all excuse

TR84 stresses that when an investigation attributes the root cause to "human error", it must first be confirmed that no process, procedural or system root cause has been overlooked. FDA investigators are unhappy with the overuse of "human error" as a root cause, because it often means the company has not dug into systemic problems.

Correct approach: rule out systemic causes first (unclear SOPs, equipment defects, system loopholes) before attributing the root cause to human factors.

The 6W1H Framework for DI Event Investigation

TR84 sets out the core questions that must be answered when investigating a DI event:

  • What: the nature of the event and the systems affected
  • When: the timeline and duration
  • Who: the people involved
  • Why: the management gaps that allowed the event to happen
  • How: a step-by-step account of how the event occurred
  • Where: the scope of the systems affected
  • Impact: the effect on product quality and patient safety

Risk Control Strategies

Balancing automation, procedural controls, and supervisory review

Original Text (English)

The potential impact on data must be determined and documented and, following the risk assessment, mitigations must be determined to reduce risk. For risks that have been identified as having a high probability of occurrence and a high impact on product quality or patient safety, a balanced risk control strategy will need to be implemented. Understanding the manufacturing process and data lifecycle processes is crucial to developing successful risk control strategies.

Although automation is not the solution to every problem, it can be a useful tool as part of a risk control strategy. Intelligent barcodes can be used to confirm product and component identity and can prevent a process from proceeding until the error is rechecked and corrected. The time-honored use of a second person checking or a supervisor reviewing is also important, provided that it is timely. Frequently, the supervisory review takes place too long after an event to confirm that it occurred as recorded. Moving further into the digital age, electronic automation will replace such reviews when implemented and qualified appropriately.

Analysis and Application (Commentary)

Core Concept: Three Layers of Risk Control Strategy

The risk control strategies in TR84 can be summarized in three layers:

  1. Automation: remove human intervention points through system design, for example an intelligent barcode that forces verification of material identity
  2. Procedural: second person checking and supervisory review
  3. Cultural: building a quality culture, training and an open reporting environment

The most effective strategy works from the top down: first eliminate the source of risk (automation), then detect risk (procedures), and only last rely on human behavior (culture).

Regulatory Warning: The Timeliness Problem of "After-the-Fact Review"

TR84 points out a common problem: supervisory review often takes place so long after the event that the authenticity of the record can no longer be confirmed effectively.

For example, if QA review of a batch record takes place two weeks after production is complete, it is hard to reconstruct what actually happened at the time, even if an abnormality is found.

Best practice: review should be as close to real time as possible, or at least completed within the same shift.

CDMO Practical Scenarios

Application of intelligent barcodes on an aseptic filling line:

  • Each drug solution container carries a unique 2D barcode, which must be scanned and confirmed before the filling machine starts
  • If the barcode does not match the batch formula, the system locks automatically and filling cannot start
  • This poka-yoke (error-proofing) mechanism removes the possibility of the operator picking up the wrong drug solution

This is orders of magnitude more reliable than the traditional "second person visually checks the material label".

Analogy: "The evolution of the aircraft cockpit"

Early aircraft relied entirely on manual control by the pilot (= manual recording). Later a co-pilot cross-check was added (= second person checking). Modern airliners have autopilot, ground proximity warning and automatic collision avoidance (= automated controls). The direction TR84 encourages is the same: let the system act as the gatekeeper instead of relying on people never making mistakes.

Using the Data Vulnerability Grid

Original Text (English)

The data vulnerability grid (9-Box), as depicted in the flowchart in Figure 4.2-1, will not provide one overall categorization of a company's data vulnerability, but offers a tool to use for each process and subprocess of operations that involve GMP data. The 9-Box is a holistic approach that allows users to categorize the risk to the data in their current manufacturing processes and data lifecycle processes using internal data criticality and data control assessments. The 9-Box can also be used before implementing new or revised processes. The outcome of the assessments will be only as good as the completeness and objectivity of the internal assessments. Underestimating risk factors and overestimating current data integrity controls will diminish the effectiveness of the outcome.

The 9-Box also allows users to identify controls that could be implemented to decrease the risk to data in the manufacturing process. Implementation of those controls could decrease data vulnerability from a high level to a lower level (from right to left in the grid). Examples for implementing the 9-Box in specific scenarios are included in Appendix I.

This technical report recommends, as the first step, selecting a product and identifying the data that is critical for that product to meet its approved specifications and its intended use (see Section 4.2.1.) The critical data for the product should have been identified already as part of the product development process and included in corresponding documentation. After identifying the data that impacts the product CQAs and the processes that are critical in its manufacture, the user can prioritize internal assessments, identify risk factors, and categorize the data vulnerability for these critical processes.

After completing this analysis for a specific process or unit operation, the completed information may be leveraged for the same process or unit operation used for another product, giving due consideration to potential differences.

For data management, the full lifecycle of the data needs to be considered to determine areas of vulnerability. This includes how the data is captured (manual/automated/hybrid system), frequency of collection, validation of any automated systems used to capture the data, migration of production data to data analysis tools, data transcription, and data archiving. Here, again, existing data flow maps from previous assessments can be leveraged.

For human factors, points of human intervention in the data lifecycle need to be considered to determine data vulnerability. As people are prone to error, processes that rely heavily on manual data capture and review are considered inherently more vulnerable than those that are automated. To determine the reliability of the current processes, the assessment should consider trends related to data transcription errors and poor application of good documentation practices. Conversely, automated data collection, properly validated, will be less prone to error and more likely to yield high quality data.

For GMP process, the complexity and robustness of the manufacturing process being evaluated should be considered to determine areas of data vulnerability. More complex processes are likely to produce a larger volume of data, which could increase the number of factors that impact the data vulnerability. The assessment needs to look at all aspects of the manufacturing process and evaluate each process on a case-by-case basis. A newer manufacturing process that has been validated recently and that lacks a history of robustness will have greater data vulnerability than an established process with a high-process capability. Trends related to change control, out of specification (OOS) results, alarms, and out-of-calibration instances can also illustrate the vulnerability of data being collected for the manufacturing process. Once identified, the trends should be investigated to determine their relationship to data vulnerability.

Data Process Flow Maps (DPFM)

Visualizing data movement through operations to identify high-risk points

Original Text (English)

Data process flow maps, a GMP requirement in certain parts of the world, are an extremely useful tool for visualizing the movement of data through a given operation and can facilitate the identification of areas of high risk to the data. Every step from start to end of a given process (e.g., from order creation to release of finished product) should be identified and documented in order to obtain a complete picture of the data flow in the process. For each step, the data process flow map should identify the activity performed, how the activity is performed, the systems used to perform the activity, the data created, the method of recording the activity, and the decisions made from the data. It also should include details on how data flows between systems or process steps and the means used to transfer data, taking the data lifecycle into consideration as part of the supporting documentation. The data process flow map should start with the point of origin of the data and include the systems (e.g., equipment, devices, applications) used in the process. Documenting the data flows related to manufacturing processes and systems through personal observation, such as through a Gemba walk, can prove helpful in this process.

Points in the process where data integrity could be compromised should be identified. Asking a series of critical-thinking questions at each stage of the data flow throughout the lifecycle can facilitate the process, for example:

  • Are unique accounts assigned to each user?
  • Is an audit trail available that demonstrates if and when data is manipulated in any way, and does it identify who has performed the operation?
  • Is data held in temporary memory? What happens if there is no power to this unit?

For instance, unauthorized access to a system could potentially allow for manipulation of batch-related data. These points where data integrity could be compromised represent areas of risk. For each, a risk assessment should be conducted, and mitigations to reduce risk identified and implemented. Differences in detectability (including elements related to electronic vs. manual processes) should be considered as part of the risk assessment.

A DPFM is like the tracking of an online-shopping parcel:

  • Dispatch warehouse (data generation point) → pickup station (initial record) → sorting center (data conversion/calculation) → delivery station (data review) → recipient (final use/archiving)
  • Every node has a timestamp, a handler and a status update
  • If a node is "broken" (no tracking information), that is a data integrity risk point

Regulatory Focus: Checklist of Key Questions

TR84 recommends asking the following questions at every node of the DPFM:

  1. Does every user have a unique account? (access control)
  2. Is there an audit trail recording any operation on the data? (traceability)
  3. Are the data held temporarily in volatile memory? What happens after a power failure? (data persistence)

These questions look simple, yet they are the lines of inquiry most often pursued during FDA inspections.

CDMO Practical Scenario: Example DPFM for a Sterile Filling Line

Data flow from solution preparation to product release:

  1. Preparation room: the weighing system records raw material weight automatically → data are sent to the MES
  2. Mixing tank: the PLC records mixing speed/time automatically → the operator confirms the on-screen values and signs the batch record
  3. Filling machine: fill weight/speed are captured automatically → a real-time report is printed → the operator compares and attaches it to the BPR
  4. Environmental monitoring: the particle counter records automatically → data are exported manually to Excel → manually transcribed into the EM report
  5. QC laboratory: the analytical instrument generates results automatically → the analyst enters them manually into the LIMS → QA review
  6. QA release: all data are compiled → batch record review → release decision

High-risk nodes identified: the "manual export to Excel" and "manual transcription" in Step 4 are the most vulnerable links: Excel has no audit trail, and manual transcription is error-prone.

Important Reminder: The DPFM Is Already a Regulatory Expectation in Some Regions

TR84 mentions that the DPFM is already a GMP requirement in "certain regions". For example, the MHRA Data Integrity Guidance (UK) and the WHO Data Integrity Guidance both recommend that companies establish data flow maps. This means the DPFM is not just "best practice"; in some markets it is mandatory.

1. Core principles of applying QRM to data integrity

  • ICH Q9 quality risk management principles run through every stage of the data lifecycle (capture, recording, transcription, transfer, archiving)
  • Risk assessment must be performed on three occasions: when a new system is introduced (proactive), after a deviation/event (reactive), and when the system changes (change-linked)
  • Control strategies must cover both technical and behavioral aspects

2. The Human Factors Matrix

  • Unintentional behavior divides into thinking errors (procedural/knowledge gaps) and action errors (slips/lapses)
  • Intentional behavior divides into misconduct (skipping controls because of improper prioritization) and fraud (malicious falsification)
  • "Human error" must not become a catch-all root cause; systemic causes must be ruled out first

3. The DI risk management model and the 9-box matrix

  • Data Criticality = how important the data are to product quality (High/Medium/Low)
  • Data Control Level = the strength of the existing controls (High/Medium/Low)
  • The 9-box cross-pairs the two and divides the result into four zones: red (significant vulnerability), orange (moderate vulnerability), green (acceptable) and blue (negligible)
  • The three aspects (technology, human, GMP process) must each be assessed with a 9-box
  • Direction of improvement: from right to left, raising the control level

4. The Data Process Flow Map (DPFM)

  • The DPFM is a tool for visualizing the complete journey of data, and is already a GMP regulatory requirement in some regions
  • Each node must record the activity, how it is performed, the systems used, the data generated, the recording method and the decisions made
  • Key questions: unique account? Audit trail? Volatile-memory power-loss risk?
  • Case 1 (manual paper) vs. Case 2 (PLC automation): the standard example of an upgrade from the H/L red zone to the H/H green zone

Core conclusion: data integrity risk management is not a one-off project but a continuous, risk-based management system that covers both technical and behavioral aspects. The 9-box matrix and the DPFM are the two most practical tools: the former helps you "locate risk" and the latter helps you "discover risk".

Data Integrity Controls — Overview

DI Controls Overview: Causes, Culture and Core Requirements

Original Text (English)

5.0 Data Integrity Controls

Many events during manufacturing may compromise the integrity of the data. Any number of factors may cause these events, including human error, insufficient training, system failure, inappropriate qualification or configuration of systems, poor procedures, or people not following procedures, as well as intentional acts of falsification.

PDA recognizes that quality culture is critically important in the prevention and detection of data integrity breaches; however, this technical report is designed to identify risk and potential mitigation of a data integrity breach regardless of where on the spectrum of data integrity (system error, individual error, individual or institutional malfeasance) that breach occurs. Of course, intentional fraud and intentional manipulation of data are unacceptable under any circumstances, regardless of data criticality, vulnerability, or existing level of controls.

In supporting and communicating expectations around a culture of quality for data integrity, PDA recommends that entities use the PDA publication, Elements of a Code of Conduct for Data Integrity in the Pharmaceutical Industry, which outlines key elements necessary to help ensure the reliability and integrity of data throughout all aspects of a product's lifecycle. It can be used, in whole or in part, as a control approach to guide a company's internal practices, to create or modify an existing data integrity code of conduct, or to develop agreements with outsourcing partners or other suppliers. The elements identified throughout that document are intended to reinforce a culture of quality and trust within the pharmaceutical industry.

An important part of preventing data integrity breaches is identifying, establishing, and maintaining proper controls, whether the data elements are electronic, paper-based, or a combination of the two. Ensuring data integrity means protecting the original data from accidental or intentional modification, falsification, and deletion. Requirements specifying that data need to be ALCOA+ throughout the data lifecycle have been included in regulations around the world for several decades. The WHO, FDA, MHRA, and PIC/S have all released documents to reeducate the industry on current data integrity concepts and expectations. These are the "what"—what is required to ensure the integrity of data.

Data integrity controls can be embedded in working procedures and practices or included as part of computer system validation and equipment qualification or usage. These controls may be used to minimize the potential that a data integrity issue will occur and to increase the probability of detecting an issue that does occur. The data integrity controls are the "how"—how to ensure that these requirements are implemented in practice.

Regulations convey the core elements that must be in place to ensure data integrity and a robust QMS, including some of the following examples:

  • Standard operating procedures (SOPs) are in place for completion and retention of records.
  • People are suitably trained for their intended job purpose.
  • Systems are validated or qualified for their intended purpose.
  • People have access to the records required to perform their activities.
  • If electronic signatures will be used, they should be attributable to an individual, unable to be altered, permanently linked to the document, and have a time and date stamp.
  • Data integrity risks should be identified as part of the QRM process and dealt with accordingly.
  • A management review process allows data integrity issues to be elevated to the highest levels of the organization.
  • The data governance policy is endorsed at the highest levels of the organization, empowering a strong data integrity culture at all levels of the organization.
  • An anonymous reporting program, supported by company policy, encourages personnel to bring breaches in data integrity to the attention of management.
  • Routine data verification and periodic surveillance checks should be performed.
  • Self-inspections should include verification of the effectiveness of the data integrity controls.
  • A strong change management system is required.
Commentary

Core Concept: The Three Causes of DI Failures

TR84 opens by identifying three levels at which data integrity (DI) failures occur:

  • System error: defects in the equipment or software itself
  • Individual error: human mistakes, inadequate training
  • Malfeasance / falsification: data manipulation by individuals or at the organizational level

The three causes call for different control strategies, but the framework in this report is designed to cover the full spectrum.

Analogy: "What" vs. "How"

Regulators (WHO, FDA, MHRA, PIC/S) tell us that data must meet ALCOA+. This is the "What" (what has to be achieved).

The TR84 DI control system is the "How": how to put these requirements into practice in daily operations.

It is like restaurant hygiene rules that require food to be held at a set temperature (What), while the refrigerator maintenance SOP, temperature log and periodic calibration are the How.

ALCOA+ Quick Review

  • Attributable: who performed the action
  • Legible: clear and readable
  • Contemporaneous: recorded at the time of the activity
  • Original: the original record
  • Accurate: correct
  • + Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available

Regulatory Focus: Culture and Technology in Parallel

PDA stresses that deliberate falsification is never acceptable, regardless of how critical the data are or how strong the existing controls are.

Technical controls (system validation, access restrictions) can reduce opportunity, but quality culture is the fundamental line of defense. For this reason PDA has a separate "Code of Conduct for Data Integrity" that addresses behavioral aspects.

Core QMS Elements Required by Regulators (12 Items)

The 12 requirements listed in the text can be grouped into four dimensions:

  • Procedures: complete SOPs, change management
  • People: training, access authorization, anonymous reporting
  • Systems: system validation, electronic signatures, data backup
  • Audit: periodic data verification, self-inspection, management review

In an aseptic filling CDMO, DI controls must span multiple client projects (many products, systems and SOP versions), a challenge far greater than that of a single brand-owner site. For example:

  • Client A requires paper batch records and Client B requires MES electronic records, so two sets of controls run in parallel
  • How Access Control is segregated on the same filling line across different shifts and different products
  • When an anomaly is found in the Audit Trail of a batch, is it a system problem or operator behavior?

The TR84 framework helps a CDMO answer these questions systematically.

Potential Differentiation of Controls

The Concept of Differentiated Controls: When It Is Allowed and When It Is Not

Original Text (English)

5.1 Potential Differentiation of Controls

While there are many requirements to ensure a robust QMS, Section 5.0 only focuses on data integrity controls where it is acceptable to differentiate the level of controls that may be applied. This means that the levels of controls applied (e.g., frequency of review, number of items to check, who performs the review) may differ depending on the criticality of the data, system, or activity. Consistent with ICH Q9, which promotes a risk-based approach, highly critical data, systems, or activities require more controls, while less critical data or activities require fewer controls.

This portion of the technical report focuses on only a small part of all data integrity controls required by regulations. Most data integrity controls required by health authority regulations apply in the same manner for any GMP data, that is, the controls do not differ based on the criticality of the processes performed by the organization. Such controls are not discussed further in this technical report.

The following examples illustrate why the same level of controls may be applied, regardless of the criticality of the activity:

  • GMP regulations require that entries in records should be indelible, made in the appropriate spaces, identify the person making the entry and, if different, identify the person who performed the task(s). Corrections, if required, should be dated and signed, leaving the original entry still readable, with a comment added to explain the correction. Envision two different scenarios in which a person makes a correction because of a typographical error: One person has recorded a critical parameter during aseptic filling, while another has documented a training, a less critical activity. In both circumstances, the individuals involved should make the necessary correction, explain why the correction was made, and sign with the time the correction was made, leaving the original entry still legible.
  • GMP regulations also require that, in electronic systems, a user's privileges should be limited to allow the employee to perform the work, without any additional unnecessary privileges. Access to change the date or time of the system, for example, should be limited to a very small group of people, ideally independent administrators. Regardless of whether the system is highly critical (e.g., granulation) or less critical (e.g., secondary packaging systems that do not store data relating to quality attributes or parameters such as expiry dates), the same controls should be in place to restrict access.

Any given system may have some controls for which it is possible to differentiate the level of controls applied, and some controls that will be the same regardless of the criticality of the activity.

Commentary

Core Concept: "Differentiable" vs. "Non-differentiable" Controls

The core TR84 framework distinguishes two types of DI controls:

  • Fixed controls (Non-Differentiable): all GMP data must be treated alike, regardless of criticality. For example, records must be legible, and corrections must be made with a single line-through that keeps the original entry readable, signed and dated.
  • Differentiated controls (Differentiated Controls): the strength, frequency and performer of controls can be adjusted according to data criticality. This is the main focus of TR84.

Analogy: Speed Limits vs. Lane Restrictions

"Whether you drive a truck or a sedan, you must stay within the lane." This is a fixed requirement, equivalent to "record corrections must use a line-through."

"A truck carrying hazardous goods must take a designated route, while an ordinary car can choose freely." This is a differentiated control, equivalent to "high-criticality batch records need a second review by QU, while low-criticality ones only need operator self-checking."

Key Point: Two Examples of "Non-differentiable" Controls

Example 1: Record corrections
Whether it is an aseptic filling CPP (Critical Process Parameter) record or a general training record, the correction method is exactly the same: line-through, original entry kept legible, signature and date, with a reason. Criticality does not affect this requirement.

Example 2: Permission to change the system time
Whether the system is used for high-criticality data (granulation process) or low-criticality data (secondary packaging with no quality-attribute data), Access Control over changing the system time must be restricted to a very small number of independent administrators.

Extension of ICH Q9 Risk Management Principles

TR84 is explicitly aligned with the foundation of ICH Q9 (Quality Risk Management):

  • High criticality = more controls, higher frequency, stricter review
  • Low criticality = controls still exist, but their intensity can be reduced appropriately
  • Criticality is assessed by the organization itself (not directly prescribed by regulation)

This makes TR84 a practical guide to applying ICH Q9 in the DI field.

CDMO Practical Scenarios

In day-to-day CDMO audits, this distinction is very useful:

  • When a client or an FDA inspector asks "Why is the review frequency different for high- and low-criticality batch records?", you can cite TR84 Section 5.1 and ICH Q9 as the basis
  • When you want to reduce the control frequency for some low-criticality documents to save resources, you must first confirm that they fall in a "differentiable" category and not in a fixed-requirement category
  • Permission to change the system time is a common source of Warning Letters and must be strictly controlled regardless of system criticality

Original Text (English)

5.2 Methodology Used to Determine Differentiated Controls

To determine the controls required for data, records, or systems, existing regulations and guidelines for data integrity were reviewed. The recommended controls were categorized against the type of document control system being employed: paper-based, electronic-based, and a hybrid. Commentary

Preventive Control vs. Detective Control

TR84 Section 5.3 clearly distinguishes two types of control, which is very important when designing a DI control system:

  • Preventive Control: stops a DI problem before it occurs. Examples: restricting who can modify Audit Trail settings (removing the opportunity for tampering), controlled printing of documents (preventing misuse of blank forms).
  • Detective Control: allows a DI problem to be discovered after it has occurred. Examples: periodic Audit Trail review (finding abnormal operations), QU review of batch records.

Section 5.3 is mainly about preventive controls, but also includes some detective controls.

Analogy: Safe vs. Security Camera

Preventive controls are like a safe: they lock up confidential material so that it cannot be reached without authorization.

Detective controls are like security cameras: you can look back afterwards to see who did what and when, but they cannot stop the action at the time.

The ideal Defense in Depth uses both: prevent first, then detect, and finally correct (CAPA).

Review of the 9-Box Grid Concept

The 9-Box Grid mentioned in TR84 is the core tool for assessing data vulnerability (Section 4.3):

  • X axis: data criticality (High / Medium / Low)
  • Y axis: risk factors (High / Medium / Low)
  • Goal: controls are sufficient so that data fall in the diagonal "green boxes" (H/H, M/M, L/L)
  • If controls are insufficient: data fall in a "red box", meaning control strength is lower than the criticality requires

The control recommendations in Section 5.3 are the practical guidance for keeping data in the green boxes.

Key Reminder: Examples Are Not Mandatory

TR84 states explicitly that all High/Medium/Low activity classifications in Section 5.3 are examples only, are not normative, and cannot be applied directly.

Each organization must:

  • Assess data criticality according to its own business characteristics
  • Determine control frequency on a risk basis
  • Write all controls (whether differentiated or not) into SOPs

This is an important basis in regulatory inspections: your SOPs must reflect the conclusions of your risk assessment.

The Concept of Defense in Depth

The "Defense in Depth" philosophy implicit in TR84 Section 5.3:

  • Layer 1: technical prevention (system design stops problems from occurring)
  • Layer 2: procedural prevention (SOPs prescribe correct behavior)
  • Layer 3: detection mechanisms (Audit Trail, review, self-inspection)
  • Layer 4: corrective response (CAPA, deviation management)

In a CDMO environment, the complexity of multiple clients and multiple systems calls for every layer to be carefully designed.

CDMO Practical Scenarios

When performing a DI Gap Analysis, you can assess each data flow with the following structure:

  1. Confirm which of the seven categories the data belong to
  2. Assess data criticality (High / Medium / Low) and document the rationale
  3. Compare against TR84 Table 5.3.X-1 to confirm whether existing controls are commensurate with the criticality level
  4. Confirm that existing controls are documented in SOPs
  5. If controls are insufficient, initiate CAPA; if controls are excessive, evaluate whether they can reasonably be reduced (to save resources)

This framework can serve as a structured tool for the annual DI internal audit.

5.3.3.3 True Copy (Paper to Electronic)

True Copy (Paper to Electronic): Scanning, Review and Destruction of Originals

Original Text (English)

5.3.3.3 True Copy (Paper to Electronic)

Table 5.3.3.3-1 illustrates the controls needed for creation of a true copy, going from a paper record to an electronic record. True copies must be maintained using the same controls that applied to the original paper record.

For High-criticality records, such as paper batch records that may be needed for downstream use by a sister site or another department, a trained user can make the true copy, but a second person from the quality unit must perform a documented review. The review must ensure the scan is legible, accurate, and complete (all pages, all entries) and confirm that all entries on the true copy provide all information and data that are contained on the original record. For example, if data is color-coded or highlighted, the true copy must reflect the same color-coding or highlighting, with the intent of capturing the context and meaning. In addition, the original record may be discarded with quality unit oversight, unless the original document has a seal, watermark, or similar identifying mark that cannot be accurately reproduced electronically, in which case it cannot be discarded. The quality unit should determine the level of oversight required for destruction, which may range from quality unit presence during destruction to periodic confirmation of compliance with procedures.

For Medium-criticality records, like a validation protocol, a trained user can make the true copy. A documented second-person review is required, though the second person need not be from the quality unit. The original record may be discarded by the operating unit unless the original document has a seal, watermark, or identifying mark. While the quality unit does not need to be directly involved in the destruction process, it must retain a suitable level of oversight to ensure the procedures are followed.

For Low-criticality records, such as completed user access authorization forms where evidence of training exists in another system, the person making the true copy must document that they reviewed the scan for legibility, accuracy, and completeness. The original may be discarded by the individual, with quality unit oversight to ensure that procedures are followed.

As with all controls relating to data integrity, processes relating to true copies, regardless of criticality, must be documented in SOPs. A record must be maintained to reflect when each document was destroyed and who destroyed it.

Commentary

Core Concept: True Copy

A True Copy is a copy that retains all the information of the original record, and it must be controlled to the same level as the original. TR84 focuses here on the paper-to-electronic scenario, in which paper records are digitized by scanning.

Key points to judge:

  • Legibility: text and data are clearly readable after scanning
  • Accuracy: content is identical to the original, including color coding, highlighter marks, etc.
  • Completeness: all pages and all fields have been scanned

Analogy: Scanning Is Like Photographing an Ancient Book

Imagine you need to photograph a precious ancient book into digital files:

  • High: museum-grade rare book → photographed by a professional photographer, checked page by page by the curator personally; if the original has a watermark or embossed seal, the original must be retained
  • Medium: library collection → photographed by trained staff, with a second colleague checking
  • Low: an ordinary borrowing register → photograph it yourself and confirm it is legible

Regulatory Focus: Three Prerequisites for Destroying Originals

TR84 states clearly that even for High criticality records, the original may be destroyed provided the following conditions are met:

  1. The True Copy has been reviewed and approved by the Quality Unit
  2. The original has no features that cannot be accurately reproduced electronically (stamps, watermarks, etc.)
  3. The destruction is documented (when and by whom)

However, if the original bears a stamp, watermark or other identifying mark that cannot be reproduced electronically, the original must not be destroyed.

CDMO Practical Application

True Copy management is especially important in a CDMO environment:

  • Client batch records: a client-supplied paper Master Batch Record, once scanned, is High criticality and requires page-by-page review by the quality unit
  • Technology transfer documents: a Validation Protocol from the client is Medium criticality and requires second-person review
  • Multi-client management: ensure True Copy files of different clients have clearly separated access rights to avoid cross-access

5.3.4 Access Controls for Electronic Systems

Electronic System User Access Control: Accounts, Passwords and Privilege Tiers

Original Text (English)

5.3.4 Access Controls for Electronic Systems

Access controls to electronic systems are required to prevent potential data integrity issues from occurring. Table 5.3.4-1🔍 describes several differentiable controls related to access to electronic systems.

For systems storing High-criticality data, such as an MES, historian, or filter integrity tester used during sterile filling, unique identification and authentication controls should be in place for each individual user. Other features also should be available for use, such as mandatory password changes every 90 days, lock-out after five failed attempts, password recycling only allowed after 10 cycles, and an annual review of user accounts.

For systems storing Medium-criticality data, like parameter-setting or executed run files for a washing machine, many of the controls for systems storing Highly critical data likewise should be applied. Certain measures can be less stringent: mandatory password changes can occur every 180 days (instead of 90), passwords can be recycled after 5 cycles (instead of 10), and users locked-out after 10 incorrect tries (instead of 5).

For Low-critical systems, for example, an HMI on a secondary packaging line that controls conveyor speed but does not store data, the access controls may be far less rigorous. For these types of systems, a passcode or group accounts are acceptable. Controls must be in place, however, to ensure segregation of duties between user levels (i.e., administrator vs. engineer vs. operator). The passcode or group accounts should be changed annually. These controls may also be used for other Low-critical activities that do not require the action to be attributable. For example, a group account may be used to press the start/stop button on a packaging line when the system requires a log-in to activate the stop/start function.

Automatic log-out is not discussed as a differentiated control but should be considered as an overall control factor. The amount of time that should be allowed to pass before an inactive user is automatically logged-out should be commensurate with the risks identified, and documented accordingly.

As stated above, the suggested frequencies at which certain activities should take place are illustrative only. Organizations should determine the frequencies that are most applicable to their operations using a risk-based approach to ensure that the periodicity of activities is commensurate with the risks identified. This assessment should consider the overall system architecture, the interfaces between systems, criticality of access by unauthorized persons, and the potential for network security breaches.

Commentary

Core Concept: User Access Control

Access Control is the first line of defense for Data Integrity. TR84 defines it as a "Prevention Control", whose core purpose is to ensure:

  • Identification: each user has a unique account
  • Authentication: a password confirms that the person operating is the actual user
  • Segregation of Duties: different roles have different privileges

Both High and Medium levels require individual accounts (User ID + Password); the Low level permits Group Accounts, but operator and administrator privileges must still be distinguished.

Analogy: Access Cards and Keys

Think of electronic system access control as building security:

  • High (e.g., MES, process historian): fingerprint + proximity card two-factor authentication, password changed every 90 days, locked after 5 failed attempts
  • Medium (e.g., washer parameter settings): proximity card authentication, password changed every 180 days, locked after 10 failed attempts
  • Low (e.g., packaging line HMI speed control): a shared door code is sufficient, changed once a year

Regulatory Focus: Automatic Log-out

TR84 stresses that although automatic log-out is not a differentiated control item, it is a holistic control element. The idle time before automatic log-out should be commensurate with the risk.

In practice, MES terminals in the aseptic filling area are typically set to log out automatically after 5-10 minutes, while systems in office areas can be set to 15-30 minutes.

CDMO Practical Application: Access Control Challenges in a Multi-Client Environment

The biggest challenge for a CDMO is that one electronic system may store process data of multiple clients. This creates additional control needs:

  • Project segregation: operators for one client may only access that client's batch data and must not view data across clients
  • Access matrix: build a three-dimensional access matrix of "client x role x system"
  • Account review: with frequent staff rotation, the annual account review must specifically confirm that access of departed or transferred personnel has been removed
  • Confidentiality agreements: access control is one of the technical means of fulfilling client confidentiality agreements (CDAs)

5.3.5 Electronic Audit Trail Review

Electronic Audit Trail Review: Basic Concepts and Scope of Review

Original Text (English)

5.3.5 Electronic Audit Trail Review

Audit trails are a form of metadata that records details such as the creation, addition, deletion, or alteration of data within a system. An audit trail provides controls for secure recording of lifecycle details, without overwriting the original record. Where equipment (e.g., instruments, devices) are used to capture, create, process, report, store, or archive relevant GMP data electronically, the system design must always include the provision and retention of a suitable audit trail.

Secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails must allow for reconstruction of the history of events relating to the record, including the "who, what, and when" of the action. Where technically possible, comments should be added, in the case of modifications or corrections, so that the why of the activity also is recorded as part of the audit trail. Where not technically possible to record in the audit trail the reasons for changes or deletions to GMP-relevant data, alternate documentation should be used.

The electronic audit trail may comprise multiple sources related to the equipment; that is, the information may be held in files called something other than "Audit Trail," for example, event log, alarm log, system log, history file, trends, or reports. Understanding where the information is recorded within the system, at both the operating system and application level, is important to help protect the data and understand where in the system to look when performing an audit trail review.

In the following sections, audit trail review is discussed for three different types of data contained within the electronic audit trails:

  • Data associated with batch setup, for example, the recipe used, and the set-points or times planned for specific activities during the batch
  • Batch run data, which is data from the manufacturing process, for example, temperatures, duration of activities, who performed the activities
  • System configuration data, which are not batch-relevant data but the data associated with the configuration of the system, for example, user-account information, privileges granted to users, where data is stored

Reviewing audit trails enables detection of potential data integrity issues. Some regulations require that audit trails be reviewed on a periodic basis. There is no expectation that every part of the audit trail will be reviewed for every batch; rather, it is expected that a risk-based approach will be taken to identify the appropriate level of review to conduct.

The person performing the audit trail review must be independent and technically capable of understanding the audit trail. The person should not have been involved in the creation or verification of the data being reviewed.

Commentary

Core Concept: Audit Trail

An Audit Trail is metadata that automatically records all critical events in the data life cycle: creation, addition, deletion and modification. Its core requirement is "Who, What, When, Why".

TR84 particularly emphasizes:

  • An audit trail is not necessarily named "Audit Trail"; it may exist as an Event Log, Alarm Log, System Log, etc.
  • The location of the audit trail must be understood separately at the operating system (OS) level and the application level
  • Not every audit trail needs to be reviewed for every batch; instead, a risk-based approach is taken

Three Categories of Audit Trail Data

TR84 divides the data covered by audit trails into three categories:

  1. Batch Setup Data: recipe, set-points, planned times, etc.
  2. Batch Run Data: process data such as temperature, operation times and operator identity
  3. System Configuration Data: account management, privilege settings, data storage locations, etc.

The review method and frequency differ for each of these categories and must be assessed separately using the ATRA tool.

Regulatory Focus: Independence of the Reviewer

The person performing Audit Trail Review must meet two conditions:

  • Independence: must not have been involved in creating or verifying the batch data
  • Technical competence: must be able to understand the content and meaning of the audit trail

This means operators cannot review their own operation records, nor can someone with no understanding of the system do the review.

Analogy: Bank Transaction Statements

An audit trail is like the transaction history of your bank account:

  • Each transaction records who (the account holder), what (deposit / withdrawal / transfer), when (timestamp) and why (remarks on purpose)
  • You do not need to check every entry every day, but you can periodically spot-check unusual transactions
  • The person checking should not be yourself, but an independent auditor

5.3.5.1 Audit Trail Review Assessment (ATRA)

Audit Trail Review Assessment: The ATRA Three-Dimension Scoring Tool

Original Text (English)

5.3.5.1 Audit Trail Review Assessment

An audit trail review assessment (ATRA) is a risk-based tool that can help determine which elements within the audit trail should be reviewed, and the appropriate frequency. The tool may be used for any system that has an electronic audit trail, for example, systems directly used in manufacturing as well as supporting systems such as building automation and water for injection.

The ATRA focuses on the detectability of changes to the data, the criticality of the data (in terms of patient safety or product quality), and the probability or capability for changes to the data to occur.

A cross-functional team should be assembled to conduct the ATRA. The team should comprise people who fully understand the technical capabilities and limitations of the system, how the system is set up (from a user management perspective), where the data is stored, what type of data is part of the audit trail. The team also should include users who are familiar with operating the system.

The first step in the process is to score each of the three factors—detectability, severity, and probability—that may require review based on the system controls in place for each element in the audit trail. For each factor, a lower score represents lower risk. Examples of scores that may be used are provided in this section, although each entity may modify the scoring based on its own analysis and risk tolerance.

Detectability Score

Detectability Score is based upon the likelihood that modification or deletion of data will be detected. There are two possible Detectability Scores: 1 or zero.

If it is possible to detect any modifications or deletions to the data contained within the audit trail, and this data is already reviewed as part of an existing review as required by SOP, performing additional review of the audit trail to detect potential data integrity issues is not required. For example, if the batch report lists the critical parameters used during manufacturing and shows any changes made to these parameters during batch manufacturing, an audit trail review of the same information need not be conducted. (This assumes that the batch report is generated automatically from the equipment and cannot be manipulated.) Score = 0.

If the data has not already been reviewed or has only been partially reviewed (i.e., not all critical data is in scope of the batch-wise data review), further assessment of the audit trail may be required. Score = 1.

Severity Score

Severity Score is based upon the data criticality; it assesses the potential impact if data is modified or deleted. There are three possible Severity Scores: 10, 4, or 1.

If the data is considered Highly critical or has a direct impact on patient safety or product quality (e.g., changing the temperature to make it appear it was within the validated range, despite being outside the range), the potential impact is of high severity. Score = 10.

If the data has an indirect impact on patient safety or product quality, i.e., changes to data that result in a compliance risk only, with indirect impact to only product quality or patient safety (e.g., changes to time zone where the raw data and batch-related data is not affected), it is of medium potential severity. Score = 4.

If the data has negligible or no impact on patient safety or product quality (e.g., the start time of an operation, provided that this is not a CPP and does not affect duration), it is of low potential severity. Score = 1.

Probability Score

Probability Score is based on the capability of the data to be modified or deleted by system users and administrators. There are three possible Probability Scores: 10, 2, or 1.

If the data can be modified or deleted by a user, the chance that such changes will happen is higher. "User" refers to any person not identified as an independent administrator; "power users" are considered users. Score = 10.

If the data cannot be modified or deleted by a user but can be modified or deleted by an independent administrator (i.e., an individual with no direct interest in the data), the chance that such changes will occur is lower. Score = 2.

If the data cannot be modified or deleted by any user or administrator, it is considered a very low probability. Score = 1.

Next, the three scores (Detectability, Severity, and Probability) are multiplied to provide the ATRA final score. The lower the score, the lower the risk.

The timeframes indicated are illustrative only. Organizations should assess their systems and apply critical thinking when considering the criticality of the activity being performed as well as the potential for impact on product quality and patient safety. Using a risk-based approach, the periodicity of reviews should be commensurate with the risks identified.

If during periodic review a number of data integrity breaches are discovered, the controls that are (or should be) in place and/or the frequency of the review should be reevaluated to prevent future breaches or to detect breaches sooner.

Commentary

Core Concept: ATRA Three-Dimension Scoring

The Audit Trail Review Assessment (ATRA) is a risk assessment tool proposed in TR84 to determine the review frequency for each element in an audit trail. Its logic resembles the FMEA Risk Priority Number (RPN), multiplying three dimensions:

  • Detectability: would a modification or deletion be detected by other existing review processes?
    Score: 0 (already covered by a review) or 1 (not yet covered by a review)
  • Severity: if the data were modified or deleted, what is the impact on patient safety / product quality?
    Score: 10 (direct impact), 4 (indirect impact), 1 (negligible impact)
  • Probability: how likely is it that the data could be modified or deleted?
    Score: 10 (can be modified by a general user), 2 (can be modified only by an independent administrator), 1 (cannot be modified by anyone)

ATRA Calculation Formula

ATRA Final Score = Detectability x Severity x Probability

Score range: 0 (lowest risk) ~ 100 (highest risk)

Typical results:
  0 = already covered by a review → no additional Audit Trail review needed
  1-10 = low risk → Event-based (review when an event is triggered)
  11-20 = medium risk → every six months to annually
  40 = high risk → weekly to monthly
  100 = highest risk → review every batch (Batch-wise)

Analogy: Home Security System

ATRA works like deciding "how often to patrol":

  • Detectability: is a camera already recording? (yes = 0, no = 1)
  • Severity: does this area hold a vault (10), office equipment (4), or garden clutter (1)?
  • Probability: can outsiders easily enter (10), do only administrators have keys (2), or is it a sealed area (1)?

Vault + no camera + accessible to outsiders = 1 x 10 x 10 = 100 → patrol every time (review every batch)

Regulatory Focus: ATRA Is Not a One-Time Exercise

TR84 states clearly that if multiple DI violations are found during Periodic Review, the following must be reassessed:

  • Are the existing controls sufficient?
  • Does the review frequency need to be increased?

This means ATRA is a dynamic tool that must be continually updated as the system's risk status changes.

CDMO Practical Application: Cross-Functional ATRA Team

TR84 recommends forming a cross-functional team to perform ATRA. In a CDMO environment, the team should include:

  • IT/Automation: understands system technical limitations and the audit trail storage architecture
  • Quality Assurance (QA): defines the data criticality level
  • Manufacturing: understands daily operating procedures and potential risks
  • CSV specialist: ensures the ATRA is consistent with the computerized system validation (CSV) documents

5.3.5.2 Practical Examples of ATRA

ATRA Case Study: Scoring Example for a Filter Integrity Tester

Original Text (English)

5.3.5.2 Practical Examples of ATRA

An audit trail contains data from (1) batch setup data, (2) batch run data, and (3) system configuration data. Each of these parts of the audit trail have a number of elements that could be considered for audit trail review. Hence, the ATRA tool should be used for each individual element to determine the final score.

Elements for the batch setup data and batch run data parts of the audit trail, where data controls may be vulnerable and may have an impact on critical batch data, might include:

  • Were any of the recipe parameters modified outside the validated range during the execution of the batch?
  • Were changes made to CPPs (set points or allowed tolerance ranges) during manufacturing? Were these within the validated ranges?
  • Were the calculation parameters edited or modified?
  • Were changes to the time made that impact the duration of (time-sensitive) parts of manufacturing, i.e., an operation ran longer or shorter than was expected?
  • Were segregation of duties in place where required and was the person who performed the activity authorized to perform it (e.g., different reviewer and approver)?
  • Were there GMP-relevant incidents, for example, GMP critical alarms?
  • Was any data relevant to batch quality modified or deleted?

Similarly, for the system configuration data, a number of elements may be vulnerable and should be considered when using the ATRA tool, such as:

  • Were changes made to the system date or time?
  • Was the report template or any batch report modified, edited, deleted, or renamed?
  • What changes have been made to user access accounts or the privileges assigned at the user level? Do these changes still ensure segregation of duties?
  • Were any edits, modifications, or deletions made to the system configuration (e.g., administration changes, database settings, change of data storage location, system settings or functionality)?
  • Were there changes, edits, or deletions of audit trails, history logs, or files? Were any renamed?
  • Was any unusual activity recorded in the user activities log (login, logoff, unauthorized logins)?

Systems with high technical capabilities that are being fully utilized (e.g., strong access control, segregation of duties) are less vulnerable, therefore, a significantly reduced frequency of audit trail review is acceptable. On the other hand, if the system has limited technical capabilities (e.g., no difference between administrator and operator privileges), the system is more vulnerable, and a more extensive review of the audit trail would be appropriate.

Table 5.3.5.2-1🔍 contains a few examples of how the ATRA tool can be used for a filter integrity tester. The example does not represent a full ATRA review for a filter integrity tester but covers a few audit trail elements for demonstrative purposes to clarify the use of an ATRA.

Conclusions that can be drawn from the examples in Table 5.3.5.2-1🔍 are:

  • An audit trail review should be performed batchwise to check for any aborted or repeat tests or aborted runs not included as part of the batch data package.
  • An audit trail review should be performed every six months to check for changes made by the administrator to recipes, user accounts, or privilege levels assigned to users.
  • A review of the audit trail for changes to pass/fail results is not required.
  • A review of the audit trail to check the storage location for data is only required should a triggering event prompt a specific review of this data.

Completing the ATRA for the other elements of the audit trail will allow the user to more fully understand the parts of the audit trail that require review and the necessary frequency of that review.

Commentary

Core Concept: ATRA Review Question List

TR84 lists specific questions to focus on during audit trail review, which fall into two categories:

Batch-related (Batch Setup + Run Data):

  • Were recipe parameters modified during batch execution to values outside the validated range?
  • Were the set-points or allowable ranges of CPPs (critical process parameters) changed?
  • Were calculated parameters edited or modified?
  • Did time modifications affect the duration of time-sensitive processes?
  • Is segregation of duties enforced? Was the operator authorized?

System Configuration:

  • Was the system date or time changed?
  • Were report templates modified, deleted or renamed?
  • Were there any changes to user accounts or privileges?
  • Is there any record of the audit trail itself being modified or deleted?

Regulatory Focus: Key ATRA Findings for the Filter Integrity Tester

Through the Filter Integrity Tester example, TR84 demonstrates the power of ATRA:

  • Repeated/aborted test (Score=100): an operator could hide a failing printed report; this is the highest risk, and it must be reviewed every batch
  • Recipe change (Score=20): operators cannot change the recipe (administrators only), but no other review covers it, so it is reviewed every six months
  • Test result (Score=0): the result is generated automatically and cannot be modified, and it is already reviewed in the batch report, so no additional Audit Trail review is needed

Key takeaway: the stronger the technical controls of a system, the lower the audit trail review frequency.

Analogy: Exam Anti-Cheating System

The logic of ATRA is like assessing "how many invigilators an exam hall needs":

  • A repeated/aborted test is like a student secretly retaking the exam → every session must be proctored (review every batch)
  • A recipe change is like altering the answer key → only the teacher can change it, but periodic checks are still needed (every six months)
  • A test result is like computer-graded marking that cannot be tampered with → no additional supervision needed

CDMO Practical Application: Building Standard ATRA Templates

It is recommended that a CDMO build standard ATRA templates for common equipment:

  • Filter Integrity Tester: as in the example above, focus on repeated tests and recipe changes
  • MES / Historian: focus on batch parameter modifications and changes to user privileges
  • BMS (Building Management System): focus on temperature and humidity data modifications and alarm acknowledgement records
  • Weighing systems: focus on calibration parameter modifications and Tare value changes

Templates can greatly reduce the ATRA workload for each client project.

5.3.6 Backup of Electronic Data

Electronic Data Backup: Frequency Strategy and Looped Memory Control

Original Text (English)

5.3.6 Backup of Electronic Data

The next category relates to preventive controls associated with the backup of electronic data. Two different scenarios have been identified where it is acceptable to differentiate the controls applied based on criticality.

The first scenario relates to the frequency of performing the backup of the manufacturing data stored electronically. Companies should consider developing a principle-based backup strategy in order to differentiate the frequency of backups. This strategy should take into account the company's risk tolerance, i.e., how manufacturing activities can be recreated in the event of a system failure when data has not been backed up. If the only evidence of an activity that impacts the safety and/or quality of the product is through the lost electronic data, the company must be prepared to reject the batch, and a more frequent backup may be warranted. On the other hand, if the major activities can be recreated using other means (e.g., printouts, manually recorded information), that may be used to justify a less-frequent backup of electronic data.

In addition, the volume of data being generated within a given time period and the capability of the electronic system to store that volume of data without overwriting existing data should also be considered. This situation will be further discussed in the second scenario.

If the equipment is capable of automated backups, backups are recommended at the same frequency at which data is being generated. If new data is being generated daily (e.g., using an MES or historian), a daily backup is warranted.

The second scenario is associated with looped memory. For the purposes of this technical report, looped memory is used in an electronic system with a limited storage capacity. Once the capacity limit is reached, new data automatically overwrites older data. For example, in a compression machine capable of storing 10 batches worth of data, the data for Batch 11 automatically overwrites the data for Batch 1.

Table 5.3.6-1 describes controls for systems with a looped memory. For systems storing Highly critical data, like certain makes or models of filter integrity testers used in sterile filling operations, the backup should occur before the data is overwritten. A similar expectation should be in place for systems storing Medium-critical data, such as a pH meter used on the shop floor. For systems storing Low-critical data, like secondary packaging systems that do not store data relating to quality attributes or such parameters as expiry dates, the backup should ensure that the data will not be overwritten before batch disposition has occurred.

Commentary

Core Concept: Data Backup Strategy

TR84 divides Data Backup controls into two scenarios:

  1. Backup frequency strategy: backup frequency is determined by asking "if the data were lost, could the manufacturing activity be reconstructed by other means?"
  2. Looped Memory control: for equipment with limited storage capacity where new data automatically overwrites old data

Core principle: backup frequency should be commensurate with the frequency of data generation. If a system generates new data every day (such as MES), it must be backed up daily.

Analogy: Dashcam

Looped Memory is like a dashcam's memory card:

  • Once the card is full, new footage automatically overwrites the oldest footage
  • If you have an accident but forgot to back up, the key evidence may already have been overwritten
  • High: like a taxi's dashcam, which must be backed up before the end of every shift (because it is legal evidence for a commercial operation)
  • Low: like the dashcam in a private car, where it is enough that footage is not overwritten before it is needed

Regulatory Focus: Data Loss = Batch Rejection

TR84 gives a very practical warning:

If the only evidence of an activity affecting product safety/quality is electronic data, and that data is lost because it was not backed up, the company must be prepared to reject the batch.

This is the best risk argument for a backup strategy: "the cost of not backing up = the loss of an entire scrapped batch".

CDMO Practical Application: Common Looped Memory Equipment

Common looped memory equipment in a CDMO environment:

  • Filter Integrity Tester (High): some models can store only 10-50 test results and must be backed up before being overwritten
  • pH Meter / conductivity meter (Medium): handheld instruments used on the production floor, with limited storage capacity
  • Compression machine: may store data for only a fixed number of batches
  • Automatic weighing systems: some models have limited internal memory

Recommendation: build an "equipment storage capacity list" noting each instrument's maximum number of stored batches and recommended backup interval.

5.3.7 Use of Electronic Data

Once electronic data is generated, the information may be further processed in numerous ways. In the two scenarios described in Sections 5.3.7.1 and 5.3.7.2, the controls to be applied may be differentiated based on criticality: when electronic data is exported to generate reports or records, and when data is transferred or migrated between electronic systems. Commentary

5.3

5.3.7.1 Exporting Electronic Data to Generate Reports and Records

Data Export and Report Generation: Validation, Change Control and Template Management

Original Text (English)

5.3.7.1 Exporting Electronic Data to Generate Reports and Records

Table 5.3.7.1-1🔍 identifies prevention controls related to reports and records generation. For reports and records used to display High-critical data, such as a batch record from an MES used for making release decisions, validation with change control and appropriate test plans is required for changes to the report template. The validation should ensure that the report or record is accurate, and that all data displayed will remain unchanged from the electronic raw data. IT or automation personnel should perform these changes to the template and ensure that the content of the reports and records are static and cannot be altered in any way.

For reports and records related to Medium-critical data, such as a building management system monitoring temperature and humidity, IT or automation personnel should perform an initial validation. Any changes to the report template should be performed following SOPs, but revalidation of the template would not be required. That the report contains limited flexibility (via filters or drop downs) and is working as intended is acceptable, so the user may run these reports based on a predefined set of filters or criteria.

For reports and records displaying Low-critical data, verification governed by work instructions should suffice. The business unit creates these reports and records, and their content may be flexible in nature, for example, exporting data into a data lake for future non-GMP analysis.

Regardless of the criticality, the quality unit should review and approve changes to report templates, especially for any changes that could impact the information displayed.

Commentary

Core Concept: Three-Level Differences in Report Template Control

TR84 tiers the control of report/record generation along four aspects:

  • Validation level: High requires full validation + revalidation after changes; Medium requires only initial validation; Low requires only verification
  • Change control: High requires Change Control + Test Plan; Medium requires procedural control; Low requires only a work instruction
  • Modification authority: for High and Medium, changes are made by IT/Automation; for Low, they can be made by the business unit itself
  • Content flexibility: High is static and fixed; Medium has limited flexibility (drop-down menus); Low can be adjusted flexibly

Analogy: Managing Excel Report Templates

Imagine your site has three types of Excel report templates:

  • High (batch release report): like a bank statement template → IT locks the format, and any modification requires full validation + change control
  • Medium (environmental monitoring report): like a preset monthly report template → fixed filter criteria, with users able to choose the date range
  • Low (trend analysis export): like a Pivot Table used for analysis → users can freely adjust fields and filters

Regulatory Focus: Quality Unit Oversight Across All Levels

TR84 stresses an important principle: regardless of data criticality level, the Quality Unit should review and approve changes to report templates, especially changes that could affect how information is presented.

This means that even for a Low-level report, if a template change alters the information displayed, the quality unit must still be involved.

CDMO Practical Application: Managing Report Templates for Multiple Clients

Report management challenges faced by a CDMO:

  • Client-specific report formats: different clients may require different batch report formats, and each format must be validated independently
  • Template version control: one system may hold report templates for 5-10 clients, multiplying the complexity of version control
  • Raw Data immutability principle: modifying a report template affects only presentation; the underlying raw data must not be changed by a template change

5.3.7.2 Data Transfer and Migration Between Electronic Systems

Electronic Data Transfer and Migration Between Systems: Ensuring Complete Data Transfer

Original Text (English)

5.3.7.2 Data Transfer and Migration Between Electronic Systems

The technical report team also identified different levels of control that may be applicable for the transfer or migration of electronic data based on criticality, as illustrated in Table 5.3.7.2-1.

For High- or Medium-critical data transfers and migrations, the transfer of the electronic data should be prepared using full change control, with a test plan in place to ensure that all data is transferred. It also is advisable to use a verification tool, such as a checksum, to confirm that all data sent was received. Two examples of this type of transfer are presented in this section.

One example is the migration of data from one platform to another as part of a software or hardware upgrade, for example, when moving from one software version to another, moving from an old server to a new server, or moving to the cloud is one type of transfer. This is generally considered a once-off activity specifically associated with the upgrade.

A second example is the transfer of High- and Medium-critical batch data (e.g., CPPs, calibration records, environmental monitoring data) from a standalone system to an upper-level system like an MES or data historian. This type of data transfer occurs on a defined periodic basis (e.g., once a day, once a week, after each batch) based on how the data transfer has been set up. For both examples, the transfer or migration of the data should be safeguarded by change control, a test plan, and a tool to verify that all the data have been transferred or migrated.

Data of varying criticalities are regularly transferred into different formats for making continuous process verification conclusions, for example, transferring data into a data lake for further trending or analysis. These are considered Low-critical transfers, so a procedural control is adequate for describing the steps to be taken to transfer the data.

Commentary

Core Concept: Data Transfer vs. Data Migration

TR84 discusses two electronic data transfer scenarios:

  • Data Migration: a one-time platform change, such as a software upgrade, server replacement or move to the cloud. It is usually accompanied by CSV (computerized system validation) activities.
  • Data Transfer: periodic sending of data, such as a standalone instrument regularly uploading batch data to the MES or Historian.

Common control requirements for both (High/Medium): Change Control + Test Plan + Checksum verification

Key Term: Checksum

A Checksum is a data integrity verification tool that uses a mathematical algorithm to calculate a fixed-length code. If the Checksum is the same before and after transfer, the data is complete and intact.

Common types include MD5 and SHA-256. In a GMP environment, a Checksum is the technical means of confirming that "all data sent has been received".

Analogy: The Inventory List When Moving House

Data transfer is like moving house:

  • High/Medium (moving important items): make a list before the move (Test Plan), sign a contract with the moving company (Change Control), and check items one by one against the list at the new home (Checksum)
  • Low (moving clutter to a storage room): simply follow the steps (Procedural Control), with no need to check item by item

Regulatory Focus: Consistency of High and Medium Controls

Notably, for data transfer/migration controls, the requirements for High and Medium are identical. This reflects TR84's position that even data that only indirectly affects product quality (Medium) must not be lost or altered when transferred between systems.

Only the Low level (such as transfer to a Data Lake for trend analysis) may use simplified procedural control.

CDMO Practical Application: Common Data Transfer Scenarios

Common data transfer/migration scenarios in a CDMO:

  • Filter Integrity Tester → MES (High): each batch's test data is uploaded to the MES and requires Checksum verification
  • EM system → LIMS (Medium): environmental monitoring data is transferred to the LIMS periodically and requires Change Control
  • MES upgrade (High): migration from the old MES version to the new one is a one-time activity and requires a full validation plan
  • Process data → Data Lake (Low): used for CPV (continued process verification) analysis; procedural control is sufficient

Special note: when upgrading systems, a CDMO must confirm that the historical batch data of all clients has been completely migrated.

Controls for Big Data as it Relates to Data Integrity

Big Data Management and Data Integrity | p38

Original Text (English)

Pharmaceutical manufacturing data is being collected at an accelerated rate, beyond human processing capability using traditional mechanisms. These challenges increasingly drive the need for governance and active controls of data and information. Processes, roles, and standards must be created to allow the effective and efficient use of information and technology tools to enable an organization to achieve its goals.

Collectively, these two technical reports will provide guidance on the management of data and big data in a regulated pharmaceutical or biopharmaceutical facility.

Commentary and Application

Core Concept: What Is the Big Data Challenge?

TR84 Section 6.0 is the forward-looking point of the whole document. Here PDA acknowledges that the traditional data integrity control framework (manual review, paper audit trails, second-person verification) cannot cope with the volume of data generated once pharmaceutical manufacturing goes digital.

Typical sources of big data in pharmaceutical manufacturing include:

  • Continuous monitoring by Internet of Things (IoT) sensors (temperature, pressure, humidity, particle counts)
  • Process parameter records generated every second by continuous manufacturing equipment
  • Spectral and near-infrared data streams from Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT)
  • Long-term trending data from the environmental monitoring system (EMS)

Analogy: What Happens When Traditional DI Methods Meet Big Data?

Imagine your warehouse handles 100 records a day, and a QC reviewer can check each one. Now the same warehouse is fitted with IoT sensors that automatically generate 2,000 temperature readings every minute, or 2.88 million a day.

Asking QA staff to "manually review the audit trail record by record" is like asking one person to read a 10,000-page book in 8 hours. It is physically impossible, yet the regulatory expectation does not go away.

This is why big data needs a new governance framework, not simply "more people".

The Regulatory Gap: The Limits of 21 CFR Part 11 in Its Era

21 CFR Part 11 was issued in 1997 and EU Annex 11 was last revised in 2011. Both predate the widespread use of IoT, continuous manufacturing and large-scale spectral data.

These regulations set clear requirements for "audit trails of electronic records" and "data backup", but give almost no practical guidance on how to manage data streams of thousands of data points per second.

TR84's candid approach: it states this gap openly and announces that "subsequent PDA technical reports will provide further guidance", rather than falsely stretching the existing framework to fit.

Core Big Data Governance Topics for Subsequent PDA Technical Reports

  • Data Classification: Which big data is GMP-critical, and which is for monitoring or trending purposes?
  • Data Ownership: Who is ultimately accountable for data integrity?
  • Access Management: How should the permission architecture of a large-scale data lake be designed?
  • Source Data: How are raw sensor readings protected from tampering?
  • Change Management: How are data continuity and compliance maintained when a big data system is upgraded?

CDMO Scenario: Is Your Site Ready?

Take a typical sterile filling CDMO and assess big data readiness:

  • Isolator monitoring data: Hydrogen peroxide concentration, differential pressure and particle counts are recorded every minute, generating about 50,000 data points during a batch. How does the current QA review process handle this?
  • Lyophilizer process data: Temperature, vacuum and freezing rate are sampled every 30 seconds, and a 5-day lyophilization cycle generates about 14,400 data points. Who reviews them, and what is reviewed?
  • Automated Alarm Management: Can the current EBR system be configured for Exception-Based Reporting, so that QA reviews only the points that fall outside the trend?

Questions for Reflection

The text of TR84 Section 6.0 has only three paragraphs, but its significance is considerable. Consider the following questions:

  1. Why did PDA choose to give "big data management" its own section in a data integrity technical report rather than fold it into the existing control framework?
  2. Why is "data ownership" especially important in a big data environment that spans systems and departments? Use a CDMO's process development (PD) and QA departments as an example.
  3. If an inspector asks, "How do you ensure the integrity of IoT sensor data?", what elements should your standard answer include?

Reference 8: ICH Q9 - The Theoretical Basis of TR84's Risk Analysis Framework

ICH Q9 (Quality Risk Management) is the foundation of TR84's whole "9-box vulnerability grid" methodology. TR84's core contribution is to apply the risk management principles of ICH Q9 specifically to data integrity:

  • Risk control → select appropriate DI controls
  • Risk review → periodically reassess the vulnerability status

  • The deviation management process requires complete, contemporaneous and attributable records

Core Scenario

Commentary

The Core DI Problem: The Broken Chain of Manual Transcription

Problem in 1a: The operator reads a value from the instrument panel and writes it by hand into the paper batch production record (BPR). This manual transcription step is the biggest data integrity weakness: values can be copied incorrectly, filled in after the fact, or even deliberately falsified, with no way to trace back to the original source.

Improvement in 1b: With a PLC (programmable logic controller) added, the equipment itself holds an electronic record, but the data still has to be copied manually from the HMI (human-machine interface) to the paper BPR. The problem is reduced but not eliminated, and if the PLC has no audit trail configured, the electronic data can still be overwritten.

Best case, 1c: The EBR (electronic batch record) is integrated directly with the PLC and data is written automatically, reducing human intervention to a residual level. The audit trail is complete and every change is recorded.

Analogy: Three Ways to Record a Thermometer Reading

Imagine a ward nurse taking a patient's temperature every hour:

  • 1a: Reads a mercury thermometer and writes the value on paper from memory an hour later.
  • 1b: Uses an electronic thermometer that displays the value, but still copies it by hand into the nursing record.
  • 1c: The electronic thermometer transmits the value over Bluetooth straight into the electronic medical record, and the nurse simply confirms it.

The pharmaceutical industry expects the reliability of 1c, especially when that "temperature" bears directly on patient medication safety (= the product CQA).

Audit Alert: The Common Trap in 1b

Many CDMOs are at the 1b stage: they have a PLC but have not configured the audit trail, or the audit trail function has been switched off "to save storage space". This is one of the most frequent deficiencies in FDA 483 observations. Key reminder: a PLC being capable of recording does not mean it is DI compliant. The audit trail must be enabled, validated, and the records must be protected from deletion.

The Logic of Medium Criticality

The vulnerability of the data remains unchanged at a Negligible level (Blue). In this example, significantly more controls are in place than are required.

Determining Low Criticality: A Wide Tolerable Humidity Range

The drug is non-hygroscopic and stable over a wide humidity range, so humidity affects neither a CQA nor critical process consistency, and the data is low criticality routine GMP data. This is the typical scenario for the bottom row of the 9-Box.

Takeaway for CDMOs: Documenting this kind of stability data clearly in early drug development gives the DI risk assessment a scientific basis rather than relying on intuition.

The regulatory implication is that if an auditor finds you have invested heavily in low-risk data while under-controlling high-risk data, that in itself signals a lapse in risk management judgment.

8.3 Sterility Assurance Examples

The following examples demonstrate how High-, Medium-, and Low-criticality data from an aseptic filling process is mapped in the 9-Box vulnerability grid. Sterility is achieved through filtration at the point of fill. The sterility assurance at the point of fill impacts a CQA of the final product. Integrity of the sterilizing filter is a CPP that is verified before use and confirmed after use by filter integrity testing (Figure 8.3-1).

According to the FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing, "Integrity testing of the filter(s) can be performed prior to processing and should be routinely performed post-use" (1). Although filter integrity testing can be performed to verify filter identity before sterilizing filtration, there is no regulatory requirement in the U.S. to perform such a test. In the EU, the regulatory requirements may differ, as Annex 1 states that "The integrity of the sterilised filter should be verified before use and should be confirmed immediately after use by an appropriate method…." (2). These examples are intended to clarify the application of the 9-Box grid; care should be taken to conform to local regulatory requirements.

Examples 7–9 demonstrate data integrity risk levels for filter integrity testing with three potential scenarios.

In Example 7, the data is considered to be of High criticality because the filter integrity testing impacts CQA (sterility) and it is a CPP that confirms the absence of viable microorganisms after aseptic filling, which is a regulatory expectation.

In Example 8, the data is considered Medium criticality under U.S. regulation because the test is a regulatory recommendation intended to verify filter integrity after sterilizing filtration but before aseptic filling.

In Example 9, the data is considered Low criticality because the test is not a regulatory requirement but is intended to verify filter identity to ensure correct filter installation.

Commentary

Core framework: why is filter integrity testing a high-risk area for DI?

The sterilizing filter is the last line of defense for sterility assurance. If the data from filter integrity testing are falsified or concealed, a defective filter could allow microbial contamination into the final product, and the patient bears the risk directly.

TR84 uses three parallel cases (Ex7 / Ex8 / Ex9) to show that the same test activity carries different data criticality depending on its purpose. This is one of the most elegant demonstrations of the 9-Box framework.

  • Ex7 (post-use): confirms the filter was not compromised after aseptic filling → affects CQAs → high criticality
  • Ex8 (pre-use, post-sterilization): confirms the filter is intact before aseptic filling → recommended rather than required by regulators (US) → medium criticality
  • Ex9 (pre-use, pre-sterilization): confirms the correct filter type has been installed → not a regulatory requirement → low criticality

Analogy: the tire check after an aircraft lands vs. the check before takeoff

Post-use integrity testing (Ex7) is like the mandatory tire inspection after landing to see whether a tire has burst: it is the final proof that the whole flight was safe, it is required by regulation, and its data criticality is the highest. Pre-use testing (Ex8) is like re-checking tire pressure before takeoff: good practice, but not mandatory in every region. Installation confirmation (Ex9) is like checking that the tire model matches the catalog: it is identity verification and has the lowest criticality.

Regulatory difference to keep in mind

The US FDA requires only post-use testing, whereas EU Annex 1 requires filter integrity to be confirmed both before and after use. Ex8 may therefore rise to high criticality at an EU site. A CDMO serving both FDA and EMA clients must design its data controls to the most stringent requirement.

Applying this to your CDMO

Take an inventory of every filter integrity tester on site: which are legacy units (paper printout only), and which already have electronic data storage and audit trails? Under the framework in this section, legacy equipment combined with a high-criticality operation (post-use testing) is the first priority for upgrade.

Commentary

The DI problem and the evolution of controls: from paper to end-to-end automation

Example 7 is the central demonstration of this section: for the same high-criticality operation, the vulnerability rating falls from Significant to Acceptable as the level of technology increases.

  • 7a (paper printout): the operator manually transcribes setup data + the legacy instrument has no electronic storage capability → two high-vulnerability factors stack up → Significant. This is the current state in many older sites.
  • 7b (automated data capture): a barcode reader populates the setup data automatically + a secure storage module → human error is reduced to a residual level → Moderate.
  • 7c (fully automated): the filter is tested in-line without the operator manually initiating the test + results are transferred automatically to secure storage → human intervention is minimized → Acceptable.

Audit focus: the three major DI risks of 7a

  • Manual transcription errors: operators copy setup values by hand into the record sheet, where numbers are easily mis-recorded or deliberately altered.
  • Paper printouts are easy to falsify: the printout from a legacy instrument can be swapped, and without a timestamp it is even harder to verify originality.
  • Reliance on peer review alone: after-the-fact supervisory review can detect only obvious anomalies, not a deliberately fabricated passing result.

A common FDA 483 observation: "Filter integrity test records lack traceability to the original instrument output."

Analogy: a handwritten driving log vs. automatic GPS recording

7a is like requiring a driver to write down speed and mileage by hand every hour: the record can be faked or left incomplete. 7b is like a trip computer that records automatically while the driver still controls the start. 7c is like GPS tracking the entire trip automatically: unless the equipment itself fails, the data cannot be tampered with. For a high-risk operation such as aseptic filling, only 7c-level controls fully reassure an auditor.

Question to consider

Is the filter integrity tester at your site at the 7a, 7b or 7c level? If it is 7a, can the supervisor's after-the-fact review really prevent an operator from falsifying a passing result? If not, what is the next improvement action?

CDMO improvement path

Short term (no new equipment): make sure the paper printout is attached to the batch record and overlaid with the operator's signature to prevent substitution. Medium term: upgrade to a filter tester with an electronic audit trail (7b level). Long term: evaluate in-line integration (7c), especially for high-throughput or high-batch-frequency product lines.

How does medium criticality shift the starting point of the vulnerability rating?

Compared with Ex7, the data criticality of Ex8 drops from high to medium. This shifts the whole vulnerability assessment down by one level:

Highlight of 8c: redundant sterilizing filters

Mfg. Operation: Planning — Creation of the process order in a validated electronic system (such as SAP). Once created, the batch-specific data in the validated electronic system (batch number, material number, etc.) is automatically added to the master batch record template and printed for use in production.

Data Criticality: Medium — Ensures unique batch number assigned, and correct master templates are used.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Procedure allows, if the validated electronic system is inaccessible, to print the master template and add a batch number manually, potentially adding an incorrect batch number.
GMP Process: Process robustness assured by validation.
Data Mgmt: Limited risks as process and transfer of batch specific information to the master batch record is controlled by a validated system.

Controls in Place: High
Human Factor: Documentation team prints the batch record only after the process order is created, allowing automatic transfer of batch number.
GMP Process: The electronic system and associated automatic transfer of batch number to an approved master manufacturing template is validated.
Data Mgmt: Reliance on validation and user access to the validated electronic system and documentation control system.

Vulnerability Level: Negligible

10a: why does the planning stage reach Negligible?

A validated electronic system (such as SAP) transfers the batch number automatically into the master batch record template, reducing the chance of manually entering the batch number to a very low level. Only one exception scenario remains as a key DI vulnerability: manual entry of the batch number is allowed when the system cannot be accessed. As long as the SOP strictly limits the conditions for using this exception and a record is kept, the overall vulnerability stays at Negligible.

The exception process is a DI gap

"Manual operation is allowed when the system is inaccessible" is a standard fallback clause in many companies' SOPs, but if this exception is not controlled, it becomes a focus item in DI audits. Recommendation: require supervisor authorization for use of the exception, keep a retrospective record, and include it in deviation management.

10b — Dispensing

Mfg. Operation: Dispensing — A spreadsheet is printed from the validated electronic system process order and given to the dispensing team detailing what to dispense and what quantity; materials are prepared and dispensed, and quantities are manually recorded directly into a master batch record.

Data Criticality: High — Need to ensure correct materials and batch numbers are selected and used; potential for product mix-up or packaging material mix-up, which can impact product quality or patient safety.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Single operator records by manual entry into the master manufacturing record actual quantities of dispensed materials.
GMP Process: Dispensing is a completely manual process; barcodes or labels are not used; no second format to confirm the quantity dispensed.
Data Mgmt: Data in the spreadsheet can be easily modified before printing, creating errors during dispensing process.

Controls in Place: Low
Human Factor: Supervisor review only at end of packaging.
GMP Process: Controlled with written procedures and training.
Data Mgmt: Reliance on converting data in validated electronic system to a spreadsheet, which is printed and given to the dispensing team.

Vulnerability Level: Significant

10b: spreadsheet-to-paper is a high-risk DI path

This is one of the most practically instructive cases in TR84. The problem is not the spreadsheet itself, but that the middle of the data chain "export from the validated system → modify the spreadsheet → print → hand to the operator" is completely untraceable:
1. Who modified the spreadsheet? When?
2. Is the printed version identical to the original system version?
3. Did the operator make errors when copying by hand into the batch record?
If any one of these links fails, the integrity of the high-criticality dispensing data is already lost.

Analogy: a hospital pharmacist copying prescriptions by hand

If an electronic prescribing system exists but the pharmacist still has to copy the prescription onto paper before compounding, this "hand copying" step is the center of the DI risk. Modern hospitals have long connected electronic prescribing directly to dispensing machines, and packaging dispensing should follow the same direct-connection path.

CDMO improvement direction

Fastest improvement: eliminate the intermediate spreadsheet step and print dispensing slips with unique serial numbers (which cannot be modified) directly from the ERP/MES. Medium term: introduce barcode scanning to confirm material identity + electronic balances that write weights back to the system automatically, eliminating manual recording.

10c — Line Clearance

Mfg. Operation: Line Clearance — Performed before dispensed materials are brought to the packaging line; documented directly into master manufacturing template; sensors installed in hard-to-clean parts of the equipment.

Data Criticality: High — If not performed robustly, packaging components or finished product from the previous run may be mixed into the new packaging order.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Reliance on manual confirmation that line clearance was performed correctly; chance of both operators missing the difficult-to-clean parts of the equipment, but sensors installed in these areas reduce the risk.
GMP Process: Sensors in place only in parts of the line; reliance on human confirmation of line clearance in other parts.
Data Mgmt: Limited risks as process is controlled by validated systems; user access is well controlled.

Controls in Place: High
Human Factor: Real-time four-eyes check performed by certified peer; quality unit review at release.
GMP Process: Controlled with written procedures, training of first operator, and certification of a peer performing four-eyes review.
Data Mgmt: Reliance on validation and user access to the validated electronic system, documentation control system, and sensors.

Vulnerability Level: Acceptable

10c: the DI value of the four-eyes check

Line clearance is a high-criticality operation, but with real-time two-person verification (the four-eyes principle) + sensors installed in hard-to-clean areas, even a method that relies mainly on manual checks can reach an Acceptable vulnerability rating.

The key is the "certified peer": the colleague who performs the four-eyes check must be certified, not assigned arbitrarily. This greatly increases the reliability of a manual control.

Common DI problems in line clearance records

  • Completing the line clearance record before or after the activity (the "pre-filling" problem)
  • Hard-to-clean dead spots not included in the line clearance checklist
  • Sensor alarms cleared by the operator without being recorded
  • The four-eyes check is a formality, and the second signer does not actually perform the check

Question to consider

Are the line clearance verifiers at your site formally certified? Does certification include a practical assessment, or only paper-based training? If verifiers are not certified, the DI control effectiveness of the four-eyes principle is greatly weakened.

10d — Equipment Setup

Mfg. Operation: Equipment Setup — Recipe (for CPPs) / tolerance ranges (balances, cameras, sensors) and other adjustable settings are configured; packaging equipment uses group accounts for operators, who have access to choose any recipe, and edit set-up parameters.

Data Criticality: High — Incorrect settings could potentially result in impact to product quality or patient safety.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Operator could select an incorrect recipe, impacting CPPs, or change tolerance ranges outside of required range to ensure higher pass rate (fewer rejects); possible to change CPPs outside of the validated range.
GMP Process: Set-up is manually controlled by the operator; no double-check before starting packaging.
Data Mgmt: Operators use group accounts to make the adjustments and are able to change set-up parameters.

Controls in Place: Low
Human Factor: Peer and quality unit review of batch report to confirm the correct recipes and tolerance ranges were used.
GMP Process: Controlled with written procedures and training.
Data Mgmt: Different user groups for operator, maintenance, and administrator; batch report automatically records set-up parameters/recipe used.

Vulnerability Level: Significant

10d: group accounts are a major DI loophole

This is one of the most direct DI warnings in TR84. Group accounts let several operators share one login identity, which completely violates the DI principle of traceability: when a setting is changed, the system cannot identify which individual operator did it.

A more serious problem: operators can widen tolerance ranges (such as the acceptance thresholds for visual inspection) to lower the rejection rate. This is a data falsification incentive that directly affects product quality.

Analogy: a shared access card

If the whole production department shares one access card and equipment in a room is later found to have been tampered with, there is no way to tell who went in. That is exactly the problem with group accounts in a DI audit. "Individual accounts" are not a convenience choice for the IT department; they are a basic GMP requirement.

CDMO priority improvement items

Immediate action: audit the account setup of all packaging equipment and confirm that every operator has an individual account. Medium term: add a supervisor authorization step before changes to CPP-related settings (recipe selection, tolerance ranges), and record the authorizer's information in the audit trail.

10e — Packaging Operation

Mfg. Operation: Packaging — Bulk tablets are packed in blisters, added to the carton together with the patient information leaflet, before being bundled.

Data Criticality: Medium — Once the set-up is completed correctly, potential to impact product quality is reduced.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Operator can add rejects back to packaging process or adjust the tolerance ranges during processing to reduce the rejects.
GMP Process: Operator controls the packaging process manually and can edit the CPP setpoint and sensor tolerance ranges; no double-check during processing of any edits made.
Data Mgmt: Operators use group accounts to make setting adjustments.

Controls in Place: Low
Human Factor: Peer and quality unit review of batch report to confirm the correct recipes and tolerance ranges were used.
GMP Process: Controlled with written procedures and training.
Data Mgmt: Different user groups; audit trail automatically records all changes to setpoints or tolerance ranges and includes in the batch report.

Vulnerability Level: Moderate

10e: the audit trail moves Significant down to Moderate

The equipment setup issues of 10e (group accounts, adjustable tolerance ranges) are similar to those of 10d, but the vulnerability rating falls from Significant to Moderate, because the audit trail automatically records all changes to setpoints and tolerance ranges and includes them in the batch report.

This means that although operators can still change settings, every change leaves a complete record that can be traced during later quality review. This is exactly the DI control value of the audit trail.

The DI problem of "adding rejects back"

An operator putting rejects back onto the packaging line is one of the most serious DI violations. It is not only a GMP violation but an act that directly endangers patient safety. TR84 reminds us that the system design should physically prevent rejects from re-entering the line, rather than relying only on an SOP prohibition.

Question to consider

Does your packaging equipment have an audit trail function? Does the audit trail cover all adjustable CPP setpoints and sensor tolerance ranges? When did you last verify the integrity of the audit trail?

10f — In-Process Control Testing

Mfg. Operation: In-Process Control (IPC) testing — performed by trained personnel; periodicity of IPC testing is part of batch record, with space to record all IPC results and attach all equipment printouts.

Data Criticality: Medium — IPC tests are used to adjust during the packaging process, but all parameters are also part of release testing.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Recording can be done without performing the activity, but missing equipment printout will show testing never took place.
GMP Process: Test results are transcribed into the batch record, and all printouts are manually added; possible transcription errors.
Data Mgmt: Testing equipment allows reprinting of previous tests; printout does not have the batch-specific information or the user's name; only date, time and result are captured; equipment does not store the data electronically.

Controls in Place: Medium
Human Factor: Double verification by a peer for each new IPC test; quality unit review that a new test was performed according to periodicity defined in the batch record.
GMP Process: Controlled with written procedures and training; operator adds equipment printout to the batch record, overlaying their signature.
Data Mgmt: Testing equipment generates a printout, with time and date of the test performed; operators do not have access to change the time or date on the equipment—reserved for administrators.

Vulnerability Level: Acceptable

10f: how do you control equipment that can "reprint"?

IPC test equipment that allows old test results to be reprinted is itself a DI risk: an operator might substitute an old printout for the current test result. The control: the operator's signature is overlaid on the printout (the signature crosses the edge of the paper and the batch record page), making any substitution visible.

Key safeguard: operators cannot change the time and date on the equipment (this privilege is reserved for administrators), so the timestamp on the printout becomes trustworthy raw data.

"Printouts without batch information" are a DI weakness

If a printout shows only the time, date and result, without the batch number, product name or operator ID, then in theory it could be used for any batch. This is a common problem with many older IPC instruments. We recommend also handwriting the batch-related information when attaching the printout, or upgrading to equipment that prints full batch information.

Practical application for a CDMO

Audit your IPC equipment: which units allow reprinting? Is there access control over the time and date settings? Do printouts carry enough batch identification information? For equipment that can only reprint and has no electronic record, establish clear rules for attachment and signature, and describe the anti-falsification measures in the SOP.

10g — Batch Reconciliation

Mfg. Operation: Batch Reconciliation — Batch yield is calculated, along with reconciliation of unused packaging materials.

Data Criticality: High — If not performed correctly, potential to impact patient safety.

Vulnerability Factors:
Human Factor: Operators can stop the batch early, or add rejected materials back into the line, impacting the reconciliation process.
GMP Process: Batch yield is calculated using counters (pass/reject information), that are part of the batch report; reconciliation of packaging components is completely manual process.
Data Mgmt: Operators use logbooks to record the use of packaging components during the batch; possible to miscalculate the quantity of packaging materials used.

Controls in Place: Medium
Human Factor: Double verification by a peer for each packaging component used, and quality unit review of reconciliation process.
GMP Process: Controlled with written procedures and training; operators need to complete a logbook each time a new package component is required.
Data Mgmt: Equipment automatically generates a report at the end of packaging; report template and data are locked and no one can edit the contents.

Vulnerability Level: Moderate

10g: the DI risk map of batch reconciliation

Batch reconciliation is a high-criticality operation (it affects patient safety), but its vulnerability rating is Moderate rather than Significant, mainly thanks to:
1. The equipment generates the end-of-batch report automatically
2. The report template and data are locked, so no one can modify the content
These two controls effectively limit the possibility of after-the-fact data tampering.

The remaining vulnerability is that reconciliation of packaging materials is entirely manual (a handwritten log), and in theory an operator could end a batch early or put rejects back on the line. These risks are currently controlled only by peer double verification.

Analogy: a bank teller's end-of-day reconciliation

Batch reconciliation is like a bank teller counting cash before closing: if tellers are allowed to keep their own books with no automatic system reconciliation, falsification is easy. The improvement direction for 10g (replacing the handwritten log with barcode scanning) is like a bank upgrading to an ATM that counts notes automatically, no longer relying on counting by hand.

Question to consider

Does your site still use a handwritten log to record packaging material usage in batch reconciliation? If so, is the timing of entries (real time vs. back-filled at the end of the batch) monitored? Back-filled records are a high-frequency check point in DI audits.

CDMO improvement recommendation

Upgrade the packaging material log from handwriting to barcode scanning + electronic system records, automatically recording the timestamp and operator ID each time a new lot of packaging material is drawn. This improvement can lower 10g from Moderate to Acceptable, while greatly reducing reconciliation calculation errors.

Summary

Section 6b Summary

Core insights and action guidance for CDMOs

Key insight 1: data criticality determines the minimum acceptable level of control

Ex7–9 clearly demonstrate that the same paper recording method leads to a Significant vulnerability in a high-criticality scenario (Ex7a) but only an Acceptable vulnerability in a low-criticality scenario (Ex9a). This tells us that DI control is not "the stricter the better" but must be commensurate with data criticality. Over-controlling low-criticality data wastes resources; under-controlling high-criticality data is a compliance risk.

Key insight 2: automation is the most effective systematic tool for reducing DI vulnerability

Across all nine aseptic filling cases (Ex7–9), the path from paper (a) to automated (b) to fully automated (c) steadily lowers the vulnerability rating by one to two levels. This is no coincidence: automation addresses three vulnerability factors at once by reducing human intervention, ensuring complete data transfer and creating tamper-proof storage records.

Key insight 3: DI risk in the packaging process is concentrated at "conversion nodes"

The Significant vulnerabilities in Ex10 (10b dispensing, 10d equipment setup) both occur at nodes where data are "converted" from one system to another form: printing a spreadsheet, and changing settings through a group account. Eliminating these conversion nodes (direct system connection, individual accounts, audit trails) is the priority strategy for packaging DI control.

CDMO top-priority action list

  1. Immediate: audit all filter integrity testers, confirm that post-use testing (high criticality) has an electronic audit trail, and that the way paper printouts are attached meets the anti-substitution requirement.
  2. Immediate: eliminate the intermediate spreadsheet step in the packaging dispensing process, or at least establish spreadsheet version control and a lock-after-print mechanism.
  3. Short term: make sure every packaging equipment operator has an individual account, and remove or strictly limit the use of group accounts.
  4. Medium term: introduce a barcode scanning system for packaging material reconciliation to replace the handwritten log.
  5. Medium term: evaluate the differing filter integrity testing requirements of the markets you serve (FDA / EMA) and specify them explicitly in the Technical Agreement.

Summary of the core analogies in this section

The ten cases in TR84 Section 6b are like a "DI map of the pharmaceutical process": it marks which stretches are steep high-altitude mountain roads (high criticality + high vulnerability) and which are gentle lowlands (low criticality + low vulnerability). A smart CDMO does not turn the whole road into an eight-lane expressway; it builds guardrails on the mountain road, puts barriers at the cliff edge, and keeps ordinary road standards on flat ground, so that the whole journey is kept safe with the most efficient allocation of resources.
