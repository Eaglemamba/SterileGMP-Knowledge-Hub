TR86 was created to fill a specific gap: for industry use of complex packaging systems (such as prefilled syringes and large-volume bulk packaging), the existing documents (USP <1207> and the older TR27) no longer provide adequate guidance. Introduction — Historical Context

From TR27 to TR86: Why a new technical report was needed

1.0 Introduction (Part 1) — The TR27 Era and Its Successor

For more than a decade, PDA Technical Report No. 27: Pharmaceutical Package Integrity served as an influential reference document, representing the best practices in sterile product package integrity testing. At the time it was published, the information it presented was instrumental in shaping the revision of USP General Chapter <1207> Package Integrity and Test Method Selection.

As a result, the content of USP <1207> was expanded in 2016 and currently serves as the primary reference chapter focusing on the basics of package integrity and best practices in sterile product package integrity evaluation and integrity testing.

"The intent of USP <1207> is to guide package integrity development away from testing each sample at the smallest possible defect level, because quality cannot be tested into a product."

Rather, using a risk-based approach and leveraging testing to better understand, analyze, and eliminate the risks during development phases is encouraged. Use of the appropriate testing during each manufacturing phase can help in evaluating and mitigating residual risks (i.e., in production/stability to proactively address risks/failure modes). Where feasible, regulations and quality risk management principles identified in ICH Quality Guideline Q9: Quality Risk Management should be used.

Subsequently, the PDA Package Integrity Technical Report Team reexamined the role of TR-27 and determined that a new technical report should be written, building on the package integrity basics updated by USP <1207> and focusing more on advanced issues related to pharmaceutical package integrity. They researched the existing sources of information related to the challenges encountered with complex packaging systems, such as large-volume bulk packages and prefilled syringe systems. Currently, no single source is available that thoroughly addresses these issues.

Likewise, the Technical Report Team recognized the increasing number of innovative methods using existing technologies for integrity testing that deserve consideration for their potential utility in the future of pharmaceutical package integrity.

Commentary: The Evolution from TR27 to TR86

TR27 (1998) vs TR86 (2021): Core Differences

TR27 was published in 1998 and served the pharmaceutical industry for more than 20 years. Its main contribution was to establish the foundational framework of "best practices for package integrity testing", and it directly drove the major expansion of USP <1207> in 2016.

However, with the spread of complex packaging systems (PFS, dual-chamber cartridges, large-volume bulk containers), the scope of TR27's guidance is no longer sufficient. TR86 was created precisely to fill this gap.

Analogy: An Old Map and Modern Satellite Navigation

TR27 is like a paper map from 20 years ago: very useful in flat city areas, but it fails on newly built roads in the mountains. TR86 is satellite navigation that integrates the latest road information: it still builds on the foundational knowledge of the old map, but can handle complex terrain (complex packaging systems) and new roads (innovative test methods).

Core Dogma: "Quality Cannot Be Tested into a Product"

USP <1207> states explicitly that the goal should not be to "test every sample at the smallest possible defect size", because quality cannot be tested into a product.

This principle echoes directly the QbD (quality by design) concept of ICH Q10:

  • In the development phase: use testing to understand, analyze and eliminate risk
  • In the production/stability phase: use testing to monitor residual risk and respond proactively to failure modes

This does not mean testing is unimportant; it means the purpose of testing is to understand risk, not merely to screen out defective units.

Why Is There No "Single Comprehensive Source"?

The TR86 drafting team states explicitly: "No single source currently addresses the integrity of complex packaging systems comprehensively". This reveals the state of the industry: the challenges of complex packaging are scattered across different technical publications, and the contribution of TR86 is to consolidate this knowledge into one consensus document.

Introduction — Lifecycle Approach & Three Content Areas

What TR86 covers and how it structures the package integrity lifecycle

1.0 Introduction (Part 2) — TR86 Scope & Lifecycle

This technical report is a consensus-based resource surrounding the challenges encountered in using complex package systems and introduces important elements to consider in decision-making. It also offers an examination of the technologies available for package integrity testing not yet established by peer-reviewed research.

Although pharmaceutical container closure systems serve a variety of functions, the assurance of package integrity is primarily concerned with product containment. However, these systems also serve as an effective barrier against:

  • Ingress of microorganisms
  • Ingress of potentially reactive gases such as oxygen or water vapor
  • Maintenance of a vacuum (where applicable)

Package integrity is the measure of the ability of the package to prevent product loss or maintain product sterility and the ability to maintain the internal environment (e.g., loss of vacuum seal). Package integrity testing is an ongoing process that starts in package development and continues throughout the life of the product.

When designing a container closure system and selecting and implementing an appropriate method, the goal is to reduce the risk to product sterility and quality.

The report addresses container closure integrity in three main areas:

  1. Challenges with complex systems and package integrity methods
  2. Innovative methods for integrity testing using existing technologies
  3. Additional considerations for pharmaceutical package integrity

Additionally, how quality by design (QbD) relates to package integrity and establishing a limit of detection (LOD) are discussed in the final section.

Commentary: The Lifecycle Approach and the Three Themes

The Three Functions of the Container Closure System (CCS)

TR86 clearly distinguishes the multiple functions of a CCS, but stresses that CCIT is mainly concerned with:

  • Primary function: product containment: preventing the product from leaking out or being lost
  • Barrier function 1: microbial barrier: preventing microbial ingress, the most critical requirement for sterile products
  • Barrier function 2: gas barrier: preventing ingress of oxygen or water vapor, essential for oxidation-sensitive or hygroscopic products
  • Function 3: maintaining vacuum: some lyophilized products or special dosage forms need to maintain an internal vacuum

"An Ongoing Process": CCI Is Not a Single-Point Test

TR86 stresses that package integrity testing is an ongoing process spanning the entire product lifecycle, from the start of packaging development until the product is withdrawn from the market. This means:

  • Development: select the design, establish the methods, set the MALL
  • Validation: engineering batches, process validation, analytical method validation
  • Commercial production: routine CCIT sampling, process monitoring
  • Stability studies: confirm that integrity is maintained during storage
  • Post-marketing: complaint investigations, returned goods analysis

Introduction — Risk-Based Framework & Patient Safety

Why CCI failure is a critical risk, and what a well-characterized process looks like

1.0 Introduction (Part 3) — Risk, Process Control & Patient Safety

Best practices in pharmaceutical packaging suggest that all activities be evaluated for risk to the product quality as a surrogate to patient safety. Failure of container closure integrity is a critical risk, as the lack of package integrity can lead to contamination of the product which can impact both product quality and potency. Contamination of a sterile product intended for parenteral administration could result in dire consequences for the patient.

Demonstration of container closure integrity and ensuring the integrity of the container closure system is required throughout the lifecycle of a sterile pharmaceutical product. A well-characterized and controlled process should consist of:

  • A qualified container closure design with effective, monitored process controls in place
  • Process parameters required to produce integral container closure systems are maintained
  • Appropriate specifications for incoming components
  • Qualification of manufacturing equipment, including a visual inspection system for detection of irregularities in the process
  • Appropriate sampling strategies for container closure integrity testing

Package integrity should ensure product quality is maintained through both the shelf life and life of a product. Over the past two decades, analytical technology has progressed, improving the ability to evaluate closure integrity. The information provided in this technical report is intended to assist users in advancing their current integrity assessment and testing strategies during the phases of product lifecycle.

Commentary: Risk Framework and Control Elements

Why Is CCI Failure a "Critical Risk"?

TR86 uses very strong wording: CCI failure is characterized as a critical risk, and the chain of consequences is as follows:

  • Package integrity failure → product becomes contaminated
  • Contamination affects product quality and potency
  • Contaminated sterile intravenous product → may cause serious harm or death to patients

This is the fundamental reason why FDA pays such close attention to CCI in inspections of injectable manufacturing sites.

The Five Elements of a "Well-Characterized and Controlled Process"

TR86 gives a complete framework for process control with five core elements:

  1. Qualified container closure design: the CCS design itself must be validated, including material compatibility, seal strength, etc.
  2. Effective and monitored process controls: CPPs such as capping force, capping speed and temperature must be within specification
  3. Incoming component specifications: dimensional tolerances and material specifications for stoppers, aluminum seals and vials
  4. Equipment validation including visual inspection systems: validation of the capper and stoppering machine, plus AVI (automated visual inspection)
  5. Appropriate sampling strategy: a statistically valid CCIT sampling plan

Analogy: An Aircraft Cabin Seal Is a Container Closure

An aircraft must maintain cabin pressure at 35,000 feet, which requires a complete sealing system. Airlines do not test the seals only once before takeoff: they start from the fuselage design (design validation), through routine checks before every flight (process control), to periodic major overhauls (CCIT during the stability period). The logic of managing CCI for drug packaging is exactly the same.

"Product Quality Is a Surrogate for Patient Safety"

This statement in TR86 points to the core philosophy of GMP: what we measure directly are product quality indicators (such as sterility, potency, clarity), and these are indirect (surrogate) indicators of patient safety. Therefore, any activity that affects product quality must be subject to risk assessment.

This is also why FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis) is very important in CCS design and CCIT method selection.

What TR86 covers, what it doesn't, and how it complements existing documents

1.1 Purpose and Scope

This technical report focuses on the challenges facing the pharmaceutical industry that use complex packaging systems for sterile drugs and biologics (e.g., syringes, syringe assemblies, bulk containers). It also presents information on some innovative methods for package integrity testing using existing technologies, including the potential impact of cryogenic conditions. The intent is to update information and incorporate experiential learning which is not addressed in PDA TR-27 and USP <1207>.

It also serves as a technical resource, focusing particularly on sterile products and encouraging a risk-based approach and leveraging testing to better understand, analyze, and eliminate the risks during developmental phases. Use of the appropriate testing during each manufacturing phase can help in evaluating and mitigating residual risks.

"The information in this technical report is the result of a consensus achieved by the PDA Container Closure Integrity Technical Report Team and are not necessarily the views of regulatory agencies."

TR86 covers:

  • Complex packaging systems: syringes, syringe assemblies, bulk containers
  • Innovative CCIT methods using existing technologies
  • Cryogenic conditions and their impact on package integrity
  • Experiential learning not captured in TR27 or USP <1207>
  • Risk-based approach at each manufacturing phase

TR86 does NOT replace:

  • USP <1207> — still the primary reference for basics and method selection
  • PDA TR27 — still relevant for foundational principles (until officially retired)
  • ICH Q9 — the overarching quality risk management framework

Commentary: Scope and Limitations of TR86

The Three "Focus Points" of TR86

  • Focus 1: Complex packaging systems
    It explicitly lists syringes, syringe assemblies and bulk containers: packaging forms that were not yet widespread in the TR27 era but are now mainstream for biologics
  • Focus 2: Innovative test methods
    TR86 particularly stresses "innovative approaches using existing technologies": not entirely new inventions, but extensions of existing technologies (such as HVLD and headspace analysis) into new application scenarios
  • Focus 3: Extremely low temperature conditions
    This is one of the unique contributions of TR86: the effect of extremely low temperatures on package integrity, for products such as mRNA vaccines and cell therapies that are stored at -80°C or lower

Important Statement: Not a Regulatory Authority Position

TR86 states explicitly that this report represents the consensus of the PDA technical team and does not represent the views of regulatory authorities.

What Does This Mean?

  • The recommendations of TR86 carry reference weight in FDA review, but cannot be cited directly as a regulatory requirement
  • If your method is based on TR86 recommendations but has not yet been accepted by FDA, you still need to provide scientific justification in the NDA/BLA
  • The consensus in TR86 is the collective wisdom of industry practice, but it is not regulatory guidance

Analogy: TR86 Is the "Treatment Guideline" of the Medical World

The clinical treatment guidelines issued by specialty societies are not law, but a physician who does not follow them needs sound medical reasons. Likewise, TR86 is not a regulatory requirement, but if your CCIT practice clearly deviates from the TR86 recommendations, FDA reviewers may ask you to explain why.

Commentary: The Importance of Defined Terms

Why Are Term Definitions So Important?

TR86 reminds us that different companies may understand the same term differently. This is especially important in a CDMO environment:

  • When a customer says "CCI test", it may mean a microbial ingress test, while you may understand it as HVLD
  • The scope of "probabilistic method" is defined differently in different documents
  • Consistent and precise terminology must be used in SOPs, validation reports and regulatory submission documents

Recommendation: at the tech transfer kickoff meeting, proactively confirm whether the CCIT terminology used by the customer is consistent with the TR86 definitions.

Key Glossary Relationships

Deterministic vs. Probabilistic: The Core Dichotomy

The most important conceptual distinction in TR86 and USP <1207> is the difference between deterministic and probabilistic test methods. This distinction drives method selection decisions:

  • Deterministic: Physics-based, quantitative, predictable, objective. Preferred by regulators. Example: HVLD, vacuum decay, headspace analysis.
  • Probabilistic: Biology-based (or statistically uncertain), qualitative, random outcomes. Historically used but less preferred for new applications. Example: Microbial Ingress Test (MIT/MIL), dye ingress.

MALL and LOD Relationship

The Maximum Allowable Leak Limit (MALL) and the Limit of Detection (LOD) of your test method must be scientifically related:

  • MALL = the largest defect that still puts quality at risk → defines the threshold
  • LOD = the smallest defect your method can reliably detect → must be ≤ MALL
  • If LOD > MALL: your method is not sensitive enough to catch dangerous leaks

Commentary: Logical Relationships Among Key Terms

The Mathematical Relationship Between MALL and LOD

Principle for method selection:

LOD (method limit of detection)  ≤  MALL (maximum allowable leakage limit)

If LOD > MALL → the method is not sensitive enough and is not acceptable
If LOD ≤ MALL → the method can reliably detect all hazardous leaks

Example:
MALL = 10 μm equivalent orifice diameter
→ Your CCIT method must be able to detect leaks ≤10 μm
→ LOD must be ≤10 μm

Analogy: Deterministic vs Probabilistic = Thermometer vs Judging by Complexion

A deterministic method is like taking a temperature with a thermometer: each measurement gives you an objective number (38.5°C) that is repeatable and traceable. A probabilistic method is like judging whether someone has a fever by their complexion: sometimes you can tell and sometimes you cannot, depending on the lighting, the observer and the circumstances. In a GMP environment, we naturally prefer the thermometer (deterministic methods).

Commentary: Precise Use of Abbreviations and Their Context

Three Sets of Easily Confused Abbreviations

In daily work and documents, the following three sets of abbreviations are easily used interchangeably. Be careful to distinguish them:

  • CCI vs CCIT: CCI is the attribute of container closure integrity; CCIT is the method/procedure for testing that attribute. For example: "We need to ensure CCI (the attribute)" vs "We perform CCIT (the testing activity)".
  • CPP vs CQA: a CPP is a critical parameter in the process (capping force); a CQA is a critical quality attribute of the product/package (container closure integrity itself). A deviation in a CPP may cause the CQA to fail.
  • MALL vs LOD: MALL is the quality standard (your specification); LOD is the method capability (how small a leak your method can detect). The key to method selection is to ensure that LOD ≤ MALL.

HVLD: Currently the Most Closely Watched Deterministic CCIT Technology

High voltage leak detection (HVLD) has attracted wide attention in recent years, for these reasons:

  • Non-destructive, and can be used for 100% inline inspection
  • High sensitivity, with an LOD of 2–5 μm equivalent orifice diameter
  • Suitable for packages containing conductive liquids (vials, PFS, ampoules)
  • FDA has accepted HVLD as a CCIT method in multiple ANDA/NDA cases

Limitations: less effective for lyophilized products (no conductive liquid); detection of gaseous leaks (inert gas) requires special setup.

The Logic of Applying QbD in CCIT

The logic chain for applying QbD (quality by design) to CCI:

  1. Define the target product quality: must be sterile, stable and maintain potency
  2. Identify the CQA: CCI is a critical quality attribute
  3. Establish the MALL: what level of leakage would endanger the CQA?
  4. Identify the CPPs: which process parameters affect CCI?
  5. Establish the design space: the acceptable range of the CPPs
  6. Select the CCIT method: a deterministic method with LOD ≤ MALL

FISTA and Transport Integrity

Relevance of FISTA (International Safe Transit Association) in a CCIT context: drug packaging must maintain integrity not only during production and storage, but also withstand vibration, shock and temperature changes during transport. FISTA standards provide a framework for transport simulation testing, which is especially important for assessing the transport integrity of complex packaging systems (such as PFS assemblies with multiple components).

3.0

Challenges with Methodologies

Method selection framework — sensitivity, variability, and known interference sources

3.0 Challenges with Methodologies

The selection of container closure integrity methodology is based on sensitivity, reduced variability, and clarification of the intended result of leak detection. The selected method will present challenges to the investigator, however, inherent characteristics of the chosen method and other unknown factors can increase variability, for example:

  • Formulations that impact flow through a leak or that create blockages within the leak.
  • Methods that impact formulations, e.g., a method that applies an energy level or introduces a new chemical compound that impacts the original formulation in any way.
  • Unintended impacts of method on a container closure system (CCS) potentially introducing a defect to or masking a defect in an otherwise unadulterated sample, e.g., pressure changing the shape of the container and potentially the nature of the defect.

Section 3 addresses the challenges and potential impact of emerging issues on methodology design and selection of container closure integrity testing (CCIT).

Commentary | Core Concepts

Three Principles of Method Selection

PDA TR86 opens by identifying three core criteria for selecting a CCIT method:

  • Sensitivity: how small a defect can the method detect?
  • Reduced Variability: is the reproducibility of results sufficient?
  • Intended Result: are you looking for the risk of microbial ingress, or for gas egress? A different goal calls for a different method.

Three Main Sources of Interference

  • Interference from the formulation itself: high protein concentration, high-viscosity solutions and suspended particles can block the defect channel and make a leak difficult to detect.
  • Effect of the method on the formulation: for example, a high-differential-pressure test may cause protein aggregation or break an emulsifier, changing the properties of the sample itself.
  • Effect of the method on the container: for example, once pressure is applied, a hole in a flexible package may shrink or enlarge, so the defect size differs before and after the test.

Analogy: Leak-Testing a Water Pipe

Imagine testing a water pipe for cracks with a pressurized water column. If the water contains a lot of flour (a high-viscosity formulation), the crack may be plugged by the paste and go undetected. If the pressure is too high (the method interferes with the container), a very fine crack may be forced wider, giving a falsely amplified leak signal. This is the mindset of method limitations that Section 3.0 emphasizes.

CDMO Application Tip

In a CDMO setting, the same filling line often handles different formulations (liquid injectables, lyophilized powders, protein drugs). When selecting a CCIT method, you must assess the three types of interference above for each product; one method cannot be applied to all products. Take particular care with protein drugs and high-viscosity ophthalmic formulations, which are the products most prone to blockage interference.

3.1

Positive Controls (Leak Artifacts)

Role, types, and lifecycle applications of leak artifacts in CCIT

3.1 Positive Controls

Container closure integrity (CCI) methods use positive control units, also called "leak artifacts." These controls are useful for different purposes and during different stages of test methods and product development, such as:

  • Design and development of the container
  • Development of a test method
  • Determination of the level of sensitivity to various types of leaks
  • Determination of detection limits
  • Validation of a test method
  • Validation or system suitability of the level of sensitivity to various types of leaks
  • Testing of containers for their stability properties
  • Development of container manufacturing process (quality control) parameters

Naturally occurring leaks are valuable positive controls during qualification of a CCIT method. Leaks that occur naturally also have a limited shelf life; they may be measurable today but not tomorrow. These samples might best be used for real-condition comparison of the method to better characterize the capability of the method to detect actual leak paths when engineered leak paths are used to characterize and develop a method.

Acquiring a sufficient set of leak artifacts for development purposes is a challenge due to the difficulty of using the normal container production processes to generate consistent, naturally occurring leaks. Therefore, positive control units with simulated defects can be produced to represent potential container and closure defects to use in method development and verification.

Naturally occurring leaks can also be used during process and product development. In most cases, the geometry and leak size of naturally occurring leaks are unknown, whereas positive controls with simulated defects (such as hole-type defects with channels) usually have leak paths with known geometry that can be generated using a controlled process. Use of both types of positive controls can provide useful information to develop methods for integrity testing.

Some CCIT system instruments can use calibrated leaks, another type of positive control. Calibrated leaks are systematically built into the CCIT system hardware to simulate a leak. The leaks are calibrated against traceable standards and installed inside robust housing (not the product container). If possible, the calibrated leak size should be equal to or less than the maximum allowed leak (MALL) for the specific container design. For a rapid 100% CCIT method, the MALL may not be achieved.

The calibrated leaks are used in routine system performance verification and, for some CCIT methods, as tools for routine and frequent calibration. The way in which the leaks were created and calibrated should be completely described.

Note: References that provide a broad overview of historical approaches to defect development and application can be found in the additional reading section.

Commentary | Nature and Purpose of Positive Controls

What Is a "Leak Artifact"?

A positive control is a package unit that is deliberately manufactured, or deliberately retained, with a defect, and is used to confirm that the CCIT method can actually detect a leak. TR86 uses a more descriptive term, Leak Artifact, emphasizing that this is a purposely created "artificial defective unit" and not a genuinely defective one.

Eight Use Cases for Positive Controls

  • Container design development: evaluating the sealing performance limits of a new container design
  • Test method development: establishing the basic operating parameters of the method
  • Confirming the sensitivity range: understanding the method's ability to detect different types of defects
  • Limit of detection (LoD) determination: confirming the smallest defect size the method can detect
  • Method validation: the formal validation activity required by regulations
  • System suitability: instrument confirmation before routine testing
  • Stability testing: monitoring changes in container closure integrity under storage conditions
  • Process development (QC parameters): optimizing process parameters such as sealing and capping

MALL — Maximum Allowable Leakage Limit

MALL (maximum allowable leakage limit) is a critical value determined by product characteristics; below this leak rate, product sterility and quality are still considered acceptable. The size of a calibrated leak should ideally be equal to or smaller than the MALL, so that the method is able to detect clinically meaningful defects.

In practice, 100% in-line CCIT sometimes cannot meet the MALL sensitivity requirement because of speed constraints; such cases must be explicitly addressed in the risk assessment.

Analogy: Fire Drill

A positive control is like the simulated fire alarm in a fire drill. You do not need an actual fire to confirm that the fire protection system works. The source of the alarm in each drill is known and controlled, which is the spirit of an "engineered simulated defect". A natural leak is more like the remains of a real fire found by chance: it is genuine, but with time it may no longer be reproducible.

3.1.1

Considerations for Selecting Positive Controls

Product package profile, method type, lifecycle phase, and defect size range

3.1.1 Considerations for Selecting Positive Controls

Positive controls should be selected based on product package profile, method type, and intended outcome of the method used during the intended lifecycle phase. Positive controls used for method development and validation should include a range of defect sizes and can include a set of different types of defects.

At a minimum it should include a set of surrounding leak sizes (above and below) that include the MALL rate for the specific product package and the intended limit of detection for the use of the selected method.

There is a limit to these types of defects; therefore, the method cannot be fully characterized to understand its capability or the true MALL of a CCS.

Commentary | How to Select the Right Positive Control

Three Selection Criteria

  • Product package profile: a vial has a rubber stopper and a prefilled syringe has a plunger; the locations and forms of potential defects are completely different, so different positive control designs are needed.
  • Type of test method: high voltage leak detection (HVLD), headspace analysis (HSA) and vacuum decay (VD) each have different sensitivity characteristics and therefore different minimum detectable defect sizes.
  • Intended use at the lifecycle stage: positive controls for method development can be less stringent, but those for validation must correspond strictly to the MALL and LoD.

"A Range of Defect Sizes Around the MALL" Is the Minimum Requirement

Positive controls for validation must include:

  • Defects smaller than the MALL (which the method should not detect, or should detect with low probability)
  • Defects equal to the MALL (the critical point, which the method should detect with high confidence)
  • Defects larger than the MALL (which the method must detect with a reliability of ≥95%)

This three-tier design lets you construct the method's probability of detection (POD) curve, which is a core item in regulatory review.

Selection Logic Diagram

Product package profile  ──→  Location and form of potential defects
Method type              ──→  Sensitivity range and LoD
Lifecycle stage          ──→  Stringency of validation

Intersection of the three ──→ Defect size range of the positive controls
                          ──→ Defect types of the positive controls
                          ──→ Quantity requirements of the positive controls

Analogy: Calibrating a Weighing Scale

Selecting positive controls is like choosing calibration weights for a weighing scale. You would not test with a single weight; you would use a series of weights, including the minimum, maximum and midpoint of the range, to confirm that the whole measuring range is accurate. Positive controls for CCIT validation likewise need to "cover the range above and below the MALL" rather than picking just one size.

3.1.2

Considerations When Using Positive Controls

Handling challenges, plugging risk, and detection confidence requirements

3.1.2 Considerations When Using Positive Controls

Naturally occurring positive controls generated from manufacturing process failures present a challenge when attempting to control the size of leaks. In many cases, these types of defects generate large leaks, or leaks that are fragile and can be easily plugged by product. If found, these types of defects may be valuable to understand method performance against naturally occurring defects but naturally occurring defects should not be used for method validation due to the wide variability they exhibit.

Positive controls with simulated defects can also be easily plugged during handling and storage; therefore, proper storage and handling precautions should be used.

During the development and validation or qualification of a CCIT method, the test system must be challenged with positive controls. Positive controls should simulate large or gross leaks and those that simulate small leaks. Table 3.1.4-1 offers a summary of common positive controls or engineered defects. Engineered defects for positive controls can have the added advantage of being a micron-sized defect, but other factors should also be considered:

  • Percentage of the micron-sized defects that can be consistently and reliably detected using a chosen CCIT method (NOTE: All positive controls with leaks at or above the claimed limit of detection (LoD) of the test method must be detected with high confidence, e.g., 95%).
  • Size and geometry of leaks that occur in real life and the ability of the test method to accurately detect those defects.
  • Leak size needed for microbial contamination to occur and pose a risk to product sterility or quality over the entire lifecycle of the product.

Commentary | Key Points When Using Positive Controls

Natural Defects Cannot Be Used for Method Validation: Three Reasons

  1. Size cannot be controlled: defects caused by process failures are usually on the large side (for example, a sealer set up incorrectly) and do not represent the smallest critical defect.
  2. Highly fragile: natural defects are easily plugged by the product, giving poor test repeatability.
  3. Wide variability: natural defects found in the same batch vary greatly in geometry and size, so they cannot be used to quantify sensitivity.

95% Detection Confidence Requirement

PDA TR86 explicitly requires that all positive controls equal to or larger than the method LoD must be detected with high confidence (for example, 95%).

This requirement echoes the statistical concept of probability of detection (POD). During validation, a sufficient sample size (for example, n=20) is usually needed to confirm statistically that a detection rate of ≥95% is achieved.

Simulated Defects Can Also Be Blocked!

Even carefully made engineered simulated defects can become blocked in the following situations:

  • Particles or protein from the product deposit in the defect channel
  • Improper handling (vibration, dropping) breaks or displaces the micropipette
  • Environmental contaminants enter the defect channel during long-term storage
  • Temperature changes cause the elastomer to rebound and narrow the defect opening

Positive controls therefore need dedicated storage conditions and a handling SOP, plus pre-use verification.

Validation Requires a "Large Defect + Small Defect" Dual Challenge

Method validation is not only about confirming detection of the smallest defect; it must also:

  • Gross leaks: confirm that the method does not saturate or misclassify when the signal is very strong
  • Small leaks: confirm that the method classifies correctly near the critical point

Analogy: Calibrating a Thermometer

To verify the accuracy of a medical thermometer, you would not test only at 37°C; you would also test at 35°C (low) and 40°C (high) to confirm reliability across the whole clinically relevant range. The principle of using both large and small defects in CCIT positive controls is exactly the same.

3.1.3

Types of Simulated Positive Controls

Glass micropipettes, wires, laser-drilling — mechanisms, sizes, and limitations

3.1.3 Types of Simulated Positive Controls

USP <1207.1> describes defect types as "positive controls representing realistic package flaws." Along with the examples given, it acknowledges that a chosen CCIT method may not be able to accurately detect some type defects in a given package design, so an alternative method should be identified. When a method cannot be identified, any information and design that can reduce the possible occurrence of such defects in the CCS should be sought.

Common materials used to create simulated defects include:

  • Glass micropipette
  • Wire
  • Laser-drilled

Glass Micropipettes

Glass micropipettes are available in diameter sizes from 0.1 µm to 10 µm. The main advantage of a glass micropipette over laser-drilled and wire-simulated defects is the ability to generate relatively consistent defects at small sizes.

Use of a glass micropipette is considered a short microchannel of small diameter (5 µm and under); diameters under 2 µm are not perfectly round.

The micropipette is inserted through a protective tool (such as a hollow metallic syringe needle) into the septum or stopper and bonded to the container with epoxy, or through a hole drilled into the side glass or rigid wall and bonded with epoxy. A microscopic examination of the micropipette tip can be used to verify that the micropipette tip was not broken during sample preparation; then, a test cycle can be performed with an equivalent simulated flow rate for comparison to verify the actual performance of the micropipette.

Glass micropipettes are fragile, and the tip can be easily broken during sample preparation. Broken micropipette tips will typically yield an unusually higher flow rate; such a sample should not be used as a positive control.

Wires

Wires can simulate foreign objects, such as a human hair (diameter range of 80 µm to 100 µm) at the sealing area. Various wire material and sizes are available in diameters of 10 µm and greater. For some sterile glass containers, a wire can be placed between the plunger and the glass barrel (syringes, cartridges) or between the aluminum-crimped stopper and the glass vial.

The elastomeric plunger or stopper, which has been press-fitted to the container, will not fully deform around the wire; therefore, small leak paths can be generated. This type of leak is also considered a microchannel. The defect size is dependent on, for example, the wire size, elastomeric material formulation and hardness, press-fit forces, or assembly forces (crimping for vials).

Leak paths with calculated or estimated sizes of 2 µm to 10 µm and less have been created for glass syringes and glass vials using nickel-titanium wires with diameters of 25 µm and greater.

Stainless steel wires (typically 50 µm and larger diameter) are used as simulated defects for heat-sealed or ultrasonically sealed trays and pouches. For lidded trays and pouches, where the container and lid material do not conform well to the wires generating a very large defect, the wire is pulled out shortly after sealing (and before full seal-curing) to generate a smaller leak path.

Laser-Drilled Holes

A laser at various wavelengths, when applied to the container surface, generates localized heat, melting the material and creating an opening (or hole) in the package wall. Laser-drilling on the container walls will generate defects from equivalent sizes of 2 µm and greater. Containers are typically filled and sealed after the laser-drilling process.

Laser-drilling of glass products (e.g., vials, cartridges, and syringes) is done by drilling a tapered hole in the glass, where the hole does not protrude through the glass wall. The heat generated by this process results in cracking the glass near that hole, creating the leak path through the cracks. The geometry of such cracks is not consistent; however, the defect size and its behavior are hard to define. Its behavior can be a sharp-edge orifice, a very short microchannel, or a combination of both.

Updated laser-drilling technology can increase the time interval between laser shots to allow heat dissipation, effectively eliminating glass "cracking." These defects are essentially wormholes that have some consistent diameters across the entire glass wall thickness (i.e., hole-like defects).

Laser-drilling manufacturers can correlate the defect "equivalent diameter" using air flow and empirical air flow calibration curves generated from known sharp-edged metallic orifices. Defect sizes less than 10 µm in glass products may show larger variability. The size of laser-drilled defects in glass can be estimated to "equivalent micropipette" size using calibration curves. Calibration curves should not be extrapolated beyond their ranges.

Laser-drilled holes are often the most effective and reliable method for simulating positive controls in flexible packaging. They may be the preferred way to introduce engineered defects in such flexible containers as single-use systems (e.g., for bulk storage) or IV bags.

An alternative to laser-drilling directly into a container is to use stainless-steel discs. These can be subjected to laser-drilling to create defects as small as 0.5 µm and, generally, are more consistent defects to use in equipment.

Commentary | The Three Main Simulated Defect Techniques in Detail

Link to USP <1207.1>

TR86 echoes the requirement of USP <1207.1> here: a positive control must be a "positive control sample that represents a real package defect". This means you cannot simply use any container with a hole in it; the form, size and location of the defect must be considered at the design stage so that it approximates the type of defect most likely to occur in the real production environment.

Method 1: Glass Micropipette

How it works: an extremely fine glass capillary (0.1–10 µm inner diameter) is inserted into a rubber stopper or glass wall and fixed with epoxy resin, forming a channel defect of known size.

Key advantage: it can produce the smallest sizes (down to 0.1 µm), suitable for validating very high sensitivity methods.

Main limitations:

  • Very easy to break: slight external force during sample preparation can snap the tip
  • After breakage the flow rate rises abnormally, but this is difficult to recognize by appearance
  • Microscope confirmation + flow verification must be performed before use
  • If used for dye ingress or microbial challenge testing, the channel must be primed with medium beforehand

Method 2: Wire Insertion

How it works: before capping or assembly, a fine metal wire is clamped between the rubber stopper/plunger and the glass; because the elastomer cannot fully conform around the wire, a micro-channel defect forms along the wire.

Two main applications:

  • Vials / syringes: nitinol wire (25 µm diameter or larger) → produces a 2–10 µm equivalent defect
  • Heat-sealed pouches / trays: stainless steel wire (≥50 µm), pulled out after sealing, leaving a channel

Factors affecting defect size: wire diameter, elastomer hardness, capping/assembly force, time interval between placement and testing, and temperature (which affects rubber elasticity)

Note: wire simulates real defects of the "foreign material trapped in the sealing surface" type, for example a hair or fiber falling into the seal area during production, which is one of the most common container closure failure modes in a CDMO.

Method 3: Laser Drilling

How it works: a laser beam generates localized high temperature on the container wall, melting or decomposing the material to form a hole.

Special features of glass containers:

  • The laser only drills close to, but not completely through, the glass wall → thermal stress produces a crack → the crack is the actual leak path
  • The crack geometry is irregular: it may be a sharp orifice, a micro-channel or a combination of both
  • Newer techniques (increasing the cooling time between two laser pulses) can produce more regular "wormhole" type defects

Application to flexible packaging: laser drilling is the preferred way to create defects in flexible containers such as IV bags and single-use systems (SUS).

Stainless steel disc alternative: holes down to 0.5 µm can be laser-drilled in a steel disc, which is then installed in the instrument's test circuit; this is more consistent and durable than drilling the glass directly.

Sizing and Calibration of Laser-Drilled Holes

The "equivalent diameter" of a laser hole is derived indirectly from air flow and a known orifice calibration curve; it is not measured directly. This means:

  • The calibration curve must not be extrapolated (a curve for the 5–50 µm range cannot be used to estimate a 1 µm defect)
  • Defects below 10 µm in glass inherently show greater variability
  • Verify the actual flow before use to avoid false negatives caused by drift in hole size

Calibrated Leaks

NIST-traceable standards, He leak rates, equipment integration and system verification

3.1.4 Calibrated Leaks

Calibrated leaks are designed to yield a given leakage rate calibrated against independent, traceable calibration standards. A calibrated leak is typically an independent component that is introduced into the container barrier or into the test system volume to introduce a certified known flow rate into the test system during a challenge leak test.

These calibrated leaks are engineered inside a robust metallic housing and are protected from plugging via filter or clean-supplied gas. Generating a calibrated leak to mimic the headspace of sterile containers (a space with a limited air supply) is difficult because calibrated leaks need an infinite gas supply.

The inlet to the calibrated leak is normally open to the gas supply source, while the outlet of the calibrated leak is connected to the equipment test circuit or test chamber via a valve. When this valve is open, a known amount of gas leakage is injected either into the test circuit (in vacuum-test condition) or out of the test circuit (in pressure-test condition).

When running a test with a "master container," the calibrated leak simulates a defect that is consistent and robust, which can be used to challenge and verify the CCIT equipment performance. In this condition, the calibrated leak represents a positive control sample for the CCIT equipment to detect that leak.

Commentary | How Calibrated Leaks Work and Their Regulatory Significance

What Is a "NIST-Traceable" Calibrated Leak?

NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) traceability means that the leak value of a calibrated leak element can be traced through an unbroken calibration chain to a national or international measurement standard.

In CCIT, this means:

  • The "He leak rate = 1.2×10⁻⁴ mbar·L/s" stated for your calibrated leak is a documented value
  • This value is calibrated by the manufacturer against a NIST standard using a standard leak tester (mass spectrometer)
  • The calibration certificate states the value, the uncertainty and the traceability chain

Converting Between He Leak Rate and Water Leak Rate

Because helium (He) and water differ in viscosity, the He leak rate and water leak rate of the same hole can be converted into each other (assuming laminar flow). The commonly used formula is:

Q(water) ≈ Q(He) × (η_He / η_water) × (ΔP_water / ΔP_He)

η_He ≈ 1.96×10⁻⁵ Pa·s (dynamic viscosity of helium)
η_water ≈ 1.0×10⁻³ Pa·s (dynamic viscosity of water @25°C)

This conversion is important when deciding between helium mass spectrometry and a liquid-based CCIT method (such as dye ingress).

Key Design Features of a Calibrated Leak

  • Rugged metal housing: protects the element from handling damage
  • Filter protection: prevents particles from blocking the calibrated orifice
  • Infinite gas source connection: a calibrated leak needs a continuous gas supply, which means it cannot simulate the limited gas supply of a sealed headspace container (such as a vial)
  • Valve control: when opened, it injects a known flow for a system challenge

Analogy: Calibrating a Dynamometer

A calibrated leak is like the "standard load weight" on a car dynamometer. The dynamometer itself is only a measuring tool; the weight ensures that its readings are accurate. Checking the instrument with the weight before each start-up is "system suitability". The calibrated leak plays exactly this role in a CCIT instrument.

CDMO Application: Designing the Frequency of System Suitability

In a GMP environment, a calibrated leak is typically used:

  • Before the start of each batch: run a system suitability test to confirm the instrument sensitivity has not drifted
  • After equipment maintenance: confirm that the repair has not affected the sealing integrity of the test circuit
  • Validation activities: as a challenge standard with a known leak rate

Note: for a validated deterministic method, if the instrument is in a calibrated and maintained state, routine testing may no longer require a product positive control every time, but periodic calibrated leak verification is still required.

3.1.5

Additional Considerations for Controls

MALL variability across industry, deterministic vs probabilistic methods, system suitability

3.1.5 Additional Considerations for Controls

The MALL used to define the CCI level of sterile products varies across the industry. This MALL is used to prepare positive controls with simulated defects.

System suitability can be performed on each instrument prior to sample analysis using calibrated standards or other means of instrument verification. If a deterministic method is validated and instruments are calibrated, the use of positive controls to establish sensitivity may no longer be required for routine testing. The use of controls during validation of a deterministic method demonstrates that leaks will be detected by the method, so they may not be needed with routine testing, as long as the system is in a calibrated and maintained state.

For probabilistic methods, system suitability may need to be performed along with sample analysis.

Section 6.5 provides an example of a study using positive controls. See Section 7.0 for additional resources.

Commentary | Industry Differences in MALL and Exemption from Routine Testing

There Is No Uniform Industry Standard for MALL

Different companies and product types set the MALL differently. Reference values commonly seen in industry include:

  • Injectables (liquid): MALL is usually set at a 1–10 µm equivalent orifice diameter (based on a critical microbial ingress orifice of ~0.2–1 µm plus a safety margin)
  • Lyophilized powder injectables: dry powder carries a lower direct risk of microbial contamination, but attention is needed to quality degradation caused by moisture ingress
  • Ophthalmic formulations: stricter, because sterility requirements are extremely high

This variability means that the design criteria for positive controls differ by product and company; there is no universally applicable setting.

Prerequisites for Exemption from Routine Positive Controls

A deterministic method can be exempted from routine positive controls, but all of the following must be met:

  1. The method has passed formal validation (including a positive control challenge)
  2. The instrument is within its current calibration interval
  3. The instrument has undergone preventive maintenance as specified
  4. A system suitability test is performed before each use (using a calibrated leak or another approved standard)

If any one of these is missing, the exemption from positive controls does not apply.

Recommended Design for Routine CCIT Operations at a CDMO

Based on the principles of 3.1.5, a CDMO is advised to clearly distinguish the following in its batch testing SOP:

  • Validation batches: must use a series of positive controls (the full range from below the MALL to above the MALL)
  • Routine production batches (deterministic methods): system suitability confirmation (including the calibrated leak) + review of instrument calibration records → product positive controls may be omitted
  • Routine production batches (probabilistic methods): each batch must include positive controls for system suitability

Blockage of Leak Paths

Protein aggregates, crystallization, silicone oil — false negatives, CCI strategy beyond CCIT

3.2 Blockage of Leak Paths

Biopharmaceutical products are formulated with a wide variety of ingredients, such as proteins, nucleic acids, or living microorganisms and buffer solution. Some of these ingredients can present challenges to CCIT. For example, some CCI physical testing methods have difficulty detecting defects in samples formulated with proteins.

A study was performed to compare CCIT between a highly concentrated protein solution (120 mg/mL) and a surrogate solution with the same viscosity material property. The surrogate solution could flow out easily through defects, but the highly concentrated protein solution did not flow out. One hypothesis suggests that highly concentrated large molecules in solution are agglomerated around defect channels, completely or partially blocking the channels. The result was a solution that had difficulty flowing out through the channels. This supports the idea that proteins may play an important role in clogging the leak path.

Other types of defect clogging can occur when a product solution has ingredients that can crystallize upon drying. In this case, the solution easily leaks out through the defect channel, but the leaking solution will dry over time and turn into crystallized material in the defect channel. Once the solution has crystallized, it clogs the defect channel. This situation depends on the properties of the solution and the type of defect. Defect samples can be detected if they are tested soon after they are prepared, but the detection probability decreases as sample storage time increases.

When defect samples are clogged by proteins, vacuum or tracer gas-based CCI physical tests may not be able to detect defects due to the difficulty creating pressure, mass, or gas changes through the defect channel. Theoretically, if the clogged defect channels have some residual moisture and are still electrically conductive, high-voltage leak detection (HVLD) may be able to detect those leaks. Also, solution-based testing, such as the dye ingress test, may help to dissolve the crystallized materials in the clogged area if the materials are in direct contact with the dye solution, constituting a valid CCIT method for such products. However, that dye solution can also block small channels under certain conditions, such as drying or vacuum or pressure exposure.

When considering clogging by proteins or other ingredients in liquid-filled products over time, seal property, in addition to the CCIT, should be monitored in a stability program. This should be considered in feasibility studies and when selecting the CCIT method. CCIT may not be able to detect a defect in the seal if it is blocked by the clogging. Sterility could be breached if the product is contaminated prior to the clogging. Clogging is one of the challenges in CCIT; consequently, the drug manufacturers should not rely on CCIT to ensure the sterility but should develop a CCI strategy.

Manufacturers can develop different ways to resolve the clogging challenge. The CCI testing strategy and methods should address this clogging challenge. Visual inspection and RSF are examples. For example, when the seal is visible, like the plunger in a syringe, the plunger-rib contact length can be monitored to verify that the seal contact pressure does not change. When the seal is not visible, like the stopper in a vial or the disc seal in a cartridge, the residual seal force or rubber bulge height from the rubber compression can be used to verify that the seal contact pressure is acceptable.

Commentary | Blockage Is the Most Difficult Challenge in CCIT

The Core Logic of Blockage

The essence of blockage is this: the container does have a defect, but CCIT cannot detect it, because the defect channel has been plugged by formulation components. This is the most dangerous scenario because:

  1. The CCIT result shows "pass"
  2. But microbial ingress may already have occurred before the blockage formed
  3. Once the blockage is removed (for example by transport vibration or temperature changes), the leak may reappear

Key Study Result: 120 mg/mL Protein vs a Surrogate Liquid of the Same Viscosity

A study cited in TR86 clearly shows that even when viscosity is excluded as a factor (using a surrogate liquid of the same viscosity as a control), the high-concentration protein solution still could not pass through the defect channel. This demonstrates that:

  • Blockage is not simply a problem of "viscosity too high to flow through"
  • The physicochemical properties of the protein molecules themselves (aggregation, adsorption) are the key mechanism
  • A CCIT method validated with a non-protein surrogate liquid may fail to predict accurately the behavior of the real protein formulation

Analogy: A Pipe Blocked by Limescale

Imagine a pipe with a slight crack. At first it seeps water (leaks), but minerals in the water slowly deposit in the crack and eventually fill it completely (blockage). If you then run a leak test, the result looks normal, yet the structural defect in the pipe is still there, only temporarily covered. At the next vibration or high pressure, the deposit comes loose and the leak returns. This is an exact analogy for the CCIT blockage effect.

The Most Critical Warning

"Sterility could be breached if the product is contaminated prior to the clogging. Clogging is one of the challenges in CCIT; consequently, the drug manufacturers should not rely on CCIT to ensure the sterility but should develop a CCI strategy."

This statement is the core conclusion of TR86 Section 3.2: CCIT alone cannot assure sterility. Before blockage occurs, microorganisms may already have entered the container, and after blockage CCIT instead "cannot see" the problem. This is why an overall CCI strategy is needed, rather than a single test.

Overall CCI Strategy: Considerations Beyond CCIT

Based on the recommendations of 3.2, for products whose formulations carry a blockage risk (high-concentration proteins, lyophilized formulations and so on), an overall CCI strategy should be established covering the following elements:

  • CCIT method selection: preferably choose methods insensitive to blockage (such as HVLD or visual inspection) or a combination of complementary methods
  • Seal characteristic monitoring in the stability program: periodically measure RSF (residual seal force) or stopper bulge height
  • Visual appearance inspection: monitor plunger-rib contact length (syringes) and the appearance of the stopper/disc seal (vials/cartridges)
  • Blockage assessment in feasibility studies: assess the blockage risk with the real formulation already at the method selection stage
  • Design of CCIT test timing: consider testing as soon as possible after filling (crystalline blockage worsens over time)

Testing for CCI is intended to provide evidence of the use of appropriately matched components and an adequate closing process. Commentary & Practical Perspectives

Core Concept: The Fundamental Purpose of CCI

The purpose of CCI testing is to provide evidence that matched components were used and that the sealing process was adequate. The basis of seal assurance is consistency of design parameters.

In short: CCI is not just "leak testing"; it verifies that the design and process of the entire closure system are sound.

Traditional CCS (glass vial + rubber stopper) is like a standard door lock: used by the industry for decades, with clear sealing principles and mature verification methods.

  • New material risks: polymers and elastomers are sensitive to environmental stress
  • Method suitability: traditional CCIT methods may not be applicable to newer containers

Original Text (English) — Stopper & Lubricity

Elastomers and plunger stoppers provide excellent sealing properties when paired with glass and plastic containers. As with other pharmaceutical parenteral closures, halobutyl elastomers are most widely used for prefilled syringe plungers due to good drug compatibility. The elastomeric formulation must be carefully chosen and must be hard enough to provide sufficient rigidity for consistent functionality, while allowing the viscoelastic properties of the plunger material to provide closure properties.

The design of the plunger provides CCI and functionality of the system. To take advantage of the compressibility of the elastomer material, the plunger is designed to be slightly larger in circumference than the inner diameter of the chosen barrel.

ISO 11040-5:2012 recommends that plungers be designed to use all three ribs. In general, the rib closest to the drug is considered the primary sealing rib; the second and third ribs are meant to stabilize the seal and provide secondary CCI assurance.

The use of lubricity coatings on the syringe barrel will influence the ability of the container system to create CCI. Silicone oil and vapor-deposited coatings are the most commonly used. When silicone oil is sprayed on the inside of the barrel, the coating may or may not be heat-treated. Excess lubricity coating may result in the plunger moving too freely during transit, causing a breach in CCI.

Commentary & Plunger Design Key Points

Plunger Stopper Design: The Three-Rib Principle

The sealing principle of the plunger stopper is based on a compression fit:

  • Stopper outer diameter > barrel inner diameter → compression seal formed
  • First rib (closest to the drug solution): primary sealing rib, the main CCI barrier
  • Second rib: stabilizes the seal and provides secondary CCI assurance
  • Third rib: third layer of CCI assurance, usually also connected to the plunger rod

ISO 11040-5:2012 specifies that all three ribs should be used to maximize sealing effectiveness.

Analogy: The Two Faces of Silicone Oil

Silicone oil is like engine oil in a car: it lets moving parts run smoothly, but the quantity must be tightly controlled:

  • Too little silicone oil: break-loose force of the plunger is too high → difficult to inject, poor function
  • Right amount of silicone oil: normal function, and the stopper presses tightly against the barrel wall to maintain CCI
  • Too much silicone oil: the stopper may slide freely during transport → drug solution leaks out, CCI failure

In addition, when the stopper is pushed out, pressure drives silicone oil that has not been heat-fixed away from the interface; this actually benefits CCI (the stopper is "locked"), but at the cost of a higher initial push force.

Additional Considerations for Fluoropolymer Coatings

Some plunger stopper surfaces carry a fluoropolymer laminate film to improve drug compatibility. Note, however:

  • With mechanical placement (rod insertion method), compression and pushing may create wrinkles in the film
  • Wrinkles may affect CCI performance and need to be evaluated
  • Vacuum stoppering is less prone to wrinkling, but incorrect vacuum settings may cause the drug solution to boil

Original Text (English) — Polymer PFS

Alternate materials of construction provide additional challenges with design. Prefilled syringes made of glass have long been available. Polymeric (or plastic) syringes in single-use or combination formats are available as an alternate material and have some advantages over glass. Prefilled plastic or polymer syringes contain a barrel typically made from cyclic olefin polymers or copolymers (COP/COC). Polymer barrel materials are stronger, tougher, and more flexible, allowing for improved design and dimension control; consequently, they are used more often and, in some cases, seen as a potential replacement for glass syringes.

Syringes composed of polymers or copolymers present additional CCI risks. Polymeric materials are prone to undergoing physicochemical changes upon exposure to heat, oxygen, irradiation, mechanical stresses, or various combinations of such stress conditions throughout manufacturing, sterilization, and storage. These risks are related not only to the syringe barrel design, but to its molding and sterilization processes, downstream handling, packaging, and distribution processes, all of which must be identified, assessed, evaluated, and mitigated.

The supplier of plastic syringe components is responsible for establishing and validating design requirements as well as critical process parameters (CPPs) for ensuring CCI. Appropriate CCIT methods should be applied to verify that the inherent leak rate of the syringe design conforms, at a minimum, to the MALL specified in USP <1207> for preserving product sterility and drug formulation content.

Commentary & Polymer PFS Challenges

Division of Responsibility: Supplier vs. MAH

TR86 clarifies the division of responsibility for polymer PFS:

  • Plastic syringe component supplier: responsible for establishing and validating design requirements and CPPs
  • Marketing authorization holder (MAH): responsible for defining product-specific CCI requirements (such as the MALL) and ensuring that the plastic barrel and elastomeric stopper form an integrated system that meets requirements

In a CDMO setting, this means contract specifications must clearly define each party's CCI-related responsibilities.

Analogy: "Stress Fatigue" of Polymers

A polymer PFS is like a plastic water bottle: sturdy when new, but with long-term exposure to heat, UV and mechanical stress the material gradually degrades (ages, becomes brittle). For a PFS this means:

  • Sterilization conditions (heat, irradiation) → physicochemical changes in the material → reduced sealing performance
  • Long-term storage → dimensional creep → reduced stopper compression force
  • All of these must be tracked and verified in stability studies

Original Text (English) — Filling, Stoppering & Fill Volume

Filling and stoppering a prefilled syringe may impact CCI. When using mechanical means of placing the plunger stopper, the stopper may be compressed into a tube, the tube inserted into the syringe, and a metal rod used to push the stopper out of the tube where it expands to form a compression fit inside the syringe barrel. If the plunger is laminated with a fluoropolymer coating, the compression and pushing may create wrinkles on the plunger which should be evaluated for any impact to CCI.

Vacuum stoppering is another means of stoppering the syringe; it involves creating a vacuum in the syringe, thus pulling the plunger into the syringe. Vacuum methods are less likely to create wrinkles on a fluoropolymer coating, but an incorrect vacuum setting may cause the filled liquid in the syringe to boil, creating other concerns.

The fill volume of samples should be sufficient to conduct the testing method. For example, if dye ingress testing will include spectrophotometric testing, the volume of fill must be sufficient to fill the cuvette used by the instrument. In the case of HVLD, the fill volume must be sufficient for the conducting liquid to cover any defects.

In laser-based headspace oxygen analysis, the sample fill level should allow a minimum headspace volume. The laser light should be able to pass through the headspace region of the sample without being scattered by the liquid product or by the syringe shoulder.

A filled syringe without secondary items, such as plunger rod, backstop, label, or anti-needle stick device, is referred to as a semi-finished syringe. Once all the desired components are added, the syringe is referred to as a finished syringe. Since the addition of these secondary items may impact different seal interface points, the CCI of the finished syringe should be assessed.

Commentary & Impact of Filling and Stoppering

CCI Strategy for Semi-finished and Finished Syringes

TR86 clearly distinguishes two key states:

  • Semi-finished syringe: filled and sealed, but without the plunger rod, back stop, label, needle-stick prevention device, etc. yet added
  • Finished syringe: all components installed

Because secondary packaging steps may affect the sealing interfaces, the CCI of the finished syringe must be evaluated separately and cannot rely solely on test results from the semi-finished syringe.

CDMO Practice: Impact of the Filling and Stoppering Process Choice on CCI

In CDMO fill-finish operations, the choice of stoppering process affects CCI:

  • Mechanical rod insertion: the stopper is first compressed into a guide tube, then pushed into the barrel where it expands to seal. FPT laminated stoppers are prone to wrinkling, which must be evaluated during process development
  • Vacuum stoppering: gentler and less prone to wrinkling, but the vacuum setting must be precise. Insufficient vacuum → stopper cannot be positioned correctly; excessive vacuum → drug solution boils

Test Method Considerations for Prefilled Syringes

Test Method Considerations for Prefilled Syringes • Pages p16-p17

Original Text (English)

Decisions regarding which CCIT method to select for prefilled syringes should be based on packaged product physical properties such as density, viscosity, electrical conductivity, molecule size, and evaporation capability.

Although many methods are available for CCIT of prefilled syringe or cartridge systems, CCIT after the assembly of prefilled syringes with auto-injectors or pen-injectors creates a new set of challenges. Disposable auto-injectors are designed to avoid tampering with the primary packaging container. Consequently, using the standard battery of CCIT methods like dye ingress, HVLD, and vacuum decay may not be suitable for this purpose; instead, they must be adapted. An adaptation may involve disassembly of the device before testing, which should verify that the disassembly method does not impact integrity or accept the decreased method sensitivity that testing the intact device would allow.

Risk assessment of a fully assembled syringe and device and its impact on CCIT should be considered. The following factors could compromise the CCIT and may need to be evaluated as part of the design verification:

  • Forces applied onto the primary container closure during final assembly, storage, and use of the device that may cause breakage or defects.
  • Critical forces on the needle shield (rigid or flexible needle shield), cartridge septum, or cartridge cap.
  • Critical forces on the plunger stopper (in cartridges or prefilled syringes).

Testing the actual packaged product for CCI is preferred; however, some solution properties inhibit leak detection, preventing direct testing. When the physical properties of a packaged product inhibit the use of otherwise suitable technologies, surrogate solutions may be used. For example, protein-based drug products are known for their propensity to dry in created defects; this inhibits the ability to detect those created defects by otherwise sensitive, deterministic technologies.

Stopper movement should be deliberately managed during CCIT. If the test methods exploit a pressure differential between the container interior and the test chamber (e.g., vacuum decay and mass extraction), stopper movement should be minimized. Stopper movement, and the resulting change in headspace volume, will reduce the pressure differential, possibly reducing the sensitivity of the method.

The application of a pressure differential to the prefilled syringe during CCIT may cause the stopper to move outward and then move back to its ordinary position once the pressure differential is removed. Stopper movement might allow entry of unwanted foreign matter; in addition, microbial contamination can be caused that could compromise the product. The adoption of technical solutions to prevent stopper movement during testing is recommended. For instance, in a vacuum decay system, one way to overcome this risk is to balance the outward stopper movement with a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to that of the stopper itself; this can be achieved by using a contrast pin in the test chamber.

Commentary & Test Method Selection

Plunger Movement: A CCI Testing Challenge Specific to PFS

This is the biggest CCIT challenge that distinguishes a PFS from other containers (such as vials). When vacuum decay or mass extraction methods apply a pressure differential:

  • External vacuum → relative positive pressure inside the barrel → plunger moves outward (toward the needle end)
  • Plunger movement → headspace volume increases → pressure differential decreases → method sensitivity drops
  • More dangerous still: plunger movement may introduce foreign matter or microbial contamination
  • After the pressure differential is removed, the plunger may move back, masking the contamination event

Contrast Pin: An Engineering Solution to Mitigate Plunger Movement

In a vacuum decay test system, a contrast pin can be used to resist plunger movement:

  • The contrast pin applies a force equal and opposite to the outward movement of the plunger caused by the vacuum
  • This allows a sufficient test pressure differential to be applied while keeping the plunger position stable
  • This is an important equipment design consideration for vacuum decay CCIT of a PFS

When a CDMO evaluates CCIT equipment, it should ask the supplier whether a contrast pin design for PFS is available.

Analogy: The Protein "Plugging Defect" Problem

A protein drug (such as an antibody) is like egg white: the smaller the defect, the more easily the protein dries at the defect opening into a "film" that plugs it.

This is why even a sensitive deterministic method (such as vacuum decay) may miss a real defect in a protein drug. Solution: use water for injection (WFI) as a surrogate for method performance validation, then combine it with a bridging study using real product.

Additional Considerations for Secondary Packaging

Additional Considerations for Secondary Packaging • Pages p17

Original Text (English)

A risk-based analysis should be performed to determine if any of the secondary packaging operations could impact CCI, and if CCIT should be performed to confirm that the integrity of the primary container system is maintained. If such a unit operation is identified, a CCI study should be performed on worst-case samples to demonstrate that the unit operation does not impact container integrity.

With the increasing use of self-administered prefilled syringes, secondary packaging of the filled syringe following completion of primary packaging takes place prior to distribution. Secondary packaging typically consists of (in order) plunger rod placement, finger flange placement, labeling, tray placement, and carton insertion.

Secondary packaging of a prefilled syringe can be performed manually by a technician, by a fully automated packaging line, or by a combination of manual and automated packaging.

Plunger Rod Placement: The most commonly used method is screwing the plunger rod into the back of the threaded stopper. The stopper serves as the primary barrier that maintains container integrity. Consequently, the process of inserting the plunger rod should be evaluated to determine if CCIT should be performed on the syringe after the plunger rod has been inserted to confirm that integrity has been maintained.

Finger Flange Placement: The main risk this operation poses to CCI is fracture of the syringe. To ensure that no broken or cracked syringes enter the supply chain, a 100% visual inspection must be performed. Given that the glass syringe is the primary container, an evaluation should be performed to determine if CCIT is required.

Tray Placement and Carton Insertion: The tray may contain one syringe or multiple syringes. The tray is then placed inside a carton for distribution. The operation of placing the syringe in the tray should be evaluated to determine if CCIT is required on the packaged syringe.

Risk-Assessment-Driven CCIT Decision Principles

The core message of TR86: not every secondary packaging step requires CCIT, but every step must undergo risk assessment. The assessment framework:

  1. Does the operation apply force or stress to the primary sealing interfaces?
  2. If force is applied, are its magnitude and direction sufficient to disturb the seal?
  3. If a risk exists, are other control measures (such as 100% visual inspection) sufficient to mitigate it, or is additional CCIT needed?
  4. If CCIT is determined to be needed, it should be performed on worst-case samples to confirm that the operation does not affect container integrity.

Analogy: The "Last Mile" Risk of Secondary Packaging

Imagine you have carefully packed a fragile glass artwork (= the filled and sealed PFS). The primary packaging (cushioning and inner box) is done, but the final "secondary packaging" (labeling it, fitting it into a display stand, placing it in a shipping box) could, at each step, crack or displace it without anyone noticing.

The PFS situation is more complex: its "breakage" may be invisible (microcracks), or a displacement of the sealing interface may not change its appearance yet already compromise CCI. This is why every secondary packaging step needs a systematic CCI risk assessment.

CCI Considerations: Automated vs. Manual Secondary Packaging

Secondary packaging can be fully automated, fully manual or a hybrid, each with a different CCI risk profile:

  • Fully automated line: force and speed can be precisely controlled → high consistency, but equipment setup and validation requirements are strict; equipment performance qualification (OQ/PQ) is required
  • Manual operation: large variability in force → detailed SOPs and operator training are needed; the "worst case" is hard to define precisely
  • Hybrid mode: manual operation steps must be identified as critical control points for CCI risk

Single-Use Systems (SUS) — Flexible Bulk Containers

Single-Use Systems: Flexible Bulk Containers | Pages 17-20

Overview

The term "flexible bulk container" refers to the application of single-use flexible technologies, commonly referred to as bioprocess containers, bioprocess bags, or single-use systems (SUS), for the manufacturing process of bulk drug substance and bulk drug product.

In general, these bags are available in "2-D" shapes, scaled from roughly 20 mL to 50 L, and in "3-D" shapes scaled from roughly 50 L to 3000 L in size. Historically, the use for bulk containers has been limited to less than 1000 L.

With their broad range of sizes, complexity of design, and polymer material characteristics, flexible bulk containers present unique challenges for establishing inherent SUS integrity.

Commentary: What is an SUS?

Single-Use System (SUS) refers to disposable flexible bags used in biopharmaceutical manufacturing to store, mix, and transfer drug substance or drug product. They are discarded after use and require no cleaning validation.

2-D Flat Bags
  • Capacity: 20 mL - 50 L
  • Appearance: flat, like a pillow
  • Use: small batches, laboratory scale
  • Example: Mobius® (Merck)
3-D Bags
  • Capacity: 50 L - 3000 L
  • Appearance: cylindrical, drum-like
  • Use: large-scale production
  • Example: Flexsafe® (Sartorius)

Why It Matters for CDMOs

The biggest advantage of SUS is that it eliminates cleaning validation (CIP/SIP), but the trade-off is that you are responsible for the integrity of every batch. In a CDMO setting, SUS is commonly used for cell culture media, drug substance storage, and buffer preparation before filling.

4.2.1

Design

4.2.1 DESIGN

For products not terminally sterilized, the sterility of the final drug product depends on the sterility of the bulk; therefore, the integrity of the bulk container system is crucial. An integrity assessment may even be required to support a sterile claim on the bulk container for terminally sterilized products.

The critical leak size of concern for flexible containers typically relates to microbial ingress, although liquid leaks can be a critical risk to some products, for example, environmental and operator safety related to toxicity. Gas permeation could also be a factor for product quality inside the SUS.

Barrier properties to gas exchange are established during the development of the laminate material, and the protection offered may need to be assessed during the selection stage. For establishing the integrity of the SUS, the MALL must be established based on the use conditions.

While SUS are out of the scope of USP <1207>, a lifecycle approach can be applied to CCI of these systems as well. How to evaluate the lifecycle and apply quality risk management (QRM) principles related to the SUS is described in ASTM WK64337.

Key Design Points

MALL Definition

MALL (Maximum Allowable Leakage Limit) is the largest leak that can be tolerated. For an SUS, the MALL must be established based on actual use conditions, such as hydrostatic pressure during filling, transport vibration, and altitude changes.

USP <1207> Does Not Apply to SUS

The scope of USP <1207> covers only containers with "limited-restriction sealing elements" (such as vial/stopper/cap combinations); the flexible structure of an SUS does not fit this definition. However, ASTM WK64337 is developing an integrity guide specific to SUS to fill this regulatory gap.

Analogy: Shopping Bag vs. Rigid Box

An SUS is like a soft shopping bag: you can squeeze it and fold it, but every seam junction is a potential hole. A glass vial, by contrast, is like a rigid box with limited deformation, and is much easier to assess.

Three major risk factors:

  • Microbial ingress - the most common MALL driver
  • Liquid leakage - an operator safety concern for highly toxic products (such as oncology drugs)
  • Gas permeation - affects the stability of O₂-sensitive biologics
4.2.2

Challenges

4.2.2 CHALLENGES

During routine production, transportation, and handling, various scenarios and events may arise that can affect the integrity of the SUS. Qualification and a holistic approach should be used to ensure product quality as well as ensure patient and operator safety.

A good understanding of the failure mode along the product lifecycle and the result of a risk analysis may lead to implementation of nondestructive integrity testing. This testing could be performed at the supplier's manufacturing site or at the user's site, depending on the risks to be mitigated.

One key challenge for integrity testing on bulk containers is defining the critical leak size, or MALL, that the system must satisfy. Suppliers of bulk containers own the design of the whole system and therefore should be expected to qualify the containers against such a requirement.

Existing literature does not support a universal maximum defect size for flexible bulk containers to determine the MALL for any specified test method. However, a maximum defect size for common use conditions, like the transport of filled bulk containers, must be determined in order to select a corresponding test method. The key step to determining the MALL is to measure it during the intended use condition.

Core Challenge: Joining Points = Failure Risk

70+
Large SUS assemblies may have more than 70 joining points

Key Risk Thinking

Every joining point (luer connector, hose barb, welded film, manifold connection) is a potential leak site. For a system with 70 joining points, if each has a 0.1% probability of failure, the overall probability that the assembly fails is close to 7%, far higher than the risk for a single glass vial.

Sources of SUS Complexity

  • Film-to-film seals - heat seal quality is difficult to verify 100%
  • Deformation of flexible materials - the shape changes once filled, making the test surface area hard to assess
  • Effects of radiation sterilization - gamma radiation can embrittle polymer materials and increase micro-cracks
  • Connector diversity - luer, hose barb, tri-clamp, and other connector types

Supplier vs. User Responsibilities

The SUS supplier is responsible for: design verification, MALL establishment, and manufacturing release testing.
The CDMO user is responsible for: pre-use testing, transport validation, and operational compatibility confirmation.
Both are indispensable, and responsibility cannot be transferred to one party alone.

4.2.3

Test Method Considerations

4.2.3 TEST METHODS

Microbial challenge testing is a well-known approach for identifying the MALL if prevention of microbial ingress is the critical container quality attribute. Two types of microbial integrity test methods are commonly used: testing by immersion and testing by aerosol.

Recent studies have demonstrated aerosol testing for microbial ingress to be equally as stringent as the immersion method, showing a MALL of 2 µm no matter which method is used. With aerosol testing, the ability to easily test a greater number of samples is an advantage in reducing the impact of the probabilistic nature of the test.

Once the MALL is determined, both probabilistic and deterministic physical methods are available for testing bulk containers; the industry is encouraged to evaluate deterministic methods. Helium leak detection applied to bulk containers is the most sensitive technology available. Its complex nature and relatively high investment cost suggest this technology would be used more appropriately by the supplier for release testing.

Pressure drop technology is less complex, less expensive, easier to operate, and sensitive to detecting leaks of concern to check for final integrity directly before use. Larger defects than the MALL are more likely to occur during normal operations.

  • Maximum leak size that poses no risk to microbial ingress (MALL).

Test Method Decision Framework

Microbial Challenge Testing: Comparing 2 Methods

Immersion

The SUS is immersed in a bacterial suspension and pressure is applied, then the interior is checked for contamination. Traditionally considered more stringent and statistically more reliable.

Aerosol

The SUS is exposed to a bacteria-containing aerosol environment, which better represents actual transport/use conditions and allows more samples to be tested.

New finding: The two methods have comparable sensitivity, with a MALL of 2 µm for both.

Helium vs. Pressure Decay: How to Choose?

Helium leak testing -> first choice for supplier release testing (high sensitivity, cost can be spread across batches)

Pressure decay technique -> first choice for pre-use on-site testing (simple to operate, low equipment cost)

The two are not mutually exclusive; each has a role at different points in the lifecycle.

Analogy: The MALL vs. LoD Relationship

Think of the MALL as "the largest leak size allowed to pass" and the LoD as "the smallest leak size the instrument can detect." The ideal situation is:

LoD ≤ MALL

If the LoD is larger than the MALL, the test method cannot protect the product. This is a common dilemma for large, complex SUS.

CDMO Practice: Acceptance Testing Plan

As an SUS user, the CDMO should require the supplier to provide:

  • 100% release test records for every SUS lot (helium or pressure decay)
  • Documentation of the scientific basis for the MALL
  • Transport validation reports (vibration and shock testing)
  • Integrity verification data after radiation sterilization

4.2.4

Additional Considerations

4.2.4 ADDITIONAL

Flexible bulk packaging systems are increasingly being used for drug substance, drug product, and fill-finish applications in the biotech industry. Sound, scientific test methods are needed to ensure their quality throughout the product lifecycle.

The current model to ensure integrity is based on a quality by design (QbD) and QRM approach that should be applied by suppliers and users. Suppliers must possess a strong understanding of the design and qualification of a product for its intended use. Likewise, users must ensure their handling and other processes are compatible with the design of the SUS. Both suppliers and users should consider the method and frequency of testing as part of risk mitigation.

New technologies will continue to develop to meet the market needs, such as faster inspection times and greater sensitivity. Regardless of the capability of any single test method, it must always fit into an overall approach to quality and risk mitigation through each step of the supply chain.

Ongoing research on MALLs and the comparison of real-world to model defects is required to enhance understanding and increase integrity assurance for flexible bulk container systems.

Integrated Framework: QbD + QRM for SUS

Lifecycle Integrity Management Model

CCI for an SUS is not a single test point but a management framework that spans the entire lifecycle:

  1. Design and development stage - material selection, joining point design, initial MALL establishment
  2. Supplier release stage - 100% helium/pressure decay testing
  3. Transport validation stage - vibration, shock, and altitude simulation
  4. Pre-use verification stage - on-site pressure decay testing or visual inspection
  5. Post-use evaluation stage - end-to-end integrity (end-of-use testing)

SUS Cleaning Validation vs. Integrity Testing

The biggest advantage of SUS is that it does not require cleaning validation (single use avoids cross-contamination). But this does not mean integrity testing is unnecessary; integrity testing is in fact even more important, because once a bag fails, you cannot clean it and reuse it.

Key Points for CDMO Procurement Decisions

When selecting an SUS supplier, beyond price, you should focus on evaluating whether the supplier:

  • Provides integrity documentation compliant with ASTM WK64337
  • Has dedicated integrity testing equipment (not visual inspection as a substitute)
  • Can support pre-use testing at the user site
  • Responds quickly to complaints and investigations (Field Alert history)

IV Bags — Flexible Finished Pharmaceutical Containers

IV Bags: Flexible Drug Product Containers | Pages 21-22

Overview

An IV bag is a pharmaceutical product container made of flexible material, closed at the bottom and along the sides by sealing; the top of the container may be closed by fusion to IV tubing or a medication port, depending on the intended use.

Because it comes in direct contact with the pharmaceutical product or formulation, IV bags are made of materials that will not alter the quality (efficacy or stability) of the product or formulation. Some of the information in this section may be relevant to considerations for bulk containers as well.

Key Differences Between IV Bags and SUS Bags

4.3.1

Design

4.3.1 DESIGN

Flexible packages often undergo temporary shape changes caused by forces within the environment, such as damages incurred during distribution and storage. The magnitude of forces sufficient to cause loss of package sterility by converting a defect into a leak must be considered when trying to determine an appropriate CCIT method that can reliably detect the leak of concern.

Imposed pressures capable of initiating liquid or gas flow through a defect provide the necessary conditions for ingress or egress to occur and, subsequently, result in a loss of sterility or quality of the product.

For a specific product package system, an understanding of product's liquid properties, such as surface tension and viscosity, is useful to determine the critical leak for that system.

IV Bag Design Elements

Key Design Components

  • Port design - the sealing interface between the infusion port, the medication port, and the film is the most vulnerable area
  • Overwrap - protects the primary bag from mechanical damage and gas permeation
  • Film material - different materials such as PVC and non-PVC (PP/PE multilayer film) affect permeability

How Liquid Properties Affect CCI Detection

Liquids with high surface tension (such as pure water) can form liquid bridges at tiny defects and need higher pressure to leak, so leaks are "hard to occur" but, if they do occur, are hard to detect.

Liquids with high viscosity (such as protein formulations) flow more slowly and may plug micro-pores, interfering with test results.

4.3.2

Challenges

4.3.2 CHALLENGES

When liquid-filled IV bags are tested for CCI, any leak from the container can pose a challenge if the product leaks and contaminates the test system (equipment and/or test chamber). The residual liquid or vapor that leaks into a test chamber from a defect in the container must be removed and the test system must be cleaned before testing can resume. This activity can affect the throughput of the test method.

A defect in direct contact with air may be detected at a faster rate than a defect in contact with a liquid or product solution, and the location of the defect can have a significant impact. The product solution of some IV bags can also dry and crystallize, potentially clogging micron-size defects and making leak detection a challenge.

The escape of residual volatile gases and vapors from the outside surface of IV bags when they are exposed to vacuum conditions may affect leak test results by giving false positives during testing (e.g., with vacuum decay or mass extraction test methods). With such methods, the use of special fixtures or the inclusion of equilibration time before the actual test time may be necessary.

Positive controls (or engineered defects) for IV bags can pose a challenge when considering leak detection through micron-sized holes. No one method can detect every different type of defect. Hence, the CCIT tool kit should encompass a variety of methods.

Five Major CCI Challenges for IV Bags

Challenge 1: Liquid Contaminating the Test Equipment

If an IV bag has a real leak, the drug solution enters the test chamber and must be cleaned out, directly reducing test throughput. For a production line, the cost of stopping the line for cleaning is very high.

Challenge 2: Defect Location Affects Detection Rate

Defects in contact with air are detected more quickly than defects in contact with liquid, which means defects below the liquid level may be underestimated.

Challenge 3: Drug Solution Crystallization Plugging Micro-Pores

Some IV bag solutions (such as certain injectable antibiotics and electrolyte solutions) crystallize on drying, which can temporarily plug micron-scale defects and cause "false negatives": the defect exists but the test passes.

Challenge 4: False Positives in Vacuum Testing

Residual volatile gases on the IV bag surface (such as water vapor after sterilization) are released in large amounts when a vacuum is drawn, causing false positives in Vacuum Decay or Mass Extraction methods. Solution: allow sufficient "equilibration time" before testing or use special fixtures to limit the test area.

Challenge 5: Engineering Difficulty of Positive Controls

It is very difficult to create repeatable micron-scale artificial defects (positive controls) in flexible IV bags: results vary widely with each drilling or laser-drilling, and the elasticity of the film material may cause the hole to close by itself.

4.3.3

Test Method Considerations

4.3.3 TEST METHODS

The condition of the test specimens can have a significant impact on the test results; for example, the temperature of flex bag, time between sterilization and testing, humidity, and many other factors can influence test results. Moisture may be present on the surface of liquid-filled IV bags, especially after moist heat sterilization, which may make it difficult to detect leaks with certain technologies.

Certain IV bags may not be suitable for leak detection methods that employ high vacuum or high pressure due to the potential for bursting or dislodging the container closure components.

Deterministic method options are HVLD, vacuum decay, mass extraction, force decay, and laser headspace analysis (tunable laser diode spectroscopy). Probabilistic method options are dye or microbial ingress, and internal pressure.

Certain CCIT methods, like HVLD, detect leaks in the walls of nonporous, rigid, or flexible packaging containing liquid product. Hence, this method is not compatible with dry pharmaceutical drug products or product packages with liquid on the exterior of the package (e.g., liquid loads immediately removed from a sterilizer).

Some films that are used in the manufacture of IV bags are readily permeable to inert gases like helium, which makes application of a helium leak test method a challenge for that specific type of CCS.

Natural or real-world CCI defects are generally larger in size than engineered defects and represent all possible CCI failure modes. Natural CCI defects may not be pristine holes but rather long, complex, irregular channels and tortuous pathways.

Summary of Test Methods Applicable to IV Bags

Key point: IV bag films may be permeable to helium

The multilayer films used for some IV bags (such as non-PVC PP/PE film) are inherently permeable to helium, which causes false positives in helium leak testing and makes it unsuitable. This is a material property that must be confirmed in advance when selecting a CCI method.

4.3.4

Additional Considerations

4.3.4 ADDITIONAL

Drug products can interact with defects in IV bags in ways that can interfere with the leak detection capability of the specific CCIT method. The possible impact to product stability (e.g., HVLD or short-wave infrared imaging) should be considered in the application of certain CCIT methods.

Inherent package integrity is typically established during product-package development and manufacturing qualification. The permeation rate of gaseous molecules through the material of an IV bag must be defined and differentiated from a true leak through a micron-size defect that is critical to product quality. Some gaseous permeation may be acceptable; leakage is not.

Permeation vs. Leakage: A Key Distinction

Permeation vs. Leakage

Permeation is the diffusion of gas molecules through intact film material. It is an inherent property of the material and is acceptable within a certain range.

Leakage is the flow of liquid or gas through a defect (hole, crack, or unsealed interface). It is unacceptable and directly represents a CCI failure.

In IV bag CCI studies, the inherent permeation rate of the material must be quantified first, so that the acceptance threshold for the CCI method can be set.

CDMO Inspection Readiness

When reviewing IV bag CCI validation, FDA/EMA inspectors will ask:

  • How do you distinguish the permeation signal from the leakage signal?
  • How are pre-use vs. post-use tests arranged?
  • Has the risk of photodegradation of the product by HVLD been assessed?
  • Can the positive control defects be reliably detected under the test conditions?
4.4

Cryogenic Conditions

Container Integrity Under Cryogenic Conditions | Pages 22-26

Overview

Many sterile products are stored at cold (2 °C to 8 °C), freezing (-20 °C), and ultra-cold (-80 °C to -196 °C) temperatures to limit formulation degradation and extend product shelf life. Several considerations should be addressed regarding cryogenic storage and package integrity:

  • Risks to package after fill and freeze.
  • Risks to large-scale bags after fill and freeze.
  • Loss of integrity in frozen state due to material differences at low temperatures that are restored at high temperatures.
  • Risk to package integrity in frozen state during transport and handling.

Storage Temperature Distribution

2-8 °C Refrigeration - protein formulations, vaccines
-20 °C Freezing - the next most common temperature, some small-molecule injectables
-80 °C Ultra-cold - clinical samples, live virus vaccines, mRNA LNP
-196 °C Liquid nitrogen - cell therapy, CAR-T

Why Is CCI at Low Temperature Especially Difficult?

CCI failure at low temperature has a "self-healing" characteristic: the seal fails in the frozen state, but when warmed the rubber stopper rebounds and the container looks intact. Conventional visual inspection cannot detect this type of problem at all.

4.4.1

Design: Cryogenic Storage Observations

4.4.1 DESIGN

Refrigeration storage temperatures in the 2 °C to 8 °C range are common for many products. Freezer storage at -20 °C is the next most common temperature. Ultra-cold storage, beginning at -80 °C, is most commonly used for clinical trial samples where complete stability data may not yet be available (e.g., live viral vaccines); it is also the temperature of dry ice, commonly used for the shipment of product.

Observations of overpressure have been reported in a significant number of vials after storage at -80 °C. When syringes were inserted into these vials, the plungers pushed outwards and, when the syringe was removed, product sprayed from needle puncture holes. Syringe plunger movement after being inserted into a vial, or product spraying out of a needle hole, indicates a substantial overpressure inside the vial.

When vials exhibiting the overpressure phenomena were tested using laser headspace gas analysis methods, the results clearly showed changes in headspace conditions different from the specified manufacturing process parameters. The vials should have, at room temperature, a headspace gas composition of air (20.9% oxygen / 79.1% nitrogen / 0.04% CO₂) and a pressure of 1 atmosphere.

Figures 4.4.1-1, 4.4.1-2, and 4.4.1-3 show examples of changes in headspace gas composition and pressure after storage at -80 °C on dry ice. The measurements show excessive levels of CO₂ (atmospheric air has <0.4 mbar), reduced levels of oxygen (atmospheric air has 20.9% oxygen), and elevated levels of total pressure (atmospheric air is 1000 mbar). These headspace changes have been attributed to a CCI failure while the product was stored at -80 °C.

The root cause of CCI loss at cold temperatures is believed to be a result of physical changes to packaging components at low temperatures. Elastomers and glass have markedly different thermal expansion coefficients, and stopper elastomers have glass transition temperatures similar to storage temperatures. The greater thermal contraction of elastomer materials, compared to glass, and the material transition below the glass transition temperature to more plastic-like properties, increases the possibility of a CCI failure.

When CCI is compromised, nonsterile reactive gases from the surrounding environment enter the package and are trapped; once the vial warms to room temperature, the elastomer regains its elastic properties and returns to its room-temperature dimensions and fit.

Of 20 samples stored at -80 °C (dry ice)

= direct evidence of CCI failure

Clinical Risk of Overpressure

When a syringe is inserted into a failed vial that has been warmed up, the overpressure inside the vial (>1 atm) pushes the syringe plunger out and sprays liquid when the needle is withdrawn. This is not just an "integrity issue"; it is a direct operator safety risk (especially for highly potent oncology products or biologics).

Analogy for the Physical Mechanism

Imagine an inflated balloon (the rubber stopper) placed in a refrigerator: it shrinks, leaving a gap at the vial mouth. External CO₂ (from the dry ice environment) seeps in. When the balloon (rubber stopper) returns to room temperature, it expands again and seals the gap, trapping the CO₂ inside and causing overpressure.

4.4.2

Challenges: Thermal Properties of Packaging Components

4.4.2 CHALLENGES

Glass vials used to package finished sterile products are commonly made of borosilicate materials. Stoppers are primarily butyl elastomers. Each of these components have coefficients of linear thermal expansion and may also have glass transition temperatures.

The change in temperature of a sterile product package is significant, from room temperature of 20 °C to ultra-cold temperature of -80 °C and, possibly, to liquid nitrogen temperatures of -196 °C.

Key quantitative data: The volume of a butyl elastomer stopper will decrease by 6% when the temperature drops from 20 °C to -20 °C, and decrease by 11% when the temperature drops from 20 °C to -70 °C (the glass transition temperature). Below the glass transition temperature, the thermal expansion-contraction rate of materials decreases, but a further decrease in temperature, to -196 °C, could result in a total stopper volume decrease of up to 20% compared to room temperature volume.

In contrast, over the same temperature range (room temperature to -196 °C), borosilicate glass undergoes a volume change of less than 1%.

When the temperature drops from 20 °C to -80 °C, the stopper will contract to 88% of its original volume while the glass vial contracts to 99.5%. Below the glass transition temperature, the stopper loses its elasticity and becomes rigid and more plastic-like.

As the temperature drops to -80 °C, the headspace gas pressure in the vial drops to 66% of its initial room temperature value (1000 mbar drops to 660 mbar). When a stopper–vial seal loses integrity at -80 °C, the pressure difference between the 1 atmosphere storage environment and the 0.66 atmosphere inside the vial will drive the outside gas into the vial headspace.

If a vial is stored at -80 °C on dry ice and loses CCI below the glass transition temperature of -70 °C, the vial will fill with 0.35 atmosphere of CO₂. Over a longer storage period, the oxygen in the vial will diffuse out and be replaced with CO₂. If the vial is then removed from dry ice storage and warmed, the stopper will regain elasticity at the glass transition temperature and the vial will reseal. The headspace pressure will increase from 1 atmosphere at -70 °C to 1.4 atmosphere at 20 °C.

Quantitative Analysis: The Physics of Rubber Stopper Shrinkage

Comparison of Volume Shrinkage

Temperature change: 20°C -> -80°C

Rubber stopper (butyl rubber):
  20°C -> -20°C:  volume decrease  6%
  20°C -> -70°C:  volume decrease 11% (glass transition temperature)
  20°C -> -196°C: volume decrease 20%

Borosilicate glass vial:
  20°C -> -196°C: volume decrease <1%

Difference: rubber stopper shrinkage = 20 times glass vial shrinkage

This difference in thermal shrinkage is the root cause of CCI failure at low temperature.

Headspace Pressure Calculation (Gay-Lussac's Law)

P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂  (constant volume)

Room temperature: T₁ = 293 K, P₁ = 1000 mbar
-80°C: T₂ = 193 K
P₂ = 1000 × (193/293) = 659 mbar ≈ 660 mbar

After leakage in dry ice environment, then warming:
-70°C (re-sealed) -> 20°C warming:
Pressure = 1000 × (293/203) ≈ 1443 mbar
= 1.4 atm overpressure!

Significance of the Glass Transition Temperature (Tg)

The Tg of butyl rubber is approximately -70 °C. Below this temperature:

  • The rubber stopper changes from an "elastomer" to a "rigid plastic"
  • It loses active sealing force and cannot compensate for the gap caused by shrinkage
  • The material becomes more brittle and is more likely to crack under low-temperature vibration

Storage at -80 °C is just below the Tg, making it the most dangerous storage temperature.

4.4.3

Test Method Considerations

4.4.3 TEST METHODS

Measuring changes in headspace gas composition and pressure after cryogenic storage can provide insight into whether a package has maintained CCI. A key point is that the CCI failure is temporary. As the vial warms up, the elastomer regains its elastic properties, expands, and reseals the vial.

Some methods are well suited for measuring CCI failure. Laser headspace analysis is uniquely suited to study changes nondestructively in headspace gas composition and pressure to determine CCI failures. Helium leak detection and residual seal force measurement can also be used to indicate changes from cold conditions.

When CCI is lost in a sterile pharmaceutical vial stored at cryogenic temperatures, the gas composition and pressure in the vial headspace will change, and headspace analysis can measure those changes. Depending on the cold storage atmosphere (air in a freezer or CO₂ on dry ice), the increases in CO₂ or total pressure and/or the decreases in oxygen concentration can be measured.

Core Methods for Detecting CCI at Low Temperature

Laser Headspace Analysis

Uses TDLS (tunable diode laser spectroscopy) to non-destructively measure CO₂ and O₂ concentrations and total pressure inside the vial.

Why is it the first choice for low-temperature CCI?

  • CCI failure is "transient": after warming the container looks intact, and only the headspace composition has changed
  • The laser can read the headspace without destroying the sample
  • It can measure three indicators simultaneously: CO₂↑, O₂↓, P↑
  • Suitable for 100% batch release testing

Test Timing: In-Situ vs. After Warming

In-situ testing: testing at -80 °C can directly detect the leak state, but the equipment must be compatible with low temperature, making it technically difficult.

Testing after warming: after returning to room temperature, although the vial has re-sealed, the "trace" in the headspace composition can still be detected by laser analysis. This is currently the most feasible method.

Application of Residual Seal Force (RSF)

RSF measures the compression force of the rubber stopper on the glass vial neck. A drop in RSF after low-temperature exposure is a predictive indicator of CCI risk: even if the container has not yet visibly failed, a reduced RSF is an early warning signal. In CCI validation of CAR-T cell therapy products, RSF testing is an important complementary tool.

4.4.4

Additional Considerations for Cryogenic Conditions

4.4.4 ADDITIONAL

Several studies have been published in the PDA Journal of Science and Technology or presented at PDA meetings on the study of CCI failures of sterile product packages stored at cryogenic temperatures and other established sources.

Some of these thermal properties of parenteral packaging components, found in the common glass vial, stopper, and crimp assembly, should be given consideration in the context of cryogenic storage. The lifecycle approach advocated by USP <1207> means that CCI should be assessed as part of the package development phase for all products destined for cryogenic storage.

The coldest storage temperatures are used for storing cellular materials, which use liquid nitrogen as the refrigerant at a temperature of -196 °C. Package integrity must be maintained at the lowest temperature a package will experience over its intended shelf life. The loss of CCI at any temperature leads to sterility and stability risks to the product and patient as well as to the clinicians administering the products.

Frontier CDMO Applications: Cell Therapy and mRNA

CAR-T Cell Therapy (-196 °C Liquid Nitrogen)

CAR-T products are stored in liquid nitrogen, and because each patient sample is irreplaceable, the risk of CCI failure is far higher than for typical formulations. There are currently no mature CCIT standards for these containers (usually cryobags or special cryovials), a gap the industry is working to close.

Key consideration: At liquid nitrogen temperature, the embrittlement risk of any non-metallic material requires special evaluation.

mRNA Vaccines (-80 °C)

The ultra-cold requirements of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines put the -80 °C CCI problem on the industry agenda. Maintaining CCI of multi-dose vials after repeated withdrawals (multiple punctures of the rubber stopper) is a new validation challenge. Each puncture leaves cumulative damage on the stopper, which, combined with low-temperature shrinkage, may accelerate failure.

Applying the USP <1207> Lifecycle Approach

For ultra-cold stored products, a lifecycle CCI assessment should include:

  • Development stage: low-temperature CCI evaluation during packaging component selection (material Tg analysis)
  • Manufacturing stage: CCI monitoring during freeze-thaw cycling validation
  • Shelf life: laser headspace analysis on periodically sampled units
  • Transport validation: CCI simulation during dry ice/liquid nitrogen shipment

5.1

Helium Testing

Deterministic, nondestructive CCI for flexible SUS bag systems | p26–28

Original Text — English

Helium integrity testing is a well-known, deterministic, nondestructive test method that has been used for many years in several industries to test rigid containers. The technology, which has special applications, now allows the testing of flexible polymeric container systems, such as those described in Section 4.2.

Commentary

Basic Definition of Helium Testing

  • Deterministic: results have a scientific basis and are not probabilistic
  • Nondestructive: the product can still be used after testing
  • Original use: rigid containers (such as metal or hard-walled containers)
  • Now extended to: flexible polymer container systems (such as SUS bags)
5.1.1 Application

Helium testing is suitable for test units that are dry and can withstand at least 1 psi pressure differential once they are restrained by a special holder. This test method can be used to verify the integrity of fully-assembled 2-D and 3-D single-use bags (such as disposable bioreactor or mixing and storage bags) as well as complex manifold systems consisting of multiple 2-D or 3-D bags connected to a central tubing manifold (Figure 5.1.1-1🔍). Specific components that have a natural leak rate above the determined limit, e.g., aseptic connectors with porous membranes, cannot be tested and must be excluded. Permeation leak rate of nondefective assemblies should also be considered. The complex assemblies, which have more than 100 connection points (such as barb–tube, film–film, and film–fitment joints), can be tested to a defect size of a 2 µm pinhole equivalent flow-effective diameter.

The method involves placing a finished biopharmaceutical single-use bag assembly into an enclosed test chamber, evacuating the chamber to create a vacuum, and then introducing a measured quantity of helium gas into the bag assembly being tested. A specialized pumping technique reduces the stress on the bag assembly by reducing the internal pressure of the bag along with the external (chamber) pressure. In addition, the test chamber can be equipped with restraining plates and porous spacers to mechanically support the assembly under test and avoid the masking effect of defects. The helium-filled bag assembly is then held for a fixed time while the surrounding vacuum is monitored for leaking helium using a sensitive mass spectrometer. An automatically preceded pressure-sensing gross leak check prevents the test chamber from being saturated with large quantities of helium, practically eliminating the system down times due to helium pollution. This method has the advantage that it can be made semi-automatic, reducing the role of the operator.

Figure 5.1.1-1🔍: Sample Manifold with 10 2-D Bags Attached to a Central Fluid Line with 70+ Joining Points

Applicability conditions

  • The test article must be dry
  • It must withstand a pressure differential of at least 1 psi
  • Suitable for 2-D and 3-D SUS bags (including bioreactor bags, mixing bags and storage bags)
  • Can test complex tubing manifold systems (100 or more connections)
  • Components whose natural leak rate exceeds the limit (such as sterile connectors with porous membranes) must be excluded

Process analogy: submarine pressure test

Imagine placing a SUS bag in a sealed chamber, pulling a vacuum (lowering the external pressure), and then filling the bag with helium. If the bag has a hole, helium escapes into the vacuum environment of the chamber and is picked up by the mass spectrometer. Like a submarine pressure test, wherever gas leaks out, the instrument knows.

Process diagram (text version): [SUS bag assembly] | v Place in sealed test chamber | v Pull vacuum (lower external pressure) | v Fill bag with helium | v Hold for a set time | v Mass spectrometer detects leaking helium | ┌──┴──┐ | | PASS FAIL (no leak)(leak detected)

Masking Effect

If the bag deforms under the pressure differential during testing, it can cover the defect location and cause a false negative. The solution is to install constraint plates and porous spacers in the test chamber so the bag keeps its shape under mechanical support.

Practical points for CDMOs

The SUS bag manifold systems in biopharma plants often have dozens of connections: barb-tube, film-film and film-fitment. Helium testing is the only method that can give a deterministic integrity verification of the entire system in one test, which makes it suitable as part of release testing.

5.1.2 Test Equipment and Parameters

The helium test apparatus includes a vacuum test chamber, pressure sensors, a vacuum pump, and a path providing the helium to escape. The chamber can optionally be equipped with restraining plates and porous spacers constraining the bag volume. The test chamber is connected via two lines: the first line evacuates the test chamber and, in the process, carries any helium molecules in the test chamber to the detector; the second line introduces helium into the bag assembly undergoing the test.

Typically, this test is performed in a vacuum chamber with low test pressures, at room temperature. The test time varies depending on the complexity and/or volume of the bag design being tested. The critical parameters impacting the test results are defect size, helium permeability of a single-use bag wall, test pressure, test temperature, bag-tubing surface area, helium injection volume, and test time (time during which helium is collected). Actual test parameters must be obtained by collecting data for defective versus nondefective bags that provide the highest difference between the two.

Helium molecules escaping through the defects are sensed by mass spectrometer and are related to a leak rate through a known defect size.

Key equipment components

  • Vacuum test chamber: holds the test article and maintains the vacuum environment
  • Pressure sensor: monitors chamber pressure to catch gross leaks early
  • Vacuum pump: evacuates the chamber environment
  • Mass spectrometer: detects helium molecules with high sensitivity
  • Dual-line system: one line evacuates the chamber and carries helium to the detector; the other fills the bag with helium

Critical Parameters affecting test results

  • Defect size
  • Helium permeability of the SUS bag wall
  • Test pressure
  • Test temperature
  • Surface area of the bag and tubing
  • Helium injection volume
  • Test time / collection time

All parameters must be determined by comparing data collected from nondefective and defective bags, with the goal of maximizing the separation between the two.

5.1.3 Leak Detection Limit

Helium testing can sense helium leak rates in the range of 10-8 to 10-4 atmosphere mbar L/s. The test sensitivity depends on the background helium leak rate of a nondefective bag assembly versus one with a defect. The greater the difference between the background leak rate of the nondefective bag assembly versus the defective bag assembly, the higher the confidence that a defect can be detected. Helium testing is capable of detecting 2 µm laser-drilled defects, as shown in Figure 5.1.3-1🔍 (examples of helium leak rates of a nondefective bag versus a defective bag). Just as design-specific characteristics like numbers of connections, surface area, and type of film and tubing material can highly impact the natural background leak rate of nondefective assemblies, an individual acceptance limit must be identified and proven with a statistically significant number of positive and negative controls.

Key concept for sensitivity

What really determines the sensitivity of the method is not the instrument's minimum detectable quantity but:

Leak rate of a defective bag − background leak rate of a nondefective bag = signal-to-noise ratio (S/N)

The background leak rate is affected by:

  • Number of connections (more connections, higher background)
  • Bag film surface area (larger area, more helium permeation)
  • Helium permeability of the film and tubing materials

Implications for validation strategy

Each SUS bag design must have its own acceptance limit, supported by a statistically sufficient number of positive controls (with known defects) and negative controls. This cannot be applied directly from one product to another.

5.1.4 Considerations and Challenges

Instrument performance can be verified by using standard calibrated helium leaks. A helium leak rate through the actual test sample with a known defect size versus a helium leak rate through nondefective samples helps determine the smallest detectable defect for a given test unit. Helium testing provides a quantitative leak rate. The test can detect the presence of multiple defects, but not the location of a defect.

The preferred test method uses test units with reasonable gas barrier properties. If the test unit is highly permeable to helium, then sensitivity drops significantly. The risk of false positives can be minimized by incorporating an in-line leak in the instrument to ensure that the sensor is in good working condition during each test cycle.

Five key considerations

  • Instrument performance verification: use calibrated helium leaks to verify instrument status
  • Quantitative leak rate: helium testing gives a quantitative value, not just pass/fail
  • Multiple defects: it can detect the presence of multiple defects but cannot locate them
  • Highly helium-permeable materials: if the bag film is highly permeable to helium, sensitivity drops significantly
  • False-positive control: fit an in-line leak in the instrument to confirm the sensor is working in every test cycle, reducing the risk of false positives

Analogy: it confirms, but cannot locate

Helium testing is like knowing someone in the room is talking without knowing who. It can confirm that "there is a leak" and how large it is, but finding the leak location needs other methods (such as visual inspection or segmented testing).

5.2

Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES)

Headspace gas analysis for deterministic CCI | p28–30

Original Text — English

The emission spectrum of a chemical element or compound (atom or molecule) is the spectrum of frequencies of electromagnetic radiation emitted due to the atom or molecule making a transition from high energy state to a lower energy state. The energy of the emitted photon is equal to the difference between the two states. For each atom, there are many possible electron transitions, and each transition has a specific energy difference. This collection of different transitions, leading to different radiated wavelengths, makes up an emission spectrum. Each element's emission spectrum is unique; therefore, optical emission spectroscopy (OES) can be used to identify the elements in matter of unknown composition or to perform chemical analysis of substances.

OES analysis does not require any specific tracer gas to measure total leakage on sealed containers. Instead, the gas mixture naturally present in the container headspace of the primary container is used to perform high-sensitivity tests over a large detection range.

Commentary

Basic Physics of OES

Each chemical element emits electromagnetic radiation at a unique frequency after excitation, like an "elemental fingerprint". OES uses this property to analyze the composition of gas leaking out of a container in a vacuum environment.

Analogy: flame colors

Different metals burn with different flame colors (sodium = yellow, copper = green, potassium = violet). OES works on the same principle, using spectral lines at specific wavelengths to identify which gas is leaking.

The biggest advantage of OES

No tracer gas needs to be filled in beforehand! It uses the gases already present in the container headspace (N2, CO2, O2, water vapor) as detection markers. This makes OES better suited than traditional tracer gas methods for direct application to finished, sealed products.

5.2.1 Application

The OES equipment measures the total leak rate for a specific gas from a sample under vacuum. The test method is deterministic, nondestructive, easy to use, easy to set up, and highly sensitive.

OES has been developed to measure or check the CCI of a variety of sterile CCS such as pouches, vials, prefilled syringes, and cartridges. In the case of prefilled liquid products, water leaks coming from the surface and air leaks coming from the air headspace can potentially be measured simultaneously. The ability to analyze the molecular signature of the leaking gas helps troubleshoot the source and location of the leak (e.g., for a CO2-filled liquid package, a higher level of CO2 indicates headspace leakage). In addition, the molecular signature may reduce false rejects (e.g., for dry products filled with nitrogen, an unusually high level of hydrogen may suggest moisture leakage into the chamber due to chamber-seal leakage).

For sterile glass products, the low outgassing rate of the glass vials and syringes allows testing of the products in batches of several syringes, potentially increasing the throughput while maintaining the high sensitivity of the test.

Applicable package types

  • Pouches
  • Vials
  • Prefilled syringes
  • Cartridges

Dual value of the molecular signature

Use 1: locating the leak source
CO2-filled liquid package: elevated CO2 = headspace leak

Use 2: reducing false positives
Nitrogen-filled dry product: abnormally elevated hydrogen = chamber seal leak (not a product leak), which avoids a misjudgment

Batch testing advantage for glass products

Glass has a very low outgassing rate, so multiple syringes can be placed in the chamber at once for batch testing, improving productivity while maintaining high sensitivity. This is especially valuable for high-volume injectable production lines.

5.2.2 Test Equipment and Parameters

The OES equipment includes a certified calibrated leak, which is used for the calibration. The leakage is measured in leak flow unit of mbar·L/s.

The OES sensor is connected directly to a test chamber in which the container to be analyzed is loaded. The test chamber is evacuated and, at low pressure (<5×10-2 mbar), the OES sensor is able to generate plasma. The excited atoms and ions in the discharge plasma generate light through numerous characteristic emission spectral lines generated by the elements in the gas mixture and captured by a camera. The wavelength and intensity (amplitude) of each emission line is analyzed. The emission intensity (amplitude) is dependent on the partial pressure (mol ratio) of the related element in the gas mixture, and change in intensity over time is proportional to leakage and/or outgassing.

Testing time is a direct consequence of the desired LoD in combination with the total outgassing flow, which depends on the materials under vacuum and the relative humidity in the ambient air trapped on the container.

The test sequence has three major steps:

  1. Rough Evacuation Step. The chamber starts at barometric conditions and is evacuated down to a few mbar. At the end of this step, if the set vacuum level is not reached due to a gross leak, the test will stop and a proper test failure message will be displayed.
  2. High-Vacuum Evacuation Step. When the pressure inside the test chamber reaches the maximum allowed pressure for the turbo pump (typically a few mbar), the OES sensor is in contact with the test chamber and pressure can be measured. It takes one or two seconds to reach steady measurement due to pressure fluctuations.
  3. Measurement Step. The OES sensor is measuring continuously while the pressure is continuously decreasing. The test duration has been defined during the calibration of the equipment in order to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio large enough to reach a good capability.

Three steps of OES testing

Step 1: Rough Evacuation Pump down from atmospheric pressure to a few mbar → If there is a gross leak, the target vacuum cannot be reached → Test aborts and displays an error Step 2: High-Vacuum Evacuation Pressure drops to < 5×10⁻² mbar → The OES sensor starts to communicate with the chamber → Wait 1-2 seconds for pressure to stabilize Step 3: Measurement The OES sensor measures continuously → Excites a plasma → A camera captures the characteristic spectral lines → Analyzes wavelength and intensity (amplitude) → Intensity change over time ∝ leak rate

Key physical relationships

Emission intensity ∝ partial pressure of that element in the gas mixture

The rate of change of intensity over time ∝ leak rate + outgassing

Test time is determined jointly by the required LOD and the outgassing rate of the material

Calibration requirements

The equipment must be fitted with a certified calibrated leak, and the calibration procedure includes:

  • A negative sample
  • A calibrated leak containing the appropriate gas

An initial set-up calibration is required again for each new product.

5.2.3 Leak Detection Limit

The instrument measures the leak rate for any gas in a container (e.g., oxygen, nitrogen or even water vapor) escaping from a leaking sample under vacuum. Because the test is performed under high vacuum, the driving parameter of the OES method sensitivity is the outgassing rate of the container material in contact with or exposed to high vacuum. The smaller the leak, the longer the outgassing takes for creating a measurable pressure difference, and this rate or better the amount of gas escaping from a leak determines as a consequence the LoD for a given test time. Due to the physical constraints under those high-vacuum conditions, water exists only in vapor form, which enhances the LoD towards smaller leak sizes when the leak is filled with water because an evaporating droplet of water creates significantly more gas as if it would escape from the head space of a container. In other words, in the case of a small dry leak, compared to the same small leak with liquid behind it, due to a constant water evaporation during the measurement cycle, the amount of gas escaping through the leak is far higher and the detection limit for those holes is also shifted toward much smaller hole sizes.

The upper detection limit, the limit of how large a leak can be to become detected with a differential pressure method, depends on the size of the headspace volume compared to the amount of liquid in the container. If a leak is very large relative to the container size and fill level during this evacuation cycle, all gas and water vapor could be evacuated before the test cycle can start. Under these conditions, the test will produce a false-positive detection, which means a leaking product can pass as an integral product because the container is already empty. Usually those conditions do not occur on all container and fill level variations, but it should be considered as a risk factor before choosing the method.

Factors determining the LOD

The sensitivity (LOD) of OES is determined by the outgassing rate of the container material, not by the limits of the instrument itself. The lower the outgassing rate, the lower the background noise and the smaller the leak that can be detected.

Special effect of liquid in the leak

Under high-vacuum conditions, water exists only as vapor. When there is liquid in the leak path:

  • Liquid water keeps evaporating, continuously generating a large amount of water vapor
  • The amount of gas passing through the leak is much higher than for a dry leak
  • The LOD shifts toward smaller hole sizes (more sensitive)

Therefore, for prefilled syringes or liquid drug products, OES is more sensitive than it is for dry products.

Risk: a large leak can pass the test

If the leak is very large, all the gas and water vapor are already pumped out during the evacuation stage, and by the measurement step the container is empty, so OES detects no difference in signal → false negative (the leaker passes as intact)

Recommendation: evaluate this risk before choosing this method and, where necessary, add visual inspection (to check for empty or deformed containers).

5.2.4 Considerations and Challenges

A number of factors should be considered when using OES, such as safety considerations and the design of the vacuum chamber. Local safety rules and regulations should be followed at all times, and particular attention should be paid when handling toxic drugs, especially if large leaks may be present.

Design of the vacuum chamber affects both testing and packaging. Proper chamber design is critical to contain liquid spills and minimize test volume. Tests are performed at high vacuum (under 5×10-2 bar-absolute), during which large-molecule drugs may plug the leak path; consequently, this method should be qualified for the specific drug being tested. In addition, flexible packages, or packages with moving parts (e.g., prefilled syringes), must have proper vacuum chamber design to constrain expansion from causing damage to the package. Flexible packages with large outgassing, such as IV bags, should be tested based on the specific application, as outgassing depends on the package material.

Four main challenges and considerations

  • Safety: when handling toxic drugs, a large leak raises the exposure risk for operators, so local safety regulations must be followed
  • Chamber design: needs liquid-spill protection and a minimized test volume
  • Large-molecule drugs: under high vacuum, large molecules may block the leak path and affect detection, so the method must be validated for the specific drug
  • Flexible packaging / moving parts: the plunger of a prefilled syringe may move under vacuum and damage the package, so the chamber must have an appropriate restraint design

Key judgment for a CDMO choosing OES

OES is ideal for glass containers (vials, syringes) because glass has a low outgassing rate. If the client's product is an IV bag or another high-outgassing material, first evaluate whether it will interfere with the test results. Every change of drug formulation requires revalidation.

5.3

Airborne Ultrasound

Through-transmission seal integrity for flexible and porous packaging | p31–33

Original Text — English

Ultrasonic waves are high-frequency sound waves that exhibit behaviors characteristic of sound waves. When a boundary exists between two media with varying acoustic impedances, the ultrasonic waves are partially reflected and partially transmitted through one medium into the other. Additionally, refraction, absorption, and diffraction occur at various instances. Airborne ultrasound is a point-focused signal that pulsates at 500 pulses per second.

Commentary

Basic Physics of Ultrasound

Ultrasound is a high-frequency sound wave. At the interface between two media with different acoustic impedance, the following occur:

  • Partial reflection
  • Partial transmission
  • Refraction
  • Absorption
  • Diffraction

Airborne ultrasound is a point-focused signal pulsed 500 times per second.

5.3.1 Application

Airborne ultrasound, a seal integrity test, is a "through transmission technology" that transmits ultrasound through a material and receives the signal on the opposite side of the material. Since, porous packaging film allows the passage of gases (such as air) while maintaining a sterile barrier, traditional vacuum decay or tracer gas leak tests cannot be used to ensure its integrity. Airborne ultrasound seal inspection allows the seals of porous packaging to be inspected online, quantitatively and nondestructively.

Porous sterile barrier packaging is one of a variety of flexible package formats available. Creating a functional flexible barrier package typically requires bonding two films together to create a flexible package structure, then sealing it once it has been filled with the product. Each of the pouch seals is critical to the integrity of the pouch, and measuring the quality of those seals is a means of ensuring the integrity of the flexible packaging.

Visual inspection of both porous and nonporous flexible packaging assumes that all defects are visible. The quality of a seal is dependent only on physical properties; therefore, the visual appearance of a seal area does not ensure the integrity of the seal. Heat-sealing bars can leave a seal imprint on the outside of a package, while the inner seal fails to reach bonding temperature and never seals completely or has a weak peel strength.

Why do porous packages need ultrasound?

Porous sterile barrier packaging (such as Tyvek pouches) lets gas pass through to maintain the sterile barrier, but this also means:

  • Vacuum decay: cannot be used (gas permeates the membrane material)
  • Tracer gas methods: cannot be used (tracer gas escapes through the bulk of the membrane)
  • Airborne ultrasound: can be used (it detects the physical bonding quality of the seal area, not gas leakage)

The blind spot of visual inspection

A good-looking seal is not necessarily an intact seal! A heat-sealing bar can leave a perfect seal mark on the outside of the pouch while the inner layers are not fully bonded. This kind of seal, good outside and bad inside, can only be detected by ultrasound.

Main application scenarios

  • Online inspection (scanning immediately after sealing)
  • Seal quality verification of Tyvek sterile pouches
  • Seal strength screening of flexible barrier packaging
5.3.2 Test Equipment and Parameters

The airborne ultrasound system consists of an ultrasound sensor head and a signal processing system. The sensor head is used to pulse sound at a bonded film material and measure the signal strength on the opposing side. The ultrasound signal processor gathers the ultrasound pulsing measurements and processes the measurement into quantitative summary data for evaluation.

The sound is focused on an active area relative to frequency (or wavelength) of the sound. The active area determines the resolution of the focused measurement. Airborne ultrasound is primarily suited for film materials, providing an acoustic signature of bond materials or material characteristics based on thickness, density, and material uniformity.

The principle of ultrasound measurement relates to double-paned, soundproof windows. A single-pane window allows sound to propagate through the single pane and be measured on the opposing side. A double-pane window absorbs and reflects most of the sound waves, preventing the majority of sound from reaching the opposing side. Relating this principle to airborne ultrasound, the sound waves will propagate through a well-bonded seal (a single pane). The signal transmitted through a bonded seal is measured and used to determine seal quality. A weak seal, seal defect, or point of nonbonding will prevent the ultrasound from propagating through the seal. For multiple layers (no seal), the signal through the seal is blocked completely (like the double-paned window).

The ultrasound signal is a point-focused measurement. Test data comprises signal intensity associated with a position on the material or seal area and the decibel-level signal strength. Seal defects can create additional transitions of plastic-to-air or plastic-to-foreign contaminant, causing multiple reflections and refractions. Trapped air or sufficiently large solid contamination (≥1 mm) could also cause the signal to attenuate or decrease in strength. Alternatively, the presence of cuts or holes in the seal would result in a peak in the signal strength.

The measurement can be presented in a line graph format (Figure 5.3.2-3🔍), or a composite scan can pair line scans to create an optoacoustic image (Figure 5.3.2-4🔍). The optoacoustic image is a data matrix of ultrasonic signal strength data paired with X-Y coordinates. The data is presented in the form of a bitmap file, making the image quantitative in its base measurement.

While an optoacoustic image can be used to evaluate seal quality, the linear scan inspection mode is the most appropriate for discrete defect detection. The leak detection is based on processing a series of high-frequency data points and compiling the population of data into summary statistics for each scan. Summary statistics consist of the average signal, maximum signal, minimum signal, standard deviation of the signal, and length of the scan.

A low signal value or minimum value usually indicates the presence of a defect such as contamination, inclusions, delamination, voids, or no seal. A very high signal or maximum value indicates the presence of a cut or gap in the seal, or that the test was taken too close to the edge of the sample.

The system uses the statistical summary data to determine pass/fail. If any statistical value falls outside its acceptance limit, the result is a fail. If all statistical values are within their limits, the result is a pass.

5.3.3 Leak Detection Limit

Defect detection using airborne ultrasound is based on the quantitative assessments of a scanned seal. As described in Section 5.3.2, the defect is detected by breaching a set-point limit for a summary statistic; defects can also be visually apparent in graphical form. The ability of the airborne ultrasound to detect a defect is based on a variety of factors. Defects that can be detected are typically cold seals, channel defects, wrinkles, areas of nonbonding, poor seals, material variations, seal variations, cuts, or holes. The ability to detect these defects is primarily dependent on the physics of ultrasound and the material properties.

Detectable defect types

  • Cold Seals: sealing temperature too low to reach the bonding temperature
  • Channel Defects: a channel in the seal connects to the outside
  • Wrinkles: film creases cause an uneven seal
  • Non-bonding: areas where sealing was not completed
  • Material Variations: uneven film density or thickness
  • Cuts/Holes: physical damage in the seal area

Detection capability depends mainly on the physical properties of ultrasound and the material properties.

5.3.4 Considerations and Challenges

Two additional factors that need to be evaluated before an application can be considered are the package contents and the seal geometry. With package contents, many seal defects occur due to product inclusion in the seal area. Liquids can propagate an ultrasonic signal effectively from one material to another, which means the sound would transfer through a defect if liquid were present in the defect. The presence of liquids in the seal can produce test results similar to that of a good seal.

Seal geometry can also affect seal inspection. The ultrasonic signal is a focused beam of sound, and approximately 90% of the sound remains within the target focal point. Some sound energy resides outside the focal point. If a seal is located on the edge of the material and a scan is performed on the edge of the material, sound will travel around the edge and will be measured on the receiving side. The ultrasonic sensor must be within 2 mm to 3 mm inside the pouch to produce a useful ultrasonic signal response. As the sensor nears the edge of a seal, the ultrasonic signal is impacted by sound reflecting within the seal, as well as sound passing around a seal edge to reach the receiver. These two factors limit the ability of an ultrasound to scan near the edge of a seal.

Two main challenges

1. Product Inclusion

If product liquid is trapped in the seal area (a common cause of seal defects), the liquid conducts the ultrasound signal effectively, so even when the seal is incomplete the signal may look like a good seal → risk of false negatives

2. Seal Geometry

The sensor must scan at least 2-3 mm inside the edge of the pouch to produce a valid signal. Close to the edge, sound waves go around the seal edge and reach the receiver directly, interfering with interpretation.

Analogy: sound traveling through water

The acoustic impedance of water is much lower than that of air, so sound travels more easily in water than in air. If the defect channel is filled with liquid, ultrasound passes through as smoothly as it would through water, and the instrument wrongly concludes that the seal is intact. This is why ultrasound has a blind spot for seal defects with liquid inclusion.

X-Ray Detection

Physical attribute evaluation for glass containers and assembled devices | p34–37

Original Text — English

X-ray detection may not be considered a leak test, as it neither quantitatively nor qualitatively evaluates leak rates or leakage; however, X-ray technologies support CCI evaluations in a variety of ways. Technological breakthroughs in manufacturing of less costly and more sensitive large detector arrays, as well as the increased acceptability of products exposed to X-ray radiation, have resulted in an increased potential for its use.

X-radiation is a form of electromagnetic radiation that has a wavelength in the range of 0.01 nm to 10 nm, corresponding to frequencies in the range of 30 PHz to 30 EHz and energies in the range of 100 eV to 100 keV. X-rays are shorter in wavelength than UV rays and longer than gamma rays. X-rays can be generated by an X-ray tube, a vacuum tube that uses a high voltage to accelerate the electrons released by a hot cathode to a high velocity. The high velocity electrons collide with a metal target, the anode, creating the X-rays.

Commentary

Key positioning: X-ray is not a leak test

X-ray detection evaluates leak rate neither quantitatively nor qualitatively. It is a supporting CCI assessment tool used to identify physical defects that may lead to leakage (such as glass cracks or poor stopper fit), not to measure leakage directly.

In practice it is usually used together with a true leak test (such as helium testing).

Physical Properties of X-Rays

  • Wavelength: 0.01–10 nm
  • Frequency: 30 PHz–30 EHz
  • Energy: 100 eV–100 keV
  • Shorter wavelength than UV, longer than gamma rays
  • Generation: X-ray tube (high-voltage accelerated electrons bombarding a metal anode)
5.4.1 Application

X-ray is applied to package systems to evaluate physical attributes of the test system that may not be visible to the naked eye or even to some visual inspection systems. For this reason, X-ray detection, in its current state, is best used as a supporting technology rather than a leak test, as a supplemental means of characterizing a package system with respect to CCI. Physical attributes may include defects conducive to leakage, such as poor stopper fit or cracks in a glass vial or syringe, but not necessarily the propensity for gas or liquid to leak, or flow, through such defects.

Where X-ray fits

X-ray can detect physical defects that the naked eye or standard vision systems cannot see:

  • Cracks in glass vials/syringes (glass cracks)
  • Poor stopper fit
  • Glass defects under the aluminum cap (defects under crimp)
  • Functional defects inside assembled devices

Note: X-ray can show that a defect exists, but it cannot prove that gas or liquid would actually leak through that defect.

5.4.2 Test Equipment and Parameters

For pharmaceutical CCI applications, an X-ray image can be produced in a number of ways. The first is a line scan technique (Figure 5.4.2-1🔍), where the object to be inspected is slowly moved through a plane of X-ray radiation and the picture is created in a linear X-ray-sensitive diode array. This technique is called fan or line-beam X-ray scanning. The advantage is high resolution, although from a very low speed, and the scan speed defines the resolution.

A variation of this scanning technique known as X-ray tomography, or CT (computerized tomography) scan, employs the use of multiple X-ray scans at different angles to reconstruct a 3-D volume rendering of the external and, importantly, internal geometries of the sample. CT scans have the advantage of high resolution and complete 3-D rendering, enabling the operator to evaluate a single package from an array of angles. However, a single scan may span minutes, hours, or even days depending on instrument capability and resolution.

Another technique for X-ray analysis is the area X-ray camera, which works much like a simple photography camera. Here, the object will be penetrated by the radiation, attenuated by the object, and the result captured as a gray-scale image on a large X-ray-sensitive diode array (See Figure 5.4.2-2🔍). This scanning process produces 2-D as well as 3-D pictures.

The area imaging technique is fast and reliable because the pictures are taken during a complete standstill of sample and beam. With this technology, a picture can be produced and evaluated within 100 ms or faster.

There are two types of X-ray tubes. The first is the closed-tube design, which produces radiation with a focus between 800 µm and 1500 µm. The most useful application field, as it pertains to CCI evaluation, is referred to as the microfocus tube design, which has a focus between 1 µm and 5 µm.

5.4.4 Considerations and Challenges

An increasingly popular application of X-ray technology in CCI occurs in the package design and development stages. X-ray might be used to support package component selection and assembly parameters, or even to evaluate the impact of storage conditions, such as frozen conditions, on sealing geometries. Low temperature storage is known to contribute to stopper shrinkage as a glass transition state (Tg) is reached; see Section 4.4. This effect may be characterized by X-ray imaging. These efforts are typically performed in tandem with a true leak test, such as helium leak detection or headspace analysis.

In a 2016 study, Mathaes et al. performed a comprehensive capping optimization study, evaluating correlations between capping settings, stopper compression, residual seal force, and CCI as tested by helium leak detection. CT scans using X-rays were used to both calculate stopper compression, a critical assembly parameter, and provide visualization of sealing geometries for a range of stoppers.

In a 2016 study by Nieto et al., CT scans were used to visually and geometrically explain a difference in leak test results between two stopper groups stored at frozen conditions. CT scans identified gaps between the flip-off cap and the elastomer, as well as the outer edge of the land seal in the group with fewer ideal leak results.

CDMO Validation Strategy & Regulatory Considerations

Practical guidance for implementing innovative CCI methods in a pharmaceutical contract manufacturing context

Validation Framework for Innovative CCI Methods

General Validation Requirements

All CCIT methods, whether deterministic or probabilistic, require a documented method validation package before use in GMP release or in-process testing. For the four innovative methods in Section 5, the following validation elements are universally applicable:

  1. Sensitivity / LOD determination — establish the smallest detectable defect using positive controls with known defect sizes (e.g., laser-drilled pinholes of 2 µm, 5 µm, 10 µm).
  2. Specificity — demonstrate that the test discriminates between defective and nondefective units with statistical confidence (false positive rate and false negative rate).
  3. Repeatability and reproducibility — run the same sample multiple times and across different operators/instruments to confirm consistency.
  4. Robustness — evaluate the effect of small but deliberate changes in key parameters (pressure, temperature, test time) on test outcome.
  5. Calibration traceability — all reference leaks and calibrated standards must be traceable to national or international standards.

Reference Standards

  • USP <1207> Package Integrity Evaluation — Sterile Products
  • ASTM F2338 (vacuum decay), F2981 (OES), F3287 (ultrasound)
  • ISO 11607 (sterile barrier systems)
  • PDA TR27 (pharmaceutical package integrity)
  • FDA Guidance: Container Closure Integrity Testing in lieu of Sterility Testing

Method Qualification vs. Validation

For new container/closure systems introduced by a CDMO client, a full method validation is expected. For minor changes to an already-validated container (e.g., different vial size but same material), a method qualification or verification study may be sufficient, subject to risk assessment and regulatory strategy.

Commentary — CDMO Validation in Practice

Essential elements of a validation package

  • Establishing sensitivity/LOD: use positive control samples with known defect sizes (such as laser-drilled holes) to establish the minimum detectable defect
  • Specificity: statistically distinguish defective from nondefective samples (false-positive and false-negative rates)
  • Repeatability and reproducibility: test the same sample multiple times and confirm consistency across operators and instruments
  • Robustness: evaluate the effect of small variations in critical parameters on the results
  • Calibration traceability: all reference leaks must be traceable to national or international standards

Regulatory submission strategy

When a CCIT method is written into the CMC (Chemistry, Manufacturing and Controls) section, the applicant needs to provide:

  • Method description and principle
  • Summary of validation data (LOD, sensitivity, specificity)
  • Scientific justification for the positive control defect size
  • Ongoing monitoring plan

FDA and EMA show a stronger preference for deterministic methods, which gives helium testing and OES an advantage in regulatory review.

Common Audit Questions for Innovative CCI Methods

  • How was the positive control defect size selected, and what is the scientific rationale for it representing a relevant microbiological ingress risk?
  • What is the background leak rate of a nondefective assembly, and how does it compare to the defective assembly signal?
  • How is instrument calibration verified before each production test run?
  • What is the frequency of requalification when container/closure components change?
  • How are out-of-specification (OOS) CCIT results investigated and dispositioned?
  • Has the method been qualified for the specific drug product formulation (especially for OES with large-molecule biologics)?

Common Inspection Questions — Key Points for Prepared Answers

  • Rationale for the positive control defect size: usually based on microbial ingress studies or literature data, confirming that the size is relevant to sterility risk
  • Background leak rate vs. defect signal: statistical data are needed to show adequate separation between the two (signal separation)
  • Instrument calibration frequency: calibration status should be confirmed and recorded before each production test
  • Revalidation triggers: container/closure component changes, supplier changes, and after major equipment repair
  • OOS CCIT handling: a written OOS investigation procedure is needed, clearly defining when to quarantine the batch and when to perform additional testing

6.0 Additional Considerations for Package Integrity Profiling

Lifecycle stressors from manufacturing through distribution

Original Text — Section 6.0

A robust package integrity profile for a given product-package system considers factors experienced throughout the product's lifecycle as required by USP <1207>. As such, a package integrity profile must consider anticipated or experienced stresses, including those resulting from transportation and distribution. Such stresses may include shock, vibration, compression, temperature, pressure, and general package storage environment.

Key Concept: Package Integrity Profile

A package integrity profile is the comprehensive, lifecycle-spanning record of a product-package system's integrity status. It encompasses results from development, manufacturing, stability, and distribution phases. USP <1207> requires that this profile account for all stresses the package will experience throughout its life.

Commentary — Section 6.0

A robust Package Integrity Profile must take into account all the factors the product-package system may be exposed to over its entire lifecycle; this is a USP <1207> expectation. The package integrity profile must cover the stresses that are expected or that actually occur, including:

  • Shock/Vibration: physical impact during transport
  • Compression: pressure from stacked storage
  • Temperature: cold chain and temperature excursions
  • Pressure: the low-pressure environment of air transport
  • Storage Environment: humidity, UV light, etc.

Everyday Analogy

The "package integrity profile" is like a passport: it records every journey and ordeal of the product-package system from "birth" (development) to "retirement" (after use). Regulators expect you to be able to explain clearly: is this package still intact at every stage of its life?

6.1

6.1 Transportation and Distribution

Distribution chain stressors unique to each product-package system

Original Text — Section 6.1

A package integrity profile is unique to each product-package system, a concept that also applies to storage and distribution considerations. The types of stresses experienced in these lifecycle phases vary widely with dosage form, pack-out configuration, product requirements, and distribution chain. Additionally, the impact of any single stress factor, such as a temperature excursion, can vary with respect to product. An excursion critical to one product may be insubstantial to the quality of another.

Given the potential impact to product quality and sterility, appropriate challenges and assessments should be made to ensure that product quality is not affected during the transportation and distribution circuit. The types of challenge conditions and subsequent assessments performed, however, reflect the properly assessed risks for the system in question.

As the intent of this report is to focus on package integrity, only those distribution challenges impacting integrity are discussed here. Transportation and distribution challenges are not limited solely to integrity of the package, however. A fully integral package containing a proteinaceous product may experience vibration and shock that does not lead to integrity failure but, for example, may cause aggregation of protein. Thus, in addition to post-challenge CCIT, other assessments may be necessary to evaluate the impact of distribution on overall product quality.

Commentary — Section 6.1

Distribution stressors vary enormously with dosage form, package format, product characteristics and distribution chain. A temperature excursion, for example, can be fatal to some protein formulations yet have almost no effect on another type of product.

Important reminder: integrity is not the whole of product quality

TR86 focuses on package integrity, but remember that a package that is physically intact (no leak) can still suffer quality problems, such as protein aggregation caused by vibration. In addition to CCIT, other quality assessments are therefore needed (for example, SE-HPLC to measure protein aggregation).

The Distribution Circuit Concept

The "distribution circuit" covers the whole journey from the manufacturing site through warehouses, wholesalers, logistics centers and retail pharmacies to the patient. The stress conditions at every link should be evaluated and incorporated into the integrity profile.

6.1.1 Physical Stressors

Original Text — Section 6.1.1

Perhaps the most obvious challenges experienced during distribution are physical stresses such as shock, vibration, and compression. Often, these types of events result in gross leakage, for instance, a vial shattering. This type of gross defect is easily detected and generally prevents final use of the product. The formation of cracks, checks, chips, and other small defects, however, may result in leakage that negatively impacts product quality but is not readily detectable by the end user. Thus, these types of defects present a more latent and use-specific risk.

Furthermore, such types of defects may result in more serious integrity concerns further along in the distribution chain, as a check may develop into a crack, or a crack may widen and permit liquid loss or microbial ingress.

Consequently, to evaluate and reduce risk associated with a shipment, not only the primary package, but the secondary and tertiary packaging should be considered. Numerous standards are available from such organizations as ASTM and the International Safe Transit Association (ISTA) to reliably and reproducibly subject package systems to physical stresses that replicate a variety of distribution chains, including via truck, rail, air, sea, or others.

The effects of temperature on packaging and distribution is also discussed in Section 4.4 on Cryogenic Conditions.

Commentary — Section 6.1.1

Physical stressors are the most intuitive risk in distribution, but the degree of harm varies widely:

The Concept of "Latent Risk"

The danger of a check (fine crack) is that it is a "time bomb": at manufacture it may be only a minor defect, but during distribution it can grow into a crack and ultimately lead to microbial ingress or leakage of the drug solution. This is why the entire packaging system (primary, secondary and tertiary packaging) must be evaluated.

ISTA Standards

ISTA (International Safe Transit Association) provides a series of standardized distribution simulation tests. ISTA 2A is a commonly used transport simulation standard covering test conditions such as vibration, shock and stacking compression, and it can reproduce different distribution chains such as road and air transport.

6.1.2 Pressure

Original Text — Section 6.1.2

In cases where a low-pressure environment could present challenges to the package and product it contains, evaluation of the pressure differentials in a distribution chain could be critical. This is particularly relevant in cases of air shipment, where product may be stored in cargo areas with partial to nonexistent pressurization that, if left unevaluated, may result in unintended consequences. The ability of a sealed flexible container to resist creep or burst during air shipment is an example of risk that should be characterized.

Another example, one discussed with increasing frequency as the prevalence of prefilled syringes and prefilled syringe-based devices expands, is the concept of plunger movement. Although the degree to which plunger movement occurs is impacted by fill level, headspace volume, general package design, and pressure differentials experienced, how a low-pressure environment may impact product sterility and quality in these types of systems must also be characterized. As a plunger moves backward, the seal, and possibly the product, may move into an unsterile area of the container. In extreme cases, the plunger may completely exit the barrel.

Commentary — Section 6.1.2

Air transport is the most critical scenario for pressure assessment. The cabin pressure in the cargo hold of a commercial freighter is about 75% of ground pressure (about 0.75 atm), equivalent to the ambient pressure at an altitude of 8,000 feet. This pressure differential can affect many types of packaging:

Pressure risk: plunger movement in prefilled syringes

In a prefilled syringe (PFS), low pressure can cause plunger retraction:

  • The plunger moves back, exposing the sealing interface to a non-sterile area
  • In extreme cases the plunger can come completely out of the barrel
  • The extent of the effect depends on fill volume, headspace volume, package design and the size of the pressure differential

Analogy: a water bottle on an airplane

Have you noticed that a sealed water bottle dents slightly with the cabin pressure changes when you fly? The same principle applies: flexible packaging (such as pouches) may "inflate", and the plunger of a sealed prefilled syringe may be "pulled out". This is why pressure assessment for air transport cannot be ignored.

6.1.3 Testing Approaches

Original Text — Section 6.1.3

Numerous documents from internationally recognized organizations (e.g., ASTM, ISTA, ICH, ISO) are available for reference and provide reproducible ways of evaluating some of these stressors, including physical shock events, thermal cycling, and pressure cycling. However, the simulated distribution chain should reflect the anticipated stressors. Standards are meant to employ reproducible simulations but may not account for the variations and intricacies of specific distribution networks, such as iterative air shipments or a combination of different modes of transport. As with the application of many test methods and techniques, a proper assessment of the risks should inform decisions on evaluating such risks.

Commentary — Section 6.1.3

The purpose of using standardized test methods (ISTA, ASTM, ICH, ISO) is to ensure reproducibility, but standards are not a cure-all:

Limitations of Standards

  • Standards such as ISTA provide simulations of "generic scenarios" and may not fully reproduce the complexity of a specific distribution network
  • For example, in multimodal transport such as "road, then air, then road again", the standards may not cover the cumulative effect of the stresses in each leg
  • Key principle: the simulation test must reflect the actual expected stress conditions, rather than blindly applying a standard

6.2 100% Online Testing

In-line CCIT at fill/finish for every container

Original Text — Section 6.2

A holistic approach to controlling CCI should be taken to ensure package integrity at the time of manufacture as well as over the shelf life of the product. This holistic approach includes consideration of:

  • CCIT method (in-line, near-line, off-line)
  • Quality of primary package components
  • Primary package design
  • Manufacturing process qualification
  • Product manufacturing process
  • Change control process
  • Shelf-life assessment

The CCIT method is a critical part of the control strategy, and an in-line test can make the overall approach more robust. However, the in-line test should not be the sole point of control.

In-line testing is defined as 100% testing of all filled and sealed primary packages for CCI during the production process, after the final sealing operation.

Currently, some regulatory requirements state that all container types fused and filled on the filling line (e.g., ampules, blow-fill-seal containers) must be 100% inspected. For other products, no current regulatory requirement exists for 100% in-line testing. The decision to conduct 100% in-line testing for other products should be based on risk assessment, as part of a holistic approach.

A package that is accepted at the in-line test station is not guaranteed to maintain its CCI during the shelf life and use of the product. Conversely, a package that is rejected by the in-line test station is guaranteed not to meet the product's CCI requirements. Therefore, the in-line test station acts as a filter for rejected packages, reducing the risk of CCI failures due to random process failure modes.

Consistent failure modes that are due to equipment malfunctioning or setup can be detected by other means, such as off-line quality control testing. A particular advantage of in-line CCIT compared to off-line quality control testing is the elimination (and cost savings) of handling suspect lots of products due to sample rejection at the off-line test station.

For high-speed lines, current technologies cannot test for the small-sized defects that off-line test methods can. However, since most process failure modes result in medium to large defects in the package, widening the reject limit for in-line testing is a reasonable and low-risk decision.

While it can add value in some situations, technology is not currently available to enable 100% in-line testing for all CCS and product types. Where feasible, regulations and quality risk management principles identified in ICH Quality Guideline Q9: Quality Risk Management should be used to determine if 100% in-line testing should be implemented.

Integrating 100% in-line testing into the manufacturing process requires a strong commitment. The in-line test station requires capital investment and the operational cost of the equipment, which adds to the cost of the product. Multiple test stations may be required on each filling line to overcome differences between line speeds and test station throughput.

No one test method fits all products. In-line test methods must be nondestructive for the specific product to be tested.

Commentary — Section 6.2

100% In-Line Testing means performing CCIT on every container on the production line after filling and closing, without omitting any. It is one of the core elements of a "holistic CCI control strategy".

The Two-Sided Logic of In-Line Testing

Passing the in-line test does not guarantee that CCI is maintained throughout the shelf life (the container may still fail later because of storage, distribution and other factors)

Failing the in-line test means the container is definitively non-conforming to CCI requirements (100% certain rejection)

The function of in-line testing is therefore that of a "filter": it removes containers that are definitely defective, but cannot guarantee the long-term integrity of those that pass.

In-line vs Off-line: The Key Cost-Benefit Difference

  • Off-line test failure: the whole batch is flagged as a "suspect batch" that must be quarantined, retested and investigated, at high cost
  • In-line test failure: only that single container is rejected and the batch is unaffected; feedback is immediate and production continues
  • The greatest advantage of in-line testing is therefore that it eliminates the cost of handling suspect batches

Analogy: airport security screening

100% in-line CCIT is like an airport security gate: every passenger must pass through, with no sampling. Passing does not necessarily mean there is no problem (for example, a well-hidden prohibited item), but anyone who fails definitely has a problem. Off-line QC testing is like random spot checks: efficient, but with a risk that some slip through.

Regulatory Requirements

EU GMP Annex 1 (2008/2022 versions) and EudraLex Volume 4 currently state explicitly that ampoules and blow-fill-seal (BFS) containers must undergo 100% container closure integrity testing. For other container types, whether 100% in-line testing is needed is decided by risk assessment.

6.3 Building a Quality by Design (QbD) Approach into the CCIT Program

Systematic, science-based, risk-driven framework for package integrity

Original Text — Section 6.3

Pharmaceutical QbD is a systematic approach to development that begins with predefined objectives and emphasizes product and process understanding and control based on sound science and risk management. A QbD concept can be built into a CCIT program, including such elements as:

  • Quality target product profile that identifies the critical quality attributes (CQAs) of the product-package system
  • Package design and understanding, including identification of critical material attributes that can affect package integrity
  • Process design and understanding, including identification of CPPs, linking the material attributes and process parameters to the CQAs
  • Control strategy that includes specifications for the product-package configuration with respect to CCI as well as controls during each step of the manufacturing process
  • Regular quality checks on process capability and continual improvement initiatives

One of the ways users can build QbD into a CCIT program is to employ a risk-based approach. A risk-based CCIT program is built on science-based decisions; it offers an ongoing database of product lifecycle CCIT results (also called a package integrity profile) and a risk management tool for package integrity assurance throughout the lifecycle of the product. The program should demonstrate CCI as a function of ongoing, operative variations that takes into consideration:

  • Design and material of the package
  • Package assembly
  • Processing conditions
  • Storage, distribution, and stability conditions

Key aspects for building a risk-based CCIT program using QbD principles:

  • CCI over the entire lifecycle of product: The CCIT tool kit needs to demonstrate CCI over the entire lifecycle of the product. More than one test may be employed during a given product's lifecycle based on the critical parameters to be controlled at that stage.
  • Choice of deterministic versus probabilistic methods: Deterministic methods are those where the leakage event is based on phenomena that follow a predictable chain of events. Probabilistic methods offer qualitative outcomes subject to the probability of a series of events.
  • Choice of off-line versus in-line, on-line or at-line test methods
  • Sampling: Scientifically valid sampling plans should be based on risk assessment in association with appropriate statistical criteria.
  • Defining the MALL: CCI is established when a package meets its maximum allowable leak limit. Product quality requirements define the MALL.
  • Designing a CCIT tool kit: The CCIT tool kit should encompass different test methods that can be employed to detect defect types critical to product quality and sterility.
  • Ensuring continuous improvement: In the event of a change to package design, material, or manufacturing processing conditions, the risk-based strategy should trigger a review of the CCIT tool kit.

Commentary — Section 6.3

QbD (Quality by Design) is the systematic development approach defined in ICH Q8: starting from predefined objectives and building a deep understanding of the product and process on the basis of science and risk management. Bringing QbD into the CCIT program means no longer reacting to defects but preventing them proactively.

Choosing Between Deterministic and Probabilistic Methods

  • Deterministic methods: based on predictable physical phenomena (such as helium leak rate and vacuum decay); results are quantitative and objective, and less preparation is needed
  • Probabilistic methods: results are qualitative and depend on the probability of a series of events occurring (such as microbial ingress testing); in some cases sensitivity is better, and information on defect location can be provided

The Central Role of the MALL

MALL (Maximum Allowable Leakage Limit) is the anchor point of the whole CCIT program. The selection of every test method and every LOD requirement must be premised on "reliably detecting defects equal to or smaller than the MALL". The MALL itself is derived from product quality requirements (such as maintaining sterility and dose accuracy).

6.3.1 Risk Assessment

Original Text — Section 6.3.1

A risk-based approach using the quality tools mentioned in ICH Q9 can be a very effective way to assess sterile package integrity and testing applications. Following the lifecycle phases, relevant package integrity parameters are considered to determine product-package quality intent and how to evaluate the critical parameters that effectively impact the integrity of the CCS.

A lifecycle assessment process provides ample time and opportunity to develop improved learning, better understanding, and ongoing generation of compelling data to support the risk of package integrity failure. This QbD approach consists of learning, evaluation, and control strategy determination. The impact or risk of an integrity failure is relevant to both patient and product.

A first step in risk assessment (e.g., using the Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points (HACCP) quality tool) is to identify each incremental process step and the appropriate materials relevant to that step. Next, a verification of the potential risk of failure (i.e., a leak) at the process step is made and a list drawn up of causal factors that might lead to each failure type. Another list is developed that indicates what controls may exist that could mitigate the risks. Subsequently, the risk tool can be used to determine what types of testing (monitoring) are valuable, how frequently testing should be performed, and where or what types of samples should be taken for evaluation.

Similar to Six Sigma thinking (define-measure-analyze-improve-control), the risk assessment process is an ongoing methodology for quality improvement.

Commentary — Section 6.3.1

Risk assessment for package integrity can borrow from the various tools introduced in ICH Q9:

Analogy: the Six Sigma DMAIC cycle

Package integrity risk assessment follows a cycle similar to Six Sigma:
Define → identify process steps and materials
Measure → assess failure risks and potential defects
Analyze → list failure causes and existing controls
Improve → develop the test strategy and design control points
Control → monitor continuously and build the integrity profile database

The Two Dimensions of Impact in Risk Assessment

The impact of a package integrity failure is not limited to the product; it also concerns patient safety:

  • On the product: contamination, oxidation, loss of potency, pH change
  • On the patient: infection (microbial ingress), overdose/underdose, adverse reactions

6.4 Bulk Container Lifecycle Approach

Single-use systems: from supplier manufacturing through user deployment

Original Text — Section 6.4

Bulk containers go through two distinct phases of lifecycle: First, the development phase that encompasses material selection, design, and qualification and, second, the product phase, from manufacturing steps through supply chain to point of use. QbD and QRM principles can be applied throughout both phases, during which specific risks are identified and evaluated using appropriate methods.

Increasingly, according to the criticality of their application, users are asking suppliers to conduct testing, or choose to do it themselves to mitigate the risk of leakage or contamination. The materials used for these applications are engineered polymers that must be specified and controlled appropriately up the supply chain to ensure consistency of product quality.

For the flexible container manufacturer, test methods may differ between the phases of the lifecycle. At the supplier site, specific test methods are used during the design phase to establish the capability for integrity assurance. The right materials in a laminate structure are selected and the welds and seals are designed for the intended use. Other tests are applied during the manufacturing phase, such as those used for in-process controls.

Commentary — Section 6.4

The lifecycle of a bulk container, particularly a single-use system (SUS) such as a bioprocess bag in biopharmaceutical manufacturing, covers two major phases, each with a different testing focus:

The Two Major Phases of the SUS Lifecycle

Phase 1: Development Phase

  • Material selection: specifications and compatibility of engineered polymers
  • Design qualification: verification of weld and seal design
  • Integrity testing at the supplier: establishing integrity assurance capability

Phase 2: Product Phase

  • Supply chain control: from shipment by the manufacturer to receipt by the user
  • Testing by the user before, during and after use
  • Supporting evidence for the sterility of the final product

The Regulatory Significance of Pre-use vs Post-use Testing

  • Pre-use Leak Testing: verifies that handling after receipt has not compromised integrity, supplementing the assurance given by the supplier's release testing

The ingredients (the SUS bag) leave the farm (the supplier), go through refrigerated transport (before arrival at the site), kitchen storage (assembly and holding), pre-use checks (visual inspection, leak test), the cooking process (use in the bioprocess), and finally a last confirmation (post-use test). Every step has a checkpoint, and a failure at any one of them can affect the final quality.

6.5 Educational Simulation: LOD and Method Selection

PharmaCo-A case study — screening, optimizing, and validating four leak-test technologies

Original Text — Section 6.5 Overview

In this textual illustration of the key role leak testing can play in the lifecycle of a parenteral product, fictional firm PharmaCo-A is working to bring a new parenteral product to market. During development, the inherent integrity of the primary package met the MALL for the intended product. However, during scale-up, poor quality package components and line equipment issues resulted in defective packages.

This illustration is purely fictional, and the content is provided for educational purposes only. There is no requirement to follow this approach in content, specificity, or scale.

Package Development

PharmaCo-A's CCS was selected based on package selection criteria. Empty package units were assembled and processed on mock-production equipment and leak tested using a highly sensitive quantitative test method. Results showed gaseous leakage rates met the MALL when properly assembled. These studies provided information on optimum component design, component processing, and package assembly parameters.

Package Processing and Assembly Validation

During scale-up, cracked containers, torn closures, and misassembled container closures were noted. Failure mode analysis linked defects to insufficient process control, outdated production equipment, and a lapse in component quality. An action plan addressed each issue. Additionally, PharmaCo-A decided to identify and implement a nondestructive leak test method for monitoring batch quality and culling out problematic packaged product units.

Leak Test Method Selection

Requirements: compatible with product-filled package units in routine manufacturing; nondestructive; accurately screen out defective packages; suitable also for QA stability study samples.

Leak Test Method Feasibility

Feasibility studies used nondefective units (negative controls) plus defective units (positive controls): rejects from manufacturing scale-up, simulated rejects (type defects), and laser-drilled packages. All four technologies showed potential to identify larger defects.

Optimization and Validation

Optimization aimed to achieve maximum defect detection sensitivity while minimizing false rejection of good units. Each method was evaluated for specificity (ability to detect leaking packages despite interfering factors) and robustness (ability to detect defects despite shifts in test parameters, equipment operation, environment, and units). All four methods performed satisfactorily.

  - On all test days
  - ≥95% of the positive controls in the smallest-size subset are detected
  - and 100% of all larger defects are detected

β-Content (for example, 95%) = the expected detection proportion
Confidence Level = the statistical credibility of the data across the days of the study

  LOD ≤ MALL → the method is accepted
  LOD > MALL → the method is not sensitive enough and must be replaced or optimized

Deep Dive: QbD Decision Flow for a CCIT Program

Step-by-step framework integrating MALL, risk assessment, method selection and lifecycle control

Decision Framework: From Product to Validated CCIT Program

Building a complete CCIT program under QbD principles requires a structured decision process that begins with product quality needs and ends with a validated, lifecycle-spanning control strategy. The following framework integrates the concepts from sections 6.2, 6.3, and 6.5.

Step 1 — Define the QTPP and CQAs

Identify the product's Quality Target Product Profile. For a sterile injectable, key CQAs include: sterility, particulate matter absence, potency, pH stability. The CCS must support all CQAs throughout shelf life. This defines the starting point for integrity requirements.

↓

Step 2 — Determine the MALL

Based on the CQAs, determine the Maximum Allowable Leakage Limit (MALL). Common approaches: (a) microbial ingress studies to establish the leak size at which microbial contamination risk becomes unacceptable; (b) moisture vapor or oxygen transmission studies for moisture- or oxygen-sensitive products; (c) headspace pressure requirements for lyophilized products. The MALL is a specific physical parameter (e.g., equivalent helium leak rate, nominal defect diameter) that defines the integrity threshold.

↓

Step 3 — Identify Process Failure Modes (Risk Assessment)

Apply HACCP or FMEA to map each step of the CCS assembly, filling, and sealing process. For each step, identify: potential failure modes (e.g., under-crimped vial, cracked barrel, misaligned stopper), potential causes (worn tooling, operator error, component variability), and existing controls. Rank by risk priority number (RPN) or risk level. High-risk failure modes drive the choice of testing approach (100% vs. statistical sampling).

↓

Step 4 — Select CCIT Methods (Tool Kit Design)

Select methods whose LOD ≤ MALL. Criteria include: deterministic vs. probabilistic, destructive vs. nondestructive, in-line vs. off-line applicability, testing speed and throughput, compatibility with product and package materials, and regulatory acceptance. More than one method may be needed across different lifecycle stages (development, manufacturing, stability, post-distribution).

↓

Step 5 — Develop and Validate the Selected Method(s)

Follow the PharmaCo-A model: feasibility → optimization → LOD/accuracy determination → full validation. Establish LOD using positive controls (laser-drilled or characterized defects) and confirm accuracy using negative controls. Document predefined acceptance criteria. For in-line methods, qualify the equipment and establish alert/action limits.

↓

Step 6 — Define the Control Strategy and Sampling Plan

Determine: testing frequency, sample size (based on statistical confidence and risk level), acceptance criteria, and response to failures. Where feasible, consider 100% in-line testing for high-risk or regulatory-mandated container types. For other containers, design statistically valid sampling plans referencing applicable standards.

↓

Step 7 — Build the Package Integrity Profile Database and Continuous Improvement Loop

Collect and trend CCIT results over time. Include data from manufacturing, stability studies, post-distribution testing, and complaint investigations. Use this database to trigger reviews when process changes occur, to detect trends before failures become OOS events, and to continuously improve the control strategy as knowledge accumulates over the product lifecycle.

Commentary — Step-by-Step Explanation of the QbD Decision Flow

Integrating QbD into the CCIT program means shifting from a reactive mindset of "testing quality in" to a proactive mindset of "designing quality in". The seven-step decision framework is explained in detail below:

Step 1 — Define the QTPP and CQAs

The Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP) is the starting point of QbD. It defines "what standard the product must ultimately achieve", while the CQAs are the specific list of quality attributes. In the context of package integrity, the CQAs include maintenance of sterility, protection against oxidation, headspace gas integrity and so on.

Step 2 — Determine the MALL

How the MALL (maximum allowable leakage limit) is set depends on the product characteristics:

  • Injectables (sterility requirement): determine through microbial ingress studies which orifice size allows microorganisms to pass
  • Lyophilized products: set a water vapor transmission rate limit based on the effect of residual moisture on shelf life
  • Oxygen-sensitive products: set an oxygen transmission rate limit based on the oxidation rate
  • CO₂-filled products: set a gas leak rate limit based on the headspace CO₂ concentration requirement

An Intuitive Understanding of MALL Setting

The MALL is like a "red line": once the leakage of a package exceeds this line, product quality is affected unacceptably. The LOD of your test method must be more sensitive than this line; only a method that can "see" problems before the red line is crossed is an effective monitoring tool.

Step 3 — Practical Points of Failure Mode Analysis

In FMEA/HACCP analyses, the most common high-risk failure modes for package integrity include:

  • Vials: insufficient crimping force (residual seal force out of specification) → the crimping/capping process is a CPP
  • Prefilled syringes: improper plunger placement → seal failure or inaccurate dose
  • Single-use bags: insufficient weld strength → creep or rupture
  • Cartridges: poor recovery of the rubber stopper after puncture → leakage after multiple uses

Step 7 — Triggers for Continual Improvement

Any of the following events should trigger a review of the CCIT toolbox:

  • Changes to package components or materials (new supplier, new lot specification)
  • Changes to process parameters (capping machine adjustment, fill volume change, sterilization condition change)
  • A trend in stability results (rising CCIT failure rate)
  • Customer complaints or integrity problems in market returns
  • Regulatory updates (such as the 2022 revision of EU GMP Annex 1)

Deep Dive: Statistical Concepts in LOD — β-Content and Tolerance Intervals

How to establish defensible detection limits using statistical tolerance intervals

The Statistical Basis for LOD in CCIT

The detection limit (LOD) in leak testing is not a single-point measurement. It is a statistical statement about the probability of detecting a defined type of defect with defined confidence. Two key statistical concepts underpin the LOD determination as used in TR86's PharmaCo-A example:

Concept 1: β-Content Tolerance Interval

A tolerance interval specifies a range within which at least a proportion β (the "content") of the population falls, with a specified confidence level (1-α).

In CCIT LOD context:
  β-Content = proportion of defective packages
              of a given size that will be detected
              (e.g., 95% = at least 95% detected)

  Confidence Level = statistical confidence that
                     the β-content requirement is met
                     across repeated studies

PharmaCo-A's definition:
  LOD = smallest defect size where ≥95%
        of that size subset is detected
        on ALL test days (across operators,
        days, instruments)

This provides a conservative, reproducible
definition of method capability.

Concept 2: False Positive Rate (Method Accuracy)

False Positive = a good package (no defect)
                 incorrectly identified as leaking

In PharmaCo-A's study:
  Negative controls (n=300 per method)
  were tested across all days

  Method A: 0.3%, 0%, 0.7% false positives
  Methods B, C, D: 0% false positives

Implication:
  False positive rate affects:
  - Yield loss (good product rejected)
  - Investigation burden for QA samples
  - Confidence in positive findings

Acceptance criterion:
  All negative controls pass = 0 false
  positives → confirmed method accuracy

Concept 3: Sample Size Considerations

For LOD studies, sample size per defect
size subset affects statistical power.

PharmaCo-A used n=40 per defect size subset.

To detect 95% defect detection with
95% confidence, the minimum sample
size can be estimated using:

  n ≥ log(1 - CL) / log(β)
  where CL = confidence level (0.95)
        β = proportion to be detected (0.95)

  n ≥ log(0.05) / log(0.95) ≈ 58.4 → 59

Note: PharmaCo-A used n=40, which is
a commonly applied practical number.
Consult a statistician for formal sample
size justification in validation studies.

The number of test days and operators also contributes to the statistical robustness. PharmaCo-A used 3 days and 2+ operators, providing a preliminary estimate of intermediate precision (between-day, between-operator variability).

Commentary — Statistical Basis of LOD

The LOD (limit of detection) in leak testing is a statistical statement, not a single measured value. Understanding its statistical basis is essential for correctly designing and interpreting LOD studies.

An Intuitive Understanding of the β-Content Tolerance Interval

When we say "the LOD of the method is 6 µm", what we really mean is:

  • If you test 100 packages with a 6 µm orifice defect,
  • under normal operating conditions (different days, different operators),
  • at least 95 will be correctly identified as leaking.

This "95%" is the β-Content. A stricter definition also requires that this 95% be achieved statistically at a 95% confidence level (the "double 95%" criterion).

Analogy: the sensitivity setting of a metal detector

An LOD analogy with an airport metal detector: "It detects 100% of metal over 10 g, 95% of 5 g metal, and about 30% of 1 g metal." The definition of LOD in leak testing is exactly the same: it describes the detection probability curve of the method at different defect "sizes", and the LOD is the choice of an acceptable operating point on this curve.

The Practical Impact of False Positives

Although the false positive rate of Method A is very low (<1%), its impact on QA stability studies cannot be ignored:

  • Stability studies usually have small sample sizes (perhaps only 3-6 per time point), so a single false positive can trigger an OOS investigation
  • OOS investigations are time-consuming and laborious, and may wrongly call the product batch into question
  • Solution: require replicate testing of positive results, and verify the instrument status with a known good unit (master) after every suspected failure

LOD vs MALL: The Key Inequality

Basic requirement:
  LOD ≤ MALL

Meaning:
  The method must be able to reliably detect defects
  equal to or smaller than the MALL

Example:
  If MALL = 10 µm (equivalent nominal orifice size)
  then the selected method must have LOD ≤ 10 µm

  Method A (LOD = 6 µm): acceptable ✓
  Method B (LOD = 20 µm): not acceptable ✗
  Method C (LOD = 10 µm): borderline acceptable,
    but day-to-day stability must be considered ⚠

Safety margin recommendation:
  In practice, LOD ≤ 0.5 × MALL is recommended
  to provide an adequate method safety margin

Recommendations for the Design of the Actual Validation Study

Based on the PharmaCo-A example in TR86, recommendations for designing an LOD validation study:

  • Selection of positive controls: preferably use artificial defects whose size can be verified (laser-drilled), ranging from 0.5 times to 2-3 times the expected LOD
  • Size of the negative control set: n≥200 (TR86 used n=300), to ensure adequate statistical sensitivity for the false positive rate
  • Number of test days: at least 3 days (to evaluate day-to-day variation), using a new subset of positive controls each day
  • Number of operators: at least 2 operators (to evaluate operator-to-operator variation)
  • Predefined criteria: fix the LOD acceptance criteria in writing before the study begins

Key considerations:

  • Pressure differential from sea-level manufacturing to cargo hold: approximately 20–25% reduction
  • Duration of exposure: may extend 6–12 hours for intercontinental flights
  • Combined effect with temperature changes in cargo holds (can drop below 0°C on some routes)
  • Pressure cycles: repeated pressurization/depressurization on multi-leg journeys create fatigue stress on closures

Prefilled Syringe Plunger Retraction — Calculation Example

For a prefilled syringe with:

  • Barrel inner diameter: 6.35 mm
  • Headspace volume: 0.1 mL (at sea level, 1 atm)
  • Fill volume: 1.0 mL

At 0.75 atm (aircraft cargo), the headspace volume expands per Boyle's Law:

V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂) = 0.1 mL × (1.00/0.75) = 0.133 mL

Volume increase = 0.033 mL, corresponding to a plunger retraction of approximately 1.0 mm — potentially moving the plunger stopper into an unsterile zone of the barrel if the stopper travel clearance is insufficient.

Commentary — Distribution Testing and Air Transport Pressure in Detail

Choosing the right distribution simulation standard is the first step in ensuring that the test "reflects real stresses". A practical explanation of each standard follows:

ISTA vs ASTM: How to Choose?

  • ISTA 2A/3A: the most commonly used by pharmaceutical companies, especially for package design verification submitted to the US FDA/EMA. ISTA 3A is more comprehensive, includes temperature conditioning, and suits international routes
  • ASTM D4169: widely accepted by US industry; test severity is designed according to the assurance level (Level I/II/III), which gives greater flexibility
  • ISO 4180: more often referenced by international medical regulations (such as EU MDR/IVDR), which helps with EU submissions

Recommendation: choose the standard that corresponds to the main target market, or run both ISTA and ASTM to cover the widest range of regulatory expectations.

A Feel for the Air Pressure Numbers

A pressure of 0.75 atm is equivalent to standing on a mountain at about 2,500 meters (about 8,200 feet). Have you ever felt your ears ring or pop when climbing (the lower air pressure unbalances the pressure in the ear)? The plunger of a prefilled syringe faces a similar "pressure imbalance", except that the gas involved is the headspace gas and the consequence can affect the sterility of the drug product.

Methods for Evaluating Plunger Retraction

To evaluate plunger stability of a prefilled syringe under the pressure differential of air transport, the following methods are commonly used:

  • Altitude chamber test: place the PFS in a controlled pressure chamber, simulate a pressure cycle from 1 atm down to 0.75 atm (or lower), and measure the plunger displacement before and after
  • X-ray or CT scan: quantify the change in plunger position non-destructively
  • Before/after CCIT comparison: perform CCIT before and after the pressure cycle to confirm that seal integrity has not been affected
  • Visual inspection: confirm that the plunger has not come completely out of the barrel

Cumulative Stress Effects of Multimodal Transport

For a pharmaceutical product shipped from Taiwan to the US and then transferred to Europe, the distribution route may include:

  1. Factory → airport: road transport (vibration, shock)
  2. Taiwan → US: air transport (1 pressure cycle)
  3. US warehouse: temperature fluctuation
  4. US → Europe: air transport (1 pressure cycle)
  5. Europe → distribution center: road transport (vibration)

Standardized tests cover only some of these scenarios, so a customized test program must be designed according to the actual distribution route.

Regulatory Landscape for CCI

Container Closure Integrity is addressed across multiple regulatory frameworks. Understanding how these documents relate helps practitioners build compliant programs that satisfy inspectors from different health authorities.

Commentary — The Overall CCI Regulatory Framework

Regulatory requirements for CCI testing are spread across several documents. Understanding the hierarchy of these documents helps in building a complete program that satisfies FDA, EMA and PIC/S inspections at the same time.

The Three-Tier Regulatory Document Framework

Tier 1: Mandatory regulations

  • EU GMP Annex 1 (2022 version): a mandatory requirement for the EU market, setting the minimum standard for CCIT
  • 21 CFR Part 211 (US cGMP): the regulatory basis for packaging component and container requirements

Tier 2: Official standards/guidance

  • USP <1207>, <1207.1>, <1207.2>: official United States Pharmacopeia chapters, referenced by the FDA
  • ICH Q8/Q9: international harmonized guidelines defining the QbD and QRM frameworks
  • FDA 2008 CCIT Guidance: guidance on using CCIT in place of sterility testing in stability studies

Tier 3: Technical guidance (best practice)

  • PDA TR86 (this report): in-depth technical guidance for complex packaging systems
  • PDA TR27: the historical foundation, now extended by TR86
  • ASTM/ISTA/ISO test standards: standards for specific test methods

EU GMP Annex 1 2022 — Key Updates

The 2022 version of Annex 1 (mandatory since August 2023) significantly strengthens the CCIT provisions:

  • Section 8.90: requires all sterile drug manufacturers to establish a written container closure integrity control strategy
  • Section 8.91: requires process validation of the sealing process, not just end-product testing
  • Section 8.92: explicitly requires seal integrity studies for prefilled syringes, with specific mention of the plunger retraction risk
  • Section 8.93: requires a continuing package integrity monitoring program, integrated with the APR/PQR

The framework provided by TR86 (in particular the QbD approach, the lifecycle approach and the LOD study design) is highly consistent with the requirements of Annex 1 2022.

Preparing for Common Inspection Questions

In GMP inspections, common CCI-related questions include:

  • "How did you determine your MALL? What is the scientific basis?" (a study report on MALL setting is needed)
  • "What is the LOD of your CCIT method? How do you confirm that it is below the MALL?" (an LOD validation study is needed)
  • "What are the CPPs of your sealing process? How do they affect CCI?" (a process validation report is needed)
  • "Have you evaluated the impact of distribution on package integrity?" (a transport validation study is needed)
  • "At the most recent change of a package component, did you reassess the CCIT program?" (change control records are needed)
