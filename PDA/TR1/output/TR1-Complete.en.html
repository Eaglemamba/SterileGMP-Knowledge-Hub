Introduction

Historical context, objectives, and task force composition

Original Text (English)

PDA's original Technical Monograph No. 1, Validation of Steam Sterilization Cycles, published in 1978, introduced the principles of steam sterilization to an entire generation of pharmaceutical scientists and engineers. This revision retains the focus on microbiology and engineering of moist heat sterilization in the original document, and updates it by including contemporary subject matter. References to appropriate and up-to-date scientific publications, international regulatory documents, journal articles, technical papers and books are used where more detail and supportive data can be found.

The primary objective of the task force was to develop a scientific technical report on moist heat sterilization that may be used in all regulatory environments. The report does not always address region-specific regulatory expectations, but provides up-to-date, scientific recommendations for use by industry and regulators. This report should be considered a guide and is not intended to establish standards for sterilization validation. It is intended to be a single-source overview that complements existing documents listed in the reference section.

The task force was composed of European and North American industry and regulatory professionals to ensure the methods, terminology and practices of sterilization science presented reflect sound science and can be used globally. This technical report was disseminated in draft for public review and comment prior to publication to ensure its suitability as a valuable guide to industry in steam sterilization.

The overarching goal was to provide enough information to convey an understanding of the science of moist heat sterilization with sufficient technical detail to assist in developing a sterilization policy.

The Standing of TR1

The predecessor of PDA TR1 was the 1978 technical monograph "Validation of Steam Sterilization Cycles," published more than 45 years ago. The 2007 revision retitled it "Validation of Moist Heat Sterilization Processes" and covers cycle design, development, qualification and ongoing control, with a broader scope and a firmer scientific basis.

The document was written jointly by industry and regulatory experts from Europe and the US. It represents the consensus on sterilization science at the time and remains an industry guide frequently cited during FDA and EMA inspections.

Important Positioning Reminder

TR1 is a "guide," not a regulatory standard. It does not mandate specific methods but offers scientific recommendations. In practice, you must still refer to the regulations in each region (FDA, EMA, PIC/S, etc.).

Analogy

Think of TR1 as a "best-practice textbook," not a legal text. It tells you "why doing it this way is more scientific," not "you must do it this way." But if your practice deviates from TR1 during an inspection, the inspector may ask why, and you will need a scientific justification.

Glossary - Part 1: Lethality & Measurement Concepts

Term Definitions (English)

D-Value
The time in minutes required for a one-logarithm, or 90%, reduction of the population of microorganisms used as a biological indicator under specified lethal conditions. For steam sterilization, the D-value should always be specified with a reference temperature, DT. For example, a BI system with a D121°C = 1.4 minutes requires 1.4 minutes at 121°C to reduce the population by one logarithm.
z-Value
The number of degrees of temperature change necessary to change the D-value by a factor of 10. The z-value allows integration of the lethal effects of heat as the temperature changes during the heating and cooling phases of a sterilization cycle.
F-Value (Lethality Factor)
A measurement of sterilization effectiveness. FT,z is the calculated equivalent lethality (using a specified z-value), in terms of minutes at a reference temperature (Tref), delivered by a sterilization cycle to an item.
Fphysical
A term used to describe the delivered lethality calculated based on the physical parameters of the cycle. The Fphysical-value is the integration of the lethal rate (L) over time. The lethal rate is calculated for a reference temperature (Tref) and z-value using the equation: L = 10(T–Tref)/z.
F0
A term used when the specific reference conditions of Tref = 121.1°C and z = 10°C are used to calculate the equivalent lethality. For example, a cycle with an F0 equal to 8 minutes is equivalent (in terms of delivered lethality) to a square wave cycle of 8 minutes at 121.1°C. Note: 121°C is used throughout this report for brevity.
Fbiological
A term used to describe the delivered lethality, measured in terms of actual kill of microorganisms on or in a BI challenge system. The Fbiological-value is calculated as DT × LR, where DT is the D-value of the BI system at the reference temperature (T) and LR is the actual logarithmic reduction (log N0 – log NF) of the BI population achieved during the cycle.
Lethality — see F-Value, F0, Fphysical, Fbiological above.
Sterility Assurance Level (SAL)
Probability of a single viable microorganism occurring on an item after sterilization. [Synonym: PNSU]
NOTE: The term SAL takes a quantitative value, generally 10–3 or 10–6. An SAL of 10–6 has a lower value but provides a greater assurance of sterility than an SAL of 10–3.
Probability of a Non-Sterile Unit (PNSU)
The number that expresses the probability of occurrence of a non-sterile unit after exposure to a sterilization process. Within the pharmaceutical industry, a design end point better than or equal to 10–6 (one non-sterile unit per million) is expected. [Synonym: SAL]
Survivor Curve
Graphical representation of the inactivation of a population of microorganisms with increasing exposure to a microbicidal agent under stated conditions.

Terminology Study - Lethality Concepts (Commentary)

Relationship Between D-value, z-value and F0 Value

D-value: the number of minutes required to kill 90% of the microorganisms (at a specific temperature)
z-value: the temperature change (°C) required to change the D-value by a factor of 10
F0 value: the equivalent sterilization time (minutes) referenced to 121°C and z = 10°C

Formula: L (lethal rate) = 10^[(T - 121) / z]
F0 = ∫L dt (integrated over time)

Example: if D121°C = 1.5 minutes, the microbial population is reduced by 90% (one log) every 1.5 minutes.

SAL vs PNSU - Easily Confused Twins

SAL (sterility assurance level) and PNSU (probability of a nonsterile unit) are different names for the same concept. The pharmaceutical standard is PNSU ≤ 10–6, i.e., no more than 1 nonsterile unit per million products.

Note the counterintuitive logic: the smaller the SAL number (10–6 < 10–3), the higher the sterility assurance.

Fphysical vs Fbiological: Two Validation Perspectives

  • Fphysical: calculated from temperature sensor data → physical qualification
  • Fbiological: calculated from the actual BI survivors → biological qualification

The two must confirm each other: the physically calculated F0 should predict the kill achieved on the BI.

Analogy: the F0 Value Is Like "Equivalent Working Time"

Imagine working 8 minutes at 121°C is equivalent to working 25.9 minutes at 116°C (F0 is 8 minutes in both cases). The higher the temperature, the greater the kill effect per minute (lethal rate L). The z-value describes how strongly temperature affects efficiency.

Glossary - Part 2: Biological Indicator Concepts

Term Definitions (English)

Biological Indicator Challenge System (BI)
A test system containing viable microorganisms of a pure, specified strain providing a defined resistance to a specified sterilization process. [Synonyms: BI Challenge System, Microbial Challenge, Microbiological Challenge System]
Biological Qualification
A component of performance qualification that demonstrates, by use of biological indicators, that the required lethality (F0) is achieved consistently throughout the load.
Half-Cycle Qualification
A qualification method that uses fifty percent of the exposure time to demonstrate sterilization cycle efficacy. The physical and biological lethality values achieved in the half-cycle exposure time are doubled to project the lethality that will be achieved by the full cycle.
Fraction-Negative Methods
Methods that use the starting population of a biological indicator (N0) and data in the quantal range to create a two-point line from which the DT-value can be determined. The quantal range is the exposure period over which a set of replicate test units exhibit a dichotomous response — some are positive for growth and the rest are negative for growth.
Resistometer
Test equipment designed to create defined combinations of the physical and/or chemical variables of a sterilization process. Formerly called biological indicator evaluator resistometers (BIER) vessels. Used primarily in the laboratory to determine D- and z-values.

Terminology Study - Biological Indicator Concepts (Commentary)

The Function of Biological Indicators (BI)

The BI is the "ultimate test" in sterilization validation: a pure culture of microorganisms of known number and known heat resistance is placed in the sterilizer and actually challenged. The most commonly used is Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores, because of their high resistance to moist heat.

The D121°C of a BI is typically 1.5–2.5 minutes, with an initial spore count of about 106, used to simulate the worst case.

Half-Cycle Qualification

This is a common qualification strategy in the overkill approach: testing is performed with half the exposure time of the full cycle. If all BIs are inactivated, the full cycle is inferred to have at least twice the kill capability, conservatively sufficient to achieve an SAL of 10–6.

Fraction-Negative Method Analogy

Imagine testing a batch of BIs: at a given exposure time, some BIs show growth (positive) and some do not (negative). This "mixed zone" is the quantal range, used to calculate the D-value precisely. It is statistically more powerful than simply using "all killed / all survived."

Glossary - Part 3: Process & Cycle Concepts

Term Definitions (English)

Sterilization Cycle
A sequence of defined operating parameters (e.g., time, temperature and pressure) and conditions required to render an item sterile.
Overkill Design Approach
A sterilization design approach where minimal information is required about the product bioburden. A worst-case bioburden assumption is used to determine the delivered lethality needed to achieve a PNSU of 10–6. When using this approach, the qualification program must demonstrate that both the Fphysical and Fbiological are greater than 12 minutes.
Product-Specific Design Approach
A sterilization design approach that is based on the characteristics of the bioburden (on or in the load) and the heat sensitivity of the product that delivers the lethality needed to achieve a PNSU of 10–6.
Process Performance Qualification (PPQ)
Documented verification that a system is capable of consistently performing or controlling the activities of the processes it is required to perform or control, according to written and preapproved specifications, while operating in its specified operating environment.
Physical Qualification
A component of performance qualification that demonstrates that predetermined physical requirements, including temperature distribution and heat penetration, are achieved consistently throughout the load.
Parametric Release
A sterility release system based upon effective control, monitoring, documentation, and batch records review of a validated sterilization process cycle in lieu of release procedures based upon end-product sterility testing.
Minimum Acceptable Cycle (MAC)
The minimum cycle (in terms of delivered lethality), as specified in operating procedures, that would be considered acceptable for load release.
Routine Operational Cycle
Parameters that are specified for ongoing sterilization operations. The operational cycle is typically controlled to produce additional lethality over the qualified minimum acceptable cycle in order to provide increased sterility assurance.
Bracketing Approach
A scientific approach for defining product/load characteristics (e.g., viscosity, container sizes, fill volumes, item sizes, loading configurations) that are tested at upper and/or lower limits.
Worst-Case Load
The load configuration that is determined to be most difficult to sterilize. This is a function of the cycle control strategy and load item characteristics (e.g., mass, configuration, or air removal challenges). For porous/hard goods loads, this may not necessarily be the minimum or maximum load.
Chamber Cold Spot
The location(s) within the load zone that achieves the lowest process lethality (F0) and/or the lowest distribution temperatures during the sterilization process.
Container Cold Spot
The location within a sealed liquid container that achieves the lowest process lethality (F0) during a sterilization process.
Terminal Sterilization
A process whereby product is sterilized within its sterile barrier system.
Validation
A documented program that provides a high level of scientific assurance that a manufacturing process will reliably produce acceptable product. The proof of validation is obtained through rational experimental design and the evaluation of data, preferably beginning from the process development phase and continuing through the commercial production phase.

Terminology Study - Process and Cycle Concepts (Commentary)

MAC vs Routine Cycle - an Operational Buffer

MAC (minimum acceptable cycle) is the minimum threshold for release; the Routine Cycle (routine operating cycle) is usually designed to deliver some additional lethality (lethality margin) beyond the MAC. This way, even with minor equipment fluctuations, the cycle still exceeds the MAC for release. You must be able to explain the difference between the two clearly during an inspection.

The Importance of the Cold Spot

The cold spot is the location within the sterilization load that receives the least lethality and is the main target of measurement in physical qualification. Qualification must place thermocouples at the cold spot to ensure that F0 is still achieved even in the place where it is hardest to reach high temperature.

Parametric Release

Traditional release requires sterility testing of the final product (costly and time-consuming). Parametric release allows validated physical process parameter data to replace final product sterility testing, provided the process is fully validated, well controlled and fully documented. EU regulations (Annex 1) recognize this approach.

Analogy for the Bracketing Strategy

Imagine you have 10 injection vials of different volumes (5 mL to 500 mL). The logic of bracketing is to qualify only the two extremes, the smallest (5 mL, fastest to reach temperature) and the largest (500 mL, slowest to reach temperature), and treat the sizes in between as "covered." This greatly reduces the qualification workload, but it requires a supporting scientific rationale.

Glossary - Part 4: Steam Types & Equipment Concepts

Term Definitions (English)

Moist Heat
Steam, steam-air mixtures, and superheated water used for sterilization.
Saturated Steam
Steam that is at a temperature and pressure that corresponds to the vaporization curve of water. It is in a state of equilibrium between being a liquid and a gas, with no entrained liquid water. [Synonym: Dry Saturated Steam]
Superheated Steam
Steam whose temperature, at a given pressure, is higher than that indicated by the equilibration curve for the vaporization of water.
Superheated Water Process
A sterilization process in which the heating medium is superheated water that is continuously circulated with air overpressure. This process requires air overpressure to keep the water in a liquid state. [Synonyms: water cascade, water spray process, water immersion process, water submersion process, raining water process, steam-air-water process]
Steam-Air Mixture (SAM) Process
A sterilization process in which the heating medium is a mixture of air and steam, typically used for liquid loads. This addition of air results in an air overpressure condition.
Air Overpressure Sterilization Process
A moist heat sterilization process that operates at a controlled pressure greater than saturated steam pressure and typically uses compressed air to bring the chamber to the desired pressure.
Gravity Displacement Process
A sterilization process based on the principle that cold air within the chamber is heavier than the steam entering and will sink to the bottom. As steam enters the chamber, air is pushed out the bottom drain and exits, with the condensate, through a steam trap.
Prevacuum Process
A sterilization process in which air is removed from the chamber using a vacuum pump or other mechanical system before the exposure phase begins. Particularly suited to load items that can trap air such as tubing, filters and filling machine assemblies.
Pure Steam
Steam whose condensate complies with the compendial monograph, Water for Injection (WFI).
Noncondensable Gases
Air and other gases that will not condense to liquid state, thereby not releasing latent heat under the conditions of sterilization.
Dryness Fraction
An absolute measure of the actual latent heat of a sample of steam relative to the theoretical latent heat of saturated steam.
Porous/Hard Goods Load (P/HG)
A load consisting of items in which the bioburden is inactivated through direct contact with saturated steam. Includes filters, stoppers, tubing (hoses), mops, garments, cleaning equipment, or machine change parts.
Liquid Load
A load consisting of closed containers of aqueous liquids. Sterilization is achieved through transfer of energy through the container into the aqueous liquid.
System Suitability Evaluations
Physical evaluations (e.g., chamber integrity or air removal) conducted on a scheduled frequency to demonstrate ongoing control of the sterilizer system.
Calibration
The demonstration that an instrument or device produces results within specified limits when compared to those produced by a reference standard or a standard that is traceable to national or international standards over an appropriate range of measurements.

Terminology Study - Steam Types and Equipment (Commentary)

The Danger of Superheated Steam

Although superheated steam is hot, its sterilizing effect is poorer than that of saturated steam. The reason is that superheated steam lacks moisture (latent heat) and cannot effectively transfer heat to the proteins of the microorganisms. Condensation of saturated steam is the main mechanism of moist heat sterilization.

Gravity Displacement vs Prevacuum

  • Gravity displacement: suited to simple liquid loads or nonporous loads; air is expelled from the bottom, but air removal is incomplete.
  • Prevacuum: suited to porous, tubular or complex-shaped hard goods; a mechanical pump forces a vacuum, giving better air removal. It must be used for filling machine components (filling line parts).

The Problem of Noncondensable Gases

If air (a noncondensable gas) remains in the sterilization chamber, it can form "air pockets" in certain locations, leaving those areas at a lower temperature where sterilization is not achieved. This is why the air removal test (Air Removal Test / Bowie-Dick Test) is a key step in the qualification of porous/hard goods (P/HG) loads.

Pure Steam vs Industrial Steam

The condensate of Pure Steam must meet WFI (water for injection) standards, with no pyrogens, microorganisms or excess heavy metals. Pure Steam must be used for sterilization that directly contacts the product or product-contact surfaces; industrial steam must not be used. This is a basic GMP requirement.

3.1

Sterilization Models

Semilogarithmic first-order kinetics of microbial death

Original Text (English)

The death of a homogeneous culture of microorganisms exposed to constant lethal stress (also known as the survivor curve) has been shown empirically to follow first-order kinetics. The rate of microbial death is a function of the thermal resistance of the microorganism and lethal stress and is independent of the number of microorganisms in the challenge.

The survivor curve can be described using the following semilogarithmic, first-order model:

Equation 1 — Survivor Curve Model

Log NF = −FT,ref/DT + Log N0

Where:

  • NF = Number of microorganisms after exposure of F equivalent minutes
  • FT,ref = Equivalent lethality of a cycle calculated as minutes at a reference temperature (T), using a defined temperature coefficient (z)
  • DT = Thermal resistance value, in minutes, of the microorganism at a specific temperature (T)
  • N0 = Number of microorganisms prior to exposure

Figure 3.1-1 illustrates the semilogarithmic survivor curve described above for a biological indicator.

DT is a measure (the negative reciprocal) of the slope of the semilogarithmic survivor curve; therefore, it describes the relationship between the number of survivors versus equivalent (F-value) exposure time. F-value is a term used in the model to characterize exposure time to moist heat. By definition, the F-value is expressed by a reference temperature so that it truly represents the equivalent exposure time, in terms of lethality, at that reference temperature.

Since routine operational cycles are not generally square wave cycles (i.e., the load does not come up to temperature instantaneously, remains at the precise set point throughout the exposure phase, and then cools down instantaneously), the z-value, or temperature coefficient, is used in the model to calculate the equivalent lethality at different temperatures.

In order to use this semilogarithmic model for the survivor curve in the scientific approach to microbial destruction, the challenge must consist of a homogeneous culture, and a constant lethal stress (or ability to calculate equivalent lethal stress — F-value) must be applied to the challenge.

The semilogarithmic relationship does not accurately fit all experimental microbial thermal destruction data; but, to date, no other model is known that fits all experimental data. To be useful, a model or mathematical relationship should: (a) represent the system, (b) be predictive of system performance, and (c) be reasonably easy to use and understand.

The semilogarithmic survivor curve model can be used directly and graphically, to both analyze experimental data and design sterilization processes. It allows calculations in both directions — from experimental microbial-destruction data to physical parameters, and from physical parameters to expected microbial survival data.

Commentary

Core Concept: First-Order Kinetics

Microbial death under constant heat stress follows first-order kinetics: the fraction killed per unit time is constant, regardless of how many organisms remain. It is like radioactive decay: whatever the starting population, each D-value interval of time destroys 90% of the surviving microorganisms.

Everyday Analogy: Bank Interest in Reverse

Imagine a bank account that loses 90% of its balance every minute (instead of gaining interest). Whether you start with one million or with 1, the proportional loss is the same. That is the intuition behind the semilogarithmic model: on a logarithmic scale it is a straight line.

This is why, in Figure 3.1-1, time (x-axis) and log survivors (y-axis) form a straight line, and the negative reciprocal of the slope is the D-value.

Interpreting the Equation

Log NF = −F/DT + Log N0

What this means in practice:

  • If N0 = 106, DT = 2.5 min, and the exposure F = 30 min
  • Log NF = −30/2.5 + 6 = −12 + 6 = −6
  • NF = 10−6 (a one-in-a-million probability of survival)

This "one in a million" concept is the scientific basis of an SAL (sterility assurance level) of 10−6.

Limitations of the Model

PDA TR1 is candid that the semilogarithmic model does not fit all experimental data perfectly, but no better alternative is currently available. A practical model must meet three requirements:

  1. It can describe the behavior of the system
  2. It can predict the performance of the system
  3. It is reasonable, easy to use and easy to understand

Industry accepts this model because of its conservatism: in most cases, the actual kill achieved is better than the model predicts.

3.1.1

Resistance Value (DT)

Time for one-log reduction of microbial population

Original Text (English)

DT-value is the time in minutes required for a one-log, or 90%, reduction of a microbial population under specified lethal conditions, i.e., for a temperature (T) in moist heat sterilization. The DT-value is the negative reciprocal of the slope of the survivor curve. One logarithmic cycle on the y-axis (population) represents a ten-fold change in the number of survivors; therefore, the DT-value is the time, or equivalent time (F), on the x-axis for the survivor curve to traverse one logarithmic cycle.

It is important to note that the DT-value is not strictly a genetic characteristic of any particular microorganism. The DT-value is determined empirically using the first-order survivor curve model to characterize the resistance of a BI challenge system to a lethal agent. Refer to Section 3.2.1 for discussion of factors that may affect the resistance of biological indicators.

D-values can be calculated from a survivor curve determined by either:

  1. Direct enumeration
  2. Using two data points (N0 and a point in the quantal range calculated using fraction-negative methods)

3.1.1.1 Direct Enumeration Method

This method involves sub-lethal treatment of the biological challenge followed by enumeration of the survivors (spore counts). The resulting data can be plotted on a semilogarithmic graph (logarithm of surviving population versus equivalent exposure time), and the DT-value determined from the slope of the line. The DT-value is the negative reciprocal of the line that best fits the data points. Linear regression analysis can be used to determine the slope of the best-fit line, when appropriate.

3.1.1.2 Fraction-Negative Methods

Fraction-negative methods use N0 and data in the quantal range to create a two-point line from which the DT-value can be determined. The quantal range is the exposure period over which a set of replicate test BIs exhibit a dichotomous response — some are positive for growth, and the rest are negative for growth. There are two primary methods:

  • Holcomb-Spearman-Karber Method — all data in the quantal range are combined using a weighted average technique to yield the mean time corresponding to an NF value of 0.56 average survivors per BI. Allows for calculating a confidence interval for the DT-value.
  • Stumbo-Murphy-Cochran Method — each set of data in the quantal area is individually analyzed using the Halvorson-Ziegler "Most Probable Number (MPN) Method" to determine NF and, in turn, a DT-value for each set of data. These DT-values are then averaged to determine the test DT-value.

It is important to note that the use of statistical methods for calculating DT-values requires strict adherence to statistical analysis rules. The BIs exposed throughout the study must be replicates and should be from the same BI lot. Recovery and incubation procedures should be identical for all BIs in the study. The only variable should be the exposure time to the lethal agent.

Furthermore, these fraction-negative methods should not be used to evaluate kill in a production sterilizer where BIs are placed throughout the chamber. The multiple BIs are not considered replicates, because there is no assurance of uniform lethality throughout the sterilizer.

DT-value determination studies are usually necessary for:

  • To characterize heat-resistant product isolates (obtained from heat shock testing) during design of sterilization cycles when product-specific approaches are used
  • To evaluate the effect of a formulation change on resistance
  • To characterize heat-resistant microorganisms isolated from the manufacturing environment during routine monitoring
  • To establish D-values for biological indicators prepared by directly inoculating materials or product formulations with heat-resistant spores
Commentary

Visualizing the D-value

The D-value is the time required for the survival curve to advance one logarithmic unit along the horizontal axis of a semilogarithmic plot:

If D121°C = 2.5 min, then reaching an SAL of 10−6 requires 12 × 2.5 = 30 minutes.

Biological Meaning of the D-value

The D-value is not a fixed genetic characteristic of an organism; it is the result of the interaction between the organism and its environment. The same strain of Geobacillus stearothermophilus can show D-values that differ several-fold depending on the culture medium, humidity and pH.

This is why the specification of biological indicators must be tightly controlled: only the same lot, prepared in the same way, can ensure a consistent D-value.

Important Limitation: The Fraction-Negative Method Is Not Applicable in Production Sterilizers

When multiple BIs are placed in a production sterilizer, these BIs are not replicates, because the lethality delivered at different locations in the sterilizer is not necessarily the same. The fraction-negative method assumes that every BI receives exactly the same heat exposure, which cannot be guaranteed in a production sterilizer.

3.1.2

Temperature Coefficient (z-value)

Degrees of temperature change needed to alter D-value by a factor of 10

Original Text (English)

Change in the heat resistance of spores, as a function of the temperature, is represented by the z-value. The z-value is the number of degrees of temperature change necessary to change the DT-value by a factor of 10. It is analogous to a temperature coefficient in the semilogarithmic model. The z-value is a component of the F-value calculation that is used to compare spore lethality at different temperatures.

For example, the DT of a BI challenge system with a z-value of 8°C will change by a factor of 10 for each 8°C change in temperature. If the D112°C of the BI is 1.6 minutes, then the D120°C will be 0.16 minutes and the D104°C will be 16.0 minutes. The correct unit of measurement for temperature differences is the degree Kelvin, which is identical in all respects to a change of 1°C. In the interest of simplicity, degrees Celsius will be used throughout this document.

Moist heat sterilization processes are usually carried out within a small temperature range, e.g., 110–135°C, therefore the experimentally determined z-value is usually considered constant for practical purposes. A z-value of 10°C is generally used in routine process design and evaluation.

In studies where the objective is to reconcile the physical and biological F-values delivered to a product, the actual z-value of the biological indicator system must be used in the calculation of the physical FT-values.

The z-value can be determined by several methods; however, determining the z-value from D-values is the most widely-used method. D-values for the challenge microorganism are determined at several different temperatures and then plotted with the logarithmic scale on the y-axis and with the temperature on the x-axis. A straight line is drawn through the data points. The z-value is the number of degrees of temperature for the D-value to change by a factor of 10 (one log cycle), e.g., from 2.0 minutes to 0.2 minute, or from 0.3 minute to 3.0 minutes.

Like the D-value, the z-value is the negative reciprocal of the slope of this line, which is often referred to as the Thermal Resistance Curve (Figure 3.1.2-1). It is important to consider the temperature scale when using z-values, and to express the z-value in terms of either Celsius (Kelvin) or Fahrenheit depending on the intended usage.

Commentary

z-value: Temperature Sensitivity of Heat Resistance

The z-value describes how quickly microbial heat resistance changes with temperature. A smaller z-value means temperature has a greater effect on the D-value (greater sensitivity to temperature); a larger z-value means the heat resistance of the organism is less sensitive to temperature changes.

Standard value: moist heat sterilization routinely uses z = 10°C (corresponding to Geobacillus stearothermophilus)

Analogy: The z-value Is Like the Sensitivity of a Volume Knob

Imagine two stereos: on one, turning the knob 10 degrees changes the volume tenfold (z = 10); on the other, turning it only 8 degrees does the same (z = 8). The one with the smaller z-value is "more sensitive": a small change in temperature greatly changes the sterilization effect.

This explains why the z-value must be used correctly in F-value calculations: if the z-value of the BI is 8°C but you calculate with 10°C, the physical F-value and the biological F-value will not agree.

3.1.3

Lethal Rate (L) and Lethality (F)

Integration of time-temperature data to quantify sterilization efficacy

Original Text (English)

In moist heat sterilization, the ability to relate equivalent minutes at a reference temperature (Tref) with microbial destruction at that same reference temperature is the foundation of all our biological measurements. The development of the measurement and integration of time-temperature data, using the z-value to calculate lethal rates, is what has made it possible to model microbial destruction processes.

Sterilizers are designed to operate at a specific designated temperature; however, the actual temperature may fluctuate around the target temperature within a range. While this fluctuation is usually minimal, it may have a significant effect on F-value determination, especially if the process temperature is predominantly on the low or high side of the target temperature. The F-value calculation considers all of the fluctuations around the target temperature by reducing the individual temperature observations to a common equivalent value in terms of delivered lethality. It is the integration of the lethal rate over the cycle.

3.1.3.1 Lethal Rate (L)

The lethal rate can be calculated by the following formula:

Equation 2 — Lethal Rate

LT,ref,z = 10(T − Tref)/z

Where:

  • T = Temperature of the item being heated
  • Tref = Reference temperature
  • z = z-value of the challenge organism (or 10°C if not known)

The lethal rate is an exponential function, thus small temperature differences can have a significant effect on the delivered lethality. For example, for a BI system with a z-value of 10°C, a 1°C decrease in temperature reduces the lethality by approximately 20%:

L = 10(120−121°C)/10 = 10−0.1 ≈ 0.79

Thus, for a BI with a z-value of 10°C, one minute at 120°C is equivalent to 0.79 minutes at 121°C in terms of lethality.

3.1.3.2 FT,ref Value (Fphys)

The F-value is a measurement of the lethality of a process. FT,ref is the calculated equivalent of time, in terms of lethality, at a reference temperature, Tref, and a temperature coefficient, z, that is delivered to the item being sterilized. F-values calculated from physical data (time and temperature) are also referred to as Fphys.

The F-value is the integration of the lethal rates throughout the process. In practical terms, this integration is carried out as a numerical summation using the Trapezoid model:

Equation 3 — F-value Calculation

FT,ref = d × (ΣL)

Where:

  • d = the time increment between each temperature reading
  • L = the lethal rate calculated for each temperature reading

Due to the inherent inaccuracies of the z-value, which is used to determine the lethal rate, a minimum temperature may be defined (e.g., 100°C) where the integration begins and ends. It is also important to use reference temperatures (Tref) that are close to the operating temperature of the sterilizer during the exposure phase.

3.1.3.3 F0

The term F0 is the number of equivalent minutes of steam sterilization at a temperature of 121°C delivered to a container or unit of product. This is calculated using a z-value of 10°C.

Note: The reference temperature used in calculating F0 is 121.1°C, which is the approximate mathematical equivalent of 250°F. The reference temperature of 121°C for F0 will be used throughout this report for brevity.

Wherever a value is stated in terms of F0, it is referring to the equivalent time at 121°C. If, for example, a cycle has a stated F0-value of 8.0 minutes, then the sterilization effectiveness of that cycle is equivalent to 8.0 minutes at 121°C regardless of the load/product temperature and time actually achieved in the cycle.

3.1.3.4 FBIO

The term FBIO represents the delivered lethality measured by the actual kill of microorganisms on or in a BI system. The FBIO-value is calculated using the following formula:

Equation 4 — FBIO Calculation

FBIO = DT × LR

Where:

  • DT = D-value of the BI at the reference temperature
  • LR = Log reduction of the BI population achieved during the cycle
Commentary

F0 vs FBIO: Physical and Biological Lethality

In validation studies, F0 and FBIO should agree; if the difference is too large, the z-value setting or the BI system must be investigated.

Practical Meaning of F0

F0 = 8 min means that, regardless of the actual heat-up profile, hold temperature and cool-down rate, the microbial kill delivered by this process is equivalent to exactly 8 minutes at 121°C.

This concept allows a facility to use different equipment and different formulations: as long as the F0 target is met, the same sterility assurance level is ensured. This is the cornerstone of the entire moist heat sterilization validation framework.

Comparison

Overkill vs. Bioburden-Based Approach

Two primary sterilization validation strategies

Comparison Overview

PDA TR1 recognizes two principal approaches to moist heat sterilization cycle design, both grounded in the semilogarithmic model:

  • The Overkill Approach applies a large excess of lethality to provide a high margin of safety, irrespective of the actual bioburden.
  • The Bioburden-Based Approach (also called the Probability of Survival approach) uses knowledge of the actual bioburden count and D-value to design a cycle that achieves a defined SAL.

The choice of approach depends on product characteristics, regulatory expectations, and the manufacturer's risk management philosophy.

The SAL (Sterility Assurance Level) of 10−6 is the internationally accepted standard — the probability that any single unit is non-sterile after sterilization must be no greater than one in one million.

Commentary

Meaning of an SAL of 10−6

SAL (sterility assurance level) = 10−6 means that, after a compliant sterilization process, the probability that any single unit of product is non-sterile is no more than one in a million.

Note: an SAL of 10−6 does not mean that one in every million products is contaminated; it is a probability concept, not a frequency concept. The value itself cannot be verified directly by testing and can only be assured through a properly designed and validated process.

A sterilizer operates at 121°C but temperature data shows the actual product temperature fluctuates between 120°C and 122°C during the exposure phase (z = 10°C, Tref = 121°C). One minute at 120°C contributes how much to F0 compared to one minute at 122°C?

L120°C = 10(120−121)/10 = 10−0.1 ≈ 0.794 min

Target: NF ≤ 10−6

Log NF = −F/DT + Log N0

−6 = −F/0.5 + Log 100

−F/0.5 = −8

Question 4

Why is it inappropriate to use fraction-negative methods to determine D-values from BIs placed at different locations inside a production sterilizer?

Why can the fraction-negative method not be used to determine the D-value of BIs placed at different locations in a production sterilizer?

Show Answer

The fraction-negative method assumes that all BIs receive the same dose of the lethal agent (uniform lethality). This condition can be met when testing in the laboratory with a resistometer.

In a production sterilizer, however, different locations (cold spot, hot spot, load center vs. periphery) may receive different F-values. BIs placed at different locations are not "replicates"; they are samples tested under different conditions.

Conclusion: if fraction-negative statistics are applied to such data, the calculated D-value will be a meaningless composite that does not represent any true microbial heat resistance. Such data can only be used to judge whether a particular location meets the sterilization conditions, not to calculate a D-value.

3.2.1

Biological Indicators (BIs)

Microorganism-based validation tools for demonstrating sterilization efficacy

3.2.1 Biological Indicators (BIs)

Determination of documented, acceptable probability of survival is based upon the predictable logarithmic death kinetics of microorganisms. This determination is also dependent on the sterilization process used and the sterilization approach selected. Since the biological indicator is a key component in the determination of process lethality, it is imperative that it performs both predictably and reproducibly.

The most common microorganism used as a biological indicator for the qualification of cycles using the overkill design approach is Geobacillus stearothermophilus (formerly Bacillus stearothermophilus); however, other moist heat resistant microorganisms may be acceptable. Typical microorganisms used for qualification of cycles using the product-specific design approach include: Clostridium sporogenes, Bacillus smithii (formerly Bacillus coagulans) and Bacillus subtilis 5230.

The chosen biological indicator typically contains a higher population and resistance than the bioburden on the product or item. Only spores should be used as microbiological challenges for steam sterilization processes. These spores must be:

  • Clean — essentially free from vegetative microorganisms, microscopic debris and clumping
  • Well-characterized — with respect to their thermal responsiveness
  • Obtained from recognized culture collections ("parent" cultures)

Ideally, the BI's heat resistance is determined in a calibrated physical system capable of square wave heating (e.g., resistometer). Biological indicator challenge systems are available in a variety of configurations, including spore suspensions in solutions, on paper carriers (e.g., spore strips or discs), and on other types of carriers (e.g., metal disks, cotton threads, or ampoules).

If the reliability of a vendor's Certificate of Analysis is established through a supplier qualification program, and the BI is not modified, then the supplied values for resistance of a biological indicator can be used in lieu of confirmatory testing of each lot. Due to potential shipping and handling concerns, confirmatory spore population testing should be conducted upon receipt of biological indicators. It is important to use the same enumeration methodology that the vendor used in order to minimize variables that could lead to differences in spore counts.

A determination of the stability of the population and resistance (D-value) of the BI should be performed over its shelf-life. The actual population and resistance to be used should be based on the desired lethality and the qualification approach used.

The D-value of the BI challenge system is a function of not only the species or strain used and prior history, but all facets of the test environment, including, but not limited to:

  • The test system (spores suspended in liquid or dried on a solid support; nature of environment surrounding spores during heating)
  • The medium in which the spores are suspended
  • The test temperature
  • The primary packaging
  • Exposure times that are not corrected for lags in heating and cooling
  • Temperature, time and environmental conditions between heating and recovery
  • The growth medium used to recover the heated spores
  • Incubation conditions

The operation of the test system, including equipment control and technician variability, can make the resulting D-value either larger or smaller.

It is important to evaluate the thermal resistance of the biological indicator in the actual configuration to be tested for validation, since the resistance of microorganisms is affected by the solution or substrate onto which they are placed. For example:

  • The heat resistance of G. stearothermophilus is increased in the presence of sodium chloride or potassium chloride
  • The thermal resistance of B. coagulans is increased by the presence of divalent calcium and magnesium ions
  • The thermal resistance of C. sporogenes is increased by the presence of potassium ions

The presence of chelating agents (e.g., citric acid) may also affect the thermal resistance. Strong chelating agents can compete for available ions, yielding changes in spore-coat resistance. Similar resistance differences may be observed based on the substrate that is inoculated (e.g., paper or rubber).

When performing direct inoculation onto substrates, it is important to ensure that the biological indicator remains in the area intended for inoculation and is not reduced during inoculation. The use of multiple smaller volume inoculations typically provides better inoculum placement and control than a single larger volume, which is prone to inadvertent movement.

Compendia (e.g., USP, PhEur) contain monographs defining BIs that may be used in evaluating moist heat sterilization. In the U.S., it is not a requirement to use a compendial BI for industrial sterilization processes. In other regions and countries it is recommended that consideration is given to local regulatory expectations.

Biological Indicators in Depth | Commentary

Factors Affecting D-value: Details Operators Often Overlook

The D-value is not just a property of the strain; it also depends heavily on the test environment. The same BI lot can show markedly different D-values on different substrates (paper strip vs. metal coupon). This means:

  • The D-value on the manufacturer's CoA is not necessarily the D-value in your product configuration
  • Validation must use BIs in the actual configuration of use
  • NaCl, Ca²⁺ and chelating agents can all change heat resistance

Key Points for BI Supplier Management

  • On receipt: confirmatory spore population testing must be performed
  • Enumeration method: must be consistent with the method used by the manufacturer; otherwise the counts are not comparable
  • Reliance on the CoA: requires prior supplier qualification and a BI that has not been modified
  • Stability: spore population and D-value stability must be evaluated throughout the shelf life

Direct Inoculation Technique: An Analogy

Multiple small applications vs. a single large application is like patching a wall with paint: too much at once runs and drifts, while several thin coats adhere evenly in the right place. The precision of the BI inoculation location directly affects the validity of the challenge.

3.2.2

Chemical Monitors

Chemical indicators and integrators — capabilities and limitations

3.2.2 Chemical Monitors

A chemical monitor is a device that provides an indication or response to one or more critical sterilization parameters. They can be used as qualitative indicators to verify that a product has been exposed to a sterilization process. Where their reaction can be quantified, they can be used to answer more complex questions. Regardless of their design, chemical monitors cannot be used in lieu of biological indicators and instrumented measurement of temperature/pressure/time during the validation of a steam sterilization process.

3.2.2.1 Chemical Indicators

A chemical indicator is a device that responds to sterilization process parameters in a nonquantitative fashion. Chemical indicators are used to provide immediate results that can serve as a permanent marker (e.g., color change) to indicate that a load or product has been subjected to a sterilization cycle.

Chemical indicators do not indicate that the load is sterile. They only indicate that the threshold temperature was reached, not what the highest temperature achieved was or how long that temperature was maintained. It is important that sufficient indicators be placed throughout the load to prevent inadvertent interchange of sterilized and non-sterilized items. There are several different types (e.g., tape, paper strips or small ampoules) of chemical indicators available.

3.2.2.2 Chemical Integrators

Chemical integrators are designed to react quantitatively to a particular combination of parameters in moist heat sterilization — typically temperature and time and sometimes moisture. These devices react in a quantifiable way to their exposure to certain physical conditions that are critical to the sterilization process. They deliver a measurable record of conditions that may be correlated to microbial inactivation.

When chemical integrators are used, their advantages and limitations should be clearly understood:

  • Integrators with simultaneous moisture monitoring may give additional information on the sterilizing conditions achieved in the cycle that are not readily available from temperature and pressure monitoring in commonly used sterilizers.
  • Integrators may give an indication of the sterilizing conditions achieved throughout the load that should be correlated to the temperature distribution data achieved during cycle validation.
  • Integrators cannot be used exclusively for cycle qualification.

Chemical Indicators vs. Integrators | Commentary

Important Regulatory Position

FDA and PDA are both explicit: chemical indicators and integrators cannot replace biological indicators or instrumented temperature/pressure/time measurement for steam sterilization process validation. A common deficiency: using only a chemical integrator for cycle validation, with no BIs.

Everyday Analogy

A chemical indicator is like the pop-up thermometer flag stuck in a roast (a color change means the temperature has been reached). An integrator is like a timer and thermometer combined (it records how long and at what temperature). Neither can tell you that the bacteria inside the food are actually dead; only direct microbiological testing can confirm that.

3.3.1

Thermal Science: Temperature, Heat & Steam Tables

Thermodynamic principles underlying saturated steam sterilization

3.3 Thermal Science and Steam Quality

Understanding the basics of thermal science (thermodynamics) is essential to the design and control of moist heat sterilization. There are significant differences in energy content, at a given temperature, in the various heating media used. Superheated water, saturated steam and steam/air mixtures contain different amounts of thermal energy. The strict relationships between temperature and pressure in saturated steam cycles established during cycle design must be achieved in routine production cycles to ensure the efficacy of the sterilization process.

3.3.1 Temperature and Heat

Temperature is a measure of thermal energy. Heat is energy that is transferred as a result of a temperature difference between an object and its surroundings.

It is important to understand that thermal energy contained by different heating media at the same temperature (e.g., saturated steam, air/steam mixtures or superheated water) is dramatically different.

The heat of vaporization/condensation is the primary mechanism that imparts energy to the item being sterilized in a saturated steam process. Saturated steam contains 2,675 J/g at 100°C. This is the sum of:

  • Energy in the water: 419 J/g
  • Energy required to create steam (latent heat of vaporization): 2,256 J/g

The condensation of one gram of steam imparts 2,256 joules to the object at 100°C.

At 2.0 atmospheres, when the temperature of the water reaches 121°C, it does not change to saturated steam until 2,199 J/g has been added. In the 2.0 atmosphere example, the transfer of 2,199 joules as a result of the phase change between water and steam occurs only when the system is in dynamic equilibrium — when water and steam are in equilibrium at a specific pressure and temperature.

Steam tables are developed by calculating and listing corresponding temperatures and pressures of a system in dynamic equilibrium. There is only one pressure that corresponds to a specific temperature on the saturation curve when the steam is saturated.

3.3.1.1 Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of energy through molecular agitation without any required motion of material as a whole. In sterilization science, this applies to:

  • Transfer through the container wall to the liquid being sterilized
  • Energy transfer to the surface of an item by direct contact with the sterilizing medium (e.g., steam or hot water)

3.3.1.2 Convection

Convection is the transfer of energy resulting from contact with a moving fluid. When this process occurs naturally (as in the movement of a fluid in a container being sterilized), it is referred to as natural convection. Forced convection occurs when the fluid is moved by a fan or pump.

3.3.1.3 Radiation

Radiation is the transfer of energy through electromagnetic waves. Radiant heat is the energy transfer mechanism that occurs primarily during a cycle drying phase under vacuum, but it can contribute to the heat added to the load during the sterilization phase, especially if the jacket temperature is appreciably higher than that of the chamber.

3.3.1.4 Heat Transfer Rate & Comparison of Heating Mediums

The flow of heat from the heating media to a sealed container is dependent on a number of factors including the temperature difference, the geometry and characteristics of the container, and the overall heat transfer coefficient.

Items typically heat most rapidly in saturated steam. In some cases, such as large flexible packages, a superheated water submersion/immersion sterilizer is highly efficient. Steam-air mixtures have a much lower heat capacity per unit volume than either pressurized water or steam, but can be effective with a properly developed cycle.

Based on the heat transfer mechanisms, thermocouples should preferably be placed at:

  • The cold spot: the location in the load that is hardest to heat
  • Densely packed areas: convection is impeded and conduction dominates
  • The center of liquid loads: where heat transfer lag in liquids is most pronounced
  • The bottom of containers: where condensate accumulates

Each validation run should use a sufficient number of thermocouples, covering representative locations in the load space, calibrated to ±0.5°C or tighter.

Steam Types

Plant steam, process steam, and pure steam — selection criteria for sterilization

3.3.2 Steam

Several types of steam can be used for moist heat sterilization: these include, but are not limited to, plant steam, process steam and pure steam. Since they are all application specific, it should be demonstrated which type of steam is appropriate.

3.3.2.1 Plant Steam

Plant steam is a general purpose, industrial steam generated, distributed and used for a variety of energy transfer purposes such as facility, water and process heating, or to drive engines or turbines.

For moist heat sterilization, plant steam is generally considered adequate for supplying the sterilizer jacket (provided the chamber steam is not sourced from the jacket). It is also used to heat water for superheated water processes on the non-sanitary side of heat exchangers.

3.3.2.2 Process Steam

Process steam is similar to plant steam, except the steam is generated using a controlled feed water source to which no volatile additives (amines or hydrazines) have been introduced. Process steam may be appropriate for moist heat sterilization of liquid loads when the containers are filled and sealed prior to sterilization.

3.3.2.3 Pure Steam

Pure steam (sometimes called clean steam or high quality steam) is steam whose condensate complies with the compendial Water for Injection (WFI) monograph. Pure steam is typically produced by purpose-designed steam generators or from the first effect of a multiple-effect still using a feed water of known chemical quality.

Softened, deionized, and purified water as defined in current compendia have all been successfully used as feedwater to pure steam generators. Also important to the proper operation of the system is the appropriate design, placement, and maintenance of steam traps and air vents in the steam distribution system. Condensate pooling should be avoided.

Pure steam should always be used for porous/hard goods load sterilization.

Guidance on Selecting the Steam Type | Commentary

Key Regulatory Point

Porous/hard goods loads (P/HG loads, such as stoppers, filter membranes and instruments) must be sterilized with pure steam, because the steam comes into direct contact with the item surfaces. Amines or hydrazines in plant steam would contaminate the items.

Common GMP deficiency: using plant steam to sterilize porous items has been classified as a major deviation by FDA/EMA.

Key Design Elements of a Pure Steam System

  • Feed water quality: softened, deionized or purified water are all acceptable, with quality records
  • Steam traps: properly designed and maintained to prevent condensate accumulation
  • Air vents: ensure non-condensable gases can be removed
  • Condensate testing: periodically confirm that the condensate meets WFI specifications (conductivity, TOC, bacterial endotoxin)
3.3.3

Steam Quality Testing

Noncondensable gases, dryness fraction, and superheat — the three steam quality criteria

3.3.3 Steam Quality Testing for Pure Steam

The semilogarithmic model of inactivation of microorganisms for saturated steam processes assumes that saturated steam is free from noncondensable gases and free from superheat. Wet steam, superheated steam, and steam containing noncondensable gases have the potential to adversely affect the lethality achievable in porous/hard goods load cycles.

The extent to which saturated steam sterilization processes may be affected by steam quality depends on the extent of the deviation from ideal steam conditions and the type(s) of items in the load being sterilized.

For sterilization of porous/hard goods, steam quality characteristics should be evaluated as part of the qualification of the steam supplied to the sterilizer and should be repeated at regular intervals and documented in internal company policy or in accordance with applicable regulatory requirements.

3.3.3.1 Noncondensable Gases

Noncondensable gas is gas (e.g., air, nitrogen and CO2) that may be carried over in steam from the steam generator. These noncondensable gases change the steam from being pure, vapor-phase water to a mixture of steam and gas.

3.3.3.2 Dryness Fraction and Dryness Value

The dryness value (an empirical determination of dryness fraction) of steam is a measure of the amount of liquid-phase water carried by the steam and applies to a saturated steam process. A value of 0 denotes 100% water and a value of 1.0 represents dry steam with no liquid-phase water.

In addition to the problem of causing wet loads for certain items, the energy contained in steam with a dryness value less than approximately 1.0 can be significantly less than pure saturated steam. Dryness value is determined by testing, and values are generally considered approximate.

The dryness fraction of steam is inextricably linked with the latent heat that it possesses. Steam having an energy level equal to 50% of the latent heat for its saturation pressure will have a dryness fraction of 0.5 indicating a 50:50 water/steam mixture. Therefore, only when steam has its full quotient of latent heat will it be dry saturated and have a dryness fraction of 1.0.

When 100% water is present at 121°C, there is no latent heat, and the dryness fraction is 0. When a 50:50 steam/water mixture is present, the dryness fraction is 0.5, and the latent heat is 1,099 J/g. When 100% steam is present, the dryness fraction is 1.0 with 2,199 J/g of latent heat.

3.3.3.3 Superheat

Superheated steam is steam whose temperature, at any given pressure, is higher than that indicated by the equilibration curve for the vaporization of water. For inactivation of microorganisms to conform to the semilogarithmic model, the steam should be saturated, i.e., it should be at its equilibration temperature for the particular pressure achieved.

The lethal effect of steam with superheat on microorganisms will be less than that predicted for the particular temperature registered.

The principal causes of superheat are:

  • Excessive pressure reduction near the point of use
  • The sterilizer chamber jacket is at a higher temperature than the chamber

The Three Steam Quality Parameters | Commentary

Why Is Superheated Steam a Poor Sterilant?

Superheated steam is like an "over-dry" gas: its temperature is above the saturation point, so it does not readily condense. When it contacts an item:

  1. It must first cool to the saturation temperature before it can begin to condense
  2. During cooling it releases only sensible heat, which is very little
  3. The semilogarithmic kill model is based on saturated steam and cannot be applied to superheated steam

Result: even if the thermometer shows the target is met, the actual kill may be severely inadequate.

Dryness Analogy

Imagine steam as a "courier" carrying energy:

  • Dryness = 1.0: the courier carries a full load of cargo (2,199 J/g), all delivered to the item
  • Dryness = 0.5: the courier has only half the cargo (1,099 J/g), so delivered energy is halved
  • Dryness = 0: all water, with no latent heat to release

Non-condensable gases are like a courier carrying "empty boxes": they take up space but deliver no energy, and they block other couriers from reaching the item surface.

4.1

Design Approaches

Overkill vs. Product-Specific — choosing the right framework

4.1 Design Approaches

Two principal sterilization cycle design approaches may be used for the development of sterilization processes: the overkill design approach and the product-specific design approach. Both approaches are able to provide the same level of sterility assurance to the product or materials being sterilized.

In the design of sterilization cycles, the choice between the two design approaches is largely based on the thermal stability of the product or materials being sterilized.

The overkill design approach requires less initial and ongoing information on the bioburden of the materials being sterilized. It requires a greater heat input, and consequently has a greater potential to degrade the items being sterilized.

The product-specific design approach requires a greater amount of initial and continuing information on: the items being sterilized, the indicator organisms (those test organisms shown to be most resistant to the sterilization process), and the bioburden levels. The accumulation of this information provides confidence in the values determined in development to use a lower thermal input than required for the overkill design approach.

Using a lower thermal input also has the added benefit of providing greater stability of the materials being sterilized, potentially increasing their shelf life. For this reason, the product-specific design approach is more appropriate for the terminal sterilization of heat-labile formulations in their final containers.

Commentary: Comparing the Two Design Approaches

Analogy

The overkill approach is like washing everything in the dishwasher at the highest temperature: simple and easy, but glassware may crack. The product-specific approach is like choosing the temperature by material: it needs more information, but it protects delicate items.

4.1.1

Survivor Curve in Cycle Design

Semilogarithmic model and F₀ calculation

4.1.1 Use of Survivor Curve in Cycle Design Approaches

The following semilogarithmic survivor curve equation is used in both cycle design approaches to determine the necessary lethal dose requirements to achieve the desired endpoint (NF) — a PNSU of 10−6.

Equation 1 — Survivor Curve

Log N_F = −F_BIO / D_T + Log N_0

Rearranged for F:
F_BIO = (Log N_0 − Log N_F) × D_T

Where:

  • N0 = initial bioburden population
  • NF = final (desired) bioburden population = 10−6 (PNSU)
  • DT = decimal reduction time at temperature T
  • FBIO = biological lethality delivered

From a design standpoint, the overkill and product-specific design approaches differ in the values that are used for D and N0.

Commentary: Survivor Curve and F₀ Calculation

The semilogarithmic survivor curve equation is the mathematical core of TR1 sterilization design. Both design approaches use the same equation; they differ only in the assumed values of N₀ and DT.

Key Parameters

  • N₀ (initial microbial count): The overkill approach assumes 10⁶; the product-specific approach uses measured values.
  • NF (target microbial count): Both approaches target 10⁻⁶ (PNSU ≤ 10⁻⁶).
  • DT (decimal reduction time): The time required at a given temperature to kill 90% of the microorganisms.
  • F₀ (equivalent sterilization minutes at 121°C): A composite measure of lethality.

Meaning of PNSU

PNSU (Probability of a Non-Sterile Unit) = 10⁻⁶ means that no more than one non-sterile unit is expected per million units of product. This is the globally accepted standard of sterility assurance.

4.1.1.1

Overkill Design Approach

Worst-case assumptions — F₀ ≥ 12 minutes

4.1.1.1 Overkill Design Approach

The objective of the overkill design approach is to assure a level of sterility assurance regardless of the number and heat resistance of the actual bioburden organisms in the load. The assumed product bioburden values for population and resistance are set at the following levels:

Overkill Design Assumptions

N_0     = 10⁶ organisms
D_121°C = 1.0 minute
z       = 10°C
N_F     = 10⁻⁶ (PNSU target)

Calculated minimum lethality:
F_0 = D_121°C × (Log N_0 − Log N_F)
F_0 = 1.0 min × (Log 10⁶ − Log 10⁻⁶)
F_0 = 1.0 × 12 = 12 minutes

Thus, a cycle designed with the overkill design approach can be defined as a sterilization cycle that is demonstrated to deliver an FPHY and FBIO of at least 12 minutes to the items being sterilized.

EU regulations for terminally sterilized dosage forms defines overkill as sterilization by moist heat at 121°C for 15 minutes.

Very few naturally occurring microorganisms have been found to have a D121°C-value greater than 0.5 minutes. The overkill design approach assumes both a higher bioburden population and resistance than would be expected. Because worst-case assumptions are made for the bioburden population and resistance with this design approach, there is little scientific necessity for routine bioburden monitoring of the load items.

Commentary: The Overkill Approach in Detail

The overkill approach applies "worst-case assumptions": an initial microbial count as high as 10⁶ and a D-value as high as 1 minute (well above the actual D-values of most environmental microorganisms, which are <0.5 minutes).

Key Features of the Overkill Approach

  • Minimum F₀ requirement: 12 minutes (PDA calculated value)
  • EU regulatory definition: 121°C × 15 minutes (more conservative)
  • No routine bioburden monitoring is needed (worst-case assumptions are built in).
  • Suitable for: heat-resistant instruments, hard goods, porous loads.
  • Not suitable for: heat-sensitive drug products (degradation would result).

PDA vs. EU Standard Differences

PDA calculates a minimum F₀ of 12 minutes, while EU regulations require 121°C/15 minutes. In practice, most facilities adopt 121°C/15 minutes to satisfy both standards while retaining a safety margin.

Analogy

The overkill approach is like "washing every garment at 95°C no matter how dirty it is": the procedure is designed for the dirtiest case, so the wash will always get the clothes clean, but cotton garments may shrink.

4.1.1.2

Product-Specific Design Approach

Bioburden-based cycle design for heat-labile products

4.1.1.2 Product-Specific Design Approach

An overkill design approach cannot ordinarily be used when sterilizing heat-labile products or items. A cycle must be developed which will adequately destroy the microbial load but will not result in unacceptable product degradation. The cycle is dependent on studies to determine the number and heat resistance of microorganisms in the product. Once characterized, a cycle can be designed that will result in a PNSU of 10−6.

For design purposes, the values selected for N0 and DT are based on values determined by bioburden analysis plus additional safety margins based on: 1) professional judgment; 2) the extent of the bioburden data; and 3) the degree of product bioburden testing that will be conducted on an ongoing basis.

Actual bioburden population for products made under CGMPs should be very low, 1.0–100 cfu per container. Generally, only spore-forming environmental or product isolates need be subjected to D-value determination. Heat-shocking the product for 10–15 minutes at 80–100°C can screen out less resistant organisms.

The value selected for DT should include a safety margin over the most resistant organisms detected in bioburden studies. The safety margin selected inversely correlates to the frequency and magnitude of ongoing tests conducted for bioburden population and resistance.

When multiple products are processed using the same cycle, a minimum lethality (e.g., an F0 of 8.0 minutes) may be specified, regardless of the results of bioburden testing for individual products. Ongoing bioburden monitoring is then conducted to assure that the specified cycle continues to be appropriate.

Product-Specific Examples (from TR1)

Example 1: Observed N₀ = 10¹, Dobs = 0.25 min; Design uses N₀ = 10², DT = 0.4 min → Minimum F₀ = 3.2 min (frequent bioburden monitoring required)

Example 2: Observed N₀ = 10¹, Dobs = 0.25 min; Design uses N₀ = 10², DT = 1.0 min → Minimum F₀ = 8.0 min (conservative D-value reduces ongoing monitoring burden)

Commentary: The Product-Specific Approach in Detail

The product-specific approach is the only workable sterilization design path for heat-sensitive drug products (such as protein formulations and certain small-molecule drugs). The core principle is to replace worst-case assumptions with measured bioburden data in order to reduce heat input.

Steps in Developing a Product-Specific Process

  1. Determine the actual bioburden of the product (N₀, cfu per container).
  2. Use heat shock (80–100°C, 10–15 min) to screen for spore-forming organisms.
  3. Determine the D-value of the most heat-resistant isolate (DT).
  4. Set the design N₀ and DT after adding a safety margin.
  5. Calculate the minimum F₀ = (Log N₀ − Log NF) × DT.
  6. Establish an ongoing bioburden monitoring program.

Inverse Relationship Between Safety Margin and Monitoring Frequency

The closer the design DT is to the measured value, the smaller the safety margin and the more frequent the ongoing monitoring that is needed.

If the design DT is much greater than the measured value (for example, 1.0 min is selected but only 0.25 min is measured), the safety margin is large and the monitoring frequency can be reduced.

This is a focus of regulatory document review: is the rationale for the D-value selection justified?

4.2

Load Types

Porous/Hard Goods vs. Liquid — classifying items for process selection

4.2 Load Types

The next step in sterilization process development is to determine the exact physical nature of each discrete container, package or other item that collectively constitute the load to be sterilized. By first understanding the physical characteristics of each item in the load, such as permeability to steam, the load can be further characterized (or "typed") as porous/hard goods or liquid. Based on this determination, it is possible to select an appropriate sterilization process.

Commentary: Why Load Classification Matters

Load type is the key branching point in selecting a sterilization process. Items with different physical characteristics differ completely in steam penetration and heat transfer, so the load must be classified first in order to choose the correct process.

Classification Logic

Can steam directly contact the surface of the items? → Porous/hard goods load
Is liquid filled in sealed containers? → Liquid load (heat transfer by conduction/convection)

4.2.1

Porous/Hard Goods Loads

Steam-contact sterilization — filters, closures, machine parts

4.2.1 Defining Porous/Hard Goods Loads

Porous/hard goods loads contain items where sterilization is achieved through direct contact with saturated steam. Heat is transferred when steam condenses directly on the surface of the items being sterilized. (By contrast, energy from moist heat is transferred to the contents of liquid-filled containers through conduction and/or convection.)

Porous/hard goods loads, as used in the pharmaceutical industry, encompass true porous items (such as cartridge filters and packed fabric) and hard items (such as stainless-steel vessels and filling machine parts). It is not common practice to develop specific sterilization cycles for each item type. It is common to have standard cycles that are capable of providing the same minimum level of sterility assurance, irrespective of load content.

Examples of porous/hard goods items include but are not restricted to:

  • Filters* (disc membranes, cartridge membranes, and depth filters)
  • Stoppers and other polymeric closure materials
  • Tubing and hoses
  • Garments
  • Cleaning equipment
  • Machine change parts

* Filters should be sterilized according to manufacturers' recommendations.

Commentary: Porous/Hard Goods Loads

The Cold Spot Concept

In a porous/hard goods load, the cold spot is the location in the load that is slowest to reach the set temperature. During validation, temperature sensors must be placed at the cold spot to ensure that even the coolest location achieves the required F₀.

Common cold spot locations: the center of a filter cartridge, the inner layers of tightly packed fabric, and the far end of a bend in tubing.

Analogy

A porous load is like "buns in a bamboo steamer": the steam has to penetrate every layer and every corner, and a cooked outside does not mean the inside is cooked too.

4.2.2

Liquid Loads

Sealed containers — terminal sterilization of drug products

4.2.2 Defining Liquid Loads

Sealed liquid-filled containers in a production setting are often homogeneous, comprised of containers of a single size, single fill-volume, and are from a single lot. Liquid load cycles are usually developed and validated using the product-specific design approach, although the overkill design approach may also be used.

If the product is not an aqueous solution (e.g., some oil-based products), special consideration should be made in order to ensure it is suitable for moist heat sterilization.

Examples of liquid-filled containers include, but are not restricted to:

  • Formulations (solutions, suspensions and/or emulsions) in their final product container (e.g., vial, bag, bottle, syringe or ampoules)
  • Post-test or post-process waste fluids containing potentially pathogenic microorganisms

Commentary: Characteristics of Liquid Loads

Liquid loads are most commonly encountered in the terminal sterilization of final injectable products. Heat transfer differs from that of porous loads: it relies mainly on conduction and convection rather than direct steam contact.

Key Points for Liquid Load Design

  • The product-specific design approach is usually used (because most liquid formulations have heat stability limits).
  • Container type affects heat transfer efficiency: glass vials vs. plastic bags vs. prefilled syringes.
  • Non-aqueous solutions (such as oil-based formulations) require special evaluation of their suitability for moist heat sterilization.
  • Headspace gas inside a sealed container expands on heating and generates internal pressure.

Why Do Liquid Loads Need Air Overpressure?

When sealed containers (such as prefilled syringes and plastic bags) are heated, the pressure inside the container rises. If the chamber pressure is insufficient, the container may deform or even burst (syringe plungers may be pushed out). Air overpressure is therefore needed to balance the pressure difference between the inside and outside of the container.

4.3

Sterilization Processes

Saturated steam and air overpressure processes — overview

4.3 Sterilization Processes

For moist heat sterilization, two major types of processes are typically used: saturated steam sterilization processes and air overpressure sterilization processes. Saturated steam processes are typically used for sterilization of porous/hard goods loads, while air overpressure processes are typically used for liquid product loads.

Commentary: Overview of Process Types

4.3.1

Saturated Steam Processes

Pre-vacuum (SFPP) and Gravity Displacement cycles

4.3.1 Saturated Steam Processes

There are two main types of saturated steam sterilization processes: prevacuum and gravity displacement. The principles of saturated steam are discussed in Section 3.3.3.

4.3.1.1 Prevacuum Process

The most commonly used saturated steam process is the prevacuum process. This method removes air from the chamber using a mechanical vacuum pump or steam eductor before the sterilization phase of the process begins. The prevacuum process is particularly suited for load items that can trap air, such as tubing, filters and filling machine assemblies. Vacuum pulsing processes are used frequently in the pharmaceutical industry to sterilize porous/hard goods loads that contain items from which air removal can be difficult.

Treatment of the load prior to the start of the sterilization exposure phase is important. If each vacuum pull is to 0.1 atmosphere, then each pulse will reduce the air in the sterilizer by 90%, or one log. Three vacuum pulses (three vacuum pulls with corresponding steam charges) create a 3-log reduction, effectively removing 99.9% of the air. Additional positive pulses (steam charges with corresponding evacuations above atmospheric pressure to avoid air leaking into the chamber) may be added to condition the load. Through this approach, air removal efficiency can be increased, leading to shorter equilibration times. The precise number and type of pulses should be determined during the development of the cycle.

4.3.1.2 Gravity Displacement Process

The typical gravity displacement process is based on the principle that cold air within the chamber is heavier than the steam entering and will sink to the bottom of the chamber. As the steam enters the chamber, air is pushed out of the drain at the bottom of the chamber and exits (with the condensate) through the steam trap.

The success of the process in removing air depends on the correct operation of the trap and the proper distribution of steam. Steam is injected into the sterilizer chamber through a baffle or spreader bar (such as a perforated pipe). If steam is added too rapidly or not distributed properly, air pockets may be trapped near the top of the load. If steam is added too slowly, the air can be heated, diffuse into the steam, and become more difficult to remove.

Gravity displacement sterilizers are less efficient in air removal than other designs and are not recommended for items with air removal challenges.

Commentary: Comparing Saturated Steam Processes

Pulse Logic of the Pre-Vacuum Cycle

Each vacuum pulse down to 0.1 atm removes 90% of the air (1 log).
3 pulses = 3 log reduction = 99.9% of the air removed.
Positive pulse: after the vacuum, steam is introduced to pressurize the chamber and the vacuum is drawn again, which further conditions the load and shortens the equilibration time.

Failure Risks of Gravity Displacement

Gravity displacement can fail in two scenarios:
1. Steam enters too quickly: an air pocket forms at the top, causing a cold spot.
2. Steam enters too slowly: the air is heated and diffuses, making it harder to remove.

Gravity displacement therefore requires very tight control of the steam admission rate.

Analogy

Pre-vacuum is like "using a vacuum cleaner to remove the air first, then filling with steam." Gravity displacement is like "opening the door and letting hot air push out the cold air": the latter depends on the physical density difference, and many factors affect its performance.

4.3.2

Air Overpressure Processes

SAM and Superheated Water — for sealed liquid containers

4.3.2 Air Overpressure Processes

In nearly all liquid containers, there is gas (air, nitrogen, or other inert gas) in the headspace above the liquid. As the liquid is heated, the headspace gas expands, and pressure within the container increases.

For most liquid load applications, such as prefilled syringes, some glass bottles or vials, plastic bags and semirigid containers, the chamber pressure needs to be increased to minimize the differential pressure (between the internal container pressure and the chamber), maintaining the container shape and closure integrity, and in the case of syringes, stopper position. The air overpressure needed to compensate for internal container pressure may vary significantly, depending on the item type (i.e., glass bottles versus plastic bags).

It is common to use oil-free compressed air for air overpressure processes. The quality of the air is dependent on the application. In some cases, a microbial-retentive air filter may be necessary in the air supply line.

4.3.2.1 Steam-Air Mixture (SAM) Process

When air is added to steam to create a total pressure above the saturation pressure of the steam (at the specified temperature), it is called a steam-air mixture (SAM) process. The presence of air, although necessary, reduces the heat transfer rate when compared to saturated steam. The steam-air mixture process must continuously circulate the steam and air to:

  • Prevent stratification and formation of cold spots within the load
  • Reduce depletion of steam in the steam-air mixture next to the cold container

Fans are typically used to circulate the steam-air mixture. Internal container pressure and temperature dynamics are a function of the container type (e.g., rigid or nonrigid), fill volume, head space volume, and chamber temperature.

The steam-air mixture process can use various methods to cool the product after the exposure phase. The most common method is to cool the recirculating air by adding chilled water to the sterilizer jacket or to coils within the sterilizer. Some steam-air sterilizers reduce the product temperature by spraying cooling water over the product.

4.3.2.2 Superheated Water Process

Sterilization of liquid-filled containers with recirculating superheated water is an efficient way to sterilize some products. The most common type uses a pump to continuously recirculate water from the bottom of the sterilizer (below the load zone) to spray nozzles above the load zone. A slight modification uses a water distribution pan to create a water cascade in lieu of spray nozzles.

The recirculating water can be heated and cooled, either directly by the injection of steam and cooling water or indirectly via heat exchanger(s). Use of an indirect cooling method is preferred, because the cooling water that directly contacts the sealed containers has been sterilized along with the product.

Another type involves the complete submersion of the product in water. Most superheated water processes are conducted in batch sterilizers, but continuous sterilizers can also be used.

All of these recirculating superheated water processes use air overpressure that can be controlled during the sterilization process. The minimum overpressure is determined by the temperature being used, the pressure needed to maintain desired product characteristics, and the required overpressure needed to keep the recirculation pump primed.

One of the primary advantages of the recirculating water process over other steam sterilization methods is that the heat-up and cool-down rates are easier to control and, if set up properly, are not influenced by variations in product load and utility supply.

The primary quality attribute of the water used is the microbial content. The water may be sterilized in the chamber with the load, sterilized in a separate vessel, maintained at elevated temperatures, or chemically treated to maintain the desired low microbial content. The use of recirculating water provides efficient cooling of terminally sterilized products, thereby increasing sterilizer throughput. This rapid cooling also might be necessary to help ensure product stability.

Commentary: Air Overpressure Processes in Detail

Air overpressure processes are the standard choice for liquid loads (sealed containers). The core challenge is that the pressure inside the container rises during heating, and if external pressure is not supplied to compensate, the container may deform or be damaged.

Cold Spot Risk in SAM Processes

The presence of air makes the heat transfer rate lower than that of pure saturated steam. If circulation is uneven, cold spots can form around the containers: regions where the temperature is below the set point. Validation must confirm that fan circulation eliminates stratification.

Water Quality Requirements for Superheated Water Processes

The circulating water in direct contact with sealed containers must have a low microbial content. Common methods:
1. Sterilize it in the chamber together with the product.
2. Pre-sterilize it in a separate vessel.
3. Maintain it at high temperature (to inhibit microbial growth).
4. Use chemical treatment to maintain low bioburden.

Indirect cooling (through a heat exchanger) is preferred over direct injection of cold water because the indirect method does not introduce new microbial risk.

Analogy

A superheated water process is like "cooking canned food in a pressure cooker with water": water-bath heating is uniform and heating and cooling rates are stable, which makes it especially suitable for plastic containers or biological products that are sensitive to thermal shock.

Cycle Development

Determining physical parameters of the sterilization cycle for a defined load pattern

4.4 Cycle Development

Cycle development is the process of determining the physical parameters of the sterilization cycle that will be used to sterilize the items in a defined load pattern. The goal of cycle development is to identify critical and key operating parameters that will result in a product or material that is both sterile and functional after being sterilized. This development process is often documented in a formalized development plan.

Core Concept: Objectives of Cycle Development

The Dual Objectives of Cycle Development

Sterility: ensure the product or material reaches a sterile state (meeting the PNSU requirement)

Functionality: ensure the function of the product or material is not affected by sterilization (e.g., packaging integrity, drug product stability)

GMP Practice Tip

Document the development plan in a written Formalized Development Plan that covers study design, acceptance criteria and the logic for identifying parameters. It is the basis for subsequent PQ execution.

4.4.1

Porous/Hard Goods Cycle Development

Air removal, item mapping, preparation, load patterns, operating parameters

The greatest obstacle to achieving repeatable and predictable assurances of sterility for porous/hard goods loads is the potential presence of air within the individual items. It is important to ensure that sufficient air is removed from the sterilizer chamber and items prior to the exposure phase of the cycle. A supply of saturated, dry steam to the sterilizer is a specific requirement for porous/hard goods items and may not be essential for sterilization of liquid filled containers.

Biological indicators and air removal test kits may be useful in cycle development. Development studies may vary according to prior knowledge of the sterilization process and of sterilization of similar loads.

It may be beneficial to use already developed sterilization cycles to minimize the total number of different cycles used in a company in order to reduce the ongoing costs of qualification and requalification. In this type of strategy, a company analyzes whether using an existing cycle provides sufficient assurance that the newly identified load will achieve the desired PNSU without compromising quality.

The Biggest Challenge: Air Entrapment

Everyday Analogy: Air Bubbles in a Hot Water Bottle

Imagine loading bottles into a dishwasher: if air remains inside a bottle, hot water cannot reach the bottom to clean it. In the same way, if air remains inside an item during steam sterilization, steam cannot reach the deepest point, and that area never reaches sterilization temperature.

Strategies

  • Ensure a supply of saturated dry steam
  • Effective pre-vacuum pulses
  • Air removal test kits (Bowie-Dick Test / Air Removal Test Kits)
  • Supporting verification with biological indicators

Cost-Effectiveness Considerations

Reuse validated cycles wherever possible and keep the number of distinct cycles low, which reduces the cost of ongoing requalification. This is a common cycle management strategy in pharmaceutical plants.

4.4.1.1 Slowest-to-Heat Location on an Item

Before loaded-chamber heat penetration studies are performed, item-mapping studies may be necessary to identify appropriate monitoring location(s) within individual load items. This may be referred to as item-temperature mapping because it is done to determine the location within the item or package that is the most difficult to heat.

Item temperature mapping should be conducted on the more difficult to heat items (e.g., large mass, potential for trapped air, hoses with longest lengths or any combination of these issues). When conducting item temperature mapping, it is important to consider the types of challenges the item may represent (e.g., air removal versus significant mass) and to position the temperature probes in slowest-to-heat locations.

Item mapping studies can be conducted in a sterilizer (including laboratory sterilizers) other than the one to be used in commercial production, provided that the sterilizing conditions and cycles are similar. Multiple temperature sensors may be needed to adequately measure the penetration of steam in large items. During mapping studies, the items should be prepared according to established procedures. Care should be taken when introducing temperature sensors into the item, as they may artificially facilitate or obstruct steam entry into the item.

Temperature Mapping: The Slowest-to-Heat Location

Key Points for Item Mapping

  • Item selection: large-mass items and items where air may be trapped (e.g., long tubing, containers of complex shape)
  • Probe placement: at the most difficult-to-heat position (Slowest-to-Heat Location)
  • Where to perform it: may be done in a laboratory sterilizer, provided the conditions are similar
  • Multiple measurement points: large items need multiple temperature sensors

Probe Installation Considerations

Inserting a temperature probe into an item may artificially facilitate or impede steam entry. This interference is often overlooked in item mapping and should be recorded in the study report.

Analogy: Finding the Dead Spot in a Refrigerator

Just as you would test which spot in a refrigerator is hardest to cool (a corner, or tight against the wall), item mapping aims to find the location steam reaches last, and to confirm that it also reaches sterilization temperature.

4.4.1.2 Item Preparation

Porous/hard goods items may be prepared for sterilization in a variety of ways. Examples include, but are not limited to:

  • Items contained in steam and air permeable wrappings (e.g., paper or other polymeric wrapping materials, nonshedding fabric or combinations)
  • Items in closed, but not sealed, boxes (stainless steel or anodized aluminum perforated to allow steam penetration, air removal and drainage of any condensate)
  • Items placed on open trays (with or without steam and air-permeable wrapping)
  • Items in static or rotating drum containers (e.g., stoppers)

Items for use in aseptic processing must be packaged in order to maintain sterility prior to use. A careful balance needs to be achieved between the steam permeability of wrapping materials to ensure adequate air and condensate removal with the need to provide a non-shedding microbial-protective barrier.

Preparation methods should be well defined in operating procedures. Strict adherence to these procedures is important to assure proper sterilization. Item preparation may include: cleaning, rinsing, drying, wrapping, and storage.

Wrapping materials or containers used in sterilization should be constructed of nonshedding material. Aluminum foil, glassine paper and other non-permeable materials should not be used for wrapping items to be sterilized by saturated steam.

Metal containers should be of stainless steel or anodized aluminum. Plain aluminum is a source of particulate matter and should not be used. When process equipment includes vents or filters, they should be designed to ensure rapid equilibration of pressure during the sterilization cycle.

Item Preparation: Packaging and Material Requirements

The Core Conflict in Packaging Materials

Packaging materials must meet two competing requirements at once:

  • Steam penetration: allow steam in and allow air and condensate to be removed
  • Microbial barrier: prevent post-sterilization contamination (non-shedding barrier)

Prohibited Materials

  • Aluminum foil: impermeable to steam
  • Glassine paper: impermeable to steam
  • Plain aluminum containers: a source of particulate contamination

Acceptable materials: stainless steel, anodized aluminum, and breathable non-shedding packaging materials.

Analogy: How a Surgical Mask Works

Good packaging works like a medical mask: it lets air (steam) pass through but blocks microorganisms. The design must find the best balance between the two.

4.4.1.3 Porous/Hard Goods Load Patterns

After the operational qualification and prior to beginning the performance qualification, load types and patterns need to be determined and documented. The following considerations should be given to sterilization effectiveness and production efficiency:

  • Load items should not come into contact with the interior surfaces of the chamber.
  • Contact between flat surfaces of metal boxes and trays may be minimized by use of racks with perforated, and if necessary, adjustable shelving.
  • Item orientation should be well defined to facilitate air removal, condensate drainage and steam penetration (e.g., buckets should be sterilized upside down), and should be documented.
  • Largest mass items should be placed on the lower shelves of the sterilizer to minimize wetting by condensate.
  • An important consideration is control over the number of articles in the sterilizer. In the event the load size is expected to vary, minimum and maximum loads should be identified. A sound bracketing approach to qualifying intermediate loads should include the most-difficult-to-sterilize load item(s) in the minimum load.
  • Variable loading patterns may be possible if qualification studies demonstrate item position does not affect sterilization efficacy.
  • Loading instructions should be documented and readily available for operator reference.

Load Patterns: Loading Logic and the Bracketing Strategy

Bracketing Approach

When load size is variable, only the minimum load and the maximum load need to be validated, and the minimum load must include the most difficult-to-sterilize items. Loads of intermediate size are "bracketed" and do not need to be validated individually.

This is a resource-saving validation approach recognized by both ICH Q5C and PDA.

4.4.1.4 Porous/Hard Goods Operating Parameter Determination

One of the more crucial aspects of cycle development is to identify operating parameters in order to meet the process design objectives and to determine if they are critical or key parameters.

Critical parameters are linked to safety and efficacy of the product. Failure to meet a critical parameter should result in rejection of the load.

Key parameters assure the ongoing "state of control" of sterilization runs. The failure to meet a key process parameter should result in an investigation with a documented rationale for the disposition of the load.

Critical Parameters vs. Key Parameters

Regulatory Link

This classification is consistent with FDA 21 CFR Part 211 and EMA expectations for process validation: critical parameters must be confirmed during validation and monitored and recorded for every batch.

Analogy: An Aircraft Instrument Panel

A critical parameter is like engine temperature (if it goes out of range, the aircraft is grounded immediately). A key parameter is like fuel quantity (it must be investigated for its effect on range, but generally does not ground the aircraft immediately).

4.4.1.5 Equilibration Time

Equilibration time is an important function of conditioning porous/hard goods loads that includes the number and depth of prevacuum and positive pulses.

The equilibration time is the period that elapses between attainment of the minimum specified sterilizing temperature in the chamber (chamber reference temperature — typically in the drain) and attainment of the minimum specified sterilization temperature in the load, as measured by the slowest-to-heat penetration probe. This period is an indication of the ability to properly condition the load through air removal and load heating.

Extended equilibration times can be indicative of inadequate air removal or heating, even if the desired temperature is eventually achieved. When developing a cycle, it is important to take practical precautions to minimize equilibration time. The following options can be used to reduce equilibration time:

  • Assure loads are oriented for efficient air removal (e.g., hoses not pinched)
  • Increase number of vacuum or positive steam pulses
  • Add hold steps during vacuum and/or steam pulses
  • Increase depth of vacuum pulses
  • Optimize steam exposure to load items

If none of the above actions are successful, then consideration may be given to modifying the configuration of items (e.g., shortening hose length). However, this should only be done after giving full consideration to all other risks that may compromise sterility.

Equilibration Time: An Indicator for Diagnosing Air Removal Problems

What a Long Equilibration Time Means

A prolonged equilibration time is an early warning sign that indicates:

  • Incomplete air removal (air removal inadequate)
  • Inefficient heating of the load (poor load conditioning)
  • Even if the target temperature is eventually reached, the process is not under control

Analogy: Preheating an Oven

Equilibration time is like the time food needs, once placed in a preheated oven, for its inside to catch up with the oven temperature. If the wait is too long, the food is too thick or placed badly; the oven is not the problem.

4.4.1.6 Evaluating FBIO and FPHYS Agreement

For a sterilization cycle, when the FBIO of a BI is measurable, the FBIO and FPHYS, measured at the same location, should be equal. The BI inactivation requirements of the qualification sterilization cycle are for BIs to be negative; this requires a large FPHYS.

The heat input necessary to achieve kill of BIs can be calculated. If the actual lethality (FPHYS-delivered) is substantially less than the required (FPHYS-required), the test cycle will result in BIs that are positive for growth.

Example: If a biological indicator has DT = 2.5 minutes and N0 = 3 × 106, then FBIO = DT × log N0 = 2.5 × 6.47 = 16.2 minutes. To kill to PNSU of 10-2 (NF = 10-2): FT = (log N0 − log NF) × DT = (log 3×106 − log 10-2) × 2.5 = 21.2 minutes.

If the actual biological lethality delivered is less than this predicted lethality based on physical measurements, then BIs placed in the load should be positive for growth at a rate higher than 1.0 in 100. This would indicate potential problems with the delivery of steam.

While there is no standard approach to designing studies to evaluate the agreement of FBIO and FPHYS, it is important to note that while this evaluation provides a higher degree of process understanding, many successful cycles have been developed and qualified without this evaluation.

Biological Lethality vs. Physically Measured Lethality

Relationship Between FBIO and FPHYS

F_BIO  = D_T × log(N₀)         → lethality required of the BI itself
F_PHYS = integrated value from physical temperature probes  → lethality actually delivered

Ideal case: F_PHYS ≥ F_BIO
Problem case: F_PHYS << F_BIO → BI positive → steam delivery problem

Why Compare the Two?

If the physically measured lethality (FPHYS) is far higher than the biological lethality (FBIO) but the BI is still positive, there is a steam delivery problem (e.g., residual air or uneven steam distribution). This is an important tool for verifying process understanding.

Regulatory Position

PDA TR1 encourages this evaluation to improve process understanding, but states clearly that many successful cycles have been validated without it. It is a plus, not a mandatory requirement.

Liquid Load Cycle Development

Container cold-spot mapping, load patterns, operating parameters for liquid fills

4.4.2 Liquid Load Cycle Development

The sterilization of sealed container contents is achieved through transfer of energy from the heating medium to the aqueous liquid within. The water content of the liquid product provides the moisture needed for sterilization within the container. For sterilization of aqueous suspensions and emulsions, the load may have to be kept in motion (i.e., tumbled) to facilitate internal heat circulation.

The major concern in the terminal sterilization of product formulations is the development of a sterilization cycle that ensures that sufficient lethality has been provided to the cold spot of the coldest container within the load, while at the same time making certain that the hottest container in the load retains its product quality attributes. Due to these factors, care must be taken to ensure that:

  • The load is in the same position during validation and routine processing
  • The heat input to the load is uniform to avoid inadvertent over- or under-processing
  • The bioburden of the filled containers meets established limits
  • The air overpressure (where utilized) is sufficient to minimize the breakage or distortion of containers

The following factors should be considered when developing sterilization cycles for liquid-filled container loads:

  • Efficient heating of the exterior surfaces of the fluid containers by steam, steam-air or superheated water, as needed, to attain uniform sterilizing conditions across the entire load
  • Allowance for efficient cooling of the load post-exposure to protect product quality attributes
  • Product stability
  • Container/closure integrity and minimization of container breakage or container deformation through appropriate pressure balance
  • Sterilization of fluid path (product contact) closure interface
  • Temperature mapping within the container
  • Biological indicator resistance in product formulation
  • Sterilizer racks/stacks/trays should be well designed for the type of heating medium and the type of fluid containers

The Core Challenge of Liquid Loads

Balancing Two Competing Demands

Validation of a liquid load must satisfy two opposing requirements at once:

  • The cold spot of the coldest container: must receive sufficient lethality (F₀) to ensure sterility
  • The hottest container: must not be overheated, so that product quality attributes (stability, appearance, pH, etc.) are preserved

4.4.2.1 Container Cold-Spot Mapping

The container cold spot is the location in the sealed liquid container that achieves the lowest process lethality (F0) during the process. Use of a cold spot is a conservative approach for cycle development, because it assumes all of the microorganisms in a container exist at the cold spot and are only exposed to the temperatures achieved at that location.

For large-volume parenteral loads (LVPs) (>100 mL), the cold spot is typically located between the geometric center of the product and the bottom of the product along the vertical axis and should be confirmed.

Cold-spot mapping is typically not determined in small-volume parenteral (SVPs) (≤100 mL), since the solution heats at almost the same rate as the sterilizer.

Container orientation can also affect the cold-spot location. When the container is rotated or tumbled during the process, there may be no discernible cold spot.

Small-volume parenterals (≤100 mL) are small enough that the whole container heats at almost the same rate as the chamber, with no significant temperature gradient, so a separate cold-spot study is not needed.

Analogy: Heating a Cup of Water vs. a Pot of Water

A small cup of water (SVP) in a microwave heats through almost all at once; in a large pot of water (LVP), the bottom is the last to heat through. Cold-spot identification is meant to confirm that "last place to heat through".

4.4.2.2 Liquid Load Patterns

The following factors should be taken into account when assembling sealed, liquid-filled container loads for steam sterilization:

  • Efficient penetration of the load by steam, steam-air or superheated water as needed to attain uniform sterilizing conditions across the entire load
  • Allowance for efficient cooling of the load post-sterilization to protect product quality attributes and/or growth promotion properties
  • Minimization of container breakage/deformation through appropriate pressure balance
  • If the load size is expected to vary, minimum and maximum loads should be identified

Cool and hot zones for the load should be established by heat penetration mapping for each of the container and load sizes to be processed in the sterilizer. This is accomplished through multiple runs of various load and container sizes using sealed, liquid-filled containers. During these trials, load density, rack position, tray height and other parameters should be clearly defined for the loading patterns to be tested during the challenge studies.

At the conclusion of these trials, load patterns and qualification monitoring locations should be established and documented.

Implementation Strategy for Temperature Mapping

Heat Penetration Mapping

Purpose: identify the cold and hot zones across the whole load and determine the worst-case load position.

  • Must be performed multiple times (different container sizes and loads)
  • Performed on sealed, liquid-filled containers
  • Record load density, shelf position, tray height, etc.

Determining the Monitoring Locations for Validation

After the heat penetration studies are complete, the following must be determined and documented:

  • Worst-case locations: used as the monitoring points in PQ
  • Load patterns: listed in the SOP for operator reference

An important aspect of cycle development is to identify operating parameters in order to meet process design objectives and to determine if they are critical or key parameters. Table 4.4.2.3-1 lists various parameter considerations for liquid loads.

  • Fan RPM / agitation rate: ensures uniform heating; a failure must trigger an immediate alarm
  • Pressure ramp-up / ramp-down rate: prevents container deformation (e.g., syringe plunger displacement, glass vial breakage)
  • Maximum F₀ limit: controls overheating to protect product quality. This is a consideration unique to liquid loads

The Concept of Two-Sided F₀ Control

Liquid loads (especially heat-sensitive products) require control of both:

  • Minimum F₀: assures sterility (critical)
  • Maximum F₀: protects product stability (key)

This is a fundamental difference from porous loads, where only the minimum F₀ matters.

Stability Studies

Product and material compatibility with the sterilization cycle

4.5 Stability Studies

Stability studies for terminally sterilized products are required regardless of the design approach used. Product characteristics studied to determine the impact of the terminal sterilization cycle on product stability may include:

  • Product degradation
  • Assay values
  • pH
  • Color
  • Buffering capacity
  • Product-specific quality attributes

Sterilization and degradation reactions are cumulative over both time and temperature. This means that heat-up and cool-down variations will affect stability as well as lethality. Therefore, stability studies should expose product to worst-case heat input conditions.

When cycles are controlled to obtain the same total accumulated product F0, higher temperature cycles and shorter times are believed to have a less adverse effect on products, but the actual impact of the selected cycle should be evaluated.

Stability Studies: The Last Line of Defense for Functionality

Why Are Stability Studies Mandatory?

Terminal sterilization applies high temperature. While this kills microorganisms, it can also degrade drug molecules, change pH and affect packaging integrity. Stability studies confirm that the product still meets specification after sterilization.

The Worst-Case Exposure Principle

Stability studies must use worst-case heat input conditions, because:

  • Heat exposure during heat-up and cool-down also has a cumulative effect
  • Heat-up and cool-down rates may differ from batch to batch
  • The scenario with the longest exposure time must be covered

Analogy: How Cooking Affects Food

High temperature for a short time (e.g., stir-frying) and low temperature for a long time (e.g., slow stewing) may achieve the same "kill effect", but their effects on flavor, nutrition and texture are completely different. Likewise, different combinations of sterilization parameters (high temperature/short time vs. low temperature/long time) must each be evaluated for their effect on the product.

The Advantage of High Temperature and Short Time

At the same total F₀, a higher temperature, shorter time cycle generally causes less product degradation. This is also the trend in modern sterilization cycle design, but in every case actual stability data are needed to support it.

Process Performance Qualification — Overview

Demonstrating consistent delivery of sterilization lethality across all load configurations

5.0 Process Performance Qualification

Process Performance Qualification (PPQ) is conducted to demonstrate that the sterilization process consistently meets the design criteria determined for the cycle. Performance qualification follows parameter development as an integral part of the life cycle approach to sterilization process validation and consists of the following two elements:

  • Physical qualification, which includes temperature distribution and heat penetration runs, confirms that the desired Fphys is consistently delivered throughout the load.
  • Biological qualification with appropriate microbiological challenges confirms that the required Fbio is consistently achieved throughout the load by the developed cycle.

Prior to performance qualification, the following qualification and development activities should be completed and documented:

  • Qualification of utilities (e.g., steam quality, compressed air, or coolants)
  • Qualification of the sterilizer (DQ, IQ, OQ or Commissioning) and calibration of critical instrumentation
  • Development of parameters for each phase of the cycle
  • Definition of loads, loading patterns, and determination of which loads will be used in qualification studies — minimum and maximum loads and difficult-to-sterilize items should be considered
  • Temperature mapping of the chamber and load items to identify appropriate locations for physical and biological evaluation

Consistency between physical and microbiological results is central to sterilization validation. Physical data from temperature and pressure measurements cannot alone confirm that lethality conditions have been achieved in items where steam or heat penetration may be difficult (e.g., syringe lumens, needle shields, filter membranes). Likewise, destruction of a Biological Indicator (BI) without consideration of physical parameters is not sufficient evidence of cycle suitability. Biological challenge results should be in general agreement with physical data, and vice versa.

The Minimum Acceptable Cycle (MAC), in terms of delivered lethality, should be both physically and biologically qualified. Routine operational cycles generally include a safety margin through the use of higher temperatures and/or longer exposure times.

For initial qualification, replicate runs should be conducted to confirm repeatability. Typical qualifications include three consecutive acceptable runs for each load configuration evaluated. Subsequent to the initial successful qualification, ongoing event-based and time-based requalification should be conducted.

Commentary

Core Purpose of PPQ

PPQ (Process Performance Qualification) is the key step that confirms the sterilization process can consistently and reliably meet its design criteria under actual load conditions. It answers one question: does the cycle we designed still work on real product?

Two Elements: Physical vs Biological

Physical Qualification: Thermocouples measure the heat input at each location to calculate Fphys and confirm that heat energy is evenly distributed.

Biological Qualification: Biological indicators (BIs) verify the actual lethality and Fbio is calculated. The two must be consistent with each other.

Analogy: Qualifying an Oven

Physical qualification is like using a thermometer to confirm that every spot in the oven is at the same temperature; biological qualification is actually putting food in and confirming the center is cooked through. You need both.

PPQ Prerequisites

PPQ is not the first step! Before PPQ you must complete DQ → IQ → OQ (equipment qualification), steam quality confirmation, instrument calibration and load definition.

Three Consecutive Successful Runs

Initial qualification requires at least three consecutive acceptable runs to confirm process repeatability. This is a regulatory expectation and cannot be skipped.

Key Terms

  • PPQ: Process Performance Qualification
  • MAC: Minimum Acceptable Cycle
  • Fphys: F value obtained from physical measurements
  • Fbio: F value obtained from biological qualification
  • BI: Biological Indicator

5.1 Physical Qualification

The primary objective of the physical component of cycle qualification is to obtain physical data confirming that the developed cycle consistently achieves the heat penetration requirements established during cycle design.

5.1.1 Temperature Distribution

The primary purpose of temperature distribution qualification is to verify uniform distribution of the heating medium across the load zone. Probe sensors should be placed in the load zone but should not be in contact with the load items or sterilizer hardware (e.g., carts, shelves, trays). Diagrams detailing specific temperature sensor locations for each load should be provided.

During temperature distribution qualification runs, critical and key operating parameters should be confirmed and documented. Acceptance criteria throughout the exposure phase may include a maximum:

  • Variation in the temperature measured by each probe
  • Variation in the temperature measured from probe to probe
  • Difference in temperature between the probes and the set temperature

5.1.2 Heat Penetration

Heat penetration qualification demonstrates that the desired amount of energy has been transferred to the materials (e.g., liquids) or surfaces of items within the load. Heat penetration data is used to calculate F0.

Heat penetration temperature probes should be positioned in the cold spot within containers of liquid or in the slowest-to-heat locations of porous/hard goods items determined to be most difficult to sterilize.

For liquid loads, probes are typically placed in containers throughout the load in a randomly generated or geometric pattern, as well as in any cold or hot zones within the chamber that may have been identified.

For porous/hard goods loads consisting of items with different heat penetration characteristics ("mixed loads"), probes should be placed in representative items of each item type. For loads with only one item type (e.g., stoppers), the same approach used for liquid loads should be adopted. Temperature sensor locations for each qualification load and the rationale for selecting each location should be documented.

Commentary

Temperature Distribution vs Heat Penetration: Two Different Questions

Temperature Distribution: Is the heating medium (steam) evenly distributed throughout the chamber? The probes do not touch the product; they measure the steam in the chamber space.

Heat Penetration: Does the heat actually reach the inside of the product or article? The probes are placed at the most difficult-to-heat location (the cold spot).

Analogy: Bathroom Heater

Temperature distribution qualification = confirming that every corner of the bathroom is at the same temperature (measured in the air); heat penetration qualification = confirming that the center of the towel is warm too (measured in contact with the item).

Probe Placement Principles

  • Temperature distribution probes: Do not touch the product; placed in the chamber space
  • Heat penetration probes: Placed at the "most difficult-to-heat location" (cold spot) — the center of the largest liquid container, or the deepest point of a porous/hard article
  • All probe locations must be documented in a diagram together with the rationale for their selection

Equilibration Time

The time from reaching the set temperature until the entire load has uniformly reached that temperature. Excessive equilibration time is a common cause of failure and an important item in the acceptance criteria.

F0 Distribution

One of the core outputs of physical qualification: calculate the cumulative F0 at each probe location in the load and confirm that the lowest F0 (the coldest spot) still meets the design requirement (MAC). The narrower the F0 distribution, the more uniform the heat input.

5.2

Biological Qualification

BI challenge systems, semilogarithmic model, placement strategy, and log reduction verification

5.2 Biological Qualification

The objective of the biological component of cycle qualification is to obtain biological data confirming that the developed cycle achieves the actual biological lethality requirements established during cycle design.

Biological qualification using microbiological challenges follows a straightforward sequence:

  1. An appropriate microbial challenge system is devised based on the desired lethality (F-value) determined during process design.
  2. The load is exposed to minimal or subminimal sterilization conditions.
  3. After completion of the cycle, the microbiological challenges are retrieved.
  4. Each microbiological challenge is individually incubated in appropriate media and conditions for growth of survivors.
  5. The results are evaluated to ensure that the spore log reduction achieved meets predetermined acceptance criteria.
  6. Growth of the microbiological challenge organism is required in positive controls.

5.2.1 Biological Indicator Challenge Systems

In order to assess whether the cycle delivers sufficient lethality to meet the design requirements, an appropriate microbiological challenge should be selected to give meaningful results. The challenge should be presented in an appropriate fashion (the biological indicator challenge system) that may differ for the various types of load, and should have a resistance and challenge size appropriate for its purpose. This biological qualification data is used to calculate the Fbio for the cycle.

5.2.1.1 Determination of Population and Resistance of BI Challenge Systems

The semilogarithmic model can be used to determine the attributes (population, resistance) of the BI challenge system used during biological qualification. Using Equation 1, a BI challenge system can be designed to demonstrate that the required lethality has actually been delivered:

Equation 1 — BI Design Formula

Log N_F = -(F / D) + Log N_0

Where:
  F   = desired lethality determined during process design
  D   = resistance (D-value) of the biological challenge
  N_0 = starting population of the biological challenge
  N_F = population after exposure
        (if total kill: N_F = 10^-1, i.e., less than 1 survivor)

Rearranged to find minimum N_0:
  Log N_0 = Log N_F + (F / D)

Process Qualification Examples

Example 1 — Product-Specific Approach (F = 6.0 min, D = 1.2 min, total kill):

Log N_0 = Log(10^-1) + 6.0/1.2 = -1 + 5 = 4 → N_0 = 10^4 (adjusted: 10^5)

The MAC must completely kill a BI with N0 = 105 and D = 1.2 min to qualify F ≥ 6.0 min.

Example 2 — Overkill Approach (F ≥ 12 min, D = 2.1 min, total kill):

Log N_0 = -1 + 12/2.1 = -1 + 5.71 = 4.71 → N_0 = 5.2 × 10^4

A MAC inactivating a BI with N0 = 5.2 × 104 and D = 2.1 min qualifies as an overkill cycle (F ≥ 12 min).

Example 3 — Fractional Endpoint (F = 8.0 min, N0 = 2.0 × 106, D = 2.1 min):

Log N_F = Log(2.0×10^6) - 8.0/2.1 = 6.3 - 3.8 = 2.5 → N_F = 3.2 × 10^2

The MAC must reduce the BI to below 3.2 × 102 survivors to demonstrate F ≥ 8.0 min.

Example 4 — Half-Cycle Method (overkill, Ftarget ≥ 12 min, half-cycle F ≥ 6 min, D = 1.0 min):

Log N_0 = -1 + 0.5(12/1.0) = -1 + 6 = 5 → N_0 = 10^6

Using half the MAC exposure, a BI with N0 = 106 and D = 1.0 min must be totally killed to demonstrate full F ≥ 12 min.

5.2.2 Use and Placement of Biological Indicators

For cycles designed using the overkill design approach, the challenge system is typically spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus — either inoculated on the actual items to be sterilized, on paper or other appropriate substrate, or as a commercial biological indicator.

For the product-specific design approach, lower levels of thermal input may be delivered. The challenge organism used in qualification is often less resistant than spores of G. stearothermophilus.

5.2.2.1 Liquid Load Cycle Qualification

For biological qualification of liquid loads, sealed, liquid-filled containers and closures are inoculated with appropriate microorganisms. The liquid medium may be the product or an appropriate surrogate. It may be necessary to use a surrogate fluid as the suspending medium if the liquid product contains preservatives or other antimicrobial agents that demonstrate growth inhibition. The decision to use the product as the suspending medium should be supported by studies that verify microbial growth is not inhibited.

The microbiological challenge should be placed in containers throughout the load in a random or geometric pattern, as well as in any cold zones identified in development. Heat penetration probes should be placed in the container adjacent to the inoculated containers to monitor the heat input of the cycle.

5.2.2.2 Porous/Hard Goods Cycle Qualification

Biological indicators used for biological qualification of porous/hard goods loads are typically obtained from commercial sources and may be mounted on paper, stainless steel, aluminum or other substrates. Alternatively, spores may be inoculated on designated test-piece items. The use of sealed ampoule BIs may not represent the actual conditions of a porous/hard goods load.

BI challenge systems are placed in slowest-to-heat locations in items considered most difficult to sterilize — for instance, within the pleats of cartridge filters, at the center of a length of tubing where air may be trapped, or on rubber stoppers where steam penetration may be difficult.

To evaluate the correlation between Fphys and Fbio, biological indicators should be placed near thermometric probes. Care should be taken to not artificially increase or decrease air removal or steam penetration to a particular area. It may be necessary to place duplicate items in the load — one containing a thermal probe and the other a biological indicator — to obtain representative results.

Commentary

The Logic of Biological Qualification

Biological qualification deliberately introduces a known number of heat-resistant spores (BIs) and then challenges them with the Minimum Acceptable Cycle (MAC). If the predetermined log reduction is achieved, the process delivers sufficient lethality.

A Way to Remember the BI Design Formula

Survivors after the cycle = −(F ÷ D) + initial population (in logs)

To qualify F = 8 min:
→ Use a BI with D = 2 min
→ A 4-log reduction is needed (8/2 = 4)
→ If N_0 = 10^6, then N_F ≤ 10^2 to pass

Analogy: Testing an Airbag with a Crash Test

If you know the airbag deploys above 40 G, you test it with a 40 G impact. BI design works the same way: if the target is F = 12 min, design a BI that takes 12 min to kill.

BI Placement Strategy

Liquid loads: Inoculate BIs into sealed containers and place them at various locations in the load (including the cold spot), next to the heat penetration probes.

Porous/hard article loads: Place BIs at the most difficult-to-sterilize locations:

  • Inside filter pleats
  • The center of tubing (where air may remain)
  • Inside rubber stoppers (steam penetrates with difficulty)

Place BIs as close as possible to the heat penetration probes to establish the correlation between Fphys and Fbio.

When to Use a Surrogate Fluid

If the product itself contains a preservative or antimicrobial agent (which would inhibit BI growth), a surrogate fluid must be used as the suspension medium, and the decision must be supported by a growth inhibition study.

5.3

Process Performance Acceptance Criteria

Defining physical and biological criteria that must be satisfied for cycle qualification to pass

5.3 Process Performance Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria for physical and biological qualification should be clearly defined in test protocols. These criteria should be based on the type of items to be sterilized, the sterilization method being applied, applicable regulatory expectations, and the operating parameters determined in cycle development.

Following are examples of typical physical qualification acceptance criteria:

  • Minimum and maximum time above a specified minimum temperature measured with heat penetration probes
  • Minimum and maximum total accumulated F0
  • Minimum F0 at end of the exposure phase
  • Minimum and maximum pressure during exposure
  • Correlation of temperature and pressure for saturated steam
  • Minimum and maximum chamber temperature during exposure
  • Maximum temperature or F0 variation between heat penetration probes
  • Maximum temperature variation in temperature distribution probes
  • Maximum equilibration time
  • Minimum number of properly functioning probes

Following are examples of typical biological qualification acceptance criteria:

  • Microbial challenge spore log reduction achieved meets the predetermined acceptance criteria
  • Positive and negative controls function as specified

In addition to meeting the above acceptance criteria, the overall qualification should demonstrate general agreement between Fphys and Fbio if it is not demonstrated during development.

Commentary

Design Principles for Acceptance Criteria

All acceptance criteria must be written into the protocol before execution. Changing criteria after the fact is a serious GMP violation. Criteria should be based on the product type, the sterilization method, regulatory requirements and the operating parameters established during cycle development.

Consistency of Fphys and Fbio

Final acceptance of PPQ requires the physical and biological data to be in general agreement. If they disagree (for example, a high physical F0 but BIs that were not killed), there may be a steam penetration problem that requires thorough investigation.

SAL Target and Minimum F0

For an overkill design, the criterion is F0 ≥ 12 min (corresponding to SAL = 10-6). For a product-specific design, the minimum F0 is determined from the product bioburden data and D value; there is no fixed minimum, but SAL ≤ 10-6 must generally be met.

5.4

Sterilizer Equivalence

Reducing qualification scope when multiple sterilizers of similar design are available

5.4 Sterilizer Equivalence

Where two or more sterilizers are of similar design (including utilities supplied to them), it may be possible to establish their operational equivalence and reduce the amount of process qualification testing. A robust risk management process is recommended to justify making this determination.

For example, sterilization processes that have more parameters that are difficult to control (e.g., prevacuum processes) may be more difficult to establish equivalency than those with parameters that are easier to control. Regulatory approval may be required to support a reduction in qualification testing.

Initially, all sterilizers must be qualified and meet operating parameter acceptance criteria in order to demonstrate equivalency between sterilizers.

Operational equivalence between sterilizers can be established by using the same sterilization parameters and load configuration (product, formulation, container size, fill volume, stoppers, equipment, or loading pattern) and by comparing their performance to one another. Comparable criteria to be demonstrated should include:

  • Critical and key parameters
  • Temperature distribution
  • Heat penetration
  • F0 range
  • Microbiological inactivation

Commentary

What Is Sterilizer Equivalence?

If a facility has two or more sterilizers of similar design, an equivalence study can demonstrate that their performance is the same, thereby reducing the repeated qualification workload. For example, with three identical sterilizers, a full PPQ might be performed on only one and reduced qualification on the other two, provided equivalence can be demonstrated.

Prerequisites for Equivalence

  • All sterilizers must first complete their own IQ/OQ and meet the operating parameter criteria
  • The designs must be "similar", including utility supply
  • Supported by a risk assessment
  • In some cases (such as a reduced number of qualification runs), regulatory approval may be required

Analogy: Chain Restaurant Kitchens

If three branch kitchens have identical equipment and the chefs follow the same SOP, then once one is validated successfully, the validation effort for the other two can be simplified — provided you can show that the equipment performance in all three really is the same.

The Five Elements of an Equivalence Comparison

  1. Critical and non-critical parameters (temperature, pressure, time)
  2. Temperature distribution
  3. Heat penetration
  4. F0 range
  5. Microbial inactivation results
5.5

Bracketing

Reducing qualification scope by qualifying extreme cases to cover intermediate configurations

5.5 Bracketing

Bracketing of product formulation, container size, fill volume, items or loads may be performed to reduce the amount of qualification work. Bracketing requires identification of the worst-case challenge (single-ended bracket) or the use of configurations that represent the range of items (two-ended bracket) to be qualified. Selection of worst-case challenge(s) is conducted using scientific rationale and/or determined through heat penetration or microbiological inactivation studies.

5.5.1 Product Formulation Bracketing

For biological qualification, a master solution approach may be used to bracket product formulations. The product formulation with the greatest resistance to the challenge microorganism is used as the master solution to represent product formulations in which challenge microorganisms have lower resistances. Typically a resistometer is used to determine resistance.

For physical qualification, the product formulation with the greatest viscosity can be used to represent product formulations with lower viscosities.

5.5.2 Container Size / Fill-Volume Bracketing

Bracketing of similarly designed liquid load containers of different sizes and different fill volumes can be established if using the same sterilization parameters. The largest container with the largest fill and the smallest container with the smallest fill would be qualified to bracket intermediate sizes and fills.

5.5.3 Item Bracketing

Bracketing of porous/hard goods items may be considered. The item that represents the greatest sterilization challenge is used to qualify similar items that represent lesser sterilization challenges. For example, if differing lengths of tubing with the same material, diameter, wall thickness, orientation and packaging are to be qualified, the longest length is used to qualify the shorter lengths.

5.5.4 Load Bracketing

An approach for bracketing porous/hard goods loads requires the use of items that represent the greatest heat-up (mass) and/or air-removal challenges in the minimum and maximum load qualification. For the minimum load, the greatest heat-up and/or air-removal item(s) may be the only item(s) present in the chamber during the qualification.

In the event that load size is expected to vary for operational flexibility, minimum and maximum loads should be identified and qualified. At a minimum, three consecutive acceptable biological qualification runs should be achieved for maximum loads in each sterilizer. For sterilization cycles that do not have fixed heat-up and cool-down rates/times, the minimum load typically receives lower thermal heat input than maximum loads and should also be challenged. Where risk has been assessed and deemed to be acceptable, the number of replicate runs may be reduced.

Commentary

The Core Logic of Bracketing

If you can confirm that the most extreme case passes, the cases in between will naturally pass as well. The key to bracketing is to scientifically identify the worst case and qualify it.

Analogy: Bridge Load Testing

If a bridge is designed to carry the maximum load (a fully loaded truck), a passenger car is naturally safe too. Bracketing works the same way: verifying that the heaviest truck passes means all lighter vehicles pass.

Single-Ended vs Two-Ended Bracketing

Single-ended bracket: Qualify only one worst case (for example, only the largest container). Suitable for simpler situations.

Two-ended bracket: Qualify the two extremes (for example, the largest and the smallest container), and the containers in between are automatically covered. This requires stronger scientific justification but gives broader coverage.

The Minimum Load Must Also Be Qualified

A common misconception: the minimum load is considered easy to sterilize because it has "fewer items". But for sterilizers without a fixed heat-up rate, the minimum load has a low heat capacity and may heat up faster than the maximum load, so the exposure time may be insufficient (overshoot risk). It therefore also needs to be challenged and qualified.

6.0

Ongoing Process Control

Post-qualification monitoring framework to maintain state of control

6.0 Ongoing Process Control

After completion of sterilization cycle development and performance qualification, monitoring of the sterilization process is conducted in order to assure state of control. Important elements of the ongoing sterilization program include:

  • Review of critical and key operating parameters during routine sterilization cycles
  • Confirmation of sterilizer suitability
  • Deployment of an effective change control program
  • Calibration, maintenance, and requalification of the process

Interpretation and Practical Guidance

The Four Pillars of Ongoing Process Control

Completing PQ (performance qualification) does not mean the end of the sterilization process. It moves into the "ongoing maintenance" phase. PDA TR1 groups this phase into four core activities:

  1. Cycle parameter review: the critical parameters of every sterilization cycle must be reviewed against the validated ranges
  2. System suitability: periodically confirm that the sterilizer hardware is functioning properly
  3. Change control: any system or product change must be documented and assessed for impact
  4. Requalification: periodically (typically every 12 months) reconfirm that the process remains effective

Analogy: A Vehicle Maintenance Program

After a new car passes acceptance (PQ), the driver still takes it in for regular servicing (requalification), checks its condition before every trip (system suitability), and reassesses its safety after any modification (change control). Ongoing control is the GMP version of a "vehicle maintenance plan."

6.1

Routine Release

Parametric release vs. BI sterility testing — criteria for load disposition

6.1 Routine Release

Release of sterile loads is accomplished by demonstrating that qualified critical and key operating parameters were met during routine sterilization cycles. Additionally, compendial sterility tests may be required to support product release.

Section 4.4 discusses critical and key operating parameters that are defined during cycle development and qualified during operational qualification and performance qualification in order to establish a baseline of sterilizer performance. The parameters for routine operational cycles should be evaluated to ensure they meet qualified ranges.

  • Critical parameters — All must be met for load release. No exceptions.
  • Key parameters — More indicative of state of control; should also be evaluated. A scientifically sound rationale must be developed to support release if a key parameter is not met.

Operational parameter review is essential, regardless of whether the materials processed are being parametrically released for distribution, subject to subsequent sterility testing, or sterilized to support further processing.

"All critical parameters must be met for load release. A scientifically sound rationale must be developed to support the release of a load if a key parameter is not met."

Interpretation and Practical Guidance

Practical Points

Whichever release strategy is used, review of the parameters in the cycle record is always required. Parametric release does not mean "no biological indicators"; it means "using complete process data as the primary basis for release."

6.2

Sterilizer System Suitability

Routine tests confirming equipment readiness before or during production cycles

6.2 Sterilizer System Suitability

System suitability evaluations should be incorporated into a load-release program; however, the need and frequency of these tests is determined by the end-user, according to internal and possible regulatory requirements.

Examples of system suitability evaluations include:

  • Air Removal Test — Demonstration that air removal meets applicable requirements for porous loads sterilized with a saturated steam prevacuum process.
  • Chamber Leak Test — Demonstration that the chamber leak rate is below a stated maximum rate. This test is recommended for sterilizers using a saturated steam prevacuum process for sterilization of porous loads.
  • Air Detector Device — Acceptable air detector results (supported by testing performed during cycle development and/or qualification) can be used to support the release of porous/hard goods loads using a saturated steam prevacuum process. The use of a properly qualified air-detector device may reduce the need to perform chamber leak tests and air removal tests. This device is typically installed near the drain in the sterilizer chamber.
  • Chemical Indicator/Integrator Results — Acceptable results demonstrate that the load was processed and in some cases can measure specific time and/or temperature conditions in the sterilizer.

Interpretation and Practical Guidance

The Importance of Air Detectors

An air detector installed at the chamber drain, if properly validated, can replace both the chamber leak test and the air removal test. This is of practical value in reducing the routine testing burden, but only if the equipment is continuously calibrated and the validation documentation is complete.

Analogy: The Pre-Flight Checklist

System suitability testing is like a pilot's pre-flight checklist. It does not mean stripping down the engines every time; it means confirming that the instruments, seals and control panel are all in normal condition. Frequency and scope are set by company SOPs and regulations, but the purpose is always to "confirm the equipment is ready."

6.3

Change Control

Documenting and assessing changes to sterilizer, product, or process — triggers for requalification

6.3 Change Control

In order to maintain the state of control of a sterilization system, a change control program should be in place. This program should document sterilization system or product changes and include documentation of any testing required to ensure the qualified state of control.

Changes involving modifications of the sterilizer chamber, product carrier/tray design, load arrangement, sterilization medium supply/distribution systems, or the sterilizer operation/control mode may necessitate:

  • Temperature distribution studies
  • Heat penetration studies
  • Microbiological challenge studies

Replacement of "like-for-like" sterilizer equipment system parts is generally not considered a major change, provided that it is demonstrated that sterilizer performance is not affected.

Changes to product, including design, materials of construction, item or product tolerances, mass, venting, formulation or packaging, may require temperature distribution, heat penetration and/or microbiological requalification.

Requalification of the sterilization process should be performed whenever there is a major modification to the sterilization system (including prior-to-sterilizer decommissioning) or product that has the potential to affect process efficacy.

Change Control Package — Required Elements

A change control package should identify qualification documents affected by the change and must include:

  • A description of the proposed change
  • A documented reason/rationale for the proposed change
  • A description of the tests needed to qualify the sterilization process after the change, or a technical rationale supporting that the change has no impact on process efficacy (minor change)
  • Supporting documentation for tests performed, interpretation of results and conclusions
  • Confirmation that affected documents have been updated
  • Approval of the change control package by the quality unit and other management representatives

Interpretation and Practical Guidance

The Six Elements of a Change Control Package

  • Description of the change
  • Reason/justification for the change
  • Description of the validation tests required (or the technical rationale for a minor change)
  • Test execution records, interpretation of results and conclusions
  • Confirmation that related documents have been updated accordingly
  • Approval signatures from the quality unit and management

None of these six items can be omitted, and they are frequent checkpoints during inspections.

Practical Scenario: How Do You Decide Whether a Change Is "Major"?

Ask yourself three questions: (1) Will the change alter the heat distribution of the load? (2) Has the path by which steam reaches the cold spot changed? (3) Have the bioburden characteristics or quality of the product changed? If the answer to any of them is "yes," it is advisable to start a requalification assessment.

6.4

Periodic Requalification

Frequency, scope, and documentation requirements for scheduled requalification

6.4 Periodic Requalification

Requalification should be performed on a regular basis (typically every 12 months) to ensure there has not been an undetected change in product or process.

Requalification should be performed using the same operational parameters and acceptance criteria as the original qualification runs. Supporting documentation for tests performed under the PQ program should include, as applicable, information outlined in the original qualification effort. Verification of acceptable steam quality for porous/hard goods load sterilization should also be performed at this time.

Results of the requalification study should demonstrate that the sterilizer's performance has not changed since the original effort.

The most difficult-to-sterilize load(s) should be included in requalification runs. Where equivalence has been established between sterilizers (Section 5.4), a reduction in requalification activity may be considered. For example, in a facility in which multiple product formulations are sterilized using equivalent sterilizers, it may not be necessary to requalify each product in each sterilizer.

Requalification should also include review of performance data from various monitoring sources (e.g., engineering, maintenance and calibration data) of sterilizers and supporting equipment to verify that there have been no adverse trends or drifts away from the baseline performance established during validation.

A review of change control documentation should be conducted as part of the requalification. Refer to Section 6.3 for information regarding change control.

Interpretation and Practical Guidance

Simplified Requalification for Equivalent Sterilizers

If equivalence has been established among multiple sterilizers in a facility (Section 5.4), the scope of requalification activities can be reduced. For example, it is not necessary to repeat validation of every product formulation on every sterilizer. However, this simplification must be based on a documented equivalence assessment and clearly stated in the SOP.

Analogy: The Annual Health Check

Requalification is like a personal annual health check. You do not go because you suspect illness; you go to systematically confirm that everything is still within the baseline range. If an adverse trend is found (such as calibration drift), further investigation is needed, just as an abnormal value in a health check report calls for follow-up testing.

Notes on the References

The Three Most Important References

  • #24 FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance (2004) — the core US FDA regulatory document on aseptic processing; many TR1 requirements echo it
  • #16 Pflug (2007) — the standard sterilization microbiology textbook designated by PDA, and the authoritative source for D-value/F0 calculations

Why Do the References Matter?

Every technical requirement in TR1 has an academic and regulatory basis. An inspector may ask, "What standard is your F0 calculation based on?" Being able to point to the Pflug textbook or the ISO standard demonstrates scientific justification. Knowing this reference list well helps build confidence during inspections.
