Introduction & Scope
Original Text — English

Introduction

As a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, many industries–the pharmaceutical industry included–went through a substantial disruption. Some of these disruptions included facility shutdowns, unprecedented supply chain challenges, worker availability, and quality system limitations (e.g., physical signatures, investigations, document originals, risk assessments, audits). For many pharmaceutical companies, challenges increased as they raced to provide a vaccine or a therapeutic treatment for COVID-19. Their manufacturing operations were changed over to accommodate the creation of the vaccine and, in order to get the vaccine to patients quickly, many logistical challenges arose.

As the pandemic has progressed, our understanding of the virus, its control, and treatment options have changed and advanced. When reading this document, it is important to remember that its intent is to describe the lessons learned for future benefit, especially at times when the understanding of the virus and treatment options were extremely limited. Even today, as we enter what is believed to be the last phase of the pandemic, moving into an endemic state, it is difficult to comprehend the amount of anxiety and risk that existed during the initial 12 to 16 months. During that time, some types of government-imposed state of emergency were mandated in many countries that restricted the movement of individuals and closed nonessential businesses. Hospitals operated at capacity, a significant number of Covid-related deaths occurred, and a limited amount of personal protective equipment (e.g., mask, gloves) was available.

The specifics of the pandemic or the COVID-19 virus are not detailed here, as that information is widely available. One point that does need to be made is that this pandemic, like the pandemic of 1918, went through waves, and the exact timing of each wave and the number of waves experienced varied by country. These waves were not unexpected but how the operation of manufacturing facilities should react to these first waves was not immediately clear; that developed over time. This document intends to convey the understanding and evolution of thought in response to the new data and observations presented as we worked our way through 2019, 2020, 2021, and 2022.

Scope

This document applies lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic to pharmaceutical manufacturing. Areas covered include manufacturing, people, quality, regulatory, and supply chain.

Core Background: Why Do We Need This Document?

The COVID-19 pandemic was the biggest stress test the pharmaceutical industry has faced in almost a century. The core value of PtC-9 is that it turns a crisis into systematic knowledge, so that the industry can respond faster when the next pandemic arrives.

The document covers five areas: Manufacturing, People, Quality, Regulatory and Supply Chain. This section focuses on manufacturing.

Analogy: The Pandemic as a "Stress Test" for a Pharmaceutical Plant

Imagine your plant is hit by all of these at once: a computer system crash (quality system constraints), half of your raw material suppliers shutting down (supply chain challenges), and a third of your staff unable to come to work (workforce availability). This is the kind of multiple crisis pharmaceutical plants faced in the early days of the pandemic.

This document is the pharmaceutical industry's "after-action review", recording which measures worked and which need improvement.

CDMO Operational Points

For a Contract Development and Manufacturing Organization (CDMO), the challenges during the pandemic were even more complex: serving multiple clients at the same time, managing a broader supplier network, and continuing to produce existing products while supporting new vaccine development.

The document refers to the "wave-like" course of the pandemic. A manufacturing site's response strategy has to evolve as understanding deepens with each wave, and this is the core spirit of the document.

Q1-1: Are GMP controls sufficient to protect the product?
Original Text — English

Background

Due to the novelty of the coronavirus, at the start of the pandemic, the potential impact to the safety and quality of the drugs produced as a result of infected manufacturing employees was uncertain. The virus was found to spread very efficiently, which resulted in a much higher percentage of the population being infected than with common or seasonal viral infections. As a result, the risk of a manufacturing process being exposed to the coronavirus was considered to be higher than usual, depending on the process.

Therefore, most drug manufacturers moved quickly to perform risk assessments to evaluate the established good manufacturing practices (GMP) controls in their manufacturing facilities with respect to the new potential contaminant. The expectation of risk mitigation was also later reflected in a COVID-19-specific guidance published by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) that stated manufacturers were expected to perform these risk assessments to ensure that product quality and safety, including employees' safety, are not impacted due to the pandemic.

Fortunately for the industry, careful implementation of GMP principles and practices are likely to protect product against the SARS-CoV-2 virus that causes COVID-19, as well as mitigate the risk caused by asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2-infected personnel. Some of these GMP controls include:

  • General hygiene measures and procedures in case of illness, feeling sick, open lesions, or sneezing and coughing
  • Procedures for thorough handwashing and subsequent hand sanitization before entering production areas, and regular glove disinfection during production
  • Use of environmentally controlled rooms within GMP-classified areas with specific gowning requirements (e.g., cleanroom gowns, overalls, lab coats, gloves, mask, gowning procedures) based on the operation performed
  • Restriction of access to production areas to authorized personnel only
  • Frequent cleaning cycles of the manufacturing areas and process equipment
  • Design of general process equipment to ensure manufacturing areas are as closed as possible to prevent the potential entry of contamination
  • Use of closed systems and, where product is exposed, use of isolators, restricted access barrier systems (RABS), and HEPA-filtered laminar airflow to protect the product
  • Frequent cleaning cycles of the area and of the processing equipment to further prevent contamination from spreading
  • Environmental and personnel monitoring to ensure strict adherence to GMP processes
  • Use of air cascades and pressure differentials to allow air to flow from critical to less-critical areas
  • Production flow in facilities designed to allow efficient movement and control of materials, personnel, products, equipment, and waste to prevent contamination
  • Use of gamma-irradiated, single-use process equipment interconnected with aseptic connections and typically designated as functionally closed systems
  • Stainless-steel equipment processes typically being cleaned and connected, followed by an in-situ steam sterilization (SIP) cycle
  • Process venting points outfitted with hydrophobic-membrane filters that act as barriers to potential ingress into the process

Additional considerations when working with mammalian cell lines:

  • Industry is expected to conduct adventitious-agent testing of a typical panel at the end of production cell bank. Additional risk assessment may need to be considered for banks manufactured after 2019.
  • Measures to prevent and avoid viral cross-contamination during processing are well established, including deactivation and removal of viruses. The large size of the COVID-19 virus makes it a prime candidate for removal by a 20-nm viral filter. Reports of the stability of COVID-19 over a wide range of pH values creates some potential concern around the effectiveness of a low-pH step for viral deactivation; thus, relying fully on this step for clearance is not recommended. Alternatively, detergent or solvent deactivation is effective.

Learnings

The emergence of a pandemic or similar disaster must mobilize industry to assess the continued ability of their processes and quality systems to protect product quality and patient safety. Industry must be prepared to rapidly perform such assessments in the future when a similar situation emerges. In the case of SARS-CoV-2, manufacturing processes employing strict GMP adherence in their design, personnel controls, and the environments in which they are performed likely present an extremely low risk for possible SARS-CoV-2 product contamination. In most cases, when conforming to existing GMP, no additional measures are necessary to protect product and patient. In the event of an employee operating within the GMP manufacturing environment testing positive, a specific risk assessment should be performed.

References: FDA Guidance for Industry: GMP Considerations for Responding to COVID-19 Infection in Employees in Drug and Biological Products Manufacturing (2020). Chin et al., Lancet Microbe 2020; Michen & Graule, J Appl Microbiol 2010; Miriam et al., Transfusion 2006.

Commentary

Core Conclusion: The Design of GMP Itself Is the Best Protection

The answer to this question came as a relief to the industry: existing Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) controls provide adequate protection against SARS-CoV-2.

This means that most pharmaceutical plants do not need large-scale facility modifications because of the pandemic. They only need to implement their existing GMP requirements faithfully.

Analogy: A GMP Cleanroom Is Like a "Virus Shield"

GMP manufacturing environments are designed to keep microorganisms such as bacteria and mold out of the product. The COVID-19 virus (about 100 nanometers in diameter) is much smaller than bacteria, so existing cleanroom design, including HEPA-filtered laminar airflow, closed systems and pressure differential management, offers even stronger protection against it.

It is like a sealed container designed to keep out cockroaches: it also keeps out larger pests such as rats.

CDMO Practical Points: Seven Layers of Protection

  • Personnel controls: people with symptoms of infection are barred from production areas; hand washing and sanitization are mandatory
  • Environmental control: ISO-classified cleanrooms plus gowning requirements
  • Airflow design: a positive pressure cascade from critical to non-critical areas (air can only flow outward)
  • Closed systems: isolators / RABS plus gamma-irradiated single-use equipment
  • Cleaning cycles: frequent cleaning of manufacturing areas and process equipment
  • Environmental and personnel monitoring: confirming strict adherence to GMP processes
  • Viral filtration: 20 nm virus filters can effectively retain COVID-19 (about 100 nm)

Additional Considerations for Mammalian Cell Line Processes

Adventitious-agent testing: cell banks established after 2019 need to be re-evaluated for risk.

Limitations of low pH virus inactivation: COVID-19 is stable over a wide pH range, so a low pH step should not be relied on as the sole barrier for viral clearance. Detergent or solvent inactivation is recommended as an alternative or complementary measure.

For biologics CDMOs (for example, monoclonal antibody manufacturing), this is particularly important when developing a viral clearance validation strategy.

Think It Through

If an employee at your plant tests positive for COVID-19, what should you do next according to this document?

Answer: Conduct a case-specific risk assessment of that employee's actual work situation, analyzing, identifying and evaluating the possible hazards. It does not mean an immediate shutdown of the whole plant. This reflects risk-based GMP thinking.

Q1-2: How do you develop a mRNA/gene therapy drug product rapidly (Part 1)?
Original Text — English

Background

Using messenger RNA (mRNA) to encode proteins for vaccination or protein replacement received its first in-vivo validation in 1990, when Wolff et al. demonstrated the production of a target protein after intramuscular injection in mice. The exact advantages of using mRNA products were discovered later, including:

  • Allowing human post-translational modification of protein products with the potential for less immunogenicity and full functionality
  • Permitting multimeric proteins that are not amendable to production in a bioreactor to be translated, folded, and assembled correctly in the patient's cells
  • Producing transmembrane and intracellular proteins and trafficking them to their appropriate site in the cellular environment

Learnings

The preparation of plasmid DNA (pDNA) is the first step in the production of mRNA, where functional synthetic mRNA is obtained by the in-vitro transcription of a pDNA template. The pDNA template is transcribed into the mRNA mixture containing RNA polymerase and nucleoside triphosphates. Then, a cap may be performed during the reaction, or it can be added enzymatically post-transcription. Capping improves translation initiation by recruiting translation-initiation factors, thereby protecting the synthetic mRNA against degradation. Post-transcription, the pDNA template and bacterial DNA is digested by DNase. To be used as a drug substance, mRNA has to be purified to remove contaminating transcripts, which can be done by using a single chromatographic step that will separate the mRNA based on size and result in a pure, single mRNA product.

Two forms of mRNA are being studied for use in vaccines: nonreplicating (traditional) mRNA and self-amplifying mRNA. The traditional mRNA form presents two advantages: the molecule is quite simple and small, and the possibility of an unwanted immune response is lowered because there are no other proteins encoded. However, the traditional mRNA form is limited by its transient nature. The advantage of self-amplifying mRNA is that it performs self-replication and creates high levels of the antigen not found with traditional mRNA. The self-amplifying RNA vaccine was found to show a 64-fold lower dose required for an immunologic response compared to its mRNA counterpart.

References: Wolff et al., Science 1990; Barbier et al., Nat Biotechnol 2022.

Additional Reading: Rosa et al., Vaccine 2021; Fang et al., Signal Transduction 2022; CDC Understanding COVID-19 Vaccines; Schlake et al., RNA Biol 2012; FDA Gene Therapy Products Guidance 2022.

Commentary

The History of mRNA Vaccines

mRNA is not a "new technology". Its basic research goes back to 1990. The rapid arrival of COVID-19 vaccines was the result of thirty years of accumulated basic research, not an overnight miracle.

This is critical for CDMO strategic planning: investing in platform capabilities for cutting-edge technology can quickly turn into a business opportunity when an urgent need arises.

The mRNA Manufacturing Process by Analogy: From "Blueprint" to "Instruction Manual"

Imagine that the pharmaceutical plant is a publishing house:

  • Plasmid DNA (pDNA): the original design blueprint (the template)
  • In-vitro transcription: photocopying the blueprint into a working manual (the mRNA)
  • Capping: adding an "anti-counterfeit mark" to the cover of the manual so that it is not destroyed
  • DNase digestion: destroying the original blueprint and keeping only the manual
  • Chromatographic purification: selecting only the "qualified" manuals and discarding damaged copies

The "manual" finally delivered to the cell is the purified mRNA drug substance.

CDMO Business Considerations

mRNA manufacturing involves highly specialized steps (in-vitro transcription, capping, chromatographic purification) and is extremely sensitive to RNase contamination. This places special demands on facility design, personnel training and equipment cleaning. Does your site currently have these capabilities? How high is building an mRNA platform on your list of priorities?

Conventional mRNA vs. Self-Amplifying mRNA: Key Differences for Manufacturing Decisions

For a CDMO, the "64-fold lower dose" of self-amplifying mRNA means that the same capacity can serve more patients, but manufacturing difficulty and regulatory complexity increase accordingly.

Q1-3: How do you develop a mRNA/gene therapy drug product rapidly (Part 2)?
Original Text — English

Background

Gene therapy products seek to insert genetic material into a patient's cells to treat or prevent a specific disease state. There are several types of gene-therapy products, which includes plasmid DNA, viral vectors, bacterial vectors, human gene-editing technology, and patient-derived cellular gene therapy. In much the same way, mRNA therapy products seek to insert mRNA material into a patient's cells to induce the production of a specific protein to treat or prevent a specific disease state.

The COVID-19 mRNA vaccine is made of mRNA and, to allow introduction into the cell, is encompassed by lipid nanoparticles (LNP). Once in the cells, it works by instructing the cells to make a protein that will trigger an immune response to COVID-19. While there is no specific formula for the creation of a successful mRNA LNP vaccine, the mRNA LNP design is characterized by three main elements:

  • mRNA sequence design and nucleotide modification choice
  • Optimization of a LNP formulation to encapsulate and deliver mRNA
  • Long-term storage

The first step in developing a mRNA therapy product is focusing on the API (active pharmaceutical ingredient). Considerations for designing or choosing the mRNA sequence should include the expression of encoded mRNA, immunogenicity due to recognition of mRNA as a foreign entity by intracellular RNA sensors, and mRNA stability. Nucleotide modification is equally important, as nonmodified mRNA molecules are recognized by the cellular RNA sensors, which nulls the effect of their expression. The translation efficiency and intracellular-mRNA stability can be increased substantially by mRNA capping. Additionally, the decay and translation efficiency are greatly affected by the untranslated region (UTR) selection as well as the open-reading-frames (ORF) sequence design.

The purpose of the LNP formulation is to design a nanoparticle that will shield the mRNA from degradation and enable efficient delivery into targeted cells. These formulations typically comprise a neutral structural lipid, cholesterol, a PEG-lipid, and an ionizable lipid. The ionizable lipid is incredibly important for enhancing the expression and provides the most-optimal immune response. The PEG-lipid component is helpful in increasing the shelf life of the solution by preventing aggregation. Considerations for PEG-lipids used in these formulations should take into account that, at high concentrations, they can prevent delivery of the mRNA.

Throughout the process of formulation, long-term storage and stability should be major considerations in design. The ultra-low storage requirement currently approved for mRNA vaccines due to instability at room temperature is a system constraint. To freeze LNPs, a cryoprotectant such as sucrose or trehalose should be added to the formulation.

Lessons Learned

In the case of a future pandemic, the availability of global and private funding to advance the development of research for a potential vaccine will again be critical. Governmental agencies globally should develop or revise plans related to the emergency funding of such research should the need arise.

While the most desired mRNA vaccine would be one that could be provided in a lyophilized or a liquid formulation stored under refrigerated or room-temperature conditions, such formulations can take significant periods of time to develop, if even possible. Under pandemic conditions, when a vaccine is needed quickly, the initial use of an ultra-low storage condition should be considered as it removes many of the formulation challenges that would otherwise need to be overcome.

References: ASGCT Staff 2020; CDC Understanding mRNA COVID-19 Vaccines; Kon et al., Curr Opin Biotechnol 2022.

Commentary

The Four Components of LNP: Each Plays Its Own Role in the "Courier Packaging"

The lipid nanoparticle (LNP) is the key delivery system of an mRNA vaccine, and the absence of any one component leads to failure:

  • Ionizable lipid: the core function. In the acidic environment inside the cell it becomes positively charged, binds the negatively charged mRNA, and helps release the mRNA from the endosome.
  • Neutral structural lipid: maintains the structural stability of the particle.
  • Cholesterol: assists membrane fusion and enhances delivery efficiency.
  • PEG-lipid: prevents particle aggregation and extends shelf life, but too high a concentration hinders mRNA delivery, so it must be precisely controlled.

Analogy: LNP Is Like a "Waterproof Courier Box"

The mRNA molecule is very fragile and degrades quickly on contact with RNase enzymes in the environment. The LNP acts as:

  • A waterproof shell (protecting against RNase)
  • An address label (guiding the mRNA to the target cells)
  • A key (releasing the mRNA inside the cell)

Without the LNP, an mRNA vaccine simply could not reach the cell to do its job.

Manufacturing Implications of Ultra-Low-Temperature Storage

The ultra-low-temperature storage requirement of mRNA vaccines (-70°C to -80°C) is not an ideal choice but a practical compromise, because in an emergency "speed to market" takes priority over "convenient storage".

Manufacturing implications for a CDMO:

  • Ultra-low-temperature filling lines and storage facilities are required
  • A cryoprotectant (such as sucrose or trehalose) must be included in the formulation
  • Thawing procedures must be standardized, otherwise LNP aggregation will affect potency
  • A lyophilized formulation is a long-term goal, but its development takes a long time and is not suited to an emergency response

Nucleotide Modification: Getting the Cell to "Accept" the mRNA

Unmodified mRNA is recognized by RNA sensors in the cell as a "foreign threat", triggering an immune response and degradation. This was one of the main reasons early mRNA therapies failed.

The key breakthrough by Karikó and Weissman (winners of the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine) was the discovery that replacing natural bases with modified nucleotides (such as pseudouridine) can evade the cell's immune defenses and allow the mRNA to express protein normally.

Strategic Thinking on Formulation Development

The document recommends that in an emergency pandemic, an ultra-low-temperature liquid formulation should be adopted first to reach the market quickly, rather than delaying timelines in pursuit of a room-temperature formulation. Could this principle be applied to your company's new drug development decisions? Under what circumstances is it acceptable to accept stricter storage conditions in exchange for faster time to market?

Q1-4: How did the route of administration impact manufacturing operations?
Original Text — English

Background

The COVID-19 vaccine is considered a biologic parenteral drug formulated as a solution with the route of administration being sterile injections. Therefore, manufacturing of a parental drug is much more complex than the production of solid oral dosage forms such as tablets for oral ingestion. Apart from the significant differences in manufacturing a solution versus a tablet, there are many more controls required to ensure the product remains entirely sterile, including the control and use of sterile components such as vials and stoppers.

A vital step for these types of parenteral drugs that cannot be terminally sterilized is the aseptic fill–finish process in which solution is filled into containers, such as vials, and sealed with an aseptic closure, such as a stopper, as the final step before the product is packaged and shipped for patients. For several of the vaccine products developed, rapid freezing and long-term, ultra-low storage was also required.

The formulation of a drug product can have a significant impact on the sterile fill–finish capabilities of the system. For instance, a product may require different sizes and composition of filters (membranes) to achieve sterilization of the product. Different characteristics of the drug product will also affect the parameters of the system used to fill the vials, such as flow rate and pressure. In many cases, the filling process requires specialized equipment due to specific product characteristics, such as solubility, viscosity, and the tendency of a product formulation to foam.

The fill–finish of millions of doses of vaccines is no small task; it requires significant manufacturing capacity, technical capabilities, and rigorous quality-control measures to ensure product quality. To achieve the volume of production needed, smaller pharmaceutical companies developing the vaccine teamed up with larger companies that had the capacity and technical expertise needed to produce it. Moderna, for example, utilized the sterile fill–finish capabilities of the contract manufacturer Thermo Fisher Scientific to aid in this incredibly important part of the manufacturing process. BioNTech partnered with Pfizer, an expert in the manufacture of sterile drug products.

Lessons Learned

One very valuable learning point during the pandemic was the importance and willingness of companies to partner with one another to rapidly develop the manufacturing process needed for vaccine production. In the event of a future pandemic, this will again be a crucial factor in the speed with which a novel vaccine can be commercialized.

References: Higgins-Dunn, Fierce Pharma; Etherington, TechCrunch 2020.

Commentary

Core Concept: Manufacturing Complexity of Injectables vs. Oral Tablets

COVID-19 vaccines are biologic parenteral drugs. This choice of route of administration directly determines the complexity of manufacturing operations:

  • Oral tablets: can be manufactured in a non-sterile environment, with fast production speed
  • Injectable solutions: must be sterile throughout, cannot be terminally sterilized, and require aseptic fill-finish

This means that every vaccine vial must be filled and closed in an ISO 5 (equivalent to Class 100) clean environment.

Analogy: Aseptic Filling Is Like "Assembling a Precision Watch in a Vacuum"

Manufacturing oral tablets is like baking a cake in an ordinary kitchen: the environmental requirements are relatively relaxed, and at the end you can still "bake it through" (terminal sterilization).

Aseptic filling is completely different: inside a sealed sterile glove box (an isolator), a robotic arm must precisely dispense every drop of liquid into a sterilized glass vial and seat a sterilized rubber stopper, with no opportunity for contamination at any point, and no way to "rescue" the product by sterilizing it afterwards.

CDMO Strategic Opportunity: The Market Gap Revealed by the Pandemic

Moderna (the mRNA developer) had no aseptic filling facility of its own and had to contract Thermo Fisher Scientific. BioNTech likewise needed to rely on Pfizer's manufacturing capability.

This reveals an important strategic insight for CDMOs: a CDMO with high-quality aseptic fill-finish capability is critical infrastructure for the next pandemic.

Filling parameters (flow rate, pressure) are closely linked to formulation characteristics (viscosity, foaming tendency, solubility). A CDMO therefore needs not only hardware but also deep process development capability.

Formulation Dependence of Manufacturing Parameters

Filling LNP formulations poses particular challenges:

  • Filter selection: the LNP particle size (about 80–100 nm) affects the choice of filter membrane pore size
  • Viscosity: affects the flow rate setting of the filling pump
  • Foaming tendency: the surfactant components of LNPs foam easily, requiring a special filling head design
  • Ultra-low-temperature storage: the product must be frozen quickly right after filling, requiring dedicated freezing equipment

Strategic Thinking on Partnerships

One of the most important lessons of the pandemic is that companies must be willing to cross competitive barriers and quickly establish manufacturing partnerships. Has your CDMO already built a partner network that can be activated quickly (upstream API suppliers, downstream fill-finish sites, cold-chain logistics partners)? Before the next urgent need arises, how fast can these relationships be turned into actual capacity?

Q1-5: Can vaccine manufacturing capacities be scaled up rapidly?
Original Text — English

Background

Technology is rapidly evolving. Since 1990, when mRNA was first used in vivo, decades of research have gone into understanding mRNA–LNPs, so much was already known about mRNA before the start of the 2020 pandemic. Consequently, when the need for a COVID-19 vaccine arose, it did not take nearly as long as it would have if researchers were starting from scratch. Moderna's vaccine took only two days to finalize the sequence, 25 days to manufacture the first dose, and 63 days to be made available for the first patient to be dosed in a Phase 1 Clinical Study. Although the vaccine was made quickly, it was held to rigorous standards by the FDA.

To be effective, scaling up manufacturing of the novel drug product required a platform technology approach and available facilities in which to perform the operations.

Without considering process validation and regulatory approval timelines, the design, construction, and installation of a typical manufacturing facility requires a minimum of two to three years. In the case of COVID-19, however, the capacities needed to be scaled up much more rapidly. In 2010, the U.S. government started an initiative to fund sites that could be activated either for other vaccine purposes or to fulfill the needs of a pandemic-vaccine requirement. Unfortunately, when these sites were activated, they were not able to fulfill the mission for which they had been created.

Based on the need to respond quickly, approaches were leveraged to bring new facilities online faster:

  • The use of standard designs for cleanroom infrastructure by suppliers significantly abbreviated design timelines
  • A repeat build and qualification strategy reduced normal facility construction timelines considerably
  • Use of off-site, prefabricated, modular cleanroom infrastructures — as was the case in the Pfizer Kalamazoo site — halved the construction timeline and allowed prequalification within a high-beam shell building, like a warehouse

Some companies, such as Thermo Fisher Scientific, scaled up their manufacturing efforts quickly to meet the unprecedented demand. Initially, Thermo Fisher scaled up the manufacture of raw materials, relying on their current working supply of nucleotides and enzymes for early therapeutics development, which required no infrastructure changes. The company also built a 57,000-sq. ft. facility to manufacture the needed enzymes and nucleotides at industrial-scale levels using a platform approach.

Lessons Learned

The pandemic situation emphasized the need for pharmaceutical companies and regulatory authorities to be prepared to encourage facility design, construction, and process installations that leverage and expedite innovation and the adoption of new technology with the goal of bringing the manufacturing facilities online rapidly.

For example, the use of modular facilities that allows manufacturers to continuously customize and reconfigure equipment to accommodate new products or processes could be encouraged. This use of portable modular manufacturing suites also provides new options for geographic diversification and facility reconfiguration.

In addition, global governments should think about how to incentivize companies to design facilities in the future that are flexible enough to be repurposed during a pandemic. The benefit of building flexibility into an operating facility is that the processes surrounding those manufacturing operations are upgraded over time. Building plants that are not routinely used and maintaining them in a hibernated state creates many unforeseen issues (e.g., equipment reliability, availability of trained staff, up-to-date quality systems).

A recent McKinsey report addressed the issue of being always prepared, with upfront investments over two years, followed by maintenance investments into the preparedness level. The analysis showed that the minimum economic loss globally would be 40 times higher if preparedness investments were not made.

References: Kon et al., Curr Opin Biotechnol 2022; Rosa et al., Vaccine 2021; FDA Guidance for Industry: Development and Licensure of Vaccines to Prevent COVID-19 (2020); Sealy, CNN Health 2021.

Commentary

The Key Number: Behind the 63-Day "Miracle"

Moderna's vaccine took only 63 days from final sequence confirmation to the first participant being dosed. Three key factors were behind this:

  1. Thirty years of technology accumulation: the mRNA-LNP platform was already mature, so there was no need to start from scratch
  2. Platform technology: the same process can quickly be adapted to different mRNA sequences
  3. Regulatory fast track: the FDA Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) mechanism accelerated review

None of the three can be missing: technology, facilities and regulation have to run in parallel.

Analogy: The "Hibernating Plant" vs. the "Agile Plant"

The standby vaccine facilities that the US government funded in 2010 were intended as an "emergency reserve warehouse". In reality, equipment left unused for a long time (in a hibernating state) ran into serious problems when it was activated:

  • Aging equipment and reduced reliability
  • Trained personnel had left
  • The quality system had not kept pace with regulatory updates

It is like a spare sports car left in the garage for years: only in an emergency do you discover that the battery is dead and the tires are flat.
By contrast, the "agile plant" strategy keeps existing, normally operating facilities flexible so that they can be quickly reconfigured when needed.

Modular Facilities: A Solution for Speed and Flexibility

The specific advantages of the prefabricated modular cleanrooms used at Pfizer's Kalamazoo site:

  • Up to 50% shorter construction time: prefabricated off site and installed on site
  • Can be pre-qualified: the modules have a standard design, so qualification documentation can be reused
  • Fit into existing buildings: no new building is needed, only adjustment of utilities (water, electricity, HVAC)
  • Reconfigurable: can be adapted for other products after the pandemic

This has direct implications for a CDMO's facility strategy: when building or expanding facilities, including a higher proportion of modular design can greatly improve future emergency response capability.

The Business Logic of the McKinsey Report

"The minimum global economic loss without investment in preparedness would be 40 times the cost of investing". This is not only a public health argument but also a business argument for capital investment in the pharmaceutical industry:

A CDMO with rapid response capability will, when the next urgent need arises, be able to win priority contracts, charge premium prices, and build long-term strategic partnerships. Flexible capacity is a moat, not just a cost center.

Strategic Planning Considerations

Based on the lessons of this section, if your site wanted to improve its emergency manufacturing capability, which three areas could you start with?

Suggested directions: (1) assess cleanroom space in existing facilities that could quickly be reconfigured for mRNA or gene therapy; (2) establish standard modular facility design agreements with equipment suppliers; (3) maintain trained personnel and an up-to-date quality system, ensuring a state of "readiness" rather than "hibernation".

Five Takeaways for CDMOs

  • GMP itself is the protection: strict implementation of existing GMP is the first line of defense against unknown pathogens, and there is no need for panic-driven facility modifications.
  • mRNA platform capability is a strategic asset: from plasmid DNA preparation to LNP formulation and filling, every link in the mRNA manufacturing chain is a niche market with high barriers to entry.
  • Aseptic filling is the critical bottleneck: the core weakness of vaccine developers (Moderna, BioNTech) was precisely aseptic filling capability, which is the most direct business opportunity for CDMOs.
  • Partnerships take priority over building your own: during the pandemic the fastest progress came from building partnerships rather than going it alone. Building a collaborative ecosystem in advance is more effective than looking for partners in the middle of an emergency.
  • Flexible design is a moat: modular facilities and quickly reconfigurable production lines are the best preparation for the next urgent need. A "hibernating plant" often cannot be started up when the emergency comes.

Q 2-1: How do we overcome the workforce shortage induced by COVID-19?

Background

The pandemic created an unexpected and prolonged shortage of workers. This shortage occurred because companies were neither prepared or equipped to ensure that workers could return to a safe environment. The initial worker shortage continued through the pandemic due, in part, to the impact of unvaccinated essential workers not being able to return to the workplace. Compounding this in some countries was government-augmented unemployment and housing benefits for workers, which created an economic driver for some vaccinated workers to delay their return to the workforce. The decreased availability of workers was largely also due to:

  • Lack of available childcare causing parents to stay at home
  • People voluntarily leaving the workforce entirely
  • People going into early retirement
  • People choosing to move from an essential job to a nonessential job

Low vaccination rates lengthened the severity of the pandemic as virus mutations (e.g., Beta, Delta, and Omicron) developed and moved through the population. This phenomenon of vaccine hesitancy was not limited to the United States but was also seen in many other countries where vaccines were available. In addition to the low vaccination rates, breakthrough infections of individuals who were vaccinated also resulted in a high infection rate and absence from work.

The pandemic has been emotionally challenging for many people and has changed many aspects of everyday life in unprecedented ways. One positive method to encourage employees and decrease worker shortages is to protect employees and create an atmosphere of safety. Companies began reimplementing certain aspects of their safety plans, such as wearing masks, social distancing, and frequent hand-sanitizing. Maintaining flexibility is important during transitional times while the right policies and support for workers are identified. Communicating with employees to ensure their needs are addressed is extremely important.

Lessons Learned

To reduce the level of pandemic-induced worker shortages, companies should have plans in place to minimize employee risk in the face of potential pandemics, not only for an initial outbreak but also for the recurring waves of a pandemic that might be expected. The external environment should then be continually evaluated to determine what specific steps need to be taken to minimize the risk to employees, such as returning to specific requirements, like wearing masks and social distancing, that were implemented during the initial wave of the pandemic and then relaxed as the initial wave subsided. Ensuring that workers feel safe in the workplace is essential to maintaining the workforce.

As needed, there are also other approaches that companies can use to entice workers to return, thus reducing worker shortages. One such approach is the use of special bonuses to entice workers to return as well as to attract new employees to fill openings. To further encourage workers, companies should consider other added benefits such as more flexible work-from-home options (hybrid models seem to be the most accepted in the industry when nonessential work can be done from home a few days a week), childcare funding, company cell phones, and college tuition assistance. These were all used effectively by various companies as the pandemic progressed.

Companies may require disclosure of employee vaccination status, where allowed, to encourage workers to get vaccinated. In the United States, requiring the disclosure of COVID-19 vaccination status was allowed. Ultimately, companies may mandate that workers return to work or forfeit their employment if no remote work opportunities are available.

As a result of the worker shortages, companies began offering bonuses and other incentives to entice existing workers to return and to attract new workers during the pandemic. With the shift to remote working, it also created an opportunity for employees to change jobs for increased pay — many called this phenomenon "the great resignation." Another form of enticement, once legally allowed, was a provision that permitted companies to mandate that workers be vaccinated and return to work or lose their employment. Some companies used a combination of requiring vaccination or weekly testing.

Commentary

Core Concept: The Multiple Root Causes of the Workforce Shortage

The employee shortage caused by COVID-19 did not have a single cause but several overlapping factors:

  • Unvaccinated essential workers could not return to the workplace
  • Government subsidies reduced employees' willingness to return to the job market
  • Childcare problems kept parents at home
  • Large numbers of employees chose early retirement or a move to non-essential roles

For a pharmaceutical CDMO, any one of these can leave critical production lines short-staffed, directly affecting batch completion rates and delivery dates.

Analogy: The Real Impact of the "Great Resignation"

The "great resignation" was like a "tectonic shift" in the talent market: the spread of remote work removed geographic limits, so employees could move to a better-paying company without relocating. For roles that require physical presence, such as cleanroom operators, the impact was relatively small, but roles that can be done remotely, such as technical support and QA review, suffered heavy losses.

Key Point: Design Principles for Flexible Work Models

PDA states clearly that the approach the industry accepts most is the hybrid model: non-critical work is done from home several days a week, while critical manufacturing operations must be performed on site.

For TWi, the following roles fall into the "must be on site" category:

  • Aseptic filling operators
  • Environmental monitoring (EM) personnel
  • Equipment cleaning and sterilization personnel
  • Staff executing batch records on the floor

Vaccination Status Disclosure and HIPAA Compliance

In the US, requiring employees to disclose their vaccination status does not violate HIPAA, as long as it is handled through the company's medical personnel or a designated channel. A similar principle applies in many other countries, but it must be confirmed against local regulations.

Recommended action for TWi: before the next pandemic, confirm in advance what Taiwan's regulations say about vaccination status disclosure, and include it in the BCP personnel policy.

  • Which roles are essential workers who absolutely must be on site?
  • Which retention bonus schemes could be launched quickly?
  • Is the technology for remote second-person verification in place?
Q 2-2: What restrictions should be considered for essential workers to ensure continued operation?

Background

At the start of the pandemic, few companies outlined what roles were considered essential for ensuring continued operations or what actions should be taken to protect those individuals.

Disruptions caused some companies to lose weeks and even months of manufacturing productivity as operations were shut down, limiting the spread of the virus. Even companies manufacturing the vaccine, such as Pfizer and Moderna, announced a worker shortage and difficulty recruiting people in Boston, Massachusetts.

The lack of personnel created a strain on the activities required to support production and batch release in the following areas:

  • Lack of trained and qualified operators on the manufacturing lines
  • Lack of trained technical support for setup and changeover
  • Lack of qualified personnel for batch-records review

Without the required personnel, the operations will be interrupted and items like changeovers, technical support, and batch disposition cannot be provided or performed. Additionally, batch records cannot be reviewed, manufacturing processes are interrupted, product testing will not be completed, and product is held in the warehouse and cannot be released.

Lessons Learned

To prevent a strain on activities supporting production and batch release, sites must be ready to quickly apply new approved technologies (e.g., cameras to replace second-person verifications, implementation of electronic signatures for approval of batch records, augmented reality (AR) glasses or handheld devices for problem-solving on the line). New technologies, such as cameras and AR glasses, can support manufacturing-line operations by reducing the number of staff who need to be physically at the factory. The qualified personnel can provide instructions remotely. The remote support could be used for such functions as line clearance, second-person approval, and on-the-job training. Dispensing of raw materials can be performed by having the second operator perform the verification remotely. A risk assessment would define what activities can be performed remotely and what activities cannot.

Automation of dispensing and filling lines can continue to be performed with a very limited number of resources on site and can ensure continued manufacturing using these principles.

Additionally, companies should have plans in place that outline and define what roles are considered essential for manufacturing operations. The plan should outline the emergency procedures to be implemented to protect those essential workers and account for any possible governmentally mandated regulations. While the exact characteristics of the infectious agent and its mode of transmission may not be known with all certainty, common-sense measures should be put in place, such as:

  • Use of face coverings (if not already in place) in GMP areas
  • Increased cleaning of facilities and equipment
  • Nonessential workers working remotely
  • Cafeteria, conference rooms, and other common areas closed, or the number of personnel allowed to be present restricted

A review of how essential workers commute to work should be evaluated. Where the normal mode of transportation is via mass transportation (e.g., trains, buses, subways) or carpools, alternative modes of transportation to reduce the chance of workers being exposed should be considered. Plans to provide written documentation for employees to keep on their person for transit should be in place to comply with local, state, and federal requirements.

Consideration should be given to scheduling groups of employees in shifts or units (cohorting) — having a defined group of people working together, while the other group is working from home and vice versa. If the shifts or units remain unchanged, the risk of the entire team being impacted is reduced. Personnel movement should be restricted to specific buildings or areas to minimize the possibility of a super-spreader event occurring.

Social distancing, visual cues of vaccination status, floor markers for the flow of personnel, and distance between individuals, should be implemented. If possible, electronic proximity detectors should be used to alert individuals when they are too close to one another. Physical barriers, such as plexiglass, should be installed to minimize exposure between individuals. Additionally, the speed of programs to screen workers on site should be established quickly (e.g., temperature, contact-tracing, quarantine requirements, daily health questionnaires).

Commentary

Core Concept: Definition and Tiering of Essential Workers

In pharmaceutical manufacturing, "essential workers" are those whose work cannot be performed remotely and whose absence would directly interrupt production or product release. They specifically include:

  • Manufacturing line operators (filling, lyophilization)
  • Equipment setup and changeover technicians
  • QC laboratory testing personnel
  • Batch record review and release authorization personnel (Qualified Person)
  • Warehouse and shipping personnel

If any one link is missing, the entire production chain can stall, leaving product stuck in the warehouse and unable to be released.

Analogy: Cohorting Is Like a "Football Team Rotation"

Imagine a football team that splits its players into Group A and Group B. While Group A is on the field, Group B is fully isolated on standby; if someone in Group A tests positive, Group B steps in immediately and the game goes on. This is the core logic of cohorting: by isolating fixed small groups, even if one entire group has to go into isolation, the other group can still keep the production line running.

For TWi's aseptic filling line, this means that personnel on A Shift and B Shift should avoid cross-contact in non-production areas such as break rooms and changing rooms.

Key Point: Regulatory Considerations for Remote Second-Person Verification

PDA suggests that cameras and AR glasses could replace part of the physical-presence requirement for "second-person verification". In aseptic manufacturing, however, these alternatives require:

  • A prior risk assessment that clearly defines which activities can be done remotely and which cannot
  • Updated SOPs and acceptance by the relevant regulatory authority (depending on audit requirements)
  • An electronic signature system that meets 21 CFR Part 11 requirements

Commuting Safety and Employee Transit Documentation

Many countries imposed movement restrictions during the pandemic, and essential workers at manufacturing sites needed transit documentation to get to work. Companies should prepare standardized certificate templates in advance, stating that the employee belongs to an essential industry, in order to comply with government restrictions.

Recommended action for TWi: prepare a bilingual (Chinese and English) "essential worker transit certificate" template in the BCP documents, and obtain a pre-confirmation procedure from the competent authorities (Ministry of Health and Welfare / Industrial Development Bureau).

TWi CDMO Scenario Exercise

If an operator on one of TWi's filling lines tests positive and the cohorting plan is triggered:

  • Within how many hours can the standby group (Shift B) complete the shift change and resume production?
  • Can batch record reviewers complete batch release remotely using the electronic signature system?
  • Have the AR-assisted or camera equipment completed IQ/OQ and can it be put into use immediately?
Q 2-3: What actions can be taken to minimize the risk of spreading contagions in the workplace?

Background

At the onset of the pandemic, the steps taken to minimize the spread of infection varied regionally. Companies referred to the guidance from public officials, world regulatory authorities, and regional health departments in an attempt to minimize the spread of COVID-19. In many cases, workplaces were temporarily closed, or the occupancy was limited to try and slow the spread of the infection in the population. Some companies also limited the operations to only essential workers, with supporting personnel working from home.

Some workers were classified as "essential" to the continuation of critical functions for society, and thus were required to be physically present to conduct business activities. Individuals with functions directly related to the manufacturing, testing, warehousing, and distribution of critical supplies, such as medicines, medical treatments, and medical supplies (including raw materials and test reagents), were classified in this way and continued to enter the workplace throughout the pandemic.

Lessons Learned — Engineering Controls

Manufacturers of pharmaceutical and medical-device products already have robust contamination controls in place for their manufacturing areas. These controls (e.g., gowning, high airflow and exchange, documented cleaning, disinfection programs) are already effective strategies to reduce the risk of spreading contaminants in the manufacturing area. A greater risk of transmission is in the office space and other communal areas, where controls are not commonly in place.

Engineering Controls include:

  • Modify seat or workstation placement
  • Construct physical separations, such as plexiglass partitions
  • Install floor markers for flow of personnel with the required distancing between individuals
  • Close or impose restrictions on cafeterias, common areas, and conference rooms
  • Mandate the use of electronic proximity detectors to alert individuals when they are too close to one another
  • Disinfect shared filing or storage spaces between uses
  • Replace high-touch communal items with prepackaged single servings
  • Improve ventilation in the building and consider air-filtration systems
  • Consider local filtration systems where the HVAC system cannot be improved
  • Ensure that adequate ventilation, air changes, and appropriate filtration can be used during the design of new facilities or those being renovated
  • Open windows frequently in office spaces and keep doors open in office spaces without windows

Lessons Learned — Administrative Controls

Administrative Controls include:

  • Mandate face coverings
  • Require staff to stay home if they or a family member are ill; consider requiring testing before a return to site is permitted
  • Limit maximum occupancy in common areas such as breakrooms
  • Add additional shifts or stagger shifts, start times, and break times to reduce worker density
  • Restrict personnel movement to specific buildings or areas and/or segregate working groups
  • Reassign personnel to minimize movement between buildings or areas
  • Provide personnel training on correct masking, hand sanitization, and social distancing
  • Implement programs to screen workers upon entering the site (e.g., temperature check, PCR or antigen, and completion of daily health questionnaires)
  • Establish infection notification, contact-tracing, and quarantine requirements
  • Institute a cleaning and disinfection program for common areas and office areas
  • Clean daily high-touch surfaces, such as door handles, desks, light switches, faucets, toilets, workstations, keyboards, telephones, handrails, printer/copier machines, and drinking fountains
  • Post instructions and reminders at entrances and strategic places in the building on masking, hand sanitization, and cough-and-sneeze etiquette

Consideration should also be given to sanitation concerns, such as hand hygiene and surface disinfection. Hand-hygiene stations should be placed at the entry and exits of high-traffic areas and in communal areas.

The cleaning and disinfection of communal areas and workspace surfaces should be reviewed to ensure that it is adequate and effective against contagions of concern. The frequency of disinfection should be based on risk. Proof of cleaning and disinfection of common areas, workstations, and desks should be made evident with use of status cards or color indicators.

Having a cleaning and disinfectant program in place is not enough. The products used should be efficacious. As the SARS-CoV-2 was a novel virus, none of the biocides on the market had label claims for efficacy against the virus at the beginning of the pandemic. However, the U.S. EPA issued a list (List N) of all products that the agency expected to kill the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus when used in accordance with label directions.

Requiring nonessential-worker vaccination was not commonly considered by companies until the COVID-19 vaccines were made widely available. Before allowing workers to physically return to the workplace, it is recommended that the vaccine status of employees is cataloged for business continuity and safety purposes.

Employees and companies may prefer a staggered return to work. This can be achieved by surveying employees on when and how they would like to return to the workplace setting.

Commentary

Core Concept: Engineering Controls vs. Administrative Controls

Occupational safety frameworks divide risk controls into two categories:

  • Engineering Controls: change the physical environment itself, such as barriers, ventilation systems and floor markings. Their effect is more lasting and does not depend on people's behavior.
  • Administrative Controls: manage people's behavior through policies, procedures and training, such as mask requirements, health questionnaires and contact tracing.

The two need to be used together. Pharmaceutical plants' GMP areas already have fairly well-developed engineering controls (cleanroom positive pressure, gowning procedures), but non-GMP areas such as offices, changing rooms and canteens are the main gaps.

Analogy: The Office Area Is the "Achilles' Heel of Infection Control"

A pharmaceutical cleanroom is like a "fortress" that already has tight protection, whereas break rooms, changing rooms and car parks are "open ground" with no moat. Most in-house clusters of infection (super-spreader events) occurred in non-production areas, not on the production line itself. Engineering controls must therefore extend across the whole site and not focus only on GMP areas.

Key Point: The Scientific Basis for Disinfectant Selection

When SARS-CoV-2 first emerged, no disinfectant on the market had a label claim against the virus. The US EPA published List N, a list of products expected to be effective, as interim guidance.

This experience tells us that when facing a novel pathogen in the future, we should establish a "rapid disinfectant evaluation procedure" in advance, drawing on a mechanism similar to EPA List N, rather than waiting for formal label validation.

For TWi, if the existing GMP disinfectant program (for example Sporicidin, 70% IPA) were faced with a novel virus, its broad-spectrum efficacy against enveloped viruses would need to be checked quickly.

Key Elements of Contact Tracing

An effective contact tracing system requires:

  • Accurate records of which areas each employee enters each day (access control records)
  • Defined distance and time thresholds for "high-risk contact" (usually within 15 minutes and within 1.5 meters)
  • Completion of tracing notifications within 24 hours of a confirmed case being reported
  • A mechanism for temporary staffing replacements during isolation periods

Electronic proximity detectors can greatly improve tracing accuracy, but employee acceptance in terms of privacy must be considered.

TWi CDMO Application Checklist

Priority items to review for engineering controls against infection at the TWi site:

  • Does the gowning room capacity limit clearly specify a maximum number of people?
  • Do the air change rates (ACH) in non-GMP areas (offices, canteen) meet recommended standards?
  • Has the site-entry health questionnaire been digitized so that it can quickly be expanded to a mandatory daily form?
  • Has the use of "cleaning completed" status indicator cards been standardized in common areas?
Q 2-4: Who gets the vaccine first?

Background

As the first vaccines were developed and approved for emergency use, the number of doses available were limited. The question of who should receive the first doses was a topic of great discussion and debate. After health care workers and those at greatest risk, such as the elderly in long-term care facilities, came essential workers and those over 65. It was not clear during the vaccine roll-out exactly where pharmaceutical manufacturing personnel stood in regard to their priority in receiving the vaccine.

Lessons Learned

As the continued supply of pharmaceutical products is critical, it is important that the pharmaceutical industry workers within the manufacturing facility who are directly supporting the manufacturing floor operations and whose jobs cannot be performed remotely should be on the list to receive the vaccine early, and they should be the first vaccinated within the company based on their importance to the manufacturing operations.

The line of workers that comprise manufacturing-floor operations should include:

  • The technicians directly performing testing of the products
  • The warehousing and shipping personnel responsible for moving and shipping the product
  • Other individuals who must be present for the product to be produced, released, and shipped

These workers are essential to ensure product supply. Companies should have plans in place to quickly identify these individuals in the event of another pandemic.

Commentary

Core Concept: The Argument for Vaccine Priority for Pharmaceutical Personnel

In the early stage of the COVID-19 pandemic, the vaccine priority order was generally:

  1. Healthcare workers (highest exposure risk)
  2. Residents of long-term care facilities (highest mortality risk)
  3. The general public aged 65 and over
  4. Critical infrastructure employees (essential workers)

Although pharmaceutical manufacturing personnel do not come into direct contact with patients, their importance lies in the fact that they produce the medicines that sustain the whole of society, and if a production line stops, every patient who needs medication is affected.

Analogy: A Pharmaceutical Plant Is Like an "Ammunition Factory"

In World War II, workers in munitions factories did not go to the front line, yet they were the key support behind victory in the whole war. The same is true of aseptic filling operators in pharmaceutical plants: they are not in the intensive care unit, but the injectables they produce are what allow the intensive care unit to function. This group therefore deserves the same vaccine priority as critical infrastructure personnel.

Key Point: Build a "Priority Vaccination List" in Advance

PDA's recommendation is: before the next pandemic, a company should prepare a "priority vaccination list of essential manufacturing personnel", so that it can be submitted quickly to government agencies when vaccine distribution begins, in order to secure priority access to doses.

Principles for designing the list:

  • Use "whether the work can be performed remotely" as the inclusion criterion
  • Include personnel in direct production, testing, warehousing and shipping
  • Exclude administrative, finance and sales staff who can work from home (unless there are special circumstances)

Recommended Action for TWi CDMO

It is recommended that TWi add an annex, "Priority Vaccine List for Essential Manufacturing Personnel", to the BCP document and update it regularly (every year). The list should include name, job title and a description of the nature of the work (the reason it cannot be performed remotely), and should be co-signed by HR and the manufacturing supervisor.

In the future, this list can be provided directly to the health authority (such as the Taiwan CDC) as a basis for priority vaccine allocation.

Q 2-5: How do safety and human resource policies change after a vaccine is readily available?

Background

As the pandemic progressed and vaccines became widely available, a significant percentage of the population refused to receive the vaccination. There were multiple underlying reasons for this, among them, religious beliefs, general fear of vaccinations, and fears specific to the COVID-19 vaccine due to the lack of long-term clinical data. Compounding these challenges was a preexisting and active anti-vaccination movement that caused many individuals to avoid receiving the vaccine based on incorrect safety information. A strain on the availability of vaccinated employees resulted.

Because the COVID-19 virus is highly infectious, the percentage of the population required to obtain herd immunity to the virus would be expected to be very high. Adding to the percentage required to achieve this is the question of the vaccine's protection against infection over time as well as the potential of reinfection over time. Based on these factors, governments have taken the unusual step of allowing employers to request the vaccination status of workers, apply restrictions and, in some countries, require vaccinations as a term of continued employment.

For many, this was a paradigm shift and a significant change in expectations between employers and employees.

Lessons Learned

When on-site workers or visitors test positive for SARS-CoV-2, certain preventive measures need to take place. It is important to have processes in place for contact-tracing to let other employees know that they have been in contact with this individual, that they are at risk, and that they need to quarantine as well. The infected person should stay at home until they are no longer infectious. The duration may vary depending on the strain. Companies should follow the quarantine durations established by their public health authorities.

The work area where this person was located needs to be cleaned and disinfected with an effective disinfectant. People identified through contact-tracing may need to provide a negative COVID-19 test result prior to returning to work. Personnel should be trained on masking, hand sanitization, and social distancing to lower the risk of future outbreaks and potential super-spreader events.

Companies should put processes in place where legally permitted to restrict the access to individuals (employees and visitors) who have not been vaccinated. For employees who have chosen not to receive the vaccination, companies should consider requiring a PCR or antigen test no older than 48 or 72 hours to be shown or, in some cases, should seek to replace them with employees who have been vaccinated. The vaccination status of all employees and visitors should be verified, and the records of the verification should be kept on file. Any exceptions should be rare and specific restrictions should be applied.

Commentary

Core Concept: The Impact of Vaccine Hesitancy on Manufacturing

Vaccine hesitancy took many forms during COVID-19: religious objections, concerns about the lack of long-term safety data for the novel mRNA vaccines, and the fueling effect of anti-vaccine movements. At pharmaceutical manufacturing sites, this translated directly into the problem that "the number of employees willing to be vaccinated is not enough to maintain a safety margin for cohorting".

Achieving herd immunity requires a high vaccination rate, and together with the existence of breakthrough infections, this means that even after most employees are vaccinated, a certain level of non-pharmaceutical interventions (masks, social distancing) still needs to be maintained.

Analogy: PCR Testing as a "Substitute Pass"

Requiring employees who choose not to be vaccinated to provide a negative PCR or rapid test result from within the past 48-72 hours is like requiring an unlicensed driver to pass a road test before every drive. It is a management mechanism that substitutes continuous verification for a qualification status.

This design recognizes individual freedom of choice while also protecting the collective safety of the whole workplace, striking a balance within what the law permits.

Key Point: Standard Procedure After a Confirmed Case

The procedure PDA recommends for handling a confirmed case includes:

  1. Immediate isolation: the confirmed individual isolates at home until no longer infectious (the duration depends on the virus variant; follow local health authority guidance)
  2. Contact tracing: notify all close contacts and require them to be tested and to quarantine as a precaution
  3. Area disinfection: thoroughly disinfect the confirmed individual's work area with an effective disinfectant
  4. Return-to-work conditions: those who were isolated must provide a negative test report before returning to the site
  5. Record retention: all vaccination verification records and contact tracing records must be properly retained for future reference

Legal Boundaries of Employer Vaccine Policies

PDA notes that in most countries an employer has the right to:

  • Require employees to disclose their vaccination status (this does not violate privacy law because it goes through the company's medical channel)
  • Require unvaccinated employees to submit regular negative test results as a condition of site entry
  • In some countries, even make vaccination a condition of continued employment

Taiwan's legal framework differs from that of the US, and TWi HR needs to confirm with legal counsel the policy boundaries that can be adopted during a future pandemic.

TWi CDMO BCP Policy Recommendations

It is recommended that before the next pandemic TWi complete the following HR policy preparations:

  • Establish "Vaccination Status Recording and Management Rules" (including retention period and data protection provisions)
  • Establish an "Employee Confirmed Case Reporting SOP", including contact tracing timelines, isolation notification templates and return-to-work conditions
  • Plan an "alternative screening program" for unvaccinated employees (frequency, who bears the cost)
  • Confirm whether the visitor management system can record vaccination or testing status as a condition of site entry
Section II Summary

Key Lessons from Section II: People

Section II addresses one of the most challenging dimensions of pandemic response: managing the human workforce in a critical manufacturing environment. The five questions collectively reveal that workforce disruption was not just about illness — it was amplified by psychological, economic, logistical, and policy factors at every level.

Core takeaways:

  • Q2-1: Workforce shortages are multi-causal and require layered responses — from bonuses and hybrid work to vaccination mandates. Worker safety perception is fundamental to retention.
  • Q2-2: Companies must pre-define essential roles and have documented cohorting, remote verification, and commute-safety protocols ready before a crisis hits.
  • Q2-3: Office and communal areas are the primary contagion risk in pharma facilities. Engineering and administrative controls must cover the entire site, not just GMP areas.
  • Q2-4: Pharmaceutical manufacturing staff should be proactively positioned on government priority vaccine lists, with companies maintaining updated critical-role registries.
  • Q2-5: Post-vaccine HR policies must include contact-tracing SOP, testing alternatives for non-vaccinated staff, and legally-compliant vaccination status record-keeping.

The overarching message: preparation before the next pandemic is the only effective strategy. Reactive measures during a crisis are too slow for an aseptic manufacturing environment where every day of downtime has patient safety implications.

Q3-1: How should companies assess their risk ahead of their next regulatory authority inspection?

Background

During the pandemic, the majority of global regulatory authorities eliminated most on-site inspections. Those that were conducted focused on new-product approvals, "high-risk" production sites, or inspections required to facilitate emergency authorization of COVID-19-related products. Because of this, the routine inspection schedule to which many organizations were accustomed did not occur.

During this period, two other factors impacted manufacturing facilities and their traditional operating model:

  1. Manufacturing facilities involved with COVID-19 vaccine production were required, in some cases, to rapidly scale up facilities, people, and processes. The speed at which this was required created circumstances that made it difficult at times to operate in full compliance with all current expectations regarding quality processes and procedures.
  2. The change in the staffing model—to have all employees work remotely except those deemed essential for on-the-floor manufacturing operations, combined with an overall shortage of workers—created challenges to the manufacturing process quality systems that led to an increase in nonconformances to established processes and procedures.

Lessons Learned

Companies must remain aware of the actions that must be taken in their efforts to continue to supply the market with necessary pharmaceutical products. As operations return to a more normal, pre-pandemic state and in preparation for the return of regulatory authority inspections, companies should review the nonconformances and changes that have occurred and ensure there is adequate documentation to describe the event, the assessment made in its evaluation, the final decision, and any follow-up actions taken.

Where there are gaps, identified action should be taken to eliminate or reduce those gaps. Any additions should be made as amendments to the original documentation. Where appropriate, the documentation should clearly identify the pandemic as being the cause of or a contributing factor in the nonconformance.

In addition, companies should perform a gap assessment of their current process against the applicable regulations and expectations. Any gaps identified should be closed or a plan should be established to close these gaps. These reviews and assessments, as well as the plans for closing any gaps, should be available for review during the regulatory authority inspections if needed.

Companies that are required to rapidly scale up should perform a comparative assessment, if appropriate, against existing facilities, personnel, and processes to understand the similarities and differences in the approach used to bring the new capabilities online. Any differences should be evaluated and, where compliance gaps are identified, plans should be developed to close these gaps.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Concept: How Inspection Gaps Form

During the pandemic, regulators worldwide (such as FDA and EMA) suspended most routine on-site inspections. This created two main problems:

  • Compliance gaps left by rapid scale-up: COVID-19 vaccine production facilities scaled up massively in a short time, with speed taking precedence over rigorous quality processes, which led to more nonconformances.
  • Impact of remote work models: non-production staff worked fully remotely, workforces were short, and quality management systems came under unprecedented pressure.

Analogy: A Self-Check Before the Final Exam

A regulatory inspection is like a final exam. During the pandemic the teacher (the inspector) did not come to school to proctor, and many students (companies) took shortcuts. Now that exams are resuming, companies must quickly: 1) find out where they have fallen short; 2) complete their documents and records; 3) make sure they have a remediation plan to present to the examiner.

Key Point: Three Steps of Pre-Inspection Preparation

  • Step 1: Take inventory of all nonconformances during the pandemic and confirm that documentation is complete (event description, assessment, decision, follow-up actions)
  • Step 2: Perform a gap assessment, comparing current processes against regulatory requirements, identifying gaps and developing a closure plan
  • Step 3: Facilities that scaled up rapidly must undergo a comparative assessment to evaluate the differences between old and new facilities/processes and the associated compliance risk

CDMO Practical Considerations

As a CDMO, your client may also be your auditor. When a client asks to review your nonconformance records, do the documents clearly state that "the pandemic was the cause of, or a contributing factor to, the nonconformance"? That one sentence can lead an inspector to a very different assessment of a deviation.

Q3-2: How will the "remote inspection" and/or "remote assessment" paradigm shift impact the industry?

Background

During the pandemic, regulators adapted their GMP and good distribution practices (GDP) inspection routines due to travel restrictions and safety concerns. In March 2020, the FDA paused most in-person inspections to safeguard its employees. To continue to provide oversight, the FDA began performing remote assessments. Similar reductions in on-site inspections were noted in the European Union with the relevant local agencies moving to more remote and hybrid inspections.

Per regulations in place at the time of this publication, remote inspections of pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities by FDA were not permitted, as regulations require inspectors to be physically on site within the facility as part of any inspection.

This shift to remote assessments and inspections provided many advantages in terms of planning and flexibility but, as with all major shifts made within very narrow timelines, unanticipated challenges were encountered.

Lessons Learned

During the initial stages of the pandemic in 2020, it quickly became apparent that remote assessments and inspections required more thorough preparation than on-site inspections. Some of the challenges GMP/GDP remote inspections and assessments can present are related to:

  • Planning: Preparatory conference calls, connectivity tests, and a redundancy strategy are very important.
  • Qualification Audits: Brand new manufacturing sites have no historical background on the GMP compliance level of the site being inspected.
  • Communications: Cultural issues, poor clarity in requests from inspectors lacking local knowledge, and the quality of electronic connections can present obstacles.
  • Online Tools: The use of different tools to share confidential documents, data, and information and use of mobile phones to capture live images from potentially hazardous manufacturing areas may be needed.
  • Overall Conduct and Compliance to Agenda: Adapting to different time zones can be easily overcome; however, in many circumstances, the difference can lead to longer meetings.

Both industry and regulators are on a learning curve when applying virtual tools. Companies will continue to benefit from investments they have made in facilities, equipment, and procedures for hosting live remote interactions with regulatory authorities. Firms may need to refine their hosting capabilities as they gain experience and as the approaches of global regulators evolve.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Concept: The Paradigm Shift in Remote Inspection

Before the pandemic, GMP inspections almost always had to be conducted on site. During the pandemic, FDA and EMA were forced to develop remote assessment and hybrid inspection models. Note that at the time of publication of this document, FDA regulations still required inspectors to be physically present, so remote inspection had not been formally legalized, but remote assessment had become routine.

Analogy: A Remote Inspection Is Like a Video Interview

A traditional inspection is like an on-site interview, where the inspector can see everything first-hand. A remote inspection is like a video interview: your background, network quality and screen-sharing ability all shape the other side's first impression. However capable you are, if the picture freezes or you cannot find the documents, you can still lose points.

Five Challenges at a Glance

  • Advance planning: connections must be tested and a redundancy strategy prepared
  • Qualification inspections: a brand-new site has no compliance history, so the inspector has no background to refer to
  • Communication barriers: language and cultural differences combined with connection quality greatly increase the risk of misunderstanding
  • Online tools: live video of hazardous production areas has to be captured with a mobile phone, and safety permission must be obtained beforehand
  • Time zone management: inspections across time zones may lengthen meetings and affect efficiency on both sides

CDMO Practical Considerations

Remote inspection is already routine in the CDMO industry, and a client's virtual audit can happen at any time. It is advisable to establish a "Remote Inspection Readiness" SOP that includes a predefined camera tour route, an electronic document index and a mock run plan, so that you can respond calmly whenever one is triggered.

Q3-3: What cybersecurity changes have become necessary because of COVID-19?

Background

The increased level of digitalization in the pharmaceutical industry over the last decade has been unparalleled as computer-controlled processes, electronic batch records, information management systems, documentation and investigations systems have been implemented. With the increase in digitalization has come the increased risk of cybersecurity threats.

Cybersecurity experts saw a 63 percent increase in cyberattacks related to the pandemic. From a human-error standpoint, even the change in environment has led to increases in successful phishing scams with a reported 47 percent of individuals being taken in by a phishing scam while working at home.

The rapid implementation of nonmanufacturing employees working remotely during the pandemic presented unanticipated challenges to corporate cybersecurity:

  • Instituting a "Bring Your Own Device" (BYOD) approach enabled employees to access corporate data using their personal devices, which bypassed the standard "Corporate Owned Personally Enabled" approach.
  • The use of new and rapidly evolving web-conferencing and information-sharing platforms has become commonplace, presenting new cybersecurity risks.
  • The vulnerability of home networks presented challenges as the security and reliability of these networks varied greatly.
  • Sophisticated IT systems are susceptible to human error, especially systems that require a repetitive manual step like connecting to a company's virtual private network (VPN) before sending data.

Lessons Learned

A strong cybersecurity plan should be in place long before a pandemic occurs and updated routinely. Likewise, risk assessments should be conducted that can direct priority actions, reviewed regularly and updated with more pandemic-specific information as circumstances emerge.

Specific points relevant to a pandemic that should be considered for inclusion in risk assessments include:

  • The expected increase in the number and complexity of cyberattacks related to external interest in new pandemic-related technologies the company may be developing
  • Any changes in the way employees will work and interact with the company's electronic systems that may concern cybersecurity systems
  • The current level of cybersecurity surveillance and its ability to rapidly identify cyberattacks based on expected changes due to the pandemic conditions
  • The preparedness of the company to respond to and contain a cyberattack based on expected changes in the way employees work and their locations (remote vs. in-person) due to the pandemic
  • A review of the respective business continuity and crisis management plans should a successful cyberattack occur that shuts down critical systems

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Concept: Digitalization and Cybersecurity Threats Rise Together

Over the past decade the pharmaceutical industry has pushed digitalization hard, and electronic batch records and electronic quality management systems (eQMS) have gradually become common. The other side of digitalization, however, is a rapid growth in cybersecurity exposure. During the pandemic, three main vulnerabilities appeared at the same time: BYOD devices, home networks and new video platforms.

Analogy: A Front Door at Home Without a Lock

A company's IT protection is like a solid castle, but remote work lets employees take confidential documents home, and the security of the "home" door varies from person to person. Some use public Wi-Fi, and some never update their router password. Once one of these "homes" is breached, the castle's defenses collapse with it.

Pandemic Cybersecurity Risk Data

  • Pandemic-related cyberattacks increased by 63% (Help Net Security, 2020)
  • 47% of employees working from home had been subjected to phishing attacks (Deloitte)
  • The EMA (European Medicines Agency) and several COVID-19 vaccine developers were all subjected to targeted cyberattacks

Five Elements of a Cybersecurity Risk Assessment

  1. Increased sophistication of external attacks targeting new technologies
  2. The impact of changed employee working patterns on electronic systems
  3. The ability of existing security monitoring systems to identify attacks
  4. The redundancy capability to respond to cyberattacks under remote working conditions
  5. The cybersecurity response section of the BCP and crisis management plan

CDMO Practical Considerations

A CDMO holds large amounts of clients' confidential formulations, analytical methods and batch records. If a cybersecurity incident occurs, the consequences are not only an internal matter but could also trigger breach clauses in client contracts. It is advisable to perform cybersecurity risk assessments regularly and to manage the BYOD policy, VPN usage rules and remote access audit trail within the pharmaceutical quality system (PQS).

Q3-4: What new data-integrity risks are present because of a more remote workforce?

Background

Many work activities shifted from in-person to remote access because of the pandemic. This trend continues even though workers have been allowed to return to the workplace. This hybrid mode of working involves the application of various information and communication technologies (ICT) that are used for gathering, storing, retrieving, processing, analyzing, and transmitting information. ICT comprises the variety of software and hardware that drives it, for example, smartphones, handheld devices, laptop computers, desktop computers, video cameras, and wearable technology.

The increase in remote work activities utilizing ICT presents an inherent increase in risk as workers have moved from company-owned office equipment and a structured environment to home offices where the company has less control over the ICT. This increase in the use of ICT by remote workers adds more layers of data-handling and storage which, in turn, increases the risk of potential data-integrity issues.

The risk to data integrity has been further impacted by the increasing number of audits and inspections taking place remotely; with these, a high number of external personnel and stakeholders require access to critical data, each using their own ICT, adding to the complexity.

Lessons Learned

Some companies need to proactively evaluate the ICT currently in use within the company or those being planned to enable both in-person and remote work. A set of readily available ICT solutions approved for use by a company's IT units should be readily available and updated on a periodic basis. Senior leadership needs to ensure adequate resources (headcount and funding) are available for this activity.

For example, prior to a remote audit, assessment, or inspection where external entities are involved, an agreement on mutually approved ICT solutions should be in place to minimize the risk of data-integrity issues.

Finally, in situations where paper-based systems remain (e.g., parts of or even the entire pharmaceutical quality system (PQS)), additional attention should be placed on changing them over to electronic systems. Where that is not possible or practical, developing a process for how to ensure paper-based systems can be effectively performed remotely will minimize the potential for data-integrity issues, for example, how the ICT and related procedures generate the PDF-type documents to implement appropriate e-signature solutions.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Concept: Data Integrity Risks Across Multiple ICT Layers

Data integrity requires that data meet the ALCOA+ principles: Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate, plus Complete, Consistent, Enduring and Available.

In a remote work environment, ICT devices are diverse (mobile phones, laptops, personal computers) and data passes through more layers, so every link is a potential data integrity breach.

Analogy: A Document Passed Along a Relay

Imagine a batch record that has to go from the production line to QA and then to a supervisor for signature. In the office, this is a sheet of paper handed over by the same people in person. When working remotely, the document may pass through email, a cloud drive and a screenshot from a video meeting, and each conversion may change the format, lose originality or cause problems with signatures.

Three Key Actions

  • Establish an approved ICT list: the IT department reviews and announces the tools that may be used and updates the list regularly, to prevent employees from using unreviewed cloud services on their own.
  • Pre-agreement before remote inspections: confirm in writing in advance with external inspecting parties (clients, regulators) the ICT solutions to be used jointly, to reduce the data integrity risk of data sharing.
  • Digitalize paper systems: companies that still use a paper-based PQS should give priority to an electronic signature (e-Signature) solution, to ensure document control under remote work is equally rigorous.

CDMO Practical Considerations

For a CDMO that is pursuing Type 1 electronic batch records (eBR) or Type 2 OCR handwriting recognition, this section directly supports the GMP compliance rationale for digital transformation. Once remote work becomes the norm, client auditors (QP, RA personnel) may review your batch records in different time zones using different devices. ICT standardization is therefore not only an IT issue but also a quality issue.

Q3-5: How can companies ensure optimal performance of business continuity plans during a pandemic?

Background

As an immediate response to the pandemic crisis, business continuity plans (BCPs) were activated. For many companies, the level of disruption created by the pandemic had not been envisioned in their existing BCPs. For example, few BCPs for companies with multiple manufacturing and warehousing locations envisioned a set of circumstances where all locations would be significantly impacted at the same time or one where the majority of personnel would need to work remotely.

Prior to the pandemic, the development of detailed and executable plans in BCPs varied greatly among companies. In some cases, very robust BCPs were in place and, in other cases, their scope and detail were very limited. One interesting point related to the designs of prepandemic BCPs is that their structures differed greatly. Some were holistic and well-integrated across functions; many were not, with each function having its own standalone BCP.

Also, BCPs impacted whether the activities of the affected corporate organizations were disrupted or if they were able to implement the already formalized actions. It identified the need to conduct mock-up actions prior to the actual challenge in a defined frequency, providing the opportunity to test the effectiveness of the arrangements in place, and to confirm that they remain robust and fit for use.

Lessons Learned

Companies that had robust BCPs in place were better prepared to address the unexpected and unplanned situations. Companies that had not prepared had to build robust BCPs and test them in the field as the events were occurring, which is never the desired state as it may result in unanticipated consequences.

BCPs need to be holistic and, while different functions may have their own BCPs, they all need to fit together into one overarching BCP. BCPs must have built-in flexibility and adaptability, where appropriate, as well as a defined governance structure to oversee its implementation. The fact is that all remediation actions applied at every step, apart from ensuring the health and safety aspects, must sustain the respective GMP-compliance levels.

BCPs need to:

  • Be developed, accessible, and ready for use
  • Be detailed, reflective of current operations, and holistic
  • Be flexible and adaptable with a defined governance structure to oversee its implementation; it is not possible to plan for every event, but it is important to have a plan for how to adjust the plan based on unforeseen challenges
  • Take regulatory requirements into consideration to ensure the actions implemented continue to meet those requirements
  • Take into consideration that the location of the workforce may be disrupted and the computer systems, hardware, and support that will be needed to enable it
  • Include actions to be taken to keep employees safe in the event of a regional, national, or global outbreak of a pathogen (e.g., virus)
  • Include detailed actions to be taken related to sustainability of suppliers, service providers, and the supply chain
  • Be reviewed periodically and updated appropriately

One such reference document is standard ISO 22301:2019: Security and resilience — Business continuity management systems — Requirements.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Concept: Three Levels of BCP Deficiency

The pandemic exposed three types of problems with the industry's BCP (Business Continuity Plan):

  • Scenario gap: the scenario of "all global sites shutting down at the same time" had not been anticipated
  • Structural gap: each department had its own BCP, but they were not integrated and could not work together when a crisis arrived
  • Exercise gap: no regular mock-ups were conducted, so the BCP existed in name only, and the gaps were found only when it was actually needed

Analogy: The Point of a Fire Drill

A BCP is like a fire plan. When a real fire breaks out, you cannot open the plan for the first time and read it slowly. The purpose of regular fire drills (mock-ups) is to let everyone know where the escape routes are, where the fire extinguishers are and who is responsible for raising the alarm, so that in an emergency people react automatically instead of scrambling. A corporate BCP is the same: it has practical value only if it is exercised and updated regularly.

Eight Requirements of a Good BCP (PDA Recommendations)

  1. Established, accessible and ready to activate at any time
  2. Detailed, reflecting current operations and comprehensive in coverage
  3. Flexible and adaptable, with an oversight governance structure
  4. Compliant with regulatory requirements (ensuring GMP compliance is maintained during execution)
  5. Covers remote work scenarios and the required IT system support
  6. Includes employee safety protection measures (response to pathogen outbreaks)
  7. Covers suppliers, service providers and supply chain continuity
  8. Reviewed regularly and updated as appropriate

Reference standard: ISO 22301:2019 — Security and resilience — Business continuity management systems — Requirements

The Dual Requirement of GMP Compliance Alongside Response Measures

PDA particularly emphasizes that all response measures in a BCP, in addition to protecting employee health and safety, must also maintain the level of GMP compliance. This is a point that is often overlooked: crisis response cannot be a reason to waive GMP. Every emergency step must be marked in the BCP with its corresponding compliance considerations.

CDMO Practical Considerations

As a CDMO you serve multiple clients at the same time, and a BCP failure at any one site could affect the production plans of several clients. Recommendations: 1) include supplier qualification in the BCP, so that the list of alternative suppliers can still be activated under travel restrictions; 2) add a "BCP trigger clause" to client contract reviews, specifying notification obligations and contingency arrangements; 3) run a BCP mock exercise every year and keep the records as documents available for client audits and regulatory inspections.

Q4-1 | How will regulatory authorities pivot between on-site and remote inspections?
How do regulators switch between on-site and remote inspections?

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic necessitated a change in the world's working habits, and pharmaceutical industry regulators were no exception. To maintain regulatory oversight of both GMP and good clinical practice (GCP) activities, regulators worldwide adopted remote assessments, using video-conferencing software and devices to enable continuity of compliance oversight. Such technologies enabled remote inspections, evaluations, and assessments of certain activities and sites, even as the world was locked down with social distancing and travel restrictions.

To accommodate the changes made by regulators, manufacturing sites had to be prepared for desktop audits and/or remote interactive evaluations and remote audits, if requested. Tools (e.g., virtual glasses, translators) should be kept available should regulators wish to continue with these inspection methods in the future. Desktop audits (a high-level documentary review of policies and procedures) have been conducted prior to the COVID-19 outbreak; however, FDA has created new tools that can be used in combination with the desktop audits to better evaluate the sites and products in scope.

The FDA limiting unnecessary contact by conducting only prioritized domestic and foreign facility inspections and those that are deemed mission-critical.

When a planned inspection is not mission-critical, is not a prioritized domestic inspection, or is impacted by travel restrictions resulting from the public health emergency, the FDA will use other available methods and information to support regulatory decisions and the oversight of facilities, and it will supplement the inspection with other available tools. The FDA has developed a Guidance for Industry: Remote Interactive Evaluations of Drug Manufacturing and Bioresearch Monitoring Facilities During COVID-19 Public Health Emergency that describes the various remote interactive tools that the industry may request be used to conduct an evaluation. Within the guidance, any combination of these interactive tools can be used in a remote interactive evaluation. The FDA may ask to conduct a remote interactive evaluation prior to or following other types of regulatory oversight activities (e.g., an inspection or a request for records or other information).

Lessons Learned

International regulatory authorities adapted their inspection approaches to ensure regulatory oversight of GxP activities, especially for high-priority products. Due to restrictions to protect public health, regulatory authorities used digital technologies, such as video-conferencing software and devices, to enable continuity of compliance oversight. The International Coalition of Medicines Regulatory Authorities (ICMRA) established the COVID-19 Working Group: Remote GCP and GMP Regulatory Oversight Inspections to review the adaptation of both GCP and GMP inspections during the pandemic to evaluate remote approaches. The team was chaired by the United Kingdom's Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) and had representatives of global regulatory agencies from the FDA, EMA, Health Canada, Swissmedic, HPRA Ireland, AEMPS Spain, ANSM France, PEI Germany, MHLW/PMDA Japan, TGA Australia, ANVISA Brazil, HSA Singapore, WHO, and Saudi-FDA.

The reflection paper from this working group presents the various factors that were considered by the inspectorates to assess the need for inspection on a case-by-case basis and apply established quality risk management principles and tools in their decisions on their regulatory oversight approach. Factors considered by regulatory authorities during the pandemic have included:

  • Local or international restrictions that have been in place to deal with the public health emergency
  • The need to protect the health and safety of the inspector, inspectorate staff, and staff at the inspected facility, including reducing burden while dealing with the pandemic itself
  • The regulatory compliance history of the inspected; for example, considering if there have been repeat deficiencies identified or issues associated with information provided that could have potentially been misleading to an inspector
  • The scope and objectives (preapproval, routine, or for cause) of the inspection
  • The type of site (e.g., investigator, bioanalytical, sterile manufacturing) and intrinsic risks/suitability for remote assessment in order to fulfill the inspection objective
  • The complexity of the activities of the site in terms of how challenging it may be to observe and adequately assess these remotely and by considering factors that may indicate regulatory compliance-related risks associated with the site
  • How recently the site was inspected on site as well as the findings from that inspection
  • Changes at the site since the previous inspection
  • Activities delegated to contract research organizations (CROs) and service providers
  • Accessibility of electronic systems owned and managed by service providers, CROs, and sponsors and whether these are accessible remotely owing to confidentiality rules
  • Access to source documents (e.g., validation methods, process performance qualification data) while complying with applicable regulations and/or institutional policies
  • Where redaction of documents must be performed to safeguard privacy and confidentiality
  • The inherent risk associated with the activities conducted by the site, the types of products, and the need for the product (e.g., high unmet medical need)
  • Ability of inspectors and the inspected facility to conduct inspections remotely considering resources, technical proficiency, and suitability of the site
  • Whether the location of the site to be inspected made it more efficient or practical to use the remote approach to enable regulatory compliance verification

If the risk assessment determined that the use of the remote oversight approaches was not suitable for regulatory compliance activities, two approaches were typically taken. Either the inspection or regulatory compliance activity was postponed until the risk assessment changed or the pandemic conditions ensured that an on-site approach was suitable; or a hybrid approach was adopted. The primary factor that altered the initial risk assessment was the change in national or local rules to manage the public-health emergency, which typically resulted in the postponement of the inspection until the rules were changed.

The inspectorates have thus prioritized mission-critical domestic inspections, which might generally include preapproval, prelicense, surveillance, and for-cause inspections. The relevant agency will pre-announce such prioritized domestic inspections to the company to be inspected to help ensure the safety of the investigator and the firm's employees. In-person foreign inspections would be placed on hold as long as it was necessary and mandated by local pandemic restrictions.

Based on the experiences of the authorities in 2020–2021, the ICMRA COVID-19 Working Group stated that remote inspections were an "enabling tool" that allowed the maintenance of "at least a minimal regulatory oversight during the pandemic, it is not the view of the group that remote inspections would fully replace an on-site inspection programme." They explained that is because of limitations with the current technologies that hampered remote assessment of compliance. Using the remote approach to supplement typical in-person visits could be beneficial in post-pandemic times, but the extent of its use in situations outside of a public-health emergency would need to be evaluated.

Remote inspection tools have been adopted by several national regulatory authorities (e.g., MHRA, TGA Australia, EMA, Russia's Roszdravnadzor, ANVISA Brazil), though other regulatory agencies may not align with them. Companies should communicate in advance with the relevant national regulatory authority (NRA) about recertification inspections to learn if desktop audits and/or remote interactive evaluations will be acceptable. Until on-site inspections resume, manufacturing sites should discuss the determination of certification extensions with both local and national regulators if desktop audits and remote inspections are not feasible.

Manufacturers may also want to consider using the Mutual Reliance regulatory tool, where one authority can unilaterally rely on another authority's inspection reports, as promoted by the Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S). The WHO has been a major supporter of regulatory reliance and defines it as:

…the act whereby a NRA in one jurisdiction may take into account and give significant weight to assessments performed by another authority or trusted institution, or to any other authoritative information in reaching its own decision. The relying authority remains independent, responsible, and accountable regarding the decisions taken, even when it relies on the decisions and information of others.

As part of trade agreements, Mutual Recognition Agreements (MRA) between the FDA and foreign NRAs allow drug inspectors to rely upon information from drug inspections conducted within each other's borders. Under the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Safety and Innovation Act (FDASIA), enacted in 2012, the FDA has the authority to enter into agreements to recognize drug inspections conducted by foreign regulatory authorities if the FDA determines those authorities are capable of conducting inspections that meet U.S. requirements. This concept has been used, and firms have received extensions of recognition based on these principles.

When an MRA or Mutual Reliance tool is employed, sites should propose to the NRA the use of the agreements to continue supply. Since the COVID-19 outbreak, MRAs have been used by several regulatory agencies that have resulted in waiving of audits.

Commentary and Practical Application

Core Concept: Three Inspection Models

Three main inspection models emerged during the pandemic. A side-by-side comparison:

  • Desktop Audit: a documents-only review of policies, SOPs and records. It existed before the pandemic and is the lightest-weight model.
  • Remote Interactive Evaluation (RIE): a new FDA tool that combines video conferencing with on-site staff guiding a camera through the facility; it is an alternative to a "light" on-site inspection.
  • On-site Inspection: the investigator is physically present. It remains the regulatory gold standard, and remote models cannot fully replace it.

Analogy: an inspection is like a home handover check

Imagine you are inspecting a house before taking it over:

  • Desktop audit = looking only at the building drawings and construction reports
  • Remote interactive evaluation = the agent walks you through the house by live video on a phone
  • On-site inspection = you go in person, knock on the walls, flick the light switches and smell the air

The regulators' position is clear: a live video feed helps, but you still cannot see the pipes inside the walls.

ICMRA Risk Assessment Factors for Remote Inspection (14 items)

The ICMRA working group compiled 14 decision factors, which fall into four dimensions:

  • External constraints: local lockdown rules, travel bans
  • Manufacturer's compliance history: past findings, any recurring problems
  • Purpose and scope of the inspection: preapproval vs. routine vs. for-cause
  • Technical feasibility: site type (sterile manufacturing is especially hard to evaluate remotely), remote access to electronic systems, document confidentiality requirements

Sterile filling sites (sterile manufacturing) require a high degree of process visibility, which makes them one of the most difficult types of site to inspect remotely.

Key point: remote inspection is a "supplementary tool", not a substitute

ICMRA states clearly that remote inspection is an "enabling tool" that maintained a minimum level of regulatory continuity during the pandemic, but it does not mean that it can replace the on-site inspection program.

Extending the use of remote models in non-emergency situations after the pandemic requires further evaluation. This means on-site inspection capability remains the core, and remote capability is an added competitive advantage.

Mutual Recognition Agreements (MRA) and Mutual Reliance

Mutual Recognition Agreement (MRA): regulators in two countries formally agree to recognize each other's inspection reports. For example, the MRA between FDA and the EU NRAs allows FDA to accept the inspection results of EMA member states as a basis for the US market.

Mutual Reliance: promoted by PIC/S, it is more flexible than an MRA: one NRA can decide unilaterally to rely on another NRA's inspection report, with no bilateral agreement needed. WHO's "Good Reliance Practices" (2021) is an important guidance document on this principle.

During the pandemic, several regulators invoked MRAs to waive audits and keep the supply chain running.

Practical Considerations for CDMOs

As a CDMO serving several markets at once, you need to build the following capabilities:

  • Remote inspection infrastructure: have AR glasses, translation services and high-quality cameras ready so that a remote tour can be launched at any time
  • Desktop audit document package: prepare standard packages in advance (policy summary, site master file, recent inspection history) to shorten response time
  • NRA communication strategy: proactively confirm with each NRA (FDA/EMA/TFDA) whether remote evaluation is acceptable as an alternative to a re-certification inspection
  • MRA eligibility check: confirm whether the markets covered by your key clients' products have an MRA you can invoke, as leverage when negotiating inspection waivers

Remote inspection capability has been upgraded from "emergency response" to a "permanent competitive capability"; sites that have it are at an advantage when clients evaluate and select a manufacturing site.

Q4-2 | How did global regulators collaborate during the pandemic?
How did global regulators collaborate during the pandemic?

Background

An unprecedented global pandemic of the twenty-first century required international regulatory agencies to take unprecedented collaborative actions. These collaborations played a momentous role in bringing the necessary countermeasures to the global population.

The ICMRA is an international executive-level coalition of key regulators from almost every region in the world. In 2021, it comprised 30 separate NRAs—including those of the United States, European Union, Canada, Australia, Japan, Singapore, and Brazil to name a few—with the WHO as an observer. The ICMRA provides a global strategic focus for medicine regulators and offers strategic leadership on shared regulatory issues and challenges. Priorities include a coordinated response to crisis situations.

During the pandemic, ICMRA played a key role and acted as a forum to support strategic coordination and to promote international cooperation among global medicine regulatory authorities. The aim of these activities was to expedite and streamline the development, authorization, and availability of COVID-19 treatments and vaccines worldwide. ICMRA members also worked towards increasing the efficiency and effectiveness of regulatory processes and decision-making.

During this period, other groups were also actively bringing together both industry and regulators such as the Regulatory Advisory Group (RAG) established by COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access (COVAX) and co-led by the WHO and the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI).

Lessons Learned

The COVID-19 pandemic emphasized the importance of regulatory cooperation and information-sharing, which are essential elements of mutual recognition and mutual reliance. In early 2020, ICMRA emphasized the relevance of reliance in streamlining responses and facilitating efforts to multiple regulatory activities during public-health emergencies.

The following were some of the industry topics raised to regulators at the ICMRA workshop on Enabling Manufacturing Capacity in the COVID-19 Pandemic in 2021:

  • Expanding reliance practices to include on-site inspection alternatives (e.g., virtual inspections)
  • Adopting reliance practices for NRA-issued queries and associated manufacturer responses for supplements/variations
  • Establishing collaborative review arrangements, similar to ACCESS and Project Orbis, for the review of supplements/variations for post-approval changes (PAC) of COVID-19 therapeutics and vaccines, as well as for non-COVID-19 products impacted by the pandemic
  • Reducing the need for duplicative import and release testing

As many vaccines and therapeutics were rolled out beginning in early 2021, ICMRA further espoused the reliance principles among global regulators for efficient quality and manufacturing lifecycle management of these products. This included support for establishing a collective pharmaceutical quality knowledge management capability that could have the following benefits:

  • Transitioning to harmonized, structured, and standardized electronic formats across regulatory authorities
  • Sharing secure information about pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities, which can be contributed to, and accessed by, multiple participating regulators
  • Developing a framework that might, in time, support full harmonization of data elements submitted in the quality modules of the common technical document (CTD). This could pave the way for sponsors to make simultaneous submissions within a marketing authorization application to all associated NRAs and provide improved capabilities for both industry and regulators in the management of post-approval changes (PACs)
  • Enabling more extensive mutual reliance among regulators by working to harmonize specific data expectations for sponsors and standards for review among regulators, so that regulators can be assured of the comparability of the assessments and related determinations of other regulatory authorities on whom they intend to rely

Beyond ICMRA, several regional collaborations among the regulators were also seen, such as in the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) region. Several global organizations, such as the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) and International Medical Device Regulators Forum also aided global collaboration and harmonization. There was also unprecedented regulator engagement with manufacturers developing pandemic vaccines and therapeutics from the beginning of the pandemic, as well as support offered in terms of regulatory expectations and abridged approvals.

Commentary and Practical Application

Global Regulatory Collaboration Network

A multi-layered regulatory collaboration structure took shape during the pandemic:

  • ICMRA (International Coalition of Medicines Regulatory Authorities): a strategic coordination platform at the level of agency heads, with 30 NRA members and WHO as observer. It is the top-level "regulatory G30".
  • COVAX RAG (COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access Regulatory Advisory Group): co-led by WHO and CEPI, focused on coordinating rapid review of COVID-19 vaccines.
  • ICH (International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use): drives standardization of technical guidelines so that the review standards of different NRAs are comparable.
  • APEC regional cooperation: promotes regulatory convergence in the Asia-Pacific region, which is especially relevant for Taiwanese CDMOs (TFDA is an APEC member).

ICMRA 2021 Workshop: Industry's Five Requests to Regulators

This workshop directly revealed the "negotiation list" between industry and regulators, which is especially important for CDMOs:

  • Extend the reliance principle to alternatives to inspection (virtual inspection)
  • Apply the reliance principle also to NRA questions on supplements/variations and the manufacturer's responses: if country A has already received an answer, country B can refer to it
  • Build a collaborative review mechanism for PACs (post-approval changes), modeled on the ACCESS Consortium and Project Orbis (multi-country concurrent review)
  • Reduce the need for repeated import/export release testing

PQKMS: Pharmaceutical Quality Knowledge Management System

Pharmaceutical Quality Knowledge Management System (PQKMS) is a long-term vision put forward by ICMRA: a platform for manufacturing site information shared across regulators, with the ultimate goal of standardizing the data in the CTD quality module so that manufacturers can make simultaneous submissions to multiple NRAs.

Long-term significance for CDMOs: if PQKMS matures, the future PAC submission process will be greatly simplified, with one data package sent to FDA, EMA and TFDA at the same time instead of three separately customized submissions.

Analogy: Project Orbis is like a national entrance exam

Project Orbis is a multi-country concurrent review platform led by FDA; participants currently include Canada, Australia, the UK, Switzerland, Singapore and others.

Traditional approach: the company submits to FDA first, waits for FDA approval, then submits to EMA, then TFDA, queuing at every gate.

Project Orbis: like a national entrance exam, all regulators receive the exam paper at the same time, review at the same time, and after discussion each gives its own grade. Efficiency improves greatly, with far-reaching impact for products with a global market.

The pandemic accelerated the extension of this model to PAC (post-approval change) submissions.

Additional Note on the ACCESS Consortium

ACCESS (Australia, Canada, Singapore, Switzerland Consortium): a joint review platform of four medium-sized NRAs that reduces each regulator's review workload through work-sharing. It is a regional version of the Project Orbis model.

The emergence of these multilateral review platforms shows that regulatory collaboration has moved from "crisis response" to "systematic restructuring"; the pandemic merely accelerated the trend.

Strategic Implications for CDMOs

Deeper global regulatory collaboration brings the following opportunities and requirements for multi-market CDMOs:

  • Regulatory intelligence capability: continuously track the latest policies of ICMRA, ICH and Project Orbis, because a decision on one platform affects several markets
  • Preparation for simultaneous submissions: design the CTD quality module as "one data package, compatible with multiple markets" to reduce customization cost
  • PAC strategy optimization: understand which changes can clear quickly through collaborative review and which still need country-by-country submission
  • TFDA positioning: Taiwan's TFDA is not currently a member of Project Orbis, but under the APEC cooperation framework its information sharing with other Asia-Pacific NRAs is deepening, so you need to keep track of TFDA's international developments
Q4-3 | How do you manage the reporting of deviations and other changes to the regulatory agencies during a pandemic?
How should deviations and changes be reported to regulators during a pandemic?

Background

As a result of the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, there were many unexpected emergency situations for which many firms were not prepared. The ensuing staff and material shortages significantly impacted manufacturing operations.

After operations resumed, performing site-wide and organization-wide risk assessments with a cross-functional team helped many firms identify, group, and prioritize investigations. Deviations were resolved and reported based on their criticality and priority in terms of public health and manufacturing continuity. The existing knowledge on processes, products, quality by design, established conditions, quality metrics trends, and annual report information helped firms avoid reporting specific deviations and shortened the length of time needed for deviation investigations. The types of deviations companies encountered included a broad range of situations and areas impacted (e.g., suppliers, warehousing, manufacturing, quality, distribution).

Companies revised deviation procedures to adapt to the new normal of the pandemic in regard to criticality and priority in terms of public health and manufacturing continuity, reducing the burden on the internal quality function as well as on regulatory authorities. For the deviations or events identified as low risk, deviation reporting procedures and categories were revised to acknowledge (or log) and proceed or to document and proceed. These deviations were captured within the PQS (Pharmaceutical Quality System). Some companies regularized specific events for short periods of time either through temporary change controls or by amending the procedures.

Deviations that were identified as moderate risk were in many cases reported as expected events and investigated with prior knowledge, requiring minimal new investigation activities. In general, if reporting was required, these deviations were reported in annual reports. Deviations that were identified as high risk were evaluated for their severity using existing knowledge where possible. If reporting was required, these deviations were generally reported as post-approval supplements.

To assist the industry, most regulatory agencies released guidances and Q&A documents or posted information on their websites related to deviation management and deviation-and-change reporting relief during the pandemic. These actions took time to implement, which created some challenges, especially during the initial phases of the pandemic.

Regulatory authorities eased up on PAC (post-approval change) reporting about suppliers and manufacturing or control sites not listed in the existing regulatory filings, allowing for rapid implementation when it was critical to prevent or mitigate drug product shortages. For example, the EMA provided guidance under the Exceptional Change Management Process (ECMP) to cover regulatory strategies during pandemics to continue supply in the market. "The ECMP will permit the swift implementation of changes to suppliers and/or manufacturing/control sites necessary to reduce the risks of shortages under certain conditions intended to ensure the quality of the medicinal product while deferring the full assessment of the variation."

Lessons Learned

Responding to a pandemic quickly and providing the flexibility needed in regard to deviation management and deviation and change reporting is critical for ensuring product supply. Several elements that should be considered are:

1. Inclusion of the regulatory and quality functions in the BCPs
The quality and regulatory functions should be included in the BCPs (Business Continuity Plans) with a focus on the types of adjustments in processes related to deviations, as well as to deviation and change reporting, based on expected impacts caused by a pandemic. For many manufacturers, these areas were not a significant focus in past BCPs and, as such, plans were not readily available regarding the types of changes needed.

2. Rapid issuance or activation by regulatory authorities of temporary guidance and Q&A documents
This includes specific information on changes in the regulatory reporting and handling of deviations. Most major regulatory authorities released temporary guidances and Q&A documents but, as was industry, they were not prepared for the speed at which changes occurred and industry's urgent need for direction from the regulatory authorities. Of particular importance is the reporting of changes in suppliers and manufacturing control sites not listed in the regulatory filings, as this can have a significant impact on product availability.

3. Development of process to allow manufacturers to meet with authorities at an early stage when issues arise to understand regulatory expectations and propose innovative strategies for PAC submission and approvals
While it is important for regulatory authorities to take broad actions, such as the issuance of temporary guidances, a process also needs to be put in place to allow the marketing authorization holder (MAH) to closely engage with those authorities early on about specific issues so they understand regulatory expectations and can propose innovative strategies for PAC submission and approval. This could involve such strategies as lower reporting categories, use of flexible submission strategies (e.g., ECMPs), reduced submission data requirements based on a risk assessment evaluation, and expedited assessments. Manufacturing operations within the pharmaceutical industry are diverse due to the diverse nature of the products; as such, situations will emerge during a pandemic that are specific and unique to any given product and will require unique solutions that can only be resolved on a case-by-case basis.

4. Regulatory authority acceptance of temporary changes in quality controls
MAHs facing difficulties performing the quality controls recorded in the regulatory filings, for example, due to a significant increase of manufacturing capacity to meet the demands of patients or other circumstances related to a pandemic, should contact the responsible regulatory authorities to seek temporary relief. They should be prepared to present an adapted control scheme, founded on a risk-based approach, with the goal of reaching agreement with the regulatory authority on the proposed strategy and the needed documentation to implement it.

5. Regulatory authority acceptance of bundling changes for regulatory filings
To reduce the resources needed to prepare, review, and approve individual submissions, regulatory authorities should allow for and encourage MAHs to bundle changes, with the needed supporting rationale and data, to allow them to be implemented more rapidly.

6. Regulatory authorities granting relief from specific routine reporting requirements
MAHs, for justified reasons relating to a pandemic, may be unable to continue the expected full reporting requirements for specific items (e.g., full pharmacovigilance reporting, report of temporary disruptions/closures). In cases where the regulatory agency has not published relief through issuance of a temporary guidance or other document, the MAH should contact the regulatory authorities to explain the proposed temporary changes in reporting and request relief based on circumstances related to the pandemic. Relief may be in the actual reporting requirement or the method of reporting.

7. Regulatory authorities granting temporary acceptance status of specific anticipated deviations
To assist the industry, regulatory agencies should consider granting acceptance status of specific types of anticipated deviations allowing companies to focus their resources on their manufacturing activities. A few examples are the drug supply chain security requirements waiver provided by FDA, extension of shelf life, or usage of expired product in clinical trials.

8. Comprehensive risk assessment processes to determine deviation criticality and reporting
Manufacturers should plan on carrying out a comprehensive risk assessment to evaluate the deviations being generated, allowing them to be divided into low-, medium-, and high-risk categories with decisions on reporting being made for medium- and high-risk deviations only.

  • Low risk: Could be handled as acknowledged (or log) and proceed, or document and proceed (e.g., missing process checks, second-individual in-person checking, annual reports filing, missing calibrations if equipment is found compliant in the subsequent schedule).
  • Medium risk: May be grouped and addressed under a single deviation and, where required, reported to the respective regulatory authority as a grouped submission (e.g., FDA has provided clearance to, and faster approval time for, MAHs' filing for alternate lab and manufacturing sites for several applications grouped together).
  • High/Major risk: Handled case by case (e.g., change in source for an API, change in manufacturing site for vaccine or sterile-product manufacturing).

Commentary and Practical Application

EMA Exceptional Change Management Process (ECMP)

The Exceptional Change Management Process (ECMP) is a special reporting pathway introduced by EMA during the pandemic. It allows manufacturers in an emergency to "implement first, document afterwards": securing supply comes first, and full assessment is deferred.

Conditions for applying ECMP:

  • The change concerns a supplier or manufacturing/control site that is not listed in the existing regulatory documentation
  • The purpose is to prevent or reduce the risk of a medicine shortage
  • Product quality must be maintained throughout the process

This is the mechanism closest to an "emergency change fast lane" within the EMA framework, and it matters greatly for CDMO clients facing supply chain disruption.

Analogy: deviation management is like triage in a hospital emergency room

Triage determines the order of treatment according to the severity of the patient's condition:

  • Red (immediate) = high-risk deviation: assess immediately and report case by case
  • Yellow (delayed) = medium-risk deviation: investigate once stable, report in the annual report
  • Green (self-manage) = low-risk deviation: documenting it is enough, and it does not consume investigation resources

The pandemic's contribution was to force industry to build this triage framework rather than treating every deviation as a red emergency, which in fact made the overall quality system more efficient.

Core Themes of the Eight Lessons

The eight lessons in Q4-3 can be summarized into three core messages:

  • Proactive communication first (items 3, 4, 6): do not wait for NRAs to issue guidance; MAHs should reach out proactively, propose strategies and request waivers. Regulators also need manufacturers to tell them "what is really happening on the ground".
  • Inadequate system preparedness (items 1, 2): failing to include the quality/regulatory functions in the BCP was a widespread mistake. When the next emergency occurs, regulators' guidance will not arrive immediately, so manufacturers need contingency plans of their own.
  • Risk stratification is key (item 8): a one-size-fits-all approach to deviations is not workable in an emergency; a tiered framework must be built in advance so that decisions can be made quickly under pressure.

"Bundling Changes"

The bundling strategy mentioned in lesson 5 is highly practical for CDMOs with multiple sites and products:

Traditional approach: each change is submitted as its own application and regulators review them one by one, which consumes a lot of resources.

Bundled submission: several related changes (for example, replacing several alternative suppliers at once) are combined into one submission with common supporting rationale, speeding up the overall review.

During the pandemic FDA offered expedited review of grouped submissions for alternate laboratories and manufacturing sites; this is a reporting strategy with precedent that can continue to be used.

CDMO Practical Readiness Checklist for Deviation Management

  • Write the three-tier deviation classification framework (low/medium/high) formally into the SOP, and define the reporting pathway and timeline for each tier
  • Add a "regulatory reporting contingency plan" chapter to the BCP, covering: which deviations may have reporting deferred, who is responsible for communicating with NRAs, and how to activate ECMP or an equivalent procedure
  • For each main market served (FDA/EMA/TFDA), confirm the emergency contact point and the fast reporting process
  • Prepare a "grouped submission template" in advance so that several related changes can be consolidated quickly in an emergency
  • Run a regulatory contingency tabletop exercise at least once a year, simulating the deviation classification and reporting decisions when a supplier suddenly stops supply

Q4-4 — How can the industry self-ease the burden of handling deviations? (How can industry self-ease the burden of handling deviations?)

Background

As a result of the COVID-19 pandemic, some parts of the industry experienced an increase in deviations related to the quickly changing environment such as reduced on-site staff presence, supplier delays, and supply chain interruptions. The resulting volume of deviations, many of which had no impact on product quality, required companies to act and develop new processes for the evaluation, completion, and reporting of deviations with a focus on working to maintain product supply. In many cases, new processes were developed on how to approach and how to categorize deviations based on their impact to product quality.

Lessons Learned

Looking ahead, industry should have plans in place (BCPs — Business Continuity Plans) to quickly assess the anticipated impacts of a pandemic, especially regarding its impact on the volume and type of deviations expected, how deviations are handled, and how they are reported.

Some actions to consider that allow self-easement of deviation handling are:

  • If a deviation is already open and the manufacturer can simply expand the scope of deviation to cover many similar changes in one deviation.
  • If minor deviations can be documented by the temporary change control to continue manufacturing operation.
  • If procedures that govern investigations covering discrepancies, deviations, and nonconformances are suitable during this public-health emergency or if they need to be updated.
  • If delayed and reduced testing can be implemented with sufficient data available (e.g., microbiological testing of environmental monitoring plates from low-criticality clean areas or routine stability testing supporting a marketed drug with a robust history of minimal degradation).
  • If an on-site audit of a new or existing supplier is not possible due to pandemic-related travel restrictions, then using any measures that can be implemented (e.g., remote supplier audit, reassessment, or revision of the quality agreement so the qualification program can be updated).
  • If the use of equipment changed or was reconfigured and it was not qualified prior to use, checking the possibility of retrospective evaluation of equipment performance, if change control should be considered.
  • If servicing equipment requires a technician with special expertise and that individual is unable to be on site, are there alternative means to ensure that the equipment is suitable for use (e.g., when service that is normally performed on site was conducted with remote assistance).
  • If a delay is acceptable in preventive maintenance or calibration activities for facilities or equipment impacting the function (e.g., adequacy for use) of facilities or equipment.

Commentary

Core Concept: What Is "Self-Easement"?

Self-easement does not mean giving up GMP requirements; it means handling low-risk deviations more efficiently while keeping product quality protected. During the pandemic the number of deviations surged, and if every one had followed the normal procedure, quality assurance staff would have been completely paralyzed.

Core logic: handle deviations by category according to their impact on product quality, rather than spending the same resources on every one.

Analogy: Triage in a Hospital Emergency Room

Just as an emergency room does not put a fracture patient and a minor graze in the same queue, deviation management also needs "triage": high-risk deviations (affecting sterility, content or stability) are handled first, while low-risk deviations (EM results from low-criticality cleanrooms, delayed calibration of non-critical equipment) follow a simplified process.

Eight Self-Easement Measures: A CDMO Perspective

  • Scope Expansion: consolidate multiple deviations of the same type into one to reduce duplicate investigations. Example: delays in several batches of raw material from the same supplier are combined into a single deviation record.
  • Temporary Change Control (TCC): for minor operational adjustments, use a TCC instead of the full deviation process, moving quickly under a controlled record.
  • Procedure applicability assessment: is the existing deviation investigation SOP suitable for an emergency situation? If necessary, update the procedure itself first.
  • Delayed / reduced testing: for low-criticality items supported by historical data (for example EM in low-risk areas, stability of mature products), the frequency may be reduced where scientifically justified.
  • Remote supplier audits: when on-site visits are not possible, use a paper-based review or a video audit, and update the Quality Agreement to reflect the arrangement.
  • Retrospective Evaluation of equipment: for equipment that has been used without prior qualification, evaluate retrospectively whether its performance meets requirements.
  • Remote technical support: when service engineers cannot be on site, allow a remote assistance mode and keep complete records.
  • Delayed preventive maintenance / calibration: supported by a risk assessment, allow PM / calibration of non-critical facilities to be postponed.

Key Points for CDMOs

In an emergency, a CDMO often manages the quality systems of dozens of clients at the same time. The BCP (business continuity plan) should define "emergency deviation classification criteria" in advance, specifying which deviations may follow a simplified path and which must keep a full investigation. These criteria need the clients' agreement and should be written into the Quality Agreement; otherwise they may cause disputes during an audit.

Q4-5 — How do you make sure that commitments are met, reported, and documented with deviations? (How do you make sure commitments are still fulfilled, reported and documented when deviations occur?)

Background

Sites that are commercially approved for the release of drug products typically require a periodic recertification to continue release of supply in some markets (as regulated by their NRA). During the pandemic, there have been instances where markets required an on-site inspection to recertify the site. Some NRAs conduct audits using a risk-based approach while others may schedule audits.

COVID-19 created profound disruption across the pharmaceutical industry in general, but it also impacted two specific areas of commitments from a regulatory and drug license perspective:

  1. Regulatory Commitment — a specific description of how a company intends to manufacture and control the drug product that is defined in the dossier and is subject to regulatory action if changed after approval.
  2. Post-Approval Commitments (PACs) — covers the elements of any agreement made with the regulatory authority during the drug review to fulfill after approval. For example, the FDA has a guidance for PACs that allows tracking of a new medicine's safety, efficacy, or optimal use. Similarly, commitments can be agreed upon from a chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) perspective as well as between the applicant and the regulatory authority during drug registration.

According to ICH Quality Guideline Q12, legally binding information considered necessary to assure product quality can be considered an established condition (EC). Therefore, any change to an EC requires a submission to the regulatory authority, similar to EMA variations. While the examples here are specific to EMA and MHRA, an applicant can reach out to the relevant NRA to request relief in registering changes if any deviation is observed for ECs based on the specific circumstances.

According to the European Medicines Regulatory Network COVID-19 Business Continuity Plan, variation can be delayed if it is in the interest of the applicant. Furthermore, it is exceptionally agreed for DCP/MRP/RUP, renewal and type II variation procedures to allow a "freezing" (holding the timetable at the same procedure Day and restarting it as soon as the response is received) or "rolling back" (bring the procedure back to validation phase or back to clock stop) of the procedure timetable due to unexpected and COVID-19-related capacity issues.

All manufacturers are obligated to release batches with the Qualified Person (QP) declaration whenever major CMC changes are registered for a license, in keeping with EMA guidance EMA/196292/2014. During COVID-19, certain exemptions were granted:

  • Exceptional circumstances when an on-site audit is not practical (e.g., atypical actives) are out of the scope of the declaration template.
  • An off-site, remote, or "paper-based" audit may be justifiable on a benefit-risk basis, but only considered on a case-by-case basis.
  • In these cases, a suitable quality system is expected to be applied. The QP is expected to justify the controls in place on a scientific basis and record a risk assessment on a product-specific basis.

The MHRA allowed applicants the following flexibility:

  • Suspending the 30-day limit for replies to questions on Type 1B variations.
  • Waiving the requirement for leaflet mock-ups to be submitted to support variations; only text versions will be accepted.
  • Extending the permitted implementation period for label/leaflet changes following a variation from 6 months to 9 months.

During the COVID-19 public-health emergency, the FDA has been willing to consider requests from applicants to submit certain changes using a lower reporting category based on risk-mitigation information for marketing applications related to products in shortage or intended to diagnose, treat, mitigate, cure, or prevent pandemic.

Lessons Learned

The importance of quick action by the regulators and industry to identify areas where flexibility is needed in regard to how regulatory commitments are met, reported, and documented cannot be stressed enough. While the actions taken significantly helped in working to minimize product disruption and minimize product shortages, the initial delay at the start of the pandemic created significant challenges. Developing processes to allow rapid implementation earlier, based on the knowledge gained during the COVID-19 pandemic, would be of significant benefit.

Commentary

ICH Q12 Established Conditions (EC)

EC is the core concept of ICH Q12: for all legally binding process and control information that affects product quality, any change must be submitted to the competent authority (similar to an EMA Variation).

If, during the pandemic, a site departed from an EC in response to an emergency (for example changing a raw material source or adjusting process parameters), even with good intentions it must still proactively contact the relevant NRA to request a waiver or reduced reporting requirements.

Analogy: The Commitment List Is Like a Prenuptial Agreement

Regulatory commitments are written into the marketing authorization like a contract in black and white: you must manufacture the drug in the way you committed to. A PAC is more like "documents to be supplied after the wedding": it was not completed at approval, but you promised the authority to submit it within a specified time. The pandemic disrupted the timelines for all of these commitments, so authorities in various countries began to allow "Freezing" (clock-stop) and "Rolling Back" procedures.

Key Points for CDMOs: QP Declaration and Quality Responsibility

For CDMOs serving the European market, the QP (Qualified Person) declaration is a legal requirement for every batch release. During the pandemic EMA allowed "paper-based audits" to replace on-site audits, but the QP must:

  • Provide scientific justification explaining why the alternative control measures are reasonable.
  • Perform a risk assessment for each specific product and keep the records.
  • Ensure that these alternative measures do not compromise the suitability of the active substance or the safety and efficacy of the medicinal product.

This means the CDMO's QP must be able to make an independent risk judgment rather than simply relying on the client's statements.

Lesson: The Importance of Early Action

PDA's core lesson is that the slow response in the early days of the pandemic caused the greatest supply interruptions. Had regulators and industry set up emergency flexibility mechanisms earlier, many drug shortages could have been avoided. Future BCPs should include in advance a standard procedure for "which regulatory flexibilities can be requested under which conditions", rather than negotiating ad hoc after the crisis breaks out.

Q4-6 — How do you prioritize submissions of changes in process during the pandemic? (How should submissions of process changes be prioritized during the pandemic?)

Background

During the pandemic, the pharmaceutical industry was affected at many levels. MAHs (Marketing Authorization Holders) for products against the SARS-CoV-2 virus (therapeutics, vaccines) had to prioritize submissions against unprecedented, shortened timelines, while those companies involved in non-COVID-19 products were met with a depleted workforce at the production sites, raw material shortages, and logistics delays due to cross-border movement restrictions. They also faced potential delays from regulators due to the shift in focus as a result of the pandemic.

Lessons Learned

For MAHs dealing with pandemic-related products, timing will be a highly critical factor in getting the different PACs through to submission and approved by the authorities in various jurisdictions. During the COVID-19 pandemic, in many cases vaccine MAHs adopted the grouping or bundling of product variations to reduce the number of submission packages. That, in turn, reduced the burden on regulators for individual assessments, aided timely approval, and ensured the maintenance of critical supply to the population.

For MAHs of non-pandemic products, prioritizing changes based on the criticality of the change to ensure that an up-to-date and safe product can be placed on the shelves is recommended. Risk is best managed by coupling it with a scientific understanding of the process and product design for optimal product performance (e.g., as highlighted in ICH Q12, Chapter 2). Thus, risk-based assessments such as SISPQ (safety, integrity, strength, purity, quality) and SQuIPP (safety, quality, integrity, purity, potency) should be undertaken, and agreed-upon criteria should be documented in impact assessment tools that can be archived for inspections and Response-to-Questions submissions.

For manufacturers with worldwide product supply and submission requirements, aligning with WHO guidelines on procedures and data requirements for changes and variations is recommended. Changes with no SISPQ/SQuIPP impact can be documented in the company's PQS (Pharmaceutical Quality System). Minor changes may be implemented by the MAH without prior authority review for biotherapeutic products; however, a notification to the authority within a specified time is recommended.

Additional novel tools that can be used for quick submissions and approval are:

  • Matrix approaches to rationally reduce the number of lots required to support a manufacturing change for validation and stability (e.g., for vaccine combination products), which can lead to faster submission and approval timelines and less destruction of lots.
  • Stability data approach and structured approach from ICH Q12, Chapters 8 and 9.
  • Established conditions as part of ICH Q12 implementation.

Combining these approaches for submissions that can be approved quickly according to pre-agreed change management protocols (or comparability protocols for the FDA) can enable multiple changes to be assessed and approved at the same time. For both pandemic and non-pandemic product scenarios, the MAH will need to provide a clear prioritization of changes, based on supply impact and predictive and strategic planning.

A best practice is for the MAH to clearly engage with the authorities at an early stage to understand regulatory expectations and flexibility based on the specific circumstances the MAH is facing.

Commentary

SISPQ and SQuIPP Risk Assessment Frameworks

These two acronyms are standard frameworks used by industry to assess the potential impact of a process or change on the drug product:

  • SISPQ: Safety, Integrity, Strength, Purity, Quality
  • SQuIPP: Safety, Quality, Integrity, Purity, Potency

The assessment result determines the reporting pathway: no impact → internal PQS record; minor impact → notification; major impact → prior approval.

Analogy: Grouped Submissions Are Like Group Buying

The "Bundling" strategy adopted by vaccine MAHs during the pandemic is like merging orders that would have been placed separately into one purchase order: for the authority, reviewing one large submission package is far more efficient than reviewing 20 small ones. ICMRA data from the EU show that COVID-19 vaccine applicants successfully combined dozens of PACs into a few submission packages, greatly shortening review timelines.

Three Tools to Accelerate Submissions

  • Matrix Approach: for multi-presentation products such as combination vaccines, a statistical design reasonably reduces the number of batches that need to be validated, lowering material consumption while speeding up submission.
  • ICH Q12 stability data approach (Chapters 8 & 9): use established stability model predictions to reduce the amount of stability data that new batches must submit.
  • Established Conditions (EC) mechanism: agree in advance with the authority which process parameter changes can use a fast track (Change Management Protocol), avoiding negotiation from zero each time.

Key Points for CDMOs: Engage Authorities Early and Proactively

PDA specifically emphasizes that MAHs should contact the authority proactively before problems occur, to understand its expectations and room for flexibility. For a CDMO, this means:

  • Help clients set up an "Emergency Change Management Protocol" in advance and obtain the authority's prior agreement.
  • Maintain a list of regulatory flexibility measures for each main market (EU, US, Taiwan, Japan) and keep it current.
  • Maintain high transparency on CAPA closure rates and PAC tracking to avoid surprises during audits.

The CAPA closure rate is one of the core indicators in a GMP inspection. In an emergency, a CAPA backlog caused by a surge of deviations is a typical inspection observation.

WHO Multi-Market Simultaneous Submission Strategy

For CDMO clients who need to launch in several markets at the same time, WHO provides a unified change reporting framework (TRS No. 966, 993, 1011), which allows:

  • Changes with no SISPQ impact: only need to be recorded in the PQS, with no submission to the authority.
  • Minor changes (biologicals): may be implemented first, with notification to the authority within the specified time.
  • Moderate / major changes: may be bundled and submitted in a staggered way according to similarity or order of implementation, reducing the number of submissions.

Key Takeaways (English)

  • Self-easement of deviations requires a risk-based triage system: merge similar low-risk deviations, apply temporary change controls, and defer non-critical testing only with scientific justification.

Q 5-1 | Supply Constraints for Incoming Materials

Q 5-1: Why have there been supply constraints for incoming materials, such as single-use systems, container closures, resins and filters, excipients, and APIs?

Background

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the demand for consumables and raw materials necessary for the production of vaccines and in need across the globe put a major stress on supply chains. Manufacturers of single-use systems (SUS) and excipients were not prepared for fast turnaround times and large production; they were accustomed to consistent and repeatable orders and delivery. Lead times for consumables extended by up to 12 months, and lead times for sterile filters went from 2–3 months to 9–12 months.

The system as a whole was not ready for "emergency" supply chain management and, as demand increased, old methods became ineffective. Prior to the pandemic, the demand for incoming materials was increasing, especially plastic-based SUS and primary glass containers, such as vials and rubber components, a result of emerging applications in cell and gene therapies, monoclonal antibodies, and vaccines. Since the pandemic, local authorities have prioritized incoming materials for pandemic supply and established export bans on specific items. For example, the U.S. government used rated orders under the Defense Production Act to prioritize COVID-19 vaccine production, which primarily uses SUS. That led to a very limited allocation of SUS to other biologics development programs and commercial manufacturing. The same applies to all drug substances, excipients, starting materials, intermediates, and devices.

Lessons Learned

Drug manufacturers and suppliers should work together on pandemic preparedness to assess demand and capacity, plan for supply manufacturing expansion, and act quickly on implementation. End users should understand their forecast and communicate with suppliers. Suppliers should anticipate the market demand, evaluate against their existing capacity, and fill in the gaps in a timely fashion.

Drug manufacturers should conduct a holistic risk assessment that includes alternative solutions, aside from safety stock; for example, they should anticipate validation of multiple sourcing on strategic components to increase their chances of meeting pandemic demand and maintaining continuity of supply for essential treatments.

There are a few reasons why lead times of materials were so severely affected, starting with restrictions in transportation globally and the movement of specific goods from vendors, which affected delivery times of critical raw materials or consumables. In the months leading up to the pandemic, the supply chain in the medical industry was growing far more complex, with more third-party vendors competing for business with lower pricing and faster turnaround. This growing spiderweb of supply chains and the uncertainty of demand resulted in delays that cascaded throughout the network, including uncertainty of demand, inconsistency of supply, scarcity of material, delays in transportation, constraints in capacity, and shortage of labor.

The number of facilities that could meet manufacturing capabilities was limited, and the gaps in available workforce meant it took longer to hire and train qualified personnel. Several mitigations were recommended:

  • Establish a cross-functional task force including procurement, supply chain, technical, quality, regulatory, and operations — sponsored by corporate executives.
  • Build safety stocks (wherever possible) and provide alternative supply solutions; prioritize based on medical criticality and patients' usage volume.
  • Evaluate and qualify additional suppliers; generate an emergency use guideline to allow faster qualification turnaround.

For SUS specifically:

  • Categorize SUS using the product-family approach and evaluate component interchangeability; build a library of interchangeable components collaboratively with suppliers.
  • Investigate in-house SUS assembly and steam sterilization when supplier capacity is limited.
  • Work to standardize and potentially lengthen the shelf life post-gamma irradiation (currently typically 24 months).
  • Reuse of SUS is only acceptable when original technical and quality requirements are fully met.

Pharmaceutical companies must develop Business Continuity Plans (BCPs) to maintain uninterrupted supply during pandemics. BCPs provide a roadmap for senior management to mitigate numerous potential risks. Legacy BCPs were often limited to individual risks and did not address the scale of multi-node supply chain failures encountered during COVID-19.

Key BCP elements include:

  • Assess and increase safety stock levels; location of stock matters because transportation may also be impacted.
  • Ensure alternative suppliers are qualified for all materials — including excipients, not only APIs.
  • Consider cold-chain and controlled-room-temperature (CRT) warehousing capacity needs.
  • Locate finished product storage closer to intended patient populations to prevent import disruptions.
  • Address third-party controls, shipping lane alternatives, port closures, and customs disruptions.
  • Assess stability beyond current shelf-life limits to support pandemic-related extensions.
  • Plan for counterfeit and theft risks, which increase during pandemics.
  • Consider separate BCPs for routine supply and clinical-trial supply.

Commentary and Practical Links

Core Concept: The Supply Chain Amplification (Bullwhip) Effect

When the pandemic broke out, vaccine manufacturers increased their orders at the same time, and the demand signal was amplified rapidly. Lead times for SUS (single-use systems) stretched from 2–3 months to 9–12 months, and primary packaging components such as glass vials and rubber stoppers were also in severe shortage.

Root cause: manufacturers were long accustomed to steady orders and lacked the production flexibility of an "emergency mode"; once demand jumped, the whole supply network suffered domino-style delays.

Analogy: A Pile-Up on the Highway

A supply chain is like a multi-lane highway that normally flows smoothly in every lane. Once a jam occurs somewhere (a port closes, transport is restricted), the vehicles behind (downstream manufacturers) all queue up. COVID-19 was like accidents occurring in all lanes at once: not just one node, but the entire network came to a standstill.

Key point: The US Defense Production Act

Through this act the US government prioritized SUS for COVID-19 vaccine production, so that other biologics (monoclonal antibodies, cell therapies) could hardly obtain SUS. This is a typical case of government intervention in the supply chain, and it highlights that pharmaceutical manufacturers cannot rely on market mechanisms alone to secure materials.

The Six Pillars of a BCP (Business Continuity Plan)

  • Safety stock: stock materials in tiers by medical importance, and consider warehouse locations (because transport can also be interrupted).
  • Alternative suppliers: qualify alternative suppliers in advance, not only for APIs but even for excipients.
  • Cold-chain and controlled room temperature (CRT) storage: expand cold and frozen storage capacity.
  • Deploying product close to patients: store finished product in advance near patient population centers to reduce the risk of import delays.
  • Stability data extension: build up data in advance beyond the current shelf-life studies, as the scientific basis for emergency shelf-life extension requests.
  • Third-party control: ensure that agreements cover product segregation, release and rejection procedures, and include them in the audit program.

Specific Response Strategies for SUS

  • Product-family approach: group SUS components with similar functions and build an interchangeability database, greatly shortening qualification time.
  • In-house assembly and steam sterilization: when supplier capacity is insufficient, evaluate the feasibility of assembling in-house and performing steam sterilization.
  • Shelf life after gamma irradiation: the current 24-month shelf life is conservative; manufacturers and suppliers should work together to extend it and reduce waste.

TWi / CDMO Practical Link

As a fill-finish CDMO, TWi faced direct shortage pressure during COVID on single-use bags, sterile filters, glass vials and rubber stoppers. The current response directions are:

  • Dual sourcing of critical consumables: this has already been required to be included in the BCP.
  • Complete SUS supplier qualification in advance rather than waiting until material runs short.
  • Review safety stock levels regularly and manage them in tiers according to the medical importance of clients' products.

Q 5-2 | Cold-Chain Bottlenecks in Vaccine Supply

Q 5-2: What impact did cold-chain custody and manufacturing bottlenecks have on vaccine supply?

Background

Current supply-chain capabilities were assumed to be sufficient to effectively distribute the COVID-19 vaccines during the pandemic. This was not the case, however, as the current supply chain for vaccines is quite complex and was undergoing issues before the onset of the pandemic. As a result of globalization, supply chains have become increasingly complex.

According to Dr. Robert Handfield (Poole College of Management) and Dr. Daniel J. Finkenstadt (Naval Postgraduate School), there are several key challenges associated with vaccine supply chains: national security issues, personnel shortages, lack of coordination, shortage of supplies, limited capacity, vaccine damage, gaps for rural areas, and misinformation about vaccines.

Specifically, cold-chain products are very sensitive to movement and high-touch handling. Processes had to be validated to prove that the vaccine was still effective after being handled in dry-ice packages. A narrow window existed between getting the vaccine out of cold storage and into patients before it deteriorated; this was a big challenge and a crucial step for vaccination of the population. Cold-chain issues presented an even greater challenge in rural areas than in highly populated cities, where the routes were longer and access points were more difficult.

Vaccine inequality is more prevalent in lower-income countries that have a less-developed cold supply chain. Even in the United States, the capacity for temperature-monitored cold rooms could not handle the mass volume of vaccines that needed cold storage.

Lessons Learned

When redesigning or looking to improve the cold supply chain for events such as the COVID-19 pandemic, several key points to consider:

  • Design distribution around freezer requirements. Plan the distribution of vaccines en masse as soon as they arrive in the district. Reduce storage time after arrival by hosting mass immunization events. Be creative with distribution techniques — for example, drones were used for deliveries in Ghana.
  • Optimize shipment size to limit requirements for cold-chain capacity. Transition packaging to smaller units by dividing bulk shipments into smaller packages shipped more frequently.
  • Increase cold-chain storage capacity. Solutions include renting temporary warehouse capacity from the private sector, using portable freezers or mobile cold storage, or evaluating the cost-effectiveness of repairing existing cold-chain units. This is especially important for mass immunization events and to protect against delays in vaccine distribution.

Commentary and Practical Links

Core Concept: The Three Temperature Zones of the Cold Chain

Because of their different technology platforms, COVID-19 vaccines have very different temperature requirements:

  • 2–8°C (refrigerated): traditional protein subunit vaccines and adenovirus vector vaccines (such as AZ and J&J).
  • -20°C (frozen): Moderna mRNA vaccine.
  • -60 to -80°C (ultra-cold): the Pfizer/BioNTech mRNA vaccine initially required this temperature, and was later improved to allow short-term storage at -20°C.

Ultra-low temperature cold chain barely exists worldwide (especially in low-income countries and remote areas), which directly caused inequity in vaccine access.

Analogy: The Narrow Neck of an Hourglass

Imagine the vaccine supply chain as an hourglass: above is ample vaccine production capacity, below are the people waiting to be vaccinated, but the "narrow neck" of the hourglass is the cold-chain infrastructure. Even if production is sufficient, if cold-chain capacity cannot keep up, the doses that finally reach patients are still limited. During the pandemic, this neck was constrained not only by a lack of equipment but also by shortages of staff, transport routes and dry ice, all at the same time.

Dry Ice Became a Key Bottleneck

The Pfizer vaccine initially required transport at -70°C and relied on large quantities of dry ice. But global dry ice production capacity (mainly a by-product of CO₂ capture) could not cope with the sudden surge in demand, and the CO₂ feedstock supply was also affected by industrial shutdowns, so the dry ice shortage became an unexpected supply chain bottleneck.

Summary of Three Improvement Strategies

  • Design distribution around "refrigerator needs": vaccinate on a large scale as soon as vaccine arrives in a region, reducing time in cold storage. Ghana's use of drones for delivery is a model of creative solutions.
  • Optimize pack sizes: split large lots into small packs and ship more frequently, reducing reliance on large cold storage facilities.
  • Expand cold-chain capacity: in the short term, rent private warehouses and use portable freezers; in the long term, invest in repairing existing cold-chain facilities.

TWi / CDMO Practical Link

Cold-chain capability is an important differentiator for a fill-finish CDMO:

  • TWi's current 2–8°C and -20°C refrigerated and frozen storage capability is the basic threshold for winning contracts for mRNA vaccines and low-temperature biologics.
  • Without -80°C ultra-low temperature capability, it is not possible to take on final product storage for some mRNA or CAR-T products.
  • The completeness of validated cold-chain procedures (shipping validation, thermal mapping) is both a regulatory requirement and a focus of client audits.

Q 5-3 | Vertical Integration of Vaccine Supply Chains

Q 5-3: How have vaccine manufacturing supply chains become more vertically integrated?

Background

To get vaccine manufacturing up to the demand required during the COVID-19 pandemic, many companies, such as Pfizer and Merck, had to take on some of the responsibility of obtaining the consumables and raw materials that were required to manufacture their drug products. With extended delays from suppliers overseas, the need grew for companies to supply materials in house where they could control their own lead times.

Lessons Learned

Pfizer needed extremely large quantities of a cationic lipid that was essential to their COVID-19 vaccine, which was not readily available at the start of the pandemic. Consequently, Pfizer developed a process to create the lipid needed based on one that had already been devised by a Pfizer R&D facility. Assembling a team of 50 people, they performed a technology transfer from the R&D facility to a larger manufacturing facility to create the cationic lipid. To expedite the timeline, the team found ways to consolidate steps wherever possible and, soon after, the facility was producing 20 kg of cationic lipids each week. Instead of relying on vendors, Pfizer was able to control the output production of lipid that served their purpose in creating the COVID-19 vaccine.

Similarly, acquisition of companies that produced important raw materials helped reduce lead times by streamlining communication between the two entities. Merck started production of a high-purity synthetic cholesterol product; the launch of this product followed Merck's acquisition of AmpTec, which combined AmpTec's mRNA technology with Merck's extensive expertise in lipid manufacturing. This integration of upstream capabilities into Merck's own operations reduced dependence on external suppliers and shortened lead times for critical raw materials.

Commentary and Practical Links

Core Concept: Vertical Integration

Vertical integration is a strategy in which a company brings upstream or downstream steps that previously relied on outside suppliers under its own control. In the pharmaceutical industry, vertical integration can take these forms:

  • Backward integration: making raw materials or excipients in-house (for example, Pfizer making its own cationic lipid).
  • Forward integration: building its own fill-finish capacity rather than outsourcing to a CDMO.
  • Integration through M&A: for example, Merck's acquisition of AmpTec, which gave it mRNA technology and lipid manufacturing capability in one step.

Analogy: A Farm-to-Table Restaurant

An ordinary restaurant buys ingredients from several suppliers; a "farm-to-table restaurant" grows its own vegetables and raises its own chickens, controlling every step from field to table. Pfizer's approach was similar: seeing that outside lipid suppliers could not scale up quickly, it opened its own "farm" (an in-house lipid production line) and was no longer at the mercy of a single external supplier.

Pfizer Case: In-house Cationic Lipid Manufacturing

The cationic lipid is a key raw material for mRNA vaccines: it forms lipid nanoparticles (LNP) that encapsulate the mRNA and deliver it into cells. In the early days of the pandemic there was simply no commercial large-scale supply on the market.

Pfizer's action: it assembled a 50-person team and carried out technology transfer from an R&D site to a large-scale manufacturing site, reaching an output of 20 kg per week in a very short time and taking full control of the supply bottleneck.

Merck Case: Acquiring AmpTec to Integrate mRNA Technology

Through the acquisition of AmpTec, Merck brought together in one move:

  • AmpTec's mRNA synthesis technology (in vitro transcription, IVT)
  • Merck's own lipid manufacturing expertise

The result was commercial production of high-purity synthetic cholesterol, an important excipient in the LNP formulation of mRNA vaccines. This also gave Merck an upstream position in the mRNA supply chain.

TWi / CDMO Practical Link and Strategic Warning

Vertical integration is a double-edged sword for CDMOs:

  • Threat: large companies such as Pfizer and Moderna build captive fill-finish capacity, reducing their reliance on CDMOs and squeezing the contract market.
  • Opportunity: small and mid-sized biotech companies and emerging-market manufacturers cannot afford vertical integration and instead depend more on CDMOs. TWi's opportunity lies in providing flexible, fast CMO services to this type of client.
  • Differentiation: if TWi can offer one-stop service from drug development and filling to cold-chain storage, that is itself a form of "partial vertical integration" that increases client stickiness.

  • Q5-1 Material shortages: SUS, sterile filters, glass vials, rubber stoppers, APIs and excipients all saw longer lead times during COVID-19. CDMO Strategy Summary

Three action directions most directly relevant to TWi, distilled from Chapters 5 and 6 of PtC-9:

  1. Dual sourcing: all critical consumables (SUS, filters, packaging materials) must have pre-qualified alternative suppliers, included in the BCP.
  2. Investment in cold-chain capability: -20°C and above is standard; evaluate whether to invest in -80°C ultra-low temperature capability to enter the mRNA market.
  3. CDMO value positioning: as large companies integrate vertically, small and mid-sized biotechs need flexible CDMOs more; TWi should emphasize a differentiated offering of "one-stop, fast response, flexible compliance".
