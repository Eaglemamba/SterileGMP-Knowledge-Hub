1.0Introduction

1.0 Introduction

PDA Technical Report No. 68: Risk-Based Approach for Prevention and Management of Drug Shortages was originally developed in 2014 as part of the interassociation collaborative response to the European Medicines Agency's (EMA) initiative on medicinal product shortages caused by manufacturing, quality, and/or good manufacturing practice (GMP) issues. This revision updates the 2014 version to recognize emerging regulatory expectations and current terminology. The risk-based approach and concepts in the original technical report are broadly recognized and remain unchanged.

This revision recognizes the advances made in pharmaceutical manufacturing and aligns the text of TR-68 with the current regulatory environment, for example, the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) Quality Guideline Q9(R1): Quality Risk Management, ICH Quality Guideline Q12: Technical and Regulatory Considerations for Pharmaceutical Product Lifecycle Management, regional regulations and guidelines, and terminologies used for drug shortages that have since been published. Examples for preventive measures to avoid drug shortages consider lessons learned since TR-68 was first published in 2014. Elements in the templates and forms included in this revision have been updated to reflect current regulatory expectations and terminology used in the European Union and its Member States and in the United States, while it also supports compliance with global expectations on managing and preventing drug shortages.

Sustainable access to safe, efficacious, and high-quality products every time a patient needs them is just as important as the therapeutic advances that have transformed the lives and survival of patients worldwide. The ICH Q9(R1) defines harm as "damage to health, including damage that can occur from loss of product quality or availability." Ensuring uninterrupted availability of and access to products, especially medically necessary, lifesaving or life sustaining critical medicines that do not have effective alternatives, is essential.

Commentary

Core concept: What is TR-68?

PDA Technical Report No. 68 (TR-68) is a technical report published by the Parenteral Drug Association (PDA), the pharmaceutical industry's leading association, focused on "prevention and management of drug shortages".

  • First edition: 2014, written in response to the EMA's (European Medicines Agency's) concern about drug shortages caused by manufacturing and quality issues.
  • 2024 revision: incorporates the latest regulatory frameworks of ICH Q9(R1) (Quality Risk Management) and ICH Q12 (Product Lifecycle Management).
  • Core approach unchanged: the risk-based approach to prevention and mitigation remains the backbone.

Real-life analogy: prevention is better than cure

Think of a drug shortage as an overcrowded hospital emergency room. With good preventive health management in normal times (risk control), emergency demand naturally falls. The spirit of TR-68 is exactly this: build a systematic mechanism for risk identification and prevention before problems occur, rather than scrambling to respond after a shortage has already happened.

Key point: the new ICH Q9(R1) definition of "harm"

ICH Q9(R1) defines "harm" as: "damage to health, including damage that can occur from loss of product quality or availability."

This is a major shift. In the past, "harm" mainly meant quality defects; the new definition explicitly brings stock-outs and supply interruptions into the scope of harm, directly elevating drug shortages to a core topic of quality risk management.

1.1Background

1.1 Background

Drug shortages have become more frequent, more severe, and longer-lasting globally since the early 2000s for a variety of reasons. As globalization increases, the complexity of supply chains grows, global and regional regulatory expectations evolve, and economic and business motivators change. The drivers behind drug shortages in different geographic areas and countries vary, and those causes are usually multifactorial. The range of causes includes financial, economic, and business concerns, increased or unanticipated market demand (e.g., pandemic), evolving regulatory expectations, manufacturing and quality issues, capacity constraints, lack of redundancy, and supply chain issues. Recognition has evolved that the multifactorial root causes for shortages extend beyond manufacturing and quality issues, as has the need for multistakeholder interaction and collaboration to address the local issue of a shortage in a multiregional environment.

The emerging global challenge of supply disruptions is drawing greater focus from the pharmaceutical industry, regulators, legislators, healthcare providers, patient organizations, and patients, and drug shortages are now covered in the media to a much larger extent. ICH Q9(R1) places greater emphasis on product availability risks—a noteworthy change from the original guideline—in the context of quality and manufacturing issues, including GMP noncompliance, that can be a "significant cause of product availability issues (e.g., product shortages)." Furthermore, while "manufacturing and supply chain diversity can be enablers of product availability, increasingly complex supply chains lead to interdependencies that can introduce systemic quality/manufacturing risks impacting supply chain robustness."

The legislative and regulatory processes for shortage-related issues continue to evolve. In 2012, EMA published a reflection paper and implementation plan for drug shortages that affect patients. EMA followed up by holding workshops with stakeholders in October 2013 and again in October 2015. In 2016, the European Heads of Medicines Agencies (HMA) and EMA established a joint task force to develop and coordinate actions for better prevention, identification, management, and communication of issues affecting the availability of medicines to improve the continuity of medicines supply across Europe. In 2020, the European Commission's Pharmaceutical Strategy further elaborated upon the need for proactive measures to address the issue of drug shortages, and in March 2023, the HMA/EMA Task Force held a third workshop to inform and update stakeholders on their deliverables and activities.

The focus on drug shortages has also increased in the United States. In 2013, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) established a Drug Shortage Task Force, which published a strategic plan for preventing and reducing drug shortages. The task force continues to identify the root causes of drug shortages and advance long-term solutions, reporting annually to Congress. As in many countries, notification of a potential drug shortage is a legal requirement in the United States, and early and timely notifications has reduced the number of shortages by enabling manufacturers and health authorities to jointly take steps to minimize supply disruption. The annual reports to Congress for calendar years 2021 and 2022 included the impact of the increased risk of shortages during the COVID-19 pandemic and the actions taken to address them.

A 2023 Majority Staff Report of the U.S. Senate Committee on Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs, Short Supply: The Health and National Security Risks of Drug Shortages, concluded that active drug shortages had risen by 30% between 2021 and 2022, lasted one-and-a-half years longer on average, and impacted patient care. According to the report, overreliance on foreign resources was deemed a national security risk, FDA lacked critical information to mitigate shortages, and both industry and regulators lacked end-to-end supply chain visibility.

It is essential for the senior management of a firm that is the Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH) to have control of and be accountable for ensuring the uninterrupted supply of their products as long as it is within their control. Senior Management decisions impact not only a firm's ability to cause shortages but also to prevent and recover from shortages in an expedient manner. As stated in ICH Q9(R1), the principles of quality risk management (QRM) that enable risk-based decision-making by both companies and health authorities are fundamental for the proactive prevention of drug shortages.

"Addressing lifecycle risks to manufacturing reliability and quality assurance is the foundation for supply predictability." — ICH Concept Paper, November 2020
Commentary

Core concept: the multifactorial roots of drug shortages

Since the early 2000s, global drug shortages have steadily worsened. The causes are "multifactorial" and mainly include:

  • Financial and commercial factors: low-margin drugs are abandoned by manufacturers
  • Globalization of the supply chain: APIs (active pharmaceutical ingredients) are concentrated in a small number of countries
  • Evolving regulatory requirements: increasingly strict GMP compliance standards lead manufacturers to cease production or recall products
  • Demand surges: for example, unexpected demand during the COVID-19 pandemic
  • Insufficient capacity and lack of backup: fragile structures built on a single site and a single source

Real-life analogy: the supply chain as a water pipe network

The global pharmaceutical supply chain is like the intricate water pipe network of a big city. Globalization has made the pipes longer and more intertwined, so a rupture in any one section (a manufacturing problem, a regulatory inspection) can affect water supply to the entire downstream city (the patients).

ICH Q9(R1) specifically points out that although supply chain diversity can improve availability, an overly complex supply chain can instead introduce systemic risk, a paradox worth keeping in mind.

Key point: the responsibility of MAH senior management

TR-68 states clearly that the senior management of the Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH) bears direct responsibility for ensuring uninterrupted supply of the product. Its decisions affect not only the likelihood of a shortage but also how quickly the supply recovers from one.

This is not only a QA department issue but a core obligation at the level of corporate governance.

Key statistics (2023 US Senate report)

  • Between 2021 and 2022, active drug shortages increased by 30%
  • The average duration of shortages lengthened by 1.5 years
  • Overdependence on overseas sources was identified as a national security risk
  • Both FDA and industry lack end-to-end supply chain visibility

1.2Purpose and Scope

1.2 Purpose and Scope

This revision of TR-68 addresses the following aspects for proactive prevention of drug shortages and provides useful tools to assist in preparation:

  • A holistic, risk-based framework at a product level for the prevention and management of drug shortages caused by manufacturing and quality issues
  • A risk triage model that can be used to assess drug shortage risks and implement appropriate controls in the end-to-end value chain for the manufacturing and distribution of a product (Section 11.0, Appendix 1)
  • Templates for developing a Drug Shortage Risk Register and a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan at a product level (Section 12.0, Appendix 2)

The concepts and approach presented in this report may be adopted and applied by other stakeholders in the entire supply chain, such as wholesale distributors, as well as suppliers and vendors of materials used in processing, for example, filters, single-use bags, flow paths, resins, and solvents.

TR-68 focuses on addressing shortages caused by manufacturing and quality issues toward batch release. The next step in the supply chain after manufacturing and release is usually called the "handover, to the first economic customer," after which the MAH often has limited-to-no visibility (e.g., shortages related to issues that arise in distribution channels, pharmacies, or hospitals). Shortages after the "handover, to the first economic customer" are out of scope of this technical report. Nevertheless, there may be situations where the MAH is responsible for distribution as well and, therefore, is responsible for mitigating shortages in these instances. TR-68 does apply in these situations.

The framework provided in TR-68 also supports and enables MAHs and manufacturers to meet the requirements of Section 506C of the U.S. Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and Article 13 of EU Commission Directive 2003/94/EC to notify relevant health authorities in the event of a drug shortage.

Development of PDA's risk-based approach for prevention and management of drug shortages at a product level began in 2012 as part of the Paradigm Change in Manufacturing Operations (PCMO®) project and incorporated input from the EMA-sanctioned industry interassociation task force and multistakeholder events. PDA also participated in various HMA/EMA multistakeholder workshops, including the one held in 2023; key outcomes from the workshop discussions have been incorporated into this revision of TR-68.

Additionally, PDA Technical Report No. 54: Implementation of Quality Risk Management for Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology Manufacturing Operations and its series of best-practice and companion case-study documents provide detailed guidance and practical application on how to implement QRM for the management of product quality risks in manufacturing operations, supply chains, and operations of third parties (e.g., suppliers, contract manufacturers). PDA Technical Report No. 59: Utilization of Statistical Methods for Production Monitoring describes additional risk-based activities that use statistical methodologies for quality control and manufacturing.

Commentary

Core concept: the scope boundary of TR-68

The scope of TR-68 has a clear "end point":

  • In scope: from the start of manufacturing to "handover to the first economic customer", i.e. from batch release to handover to the wholesaler/distributor
  • Out of scope: shortages that arise after handover, within the distribution channel, pharmacies and hospitals
  • Exception: if the MAH is itself responsible for distribution, the scope can extend to the distribution step

Real-life analogy: restaurant kitchen vs. delivery platform

TR-68 is like a "restaurant kitchen management code": it governs quality and supply stability from ingredient purchasing and cooking to serving the dish (batch release). But once the meal is handed to the delivery rider (handover to the first economic customer), problems during delivery fall outside this code.

Of course, if the restaurant also does its own delivery, the delivery process then falls within scope.

Regulatory basis: notification obligations

The TR-68 framework helps the MAH meet the following notification obligations:

  • United States: Section 506C of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act: potential shortages must be notified to FDA
  • European Union: Article 13 of EU Directive 2003/94/EC: notification of the relevant competent authority is required

Early notification has been shown to reduce the number of shortages effectively, giving manufacturers and authorities time to take preventive measures together.

Related PDA Technical Reports

  • TR-54: guidance on implementing quality risk management in pharmaceutical manufacturing (QRM implementation details)
  • TR-59: application of statistical methods in manufacturing monitoring (provides additional risk-based activities)

TR-68 is an important member of this family of risk management tools, focusing on the issue of supply reliability.

Glossary Commentary

Core concept: overview of the TR-68 terminology system

The 18 terms in this section form the semantic foundation of TR-68 and can be divided by function into four groups:

  • Drug classification: Critical Medicine, Medically Necessary Drug, Life Supporting/Sustaining Drug
  • Shortage types: Drug Shortage, Critical Shortage, Potential Drug Shortage, Meaningful Disruption
  • Risk management tools: QRM, Risk, Residual Risk, Risk Triage, Risk Priority Level
  • Management documents: Risk Register, Prevention & Response Plan, Value Stream Map

Real-life analogy: three levels of drug importance

Imagine a hospital's drug list:

  • Critical Medicine: considers the therapeutic indication, availability of alternative treatments and supply chain vulnerability. Examples: antibiotics, chemotherapy drugs.
  • Medically Necessary Drug: treats or prevents a serious disease and has no suitable alternative. Examples: certain drugs for rare diseases.
  • Life Supporting/Sustaining Drug: without it, the patient's basic physiological functions would be interrupted. Examples: insulin, drugs used with respirators.

The further down the list, the greater the urgency of a shortage, and the higher the priority of regulatory notification and mitigation measures.

Key distinction: the four key elements of "drug shortage"

The TR-68 definition of "Drug Shortage" contains four sub-elements that must be understood:

  • Shortage (the shortage itself): identifies a current, imminent or anticipated supply interruption
  • Supply: the total stock the MAH places on the market
  • Demand: requests from healthcare professionals/patients based on clinical need; the product must be available at the right time and in sufficient quantity
  • National level: supply falls short of demand at the national level; regional logistics problems (such as delivery difficulties) are not a drug shortage

Key distinction: Meaningful Disruption vs. ordinary production stoppage

A Meaningful Disruption must satisfy:

  • A manufacturing change that is reasonably likely to lead to a significant reduction in supply
  • It affects the manufacturer's ability to fulfil orders or the supply to meet expected demand

The following are not a Meaningful Disruption:

  • Routine maintenance
  • Minor production adjustments expected to be restored in the short term

This definition directly affects the trigger point for a manufacturer's obligation to submit "early notification" to FDA.

Risk definition formula

TR-68 follows the ICH Q9(R1) definition of risk:

Risk = Probability of Harm × Severity of Harm

Here "Harm" = damage to health, including damage from loss of product quality or availability

"Residual Risk" = the shortage risk that remains after risk control measures are implemented. It is an important indicator for judging whether existing controls are adequate.

Core concept: the three dimensions of Risk Priority Level

The Risk Priority Level is determined by a combination of the following three dimensions:

  1. Therapeutic use and patient impact: how severe the consequence is for patients if the product is unavailable
  2. Availability of alternatives: whether other effective alternative treatments exist
  3. Likelihood of shortage: an assessment of the probability that a supply interruption will occur

These three dimensions map to the Risk Triage Model in Section 11.0, which is the core scoring mechanism of the whole TR-68 methodology.

Why abbreviations matter: the central role of the MAH

The MAH (Marketing Authorization Holder) is the most central role in TR-68. Under the EU regulatory framework, the MAH holds the marketing authorization of the medicinal product and bears ultimate responsibility for its quality, safety and continuity of supply.

In Taiwan, this corresponds to the "drug license holder", i.e. the pharmaceutical manufacturer or distributor holding a drug license issued by the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration (TFDA), Ministry of Health and Welfare.

Key abbreviations: what ANDA vs. NDA mean for the supply chain

  • NDA (New Drug Application): the marketing application for an innovator drug; it usually carries a stronger market position, but if production stops there is no alternative
  • ANDA (Abbreviated New Drug Application): the marketing application for a generic drug; there may be several competitors on the market, so the impact of a shortage is relatively smaller

This distinction is critical when assessing "availability of alternatives" and directly affects the Risk Priority Level score.

PAC (post-approval change) and drug shortage

PAC (Post-Approval Change) is an important trigger point for supply chain risk. When a manufacturer changes the manufacturing site, a supplier or the process, it must apply to the authority for a PAC; processing can take months to years, and without adequate stock built up in the meantime, a drug shortage can result.

The TR-68 risk framework treats PAC management as one of the important considerations in preventive measures.

Recognizing Drug Shortages as a Global Problem

Drug Shortage as a Global Problem | p6–p8

Original Text (English)

The primary objective in the management of drug shortages is preventing harm to patients. In any situation, it is important to maintain the continuity of supply such that all patients receive the medicines they need every time they need them to manage their medical condition. The risk of potential drug shortages should be considered when making decisions related to the supply and/or manufacture of drug products and their corresponding active substances. This technical report addresses how drug shortages caused by manufacturing and quality issues can be anticipated and avoided using a risk-based approach at a product level.

A good example of a shortage that had global impact and needed to be managed by multiple health authorities was "The Great Acetonitrile Shortage" in 2008–2009. Acetonitrile is used as a solvent in many analytical procedures that are included in submissions for the approval of drug products and in chemical reactions for the manufacture of active pharmaceutical ingredients (API). The two main manufacturers of this solvent, both located in the same area in China, were required to stop manufacturing acetonitrile to reduce air pollution for the 2008 Summer Olympics. A few months later, in September of 2008, acetonitrile production in Texas was interrupted due to hurricanes in the Gulf of Mexico. The combination of these two events caused shortages of several APIs and drug products. Only a few companies were aware that the product would not be manufactured for a year and, subsequently, had built up their inventories of the product in advance. Most regulatory agencies accepted alternative analytical methods on a short-term basis. The FDA also allowed changes to existing validated test methods within an approved application, either a new drug application (NDA) or an abbreviated new drug application (ANDA), to accommodate using less acetonitrile or an alternative solvent to acetonitrile. The Agency allowed companies to process this as a minor change in the Annual Report for the given drug application as long as the change met the criteria stated in the FDA Guidance for Industry: Changes to an Approved NDA or ANDA and Code of Federal Regulations [21 CFR 314.70(d)(2)(vii)].

This example illustrates that, in some cases, drug shortages can occur very suddenly and for reasons that are difficult to predict. Common antibiotics such as amoxicillin have also experienced shortages globally due to supply-chain disruptions. Hence, it is important for companies and regulators alike to do their best to anticipate potential drug shortages and prevent them from happening. Improved surveillance and communications through forums and consortiums, such as the International Supply Chain Consortium (Rx-360), can help in managing widespread impact in such instances.

The 2013 Birgli® report identified three principal types of causes for drug shortages—economic, business, and manufacturing supply chain issues—that are still highly relevant today. Since then, an awareness of the diversity of possible root causes for shortages has continued to evolve. It is important to acknowledge the role of the media in public perception of shortages. Figure 3.0-1 illustrates why it is important to recognize the landscape of stakeholders and stakeholder interdependencies as a complex ecosystem. It underscores the need for appropriate and timely communications among all the players to better manage unintended consequences of actions that any one stakeholder might take to prevent or manage drug shortages.

Commentary and Application Guide

Core concept: why is drug shortage a global problem?

The first important statement of TR-68, that the primary goal of drug shortage management is preventing harm to patients, establishes the ethical stance of the document: continuity of supply is not merely a commercial consideration but an obligation for patient safety.

This section centers on manufacturing and quality problems, as distinct from commercial or economic factors, and sets the scope for the risk assessment framework in the following sections.

Case study: the acetonitrile shortage (2008-2009)

Acetonitrile is the organic solvent most commonly used in HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) and is widely used in pharmaceutical analytical methods. The 2008 "acetonitrile shortage" is a perfect illustration of the fragility of single-point dependency in the supply chain:

  • Event 1: production shut down in China ahead of the Olympics, a manufacturing-side shock
  • Event 2: a hurricane in Texas interrupted production, a geographic concentration risk
  • Combined result: global shortages of multiple APIs and drug products

Imagine "a city with only two bakeries, both located in the same low-lying area: once it floods, the whole city has no bread". For a CDMO, this means that supplier diversification for critical ancillary materials (solvents, excipients) is a necessary risk management measure, not an optional one.

Key point: what the four categories of factors in Figure 3.0-1 mean for a CDMO

The key to understanding Figure 3.0-1 is not to list the items but to recognize the interdependence of the whole ecosystem:

  • Manufacturing: directly controlled by the CDMO; process variability, equipment and capacity are all core management topics
  • Business: led by the MAH, but the CDMO needs to cooperate by providing capacity commitments and material stocking plans
  • Economic/geographic: mostly external factors, but price compression affects willingness to invest in stock
  • Reliability: includes natural disasters and pandemics, precisely the blind spot that COVID-19 exposed

The role of the media is also explicitly listed as a factor: once news of a shortage leaks out, panic stockpiling can aggravate a supply gap that was otherwise manageable, creating a vicious cycle.

3.1

Evolution of Terminologies

Evolution of Terminology | p8

Original Text (English)

Though single, internationally harmonized definitions do not currently exist for "drug shortage," "meaningful disruption," and "medically necessary product," various definitions of these terms have been published.

The FDA defines drug shortage as "a period of time when the demand or projected demand for the drug in the United States exceeds the drug's supply." In general, the FDA Drug Shortage Staff focuses on shortages of medically necessary products that have a significant effect on public health.

The EMA earlier described drug shortages as "disruptions to the manufacturing supply chain" and updated the definition of shortages in 2019 to be more specific in terms of the gap between supply and demand. In addition, individual EU member states have local definitions of drug shortages. The EU's current definition of "critical medicines" also includes the criteria of therapeutic use, availability of alternative(s), and supply-chain vulnerabilities.

The World Health Organization (WHO) refers to a "shortage" as a situation where the supply of medicines, health products or vaccines identified as essential by the health system is considered to be insufficient to meet public health and patient needs. WHO indicates that:

On the demand side: a "shortage" will occur when demand exceeds supply at any point in the supply chain and may ultimately create a "stockout" at the point of appropriate service delivery to the patient if the cause of the shortage cannot be resolved in a timely manner relative to the clinical needs of the patient.

The understanding of these terms may also vary in some respects by the different stakeholders in the supply chain (MAHs, regulatory authorities, distributors, wholesalers, providers, and patients). However, there is alignment on the concept that these definitions are based on the fundamental importance of ensuring an uninterrupted supply of medicines to prevent harm to patients. They are also aligned with public-service obligations held by MAHs and wholesale distributors in relation to ensuring the supply of medicinal products in the markets in which they operate.

Analogy: definition differences are like the criteria for "traffic jam"

Original Text (English)

Reporting potential shortages to relevant health authorities is a legal requirement. In the United States, Section 506C of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act establishes notification requirements to manufacturers of product discontinuance. In the European Union, Article 13 of EU GMP Directive 2017/1572 defines the legal requirement for a manufacturer "to inform the competent authority and, if applicable, the MAH of any defect that could result in a recall or an abnormal restriction on supply." Other health authorities (e.g., individual EU member states, Health Canada, Swissmedic, Therapeutic Goods Administration) are also implementing regulatory requirements in this regard. ICH Q9(R1) further highlights the need for appropriate and timely management of health authority notification.

While reporting requirements and mechanisms for drug shortages vary across different health authorities and are subject to varying legal requirements, the expectation of visibility to shortage information has increased over the last several years. Other associations, such as the American Society of Health System Pharmacists, also may provide information on drug shortages and their management through their websites. Health Canada has provided a multistakeholder toolkit that provides strategies and tools for key players in the supply chain. The differences in reporting requirements and approaches in different countries continue to make it challenging to draw meaningful comparisons of data on drug shortages or to quantify the problem at a global or regional level.

The European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA), European Generic and Biosimilar Medicines Association (EGA), Association of the European Self-Care Industry (AESGP), and Plasma Protein Therapeutics Association (PPTA) jointly developed a harmonized communication framework for potential drug shortages, which includes harmonized trigger points for notifying competent authorities of drug shortages that are due to quality and manufacturing problems.

In the United States, Congress legislates the FDA's authority to act in response to drug shortages. In October 2013, FDA published its Strategic Plan for Preventing and Reducing Drug Shortages. It addresses mitigation strategies for manufacturers, such as notifying the FDA of potential shortages promptly so that manufacturers and the FDA can jointly take steps to reduce supply disruption. Another element of the FDA's drug shortage strategic plan focuses on preventive strategies, such as providing positive incentives for manufacturing and quality improvements and using proactive risk-based approaches for early identification and prevention of manufacturing and quality problems.

In 2012, the EMA published a Reflection Paper on Medicinal Product Supply Shortages Caused by Manufacturing/Good Manufacturing Practice Compliance Problems and a supporting Implementation Plan 2012–2015. The increased transparency of shortages, coupled with the mandatory requirements for early notification to health authorities of potential shortages, has allowed authorities and MAHs to work together more proactively to control and prevent potential drug shortage risks before they result in a supply disruption.

Although not all shortages result in patient harm, health authorities try to determine the causes of shortages that do affect patients and "near misses" that could have resulted in patient harm. The objective of doing so is to be prepared to proactively implement actions that can prevent recurrences. For this reason, FDA, EMA, and many other health authorities have taken the crisis around drug shortages seriously and regulations continue to evolve to heighten the attention, sensitivity, and response to shortages.

  • Lack of redundancy
  • Complexity of the supply chain

Risk-based approaches and risk-based decision-making are more broadly expected, accepted, and integrated into regulations and regulatory oversight mechanisms. Core concept: the "starting point" of a shortage in the supply chain is much earlier than imagined

TR-68 points out clearly that a drug shortage does not begin when the pharmacy shelf is empty; it begins as soon as materials cannot be obtained at any point in the supply chain:

  • Active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) or its starting material
  • Excipients, reagents, solvents
  • Packaging materials

The implication for a CDMO is that risk management must start at raw material procurement, rather than responding only after manufacturing has failed.

Imagine a long chain of dominoes: API supplier → raw material testing → manufacturing → packaging → warehousing → distribution → hospital. "Country-specific import testing requirements" are an especially unpredictable domino: a supply interruption that could have recovered quickly turns into a real shortage because each country's import retesting procedure delays release by weeks.

  • Systems level: ISPE's overall supply chain design

The "risk-based framework at a product level" emphasized by TR-68 echoes the quality system frameworks of ICH Q9(R1) and ICH Q10: manufacturing compliance is not the end point but a necessary foundation for preventing shortages.

The statement "industry and regulators are still on a learning curve" is an honest assessment of the whole industry: accept regulatory inspection observations and CAPA (corrective and preventive action) as learning opportunities rather than treating them as threats.

A drug manufacturing company's interest is in developing, producing, and making pharmaceutical or biopharmaceutical medicines available to patients in a financially sustainable manner.   • Learning environment

5.0 Risk-Based Approaches for Prevention of Drug Shortages

A holistic approach to the prevention and management of drug shortages focused on patient safety is important and complex. There are multiple elements, both at the system and product levels, that work individually or in combination to prevent a shortage:

  • At the product level, an end-to-end understanding of the value chain for manufacturing and distribution provides the ability to understand and implement end-to-end controls.
  • At the system level, appropriate oversight and a robust pharmaceutical quality system (PQS) ensure compliance with applicable laws and regulations.

Both approaches should also establish mechanisms that provide early warning signals of potential shortages, including near-misses, assess potential root causes for the shortage considering multiple aspects, drive targeted manufacturing and product quality improvements (e.g., introduction of new technologies, continual improvement, innovation), and enable timely communications with key stakeholders and health authorities. Other foundational elements should:

  • Ensure strong management accountability
  • Foster a sustainable quality culture that enables employees to speak up
  • Reward behaviors that drive joint ownership between operations and quality units for timely issue resolution
  • Build people skills and capabilities that drive change leadership and excellence in pharmaceutical operations
  • Establish a governance model that tracks operational and quality performance with appropriate metrics

As part of a robust quality system, companies should implement QRM principles and knowledge management capabilities to accomplish the following:

  • Proactively identify what controls are, and are not, in place for shortage prevention (for critical products at least)
  • Proactively identify and control risks of interruption in manufacturing and product supply
  • Link to clinical and safety risk assessments for their products
  • Facilitate risk-based decision-making by companies and health authorities in response to shortages in order to control and reduce harm to patients

Opportunities for informed and timely decision-making based on QRM principles, knowledge management, and a good understanding of patient impact in the event of a shortage allow companies to proactively manage and prevent drug shortages. When drugs become unexpectedly unavailable in the manufacturing supply chain, risk-based decision-making enables companies to provide key risk-indicating information to health authorities in a timely manner before the shortage becomes severe.

In addition to internal risk and knowledge management, it is helpful to have systems that generate reliable and up-to-date information about the marketplace for a company's products (e.g., trends in demand, usage volumes, market-share data). This combined body of information can also be very useful in decision-making for both the company and health authorities in order to respond to a shortage. It can help both parties understand what the risks of a shortage are to patients, what advice to communicate to healthcare providers, and what measures to take in order to respond quickly to a drug shortage. Information about the supply position for competitor products and alternative therapies is also important for assessing the risks of shortage-related issues.

Core concept: the two-layer structure

The TR68 prevention framework operates at two levels at the same time, and neither can be omitted:

  • Product level: a thorough understanding of the end-to-end value chain of manufacturing and distribution, bringing everything from raw materials to the patient into the scope of control.
  • System level: establishing a sound Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS) that ensures compliance with applicable regulations and forms the pillar of organizational culture.

Analogy: a fire protection system vs. a building safety culture

Imagine fire protection management in a building:

  • The product level is like installing smoke detectors, sprinklers and emergency exit lights in every corridor: specific controls placed at each "node".
  • The system level is like the safety culture of the whole building, regular drills and management accountability mechanisms, which ensure that all the controls keep working effectively.

The same applies to drug shortage prevention: with product control points but no systemic support, people will still be at a loss when a crisis occurs.

5.1 Concepts of Risk and Knowledge Management Concepts of Risk and Knowledge Management

5.1 Concepts of Risk and Knowledge Management

According to ICH Quality Guideline Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System, QRM and knowledge management are enablers of the PQS. As such, QRM and knowledge management are intended to facilitate the practical integration of all elements of the PQS "to enhance the quality and availability of medicines around the world in the interest of public health."

A good understanding of the manufacturing process and end-to-end supply chain, up to the transfer of product from the MAH to the first economic customer (i.e., to wholesale distributors), is necessary for appropriate QRM to identify, assess, control, and review the risks of potential drug shortages. Once the hazards are identified and the risks are assessed, appropriate risk control measures can be implemented to reduce (control, mitigate, or eliminate) the risks of potential drug shortages before they lead to actual drug shortages. Of course, not all hazards can be anticipated; in such instances, as soon as new information on a hazard becomes available, risk-review activities can ensure timely assessment and implementation of risk control actions.

Knowledge management supports the PQS by facilitating easier access to product and process knowledge throughout the product's lifecycle. This availability of knowledge regarding process-performance and product-quality monitoring systems can enhance process understanding. In addition, ICH Q10 calls for process and product monitoring throughout product development and commercial manufacturing, so the information can be used to establish and actively manage a timely control strategy for manufacturing. Finally, management review of process performance and product quality should identify appropriate actions for continual improvement, such as the capture and dissemination of knowledge and the identification of post-approval changes (PAC).

Most companies have mechanisms to respond to drug shortages when they occur; however, these mechanisms might not be comprehensive or fully integrated into company procedures. A comprehensive approach for managing potential drug shortages should involve:

  • Ability to respond quickly to minimize the impact of a shortage
  • Framework for anticipating drug shortage triggers and risks and for establishing mechanisms to control risks and prevent shortages
  • Mechanisms and processes for timely communications

The EFPIA Good Practice Guide: Reducing Risk for Drug Product Shortages describes a "good practice" model that a company can adopt to prevent and/or manage potential drug shortage situations.

A company's QRM policy and related procedures should address risks to patient safety in the context of product quality and availability. Typically, risk ranking levels in a company's QRM policy include ranking for GMP and regulatory compliance risks and patient safety risks based on product quality. Table 5.1-1🔍 provides an example of a risk ranking scale that incorporates considerations for both product quality and product availability in the ranking of patient safety risks.

While manufacturing and quality issues could impact product quality or patient safety, there may be instances when product quality or patient safety are not impacted. Still, there may be issues with regard to compliance with GMP or GDP or regulatory commitments provided in the product dossier. When handling GMP and regulatory compliance issues or deviations that may negatively impact the release of a product, the company must quickly identify the potential issue and work on a solution(s) to mitigate the risk of a drug shortage. Hence, it is important that the education and qualification of employees who manage deviations include aspects associated with the assessment of potential supply impact in case an issue evolves further. Core concept: the two Enablers of ICH Q10

According to the ICH Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System, two elements are defined as the "Enablers" of the PQS:

  • Quality Risk Management (QRM): a systematic approach to identifying, assessing, controlling and reviewing potential risks, carried out in accordance with ICH Q9(R1).
  • Knowledge Management: ensures that product/process knowledge can be accessed, shared and used for decision making throughout the lifecycle.

Both are tools for achieving the PQS objectives, not ends in themselves; the ultimate goal is to "improve drug quality and availability worldwide in the interest of public health".

Analogy: the PDCA cycle and drug shortage prevention

The relationship between the QRM process and knowledge management can be understood through the PDCA continual improvement cycle:

  • Plan: identify hazards and assess risks based on existing knowledge → develop risk control measures.
  • Do: implement the control measures, for example by qualifying backup suppliers and increasing safety stock.
  • Check: risk review activities to confirm the controls are effective; monitor process performance and product quality.
  • Act: management review identifies improvement opportunities, captures and disseminates new knowledge, and identifies post-approval changes (PACs).

"Knowledge management" is the "memory" of this cycle: it ensures that what is learned in each PDCA round is not lost and can be used in the next round.

The core QRM framework

According to ICH Q9(R1) and PDA TR-54, the standard QRM process comprises four steps:

QRM Process =

+ (2) Assess Risks (severity × occurrence × detectability)
+ (3) Control Risks (eliminate / reduce / accept)
+ (4) Review Risks (ongoing review)

(Risk priority number = severity × probability of occurrence × detectability)

Many companies mistakenly equate "knowledge management" with "document management". The TR68 definition is broader:

5.2 The Importance of Interacting with Health Authorities The Importance of Interacting with Health Authorities

5.2 The Importance of Interacting with Health Authorities

There is a legal requirement for MAHs to notify relevant health authorities of any potential drug shortages in the European Union and in the United States. Therefore, early interactions should take place between a pharmaceutical company and relevant health authorities when a meaningful disruption in supply is expected. Laws and regulations related to drug shortages are continuing to evolve and are defining or clarifying responsibilities for stakeholders, such as industry, patient, and healthcare professional organizations, with respect to ensuring uninterrupted drug supplies to patients.

Health authorities generally prefer to be involved in several aspects of drug shortage management and response. For example, they might want to:

  • Oversee how the company or MAH is managing the shortage
  • Engage other manufacturers of the same product or alternatives to increase their production
  • Coordinate actions, communications, and responses to the shortage among various stakeholders, including other MAHs and manufacturers, as well as patients, healthcare professionals, and government agencies that purchase medicines
  • Ensure that adequate corrective actions are taken to prevent recurrences

At a higher level, health authorities may wish to contribute to an environment in which supply chains become more robust and shortages can generally be prevented. This can support the primary goal of health authorities, which is generally the protection of public health and the availability of medicinal products.

Health authorities usually take a risk-based approach to shortages. For example, they participate in the management of and response to shortages that may have an impact on patients now or in the near future. Health authorities, however, generally do not have the resources to become closely involved in all aspects of shortages. Risk assessments can help identify shortages that require their attention. Health authorities and industry have a mutual interest in understanding the causes of shortages, even where there is little to no impact on the patient population, to prevent them from recurring.

Knowing which shortages may or may not impact patients requires careful analysis as well as effective multistakeholder and media communications management, and the situation may vary from country to country. Robust risk assessment based on robust knowledge management of the manufacturing and supply processes and active, timely dialog with health authorities and other relevant stakeholders can facilitate this determination.

Commentary: regulatory obligations and practice in interacting with regulators

Core concept: notifying a shortage is a legal obligation, not an option

TR68 states clearly that in the European Union (EU) and the United States (US), notification of drug shortages by the MAH to the health authority is a legal requirement, not voluntary best practice.

  • US FDA: under 21 CFR 314.81(b)(3)(iii) and Section 506C of the FDA Safety and Innovation Act (FDASIA), manufacturers must notify FDA 6 months before an anticipated product shortage (or as soon as possible); generic drugs follow the relevant GDUFA provisions.
  • EU EMA/national competent authorities: under the EU Regulatory Framework on Shortages, the MAH must notify the competent authority of the relevant Member State of situations that may cause a supply interruption; some countries also have specific notification timelines.

The importance of "early notification" is not only compliance: the earlier the notification, the more time the health authority has to coordinate alternatives, and the smaller the impact on patients.

Analogy: notifying a flight delay

Imagine an aircraft with a mechanical problem that may delay departure:

  • The bad way: let passengers wait at the gate until 10 minutes before departure and then announce the delay; passengers are caught off guard and their connections are all missed.
  • The good way: the airline alerts ground staff as soon as it finds a potential problem, tells passengers 2 hours ahead and helps arrange connection options.

The logic of drug shortage notification is exactly the same: the health authority is the "coordination center", and the earlier it has the information, the more effectively it can mobilize resources (such as coordinating other manufacturers to increase production or issuing clinical alternative recommendations).

What health authorities want to be involved in

TR68 lists the typical roles of health authorities in shortage management:

  • Overseeing the MAH's response: confirming that the company's CAPA plan is adequate and executable.
  • Coordinating other manufacturers to increase production: where other manufacturers make the same product, the health authority can act as coordinator, encouraging or requiring increased capacity.
  • Multi-party communication and coordination: harmonizing the messages issued to patients, healthcare professionals and procurement bodies to avoid confusion.
  • Ensuring root cause improvement: requiring the company to take adequate CAPA to prevent recurrence.

In the long term, health authorities also hope to use these interactions to improve the resilience of the overall supply chain.

Key point: health authority resources are limited, so risk ranking is needed

TR68 candidly points out that health authorities have limited resources and cannot intervene deeply in every shortage event. The quality of the risk assessment report a company submits is therefore critical, since it is the basis on which the authority decides the degree of its involvement.

A good shortage notification should include:

  • Assessment of the medical necessity of the affected product
  • Estimated duration of the shortage and number of patients affected
  • Assessment of existing alternative therapies/alternative supply sources
  • Mitigation measures the company has taken or is planning
  • Best-case/worst-case estimates of the resolution timeline

Key point: situations differ by country

TR68 reminds us that the actual impact of a shortage on patients "varies by country". A situation where adequate alternatives exist in one country may have no alternatives at all in another. This means:

  • An MAH marketing in multiple countries must assess the degree of impact in each market individually.
  • The timelines and formats of notification obligations may differ under each country's regulations, so a notification matrix for each market must be established.
  • Media communication management also needs to be adapted to the cultural context of each country.

Practical application: the corresponding Taiwan TFDA requirements

Compared with US and EU regulations, the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration (TFDA) also has a drug shortage notification mechanism. A manufacturer or importer holding a drug license that foresees a supply interruption should notify the TFDA under the relevant regulations. Key corresponding points:

  • TFDA drug supply shortage notification system (Drug and Food Safety Weekly information)
  • Criteria for determining shortages of medically necessary drugs
  • Application process for emergency import or temporary flexibility measures

6.0   Risk Triage Model

6.0 Risk Triage Model

A preventive approach to managing the risk of drug shortages caused by manufacturing or quality issues includes a risk assessment method called triage. A triage approach allows a quick and easy assessment of the shortage risks of a product and prioritizes actions for proactive drug shortage management (risk control). Not all products require proactive drug shortage management. Only medically necessary and critical products and those with the greatest impact to patients require commensurate levels of effort and planning to ensure that the supply is adequate, and the availability component of the risk calculus is assessed and appropriately managed. The method and tools proposed in Section 6.0 are intended to help determine whether a proactive Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan is required for a product.

Companies should have robust QRM and knowledge management systems to both prevent (proactive) and respond (reactive) to drug shortages (see Section 5.0). Proactive QRM for drug shortage prevention should involve the following:

  • Categorizing each product by criticality based on its indication, marketplace importance, profile, current supply situation, and patients' needs
  • Establishing and periodically reviewing a Drug Shortage Risk Register—a single source of information on hazards and the related risks that can result in drug shortages—by proactively identifying and assessing risks to quality, compliance, and supply that could lead to a shortage. A Drug Shortage Risk Register (see Section 11.0: Appendix 1 for a sample risk register template) should provide the following information, which should be updated and reviewed on a regular basis:
    • Description of potential drug shortage risks for a product, including API, excipients, and sources of risk
    • Countries or regions that could be affected
    • Description of the current controls that are or are not in place, with respect to the prevention of shortages, and the known effectiveness of such controls
    • Risk level for a potential drug shortage, considering the current controls, new risk control actions that are required, owners, and due-date status
    • Key leading and lagging indicators that would trigger early and timely responses to potential shortages, including near-misses on a batch release or the ability to meet product demand
  • Ensuring timely reduction and management of risks by completing risk control actions defined in the risk control plan based on criticality of product availability. Issues to address in the risk control plan could include:
    • Inventory and safety stock planning
    • Demand and supply planning for all relevant markets using a sourcing strategy (e.g., dual sourcing)
    • Capacity planning for manufacturing and distribution (capacity reserve)
    • Availability of alternative therapies
    • Acceleration of health authority actions (e.g., approval of post-approval changes, inspections)
  • Establishing a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan, at least for medically necessary products, which could include early warning systems for potential shortages of certain products (see Section 12.0: Appendix 2 for a sample prevention and response plan template).

Responding to and disseminating information about a potential drug shortage in a timely and effective manner is especially important for medically necessary, life supporting, or life sustaining therapies. Since some shortages need to be addressed quickly, a preestablished and well-structured drug shortage response plan can be very valuable in laying out a clear path of actions to take to minimize the impact of a shortage, end the shortage, and quickly return product supply to its normal state.

Commentary

Analogy: Emergency Room Triage

"Triage" comes from emergency medicine. An emergency room receives a flood of patients every day, and the nurse must quickly decide who needs treatment immediately and who can wait. This prioritization process is called triage.

TR68 brings the same idea into pharmaceutical supply chain management: faced with hundreds or thousands of products, a company must first quickly assess "which products would harm patients most if they were short?" and then concentrate resources on those high-risk products.

Core logic: not every product needs the same level of resources; resources should go where protection is needed most.

Core Concept: The Two Roles of QRM

TR68 stresses that QRM (Quality Risk Management) must have two aspects at the same time:

  • Proactive prevention: build defenses before a shortage occurs. Examples: identifying high-risk products, building inventory buffers, assessing supplier stability.
  • Reactive response: when a shortage has already occurred, activate the response plan to minimize harm.

This section (6.0) focuses on the proactive prevention side; the details of the response plan are covered in Section 7.0.

The Drug Shortage Risk Register is the "Single Source of Truth" of the whole QRM system and must cover:

  • A description of the potential shortage risk (including API, excipients and their sources)
  • Countries/regions that may be affected
  • Current controls and an assessment of their effectiveness
  • Risk level (the residual risk after taking current controls into account)
  • Early warning indicators (leading indicators) and lagging indicators

6.1   Description of the Risk Triage Model

6.1 Description of the Risk Triage Model

The risk triage method uses a preliminary hazards analysis (PHA) approach, which simplifies the process after information is collected on the potential or actual drug shortage. A four-step PHA triage approach is used to evaluate the relationship of three risk elements—therapeutic use of a product, availability of alternatives, and likelihood of occurrence—to determine the risk of a drug shortage. The steps in the triage process depicted in Figure 6.1-1 are as follows:

  1. Identify impact level (impact on a patient) based on therapeutic use and availability of alternatives.
  2. Determine the likelihood of a drug shortage for the product, taking into account any drug shortage prevention controls that are in place and their likely effectiveness.
  3. Determine the risk priority level based on a combination of impact on the patient and the likelihood of a drug shortage for the product.
  4. Plan and implement the suggested risk controls for the assessed product based on the risk priority level.

The triage method incorporates key words used to create a simple risk assessment design approach. Rather than using very general, subjective terms to describe priority levels for each of the three elements used in the triage model (e.g., high, medium, and low severity), using discrete information allows the assessment to focus on product information rather than defining "high," "medium," and "low." Commentary

Core Concept: PHA (Preliminary Hazards Analysis)

Preliminary Hazards Analysis (PHA) is a risk assessment method from system safety engineering, suited to quick, early hazard identification and ranking when detailed information is lacking.

TR68 adopts the spirit of PHA: before a shortage occurs, only three key pieces of information (therapeutic use, availability of alternatives, likelihood of shortage) are needed to assess product risk quickly, without spending large resources on in-depth analysis.

Core Concept: The Three Risk Elements

The core of the risk triage model is an assessment at the intersection of three dimensions:

  • Therapeutic Use: What disease is this medicine used for? How serious are the consequences?
  • Availability of Alternatives: If this product is out of stock, are there other therapeutic options that can replace it?
  • Likelihood of Occurrence: Taking current controls into account, how likely is it that this product will actually become short?

The first two together determine the "impact level (Risk Level A/B/C)", which is then combined with the third to produce the "priority level (Priority 1/2/3)".

Key Point: Why Use Keywords Instead of High/Medium/Low Scores?

Traditional risk assessment often uses subjective "high/medium/low" scores, which leads to inconsistent results between assessors. TR68 instead uses specific descriptive keywords (such as "no alternatives available" and "alternative products available"), so that the assessment focuses on the actual characteristics of the product, leaving less room for subjective judgment and improving consistency and repeatability.

Companies can benefit from categorizing their products by therapeutic use, which makes it possible to apply a risk-based approach for managing drug shortage risks for their portfolio of products. A risk level indicating the severity of the impact of a shortage on the patient is assigned based on two factors: therapeutic use of the product and availability of alternatives, as shown in Table 6.1.1-1.

Table 6.1.1-1🔍   Step 1: Define Impact on Patient

"Medically necessary products," "critical medicines," and "life supporting or life sustaining products" have the highest criticality based on patient need; therefore, their shortages present the greatest risk. (Definitions of terms are provided in Section 3.0.) Ensuring an uninterrupted, reliable supply of these products at all times to all patients who need them is key; therefore, robust controls must be in place to proactively manage the risk of shortages of these drugs.

Products used to treat acute short-term conditions or manage chronic long-term diseases (e.g., Parkinson's disease, diabetes, multiple sclerosis) constitute the next level of product indication-based criticality. Although the criticality of these products is not at the same level as that of life supporting or life sustaining therapies, the unavailability of these products over an extended period could harm patients; therefore, shortages of these products cannot be tolerated for long periods.

The lowest level of product indication-based criticality is assigned to medicinal products used for other minor indications (e.g., self-care therapies for minor ailments such as cough and cold). If a product is registered for more than one indication with different levels of indication-based criticality, the more conservative or higher level of criticality should generally be applied for this assessment. For example, if one of the product indications classifies a product as medically necessary, and the second indication classifies it for treatment of a chronic long-term condition, risk planning should be done assuming the higher level of product indication-based criticality, that is, for its use as a medically necessary product.

In addition to the therapeutic use of the product, the second dimension in ranking the impact of a shortage to patients is access to and availability of alternative therapies. Relevant health authorities may need to be consulted to determine where alternatives may exist for a product and for what markets they are available. Where no alternatives are available, especially for products that are medically necessary, life supporting, life sustaining, or used to treat acute short-term or chronic long-term conditions, the impact to the patient is the highest (Risk Level A – red). Therefore, the margin for tolerance of a drug shortage with these products is minimal, and controls for these risks should ensure that:

  • Focus is on proactive prevention of a shortage
  • Time to remedy and return supply conditions toward normal is as short as possible in the event of a shortage

Where an alternative therapy is available in the form of a similar product or other presentations of the same product, the impact to the patient is lower (Risk Level B – yellow). In the event of a shortage, it is important to ensure that these alternatives are accessible to patients in a timely manner to minimize impact (e.g., by preventing interruptions in treatment).

The lowest level of impact (Risk Level C – green) is assigned to medicinal products used for other indications (e.g., self-care therapies for minor ailments such as cough and cold) for which alternatives are readily available or for products used to treat acute short-term or chronic-long terms conditions that have the same product readily available in other presentations.

6.1.2   Step 2: Assess the Likelihood of Shortage

6.1.2 Step 2: Assess the Likelihood of Shortage

In the second step of the triage, the likelihood of shortage for a product is assessed. Hazards that could increase the likelihood of a shortage should be assessed and current controls should be assessed to identify the hazards. Where good, robust controls exist for a hazard, the likelihood of occurrence would be low; if there are limited to no controls for a hazard, the likelihood of occurrence increases. Historical data may be available for certain hazards, and this historical data should be leveraged to assess the relative likelihood of risks.

For example, product sourcing may be one of the controls assessed to determine the likelihood of a shortage. Single-sourcing of a product or key material supplier increases the likelihood of occurrence of a shortage in the event of an issue at that sole-sourced facility or supplier. Other controls may be related to:

  • Infrastructure maintenance and improvements
  • Technology updates
  • Business continuity planning and capacity assessment
  • Process capability
  • Inventory levels
  • Supplier diversification

This assessment of likelihood of occurrence may not be quantitative, which is considered acceptable because it is more important to assess the controls and their likely effectiveness and to understand the relative likelihood of the occurrence of a shortage for identified hazards. In this risk triage model, a simple three-level (high, medium, low) scale for assessing the likelihood of occurrence has been proposed.

Analogy: Single-Engine vs Twin-Engine Aircraft

Imagine two aircraft crossing the Pacific:

  • Single-engine aircraft: if that one engine fails, the aircraft is in immediate danger with no backup. → Single-source supply; likelihood of shortage is High.
  • Twin-engine aircraft + reserve fuel: if one engine fails, the other can keep flying, and with reserve fuel the tolerance to a crisis is high. → Multi-source supply + adequate safety stock; likelihood of shortage is Low.

The logic is the same in the drug supply chain: the better the current controls, the lower the likelihood of shortage.

Key Point: A Qualitative Assessment Is Enough; Quantification Is Not Required

TR68 states clearly that the assessment of likelihood of shortage does not necessarily need to be quantified (for example, by giving a 30% probability). What matters is to:

  • Understand whether the existing controls are effective
  • Be able to compare the relative likelihood of the various hazards

A three-level scale (high/medium/low) is enough to meet the needs of the triage model. This differs from the detailed scoring of traditional FMEA and emphasizes practicality and efficiency.

6.2 Supply Chain and Value Stream Mapping

VSM as a proactive and reactive drug shortage management tool

6.2 Supply Chain and Value Stream Mapping

The MAH is accountable for ensuring that risks for patient safety, product quality, and product availability are adequately managed in the end-to-end product supply chain. In addition to assessing the risks with various suppliers and external service providers (e.g., CDMOs, CLOs) and addressing them through their supplier management controls, the MAH should also review the QRM processes used, especially by their key suppliers and service providers. This can help ensure that these suppliers and service providers have an adequate framework in place, in accordance with the principles of ICH Q9(R1), to assess and manage their quality and compliance risks.

Process mapping, in general, can play a key role in managing the risk of drug shortages particularly as supply chains continue to become increasingly complex. Mapping of the end-to-end product supply processes is one of the most important tools that can be leveraged for managing the risk of drug shortages due to manufacturing and supply problems. VSM (also known as material and information flow mapping), a particularly powerful tool, graphically displays the flow of the manufacturing and transportation processes for a product and highlights potential manufacturing, inventory, and supply stress points. Although VSM is often used to reduce amounts of inventory and inefficiencies across the supply chain, it is also used to maintain proper levels of in-process inventory to ensure predefined final product inventories.

While VSM is a great tool for mapping products and information flow, it is not the only approach available. Other tools that could be used are DRIVE (Define, Review, Identify, Verify, Execute), ICOR (Inputs, Outputs, Controls, and Resources), Cause-and-Effect Diagrams, and Process Flowcharting.

The following elements constitute an effective mapping tool:

  • Team Makeup: The mapping team should be cross-functional and include individuals who are intimately familiar with manufacturing and supply processes and equipment. The most successful mapping exercises involve laboratory technicians and operators. Mapping requires good facilitation, preferably by an objective, neutral individual.
  • Planning and Preparation: The scope of the mapping (local to a site or region or broader in the supply chain) should be identified in advance. The team should establish timelines and goals for the mapping (in this case, specific to drug shortages). The exercise will likely become part of a larger initiative for developing a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan.
  • Evaluation: The team should perform all steps of the mapping process and capture data and information on how the process works. The team should also have access to existing procedures as well as quality events to capture all stress points.
  • Map: After a map of the current state is drawn, listing all the steps and handoffs in the process, a future-state map can be drawn using the same steps to identify risk reducing actions that will meet the needs of the product.
  • Action List: To develop and maintain an effective Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan, an action list needs to be developed to address the gaps and risks based on the future-state map actions.

Commentary: The Core Value of VSM

Core Concept: The MAH's End-to-End Responsibility

The Marketing Authorization Holder (MAH) bears ultimate responsibility for the entire supply chain, and cannot be relieved of risk management obligations by outsourcing to a CDMO. The MAH must not only assess its own risks but also review whether the QRM (quality risk management) frameworks of key service providers such as the CDMO and CLO comply with ICH Q9(R1) principles.

Analogy: VSM Is Like a Production Board on the Shop Floor

Imagine walking into a factory with a large flow chart on the wall showing each process station, how long material stays at each station, and where the bottlenecks are. That is the essence of VSM. VSM derives from the "material and information flow map" of the Toyota Production System (TPS), and the pharmaceutical industry applies it to identify:

  • Where does inventory pile up most? (hidden capital and risk)
  • Which process step is the real bottleneck? (the ceiling on production speed)
  • Is the information flow (orders, schedules) synchronized with the material flow?

VSM turns the supply chain from a "black box" into a "transparent glass box", and is both a preventive and a reactive tool for drug shortages.

Key Point: All Five Elements Are Essential

The five elements of an effective VSM (Team → Planning → Evaluation → Map → Action List) must be completed in sequence. Common mistakes: skipping the "Evaluation" step and drawing the map directly, or finishing the map without creating an "Action List". A VSM without an action list is just a nice decoration and cannot truly reduce shortage risk.

Practical Application: Choosing Other Mapping Tools

When the VSM scope is too large or its complexity exceeds a single process, consider alternative tools:

  • DRIVE: suited to situations that need systematic problem solving
  • ICOR: emphasizes a four-dimensional analysis of inputs/outputs/controls/resources
  • Fishbone diagram: suited to root cause analysis, identifying the multiple layers of causes of a shortage

In CDMO practice, combine VSM with a fishbone diagram: use VSM first to find the bottleneck, then use the fishbone to dig into the root cause.

Analogy: What Does Each "Inventory Hours" Figure Represent?

The "inventory (hrs)" figures in a VSM are the average hours that material waits between two process steps. These are not "production time" but "idle time". The practical meaning of the key figures is as follows:

  • Receiving: 120 hrs = 5 working days
    After arrival, material waits 5 days before incoming inspection. This is a raw material buffer stock, ensuring that material is still on hand even if a supplier delivers late. But too high a figure also means tied-up capital and potential quality risk (especially for temperature-sensitive materials).
  • Incoming Inspection: 33 hrs ≈ 1.4 days
    The waiting time for incoming inspection, reflecting QC laboratory scheduling and testing cycle. It can be shortened with rapid microbiological methods (RMM) or pre-approved materials.
  • Inventory: 0.5 hrs
    Material flows almost immediately to the next step, meaning this buffer is practically absent: highly efficient, but also lacking flexibility.
  • QC & Final Release: 95 hrs = about 4 days
    This is the biggest bottleneck in the whole process! The time finished product waits for QC release accounts for 26% of total waiting time. This is the step most worth investing in for improvement.
  • Warehousing/Ship: 28.8 hrs ≈ 1.2 days
    Warehouse waiting before shipment, related to global distribution schedules.

Key Point: The QC Bottleneck Is the Silent Killer of Drug Supply

In this VSM, the 95 hrs at QC & Final Release is the largest inventory accumulation point. This means:

  • If QC has an OOS (out-of-specification result) investigation, these 95 hrs can balloon to 500+ hrs
  • Every batch that goes on QC Hold may delay shipment and accumulate into a shortage
  • A CDMO should prioritize investment in: faster alternative sterility test methods (such as rapid SAL methods), more adequate QC staffing schedules, and stability programs started earlier

Core Concept: The Dual Purpose of VSM

The biggest difference between a pharmaceutical VSM and a traditional Lean Manufacturing VSM is the purpose:

  • Traditional VSM: minimize inventory → reduce cost
  • Pharmaceutical VSM: maintain appropriate inventory → ensure no stock-outs

Take the 120 hrs at Receiving: from a traditional Lean viewpoint this is "waste", but from a pharmaceutical supply resilience viewpoint, it is a necessary safety buffer against the 3-4 week lead time of API suppliers (India/China). Whether it is reasonable depends on risk assessment, not on the pursuit of pure efficiency.

6.3 Managing Drug Shortage Risks Due to Aging Manufacturing Facilities and Technologies

Causes, continual improvement, and lifecycle management of analytical technologies

6.3 Overview & 6.3.1 Causes and Effects of Aging

Manufacturing and quality risks introduced by aging or legacy facilities and technologies (e.g., out-of-date manufacturing facilities, equipment, analytical technologies) that are not fully up to current standards or expectations can contribute to an increased likelihood of issues that could potentially result in drug shortages. These risks should therefore be proactively identified and managed during the lifecycle of the manufacturing facility, process, equipment, or analytical methods. In order to accomplish the objectives of ICH Q10, continual improvement is an important element of product realization and of maintaining a state of control.

"Product realization is achieved by designing, planning, implementing, maintaining, and continuously improving a system that allows the consistent delivery of products with appropriate quality attributes. Continual improvement is facilitated through the implementation of quality improvements appropriate to the current level of process and product knowledge."
— EU GMP Guidelines on Pharmaceutical Quality Systems (ICH Q10 context)

6.3.1 Causes and Effects of Aging Facilities, Processes, and Analytical Technologies

Some of the main reasons why facilities, processes, or analytical technologies become obsolete are:

  • Lack of long-term planning and poor understanding of the importance of reinvestment and recapitalization of facilities and processes
  • Concern about the unanticipated negative impact of improvements due to a lack of robust process knowledge or of knowledge and experience with the new technology
  • Short-term focus on maintaining or reducing costs, either cost of goods sold or capital expenses
  • Negative financial return on investment due to low drug prices (e.g., generics)
  • Challenges, long lead times, and a lack of harmonized processes for global regulatory approvals across multiple health authorities
  • Absence of positive mindset, attitude, and culture for change and continuous improvement

These factors can produce the decline that occurs over time, for example:

  • As a cost-saving effort, a company may decide to move to a limited maintenance schedule on equipment or reduce the availability of critical spare parts and stock of intermediates. The lowered or partial maintenance is believed to save costs; however, breakdowns due to the lack of maintenance or a negative trend in the manufacturing environment may stop production or lead to product rejections in the future, at a far higher cost.
  • A conscious decision to drastically reduce or eliminate facility, process, and/or equipment optimization and improvement activities, over the long run, can result in antiquated and noncompliant facilities and processes with high levels of variability and risk. Additionally, aging facilities may limit the ability to increase capacity to meet the product demand.
  • Maintaining a false assumption that equipment will not become obsolete in the future and will continue to be supported by the equipment manufacturer is imprudent. Equipment is designed with an intended life span. When the manufacturer fails to plan for equipment obsolescence, it often results in a quick upgrade done at a high cost and with some loss of production time.
  • In some cases, a process or analytical technique was designed for one product or output, and a change in a product or output might be incompatible with the old process or product.

Quality and GMP compliance gaps associated with such aging facilities, processes, and technologies should be addressed even though the existing equipment or methods may be functional. Well-trained personnel with a process ownership mentality may make such facilities run, but when such personnel retire or move to other tasks, the facility, processes, or analytical methods see their first excursions.

The continual maintenance and improvement of facilities, processes, and analytical methods is essential to maintain specified product quality and to assure supplies of the product.

Commentary: Root Causes and Consequences of Aging Facilities

Core Concept: "Aging" Is Not Just About Age

The "aging" of facilities, processes and analytical technologies does not equal a long service life. TR68 defines aging as a state of no longer meeting current standards or regulatory expectations. A facility built in 2005 and never updated may already be "aged" in GMP compliance, process capability or equipment support. The real trigger of aging is often a management decision, not time itself.

Analogy: The Deferred Maintenance Trap

Imagine a car owner who decides to "save money" by skipping regular servicing. For the first few months everything looks fine, but engine wear quietly accumulates. By the time the engine fails completely, the repair costs ten times the normal servicing, and the car is also off the road for two weeks.

Pharmaceutical facilities are exactly the same:

  • Short-term "savings": reducing the maintenance budget, delaying equipment upgrades
  • Long-term "cost": equipment failure and production stoppage, FDA Warning Letters, product recalls, even plant closure

Many cases in FDA 483s and Warning Letters stem from "Inadequate maintenance program" or "Equipment not maintained in a state of control", which is precisely the regulatory consequence of the deferred maintenance trap.

Key Point: "Key Person Dependency" Is a Hidden Aging Risk

TR68 points out an aging risk that is often overlooked: the operation of a facility/process depends heavily on the personal knowledge of a few senior employees. These people know the equipment's "temperament" and informal operating tricks, but this knowledge has never been systematically documented. When they retire or leave, their successors follow the SOPs yet deviations occur frequently. This is not a new problem; it is the first time the aging system is "honestly presented".

Countermeasure: conduct periodic "knowledge risk assessments" to identify processes with high personal dependency, and require knowledge transfer and SOP updates.

Practical Application: Aging Facility Risk Scoring Matrix

It is advisable to establish an aging risk assessment for facilities and equipment, covering:

  • Equipment manufacturer support status (whether discontinued, availability of spare parts)
  • Compliance gaps against current GMP regulations
  • Trend in maintenance event frequency over the past 3 years
  • Whether the design is prone to human error (human error vulnerability)
  • Trend of the process capability index (Cpk): is it declining?

The results should be entered in the Site Risk Register and linked directly to the Capital Expenditure Plan (CapEx Plan).

6.3.2 Continual Improvement of Aging Facilities and Processes

Technology innovation as a driver for quality and supply reliability

6.3.2 Continual Improvement of Aging Facilities and Processes

Processing technology and facility designs are forever evolving. The motivation of suppliers to develop and distribute new technologies is mainly driven by industry seeking new solutions for current processing problems. However, implementation of these solutions becomes challenging in spite of the value they can bring.

Given this, implementation of new technology has resulted not just in financial viability, but an overall improvement in quality and process efficiency. For example, single-use process technology has many advantages over traditional stainless steel equipment, such as faster equipment turnaround, which enhances production capacities. Also, single-use components or unit operations can be connected via aseptic connector or tube welders, which creates a closed system that enhances the safety of both the product and the operator. The technological improvements of single-use technologies have been widely embraced, not only for new, but existing processes, as the advantages of using this technology outweigh any challenges change commonly bring.

Facility and process improvements due to technology innovation are not always as dramatic as single-use process technology or new modular cleanroom designs; they can be gradual improvements of existing equipment to the next generation. Subtle, but continual improvements demonstrate the right quality attitude and ownership. Such continual improvement activities inevitably lower the risk of process or facility failure, leading to operations that run more reliably and efficiently and resulting in cost reduction.

[More information about single-use systems can be found in PDA Technical Report No. 66: Application of Single-Use Systems in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing.]

Commentary: The Business Case for Continual Improvement

Core Concept: Continual Improvement = a Core Requirement of ICH Q10

ICH Q10 "Pharmaceutical Quality System" lists Continual Improvement as one of the four core elements of the pharmaceutical quality system (alongside knowledge management, PQS and CAPA). This is not a "bonus item" but a basic regulatory expectation. TR68 further stresses that continual improvement is a proactive line of defense against drug shortages caused by facility aging.

Analogy: The "Same Soup, New Bowl" Revolution of Single-Use Technology

Traditional stainless steel equipment is like a large kitchen appliance that has to be cleaned and sanitized every time: time-consuming and laborious, with a cross-contamination risk at each cleaning.

Single-use technology (SUS) is like using disposable containers: throw them away after use and replace them immediately next time, which removes cleaning validation, shortens turnaround time and lowers the risk of batch-to-batch cross-contamination. This is not just a cost consideration but an improvement in supply reliability: faster batch turnaround = more batches per year.

Key Point: Gradual Improvement vs. Disruptive Innovation

TR68 specifically stresses that "gradual improvements" in continual improvement are just as important as "disruptive technological innovation":

  • Upgrade old HMI interfaces to more intuitive touchscreen systems
  • Replace manual records with electronic batch records (eBR)
  • Introduce the real-time monitoring functions of PAT (process analytical technology)

A culture built from these "small steps" is the real long-term safeguard against facility aging. Showing the right quality attitude and sense of ownership is exactly what regulators most want to see in GMP inspections.

Practical Application: Build a Technology Roadmap

Each CDMO site should build a 3-5 year "Technology Roadmap":

  • Inventory all critical equipment and the manufacturer support end dates (End-of-Life)
  • Evaluate the technical advantages of next-generation equipment and the PAC filing requirements
  • Include the PAC timeline (2-5 years) in the lead-up work of the renewal plan
  • Coordinate the regulatory filing strategy for equipment changes with the client MAH

This roadmap should be updated every year and included on the agenda of the site Management Review.

6.3.3 Lifecycle Management and Continual Improvement of Analytical Technologies

PAC timelines, periodic assessments, and control system updates

6.3.3 Lifecycle Management and Continual Improvement of Analytical Technologies

During the commercial lifecycle of a product, analytical technologies continue to improve. However, commercial product analytical control systems—in-process, release, and stability test methods, specifications and acceptance criteria, and reference-standard controls—tend to improve minimally, if at all. This slow pace of change can be attributed to such factors as high cost, resource requirements, and the complexity of the global regulatory environment for making PACs. Revisions to control systems can take two to five years, depending on their extent and the number of countries that need to approve the PAC.

A proactive approach to control system lifecycle management is important to ensure alignment with contemporary product knowledge and industry standards. Lifecycle management of analytical control systems should be reviewed periodically (e.g., every five years), not only in response to control system issues identified (e.g., through investigations), during routine commercial use.

Periodic assessments of an analytical control system should determine whether the system continues to control drug substance and product quality at the product's release and at the end of its shelf life. Such determinations should be based on comprehensive reviews of current information about the molecule, including:

  • Adequate monitoring of relevant product quality attributes
  • Current manufacturing process capability
  • Alignment of acceptance criteria with process capability
  • Compliance with evolving regulatory requirements and expectations
  • The ability to fulfill its intended purpose
  • Current analytical technologies

Periodic assessments of the control system should include evaluations of gaps or improvement opportunities, for example, when a new critical quality attribute is identified because of new information on a molecule (such as antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity), when superior technology is available, or when new regulatory requirements are implemented.

Some examples of when an update to the control system may be needed:

  • The current method has a high failure rate, low throughput rate, or analyst ergonomic or safety limitations
  • The method is no longer suitable for the control system
  • The current method validation is no longer applicable
  • Superior technology is available
  • New or revised specifications or acceptance criteria need to be implemented

Acceptance criteria for current control system test methods should be evaluated against manufacturing and process capability (via analysis of lot-release data) and stability data. Release acceptance criteria can be evaluated by comparing 95 or 99 tolerance intervals of historical batches to current acceptance criteria.

Revisions to current analytical control systems require submission of an application to the relevant health authorities. Therefore, they should be coordinated as much as possible with manufacturing process improvements, transfers, and other activities requiring regulatory approval. Early discussions with relevant health authorities regarding reporting level and documentation requirements for anticipated PACs will allow both the health authorities and the company to plan for the changes in a manner that will help reduce the risk of drug shortages.

Commentary: Lifecycle Management of Analytical Technology

Core Concept: The "Technology Lag" Problem

TR68 reveals a structural contradiction: analytical technology itself advances quickly (a new generation every 3-5 years), but the analytical control system of a drug (release methods, stability methods, specifications) stays almost unchanged. The reason is that each update requires a PAC submission, and PAC approval takes 2-5 years.

This "Technology Lag" means that more accurate and faster analytical methods are already available, but sites are still using methods from twenty years ago; it is not that they do not want to change, but that they cannot afford to or cannot finish changing.

Analogy: The PAC Timeline Is Like a Building Permit for Renovating an Old House

You want to renovate an old house (update an analytical method): you have the new design (new technology), but before starting work you must apply for a building permit from every county and city (PACs in each country). If your house exists in 5 countries, just completing all the permits takes 3-5 years, and while you wait, the problems in the house continue.

This is why TR68 stresses proactive advance planning for updating the analytical control system: start the PAC process before the current method has a problem. Applying only after an OOS or a rise in failure rate is locking the barn door after the horse has bolted, and a drug shortage is hard to avoid.

Key Point: A Periodic Five-Year Review Is a Regulatory Expectation

TR68 clearly recommends that analytical control systems be "reviewed periodically, every five years". This is not just good practice but a regulatory expectation in keeping with the ICH Q10 spirit of continual improvement. A CDMO should establish a formal "Analytical Method Lifecycle Review SOP", including:

  • A trigger point for periodic (at least every 5 years) overall evaluation of the control system
  • A list of events that trigger an immediate review (high failure rate, newly identified CQAs, updated regulatory requirements)
  • A mechanism linking the evaluation results to the PAC plan
  • Verification that the process capability index (Cpk) is consistent with the specifications

Practical Application: Tolerance Intervals and Specification Evaluation

TR68 offers a concrete statistical tool: compare the 95% or 99% tolerance interval of historical batches with the current specifications.

  • If the tolerance interval is too close to the upper/lower specification limit (low Cpk) → the specification may need tightening or the process needs improvement
  • If actual process capability is far better than the specification (Cpk >> 1.33) → there may be an opportunity to widen the release specification to reduce OOS risk

Reference: PDA TR59 (Revised 2012), Utilization of Statistical Methods for Production Monitoring, gives detailed guidance on Cpk calculation and application.

Exercise 2: Analytical Method Update Strategy for a CDMO

Suppose your CDMO is preparing to take on a new biologic contract manufacturing project, and the client's analytical method is an HPLC method developed in 2010. Consider:

  • Does this method meet the current ICH Q2(R2) validation requirements?
  • If an update is needed after method transfer, how should the PAC timeline be integrated with the commercial supply timeline?
  • Has the client MAH started a periodic review of the analytical control system? How should the CDMO assign this responsibility in the contract?
  • If the old method is found to have a high failure rate (> 2%), what is the recommended order of priority for actions?

Hint: the CDMO's Quality Agreement should clearly assign responsibility for analytical method lifecycle management.

6.4 Reducing Drug Shortages Through Expedited Post-Approval Changes

ICH Q12, global Change Protocols (gCP), and regulatory flexibility

6.4 Reducing Drug Shortages Through Expedited Post-Approval Changes

When companies face a potential drug shortage, they can mitigate the risk by expediting a change that may have already been submitted to a national health authority for approval. Current regulatory PAC processes vary among countries in the levels and terminology of change categories, requirements for documentation of changes, the need for preapproval facility inspections, and timelines for approval. For these reasons, companies usually need to run multiple versions of a manufacturing process in parallel for the same product, depending on when each national health authority is likely to approve a change.

In such cases, the company should communicate with the health authorities responsible for trade where a potential drug shortage might occur and request an expedited review of pending PACs or offer a previous version of the product if available in the inventory. Health authorities assess PACs using science and risk-based approaches. However, it would be beneficial if the regulatory approval processes for PACs could be harmonized.

An example of an effort towards harmonization is The International Coalition of Medicines Regulatory Authorities' (ICMRA) Pharmaceutical Quality Knowledge Management System Initiative (PQKMS). According to ICH Q10, Annex 1, companies that demonstrate an effective PQS and process and product understanding, including the use of QRM principles, may be eligible for enhanced regulatory approaches. This is sometimes referred to as "regulatory flexibility," where there may be an opportunity to "optimize science and risk-based post-approval change processes to maximize benefits from innovation and continual improvement."

Additionally, ICH Q12: Technical and Regulatory Considerations for Pharmaceutical Product Lifecycle Management provides a framework via PQS to facilitate the management of PACs in a more predictable and efficient manner throughout the product lifecycle. Such opportunities may include managing more PACs in the PQS only, rather than as a prior-approval PAC. The intent of the guideline is to promote continual improvement and innovation, while strengthening quality assurance and reliable product supply.

Commentary: Accelerated PAC Review and Regulatory Flexibility

Core Concept: ICH Q12 "Regulatory Flexibility"

Regulatory Flexibility is an incentive mechanism put forward in ICH Q10: if a company can demonstrate a sound PQS (pharmaceutical quality system), adequate process and product understanding, and effective use of QRM principles, regulators may allow it to manage some PACs by "post-implementation notification" instead of "prior approval", greatly shortening the change implementation cycle.

In short: the better a company's quality culture, the lighter the regulatory oversight. This is the concrete return regulators give to companies they "trust".

Core Concept: The Core Tools of ICH Q12

ICH Q12 introduces two key concepts:

  • PLCM (Product Lifecycle Management Document): define at the time of filing which future changes can be managed at a lower reporting level, so that both regulators and the company know what to expect.
  • EFPIA/PAC classification: stratify PACs by scientific risk; high-risk changes still need prior approval, while low-risk ones can be notified afterwards or managed only within the PQS.

For a CDMO, understanding the ICH Q12 framework helps assess regulatory timeline risk more accurately during contract negotiations.

Analogy: Regulatory Flexibility Is Like a "Good Driver Discount"

Insurance companies give drivers with a long accident-free record lower premiums, because they trust their safety record. The logic of ICH Q12 regulatory flexibility is the same: for a company with a robust PQS, regulators "trust" its internal quality management capability and therefore grant looser reporting categories.

Conversely, for companies with a history of Warning Letters, major deviations or a weak PQS, regulators may require review before implementation even of minor process changes.

Key Point: The Real-World Challenge of Parallel Multi-Country PACs

TR68 points out a practical dilemma: PAC classification names differ by country (US: Major/Moderate/Minor; EU: Type IA/IB/II; Japan: Major/Minor) and approval timelines vary, so the approval status of the same process change is out of sync across countries.

The result: the company is forced to maintain multiple versions of the manufacturing process at the same time, one for countries that have approved and another for those that have not. This is not just logistically complex but a nightmare for inventory management, and directly increases the risk of drug shortage.

Global Change Protocols (gCP) and Streamlined Changes

The industry should work with health authorities to develop predefined global change protocols (gCP) that can be available and used as a standard (non-product-specific) template for the categorization, predefined data requirements, and implementation of the different types of changes. Common gCPs that are developed in partnership with health authorities for the different types of changes can help standardize activities and requirements across the industry for a particular change type.

Each company could then use the gCP for the relevant type of change as a starting point in developing the submission for each of the changes in the PAC Management Protocol for their product. The company should update the predefined gCP with the specific aspects of the change for their particular product.

Streamlined Changes — Categories Eligible for Expedited Review

Companies and health authorities could work together to enable, where appropriate, streamlined review and approval of the following changes, provided that agreed-upon acceptance criteria from health authorities are met and that these changes do not introduce increased risks to patient safety or product quality or efficacy:

Enhanced Redundancy:

  • Additional manufacturing site or line already approved in at least one significant market or country that can serve as a reference
  • Additional raw material or API suppliers
  • Additional suppliers of container closure or other processing components
  • Additional dosage forms already approved in at least one reference country

Improved Process and Product Controls:

  • New or improved equipment and technology
  • Manufacturing changes that lead to improved process capability
  • New analytical technologies that enhance product controls, including adventitious agent controls

Other Types of Changes (provided that validated and preset criteria are met):

  • Change in reference standards
  • Use of new working cell banks
  • Addition of a product to a multiproduct facility
  • Scale-up of a manufacturing process
  • Extension of shelf life with supporting documentation
  • Change in utilities (e.g., HVAC, clean steam) or their operation with no impact on product quality
  • Change in equipment (design and/or operating principle) with no impact on product quality
  • Change in steam-in-place or clean-in-place process
  • Change in environmental monitoring program (e.g., sample location and frequency)
  • Change in cleaning methods

In all cases, the company concerned should be able to demonstrate that it has an effective PQS in place. The risk assessment for the change(s) should be included in the submission for the change to demonstrate that appropriate risk control measures are in place and provide assurance that patient safety, product quality, and product efficacy are not impacted.

Commentary: gCP and Accelerated Review Mechanisms

Core Concept: What Is a gCP (global Change Protocol)?

A global Change Protocol (gCP) is a "standard change template" jointly drawn up in advance by industry and health authorities of various countries. For specific types of common changes (for example, adding a manufacturing site or replacing equipment), the gCP predefines:

  • Required validation activities
  • Data requirements
  • Acceptance criteria
  • Recommended reporting category

A company need only use the gCP as a starting point and add product-specific information to complete the PAC submission, greatly shortening preparation time and improving review efficiency.

Note for CDMO Clients: Adding a Manufacturing Site PAC Benefits Directly

TR68 Section 6.4 specifically lists "Additional manufacturing site or line already approved in at least one significant market" as the first change type eligible for accelerated review.

For CDMO clients (MAHs), this means:

  • If your drug already has an approved manufacturing site in a core ICH market (US, EU, Japan), for example the original site, and the new CDMO site has an equivalent GMP level, you can apply for accelerated PAC review
  • The gCP framework can shorten approval time for a new site from 3-5 years to 1-2 years (in an ideal scenario)
  • The CDMO should help the MAH prepare standardized site qualification documents (Site Master File) so that they meet the default data requirements of the gCP

Analogy: gCP Is Like a "Universal Plug Adapter"

In the past, each country's PAC requirements were like different socket standards around the world: the same equipment needs a different adapter in each country (different filing formats and data requirements).

A gCP is like designing a "universal adapter": it pre-includes the greatest common denominator of the major requirements of each country, so a company need only add a small amount of localized content to the universal framework to make simultaneous submissions (Simultaneous Submission) to multiple markets. This is a step towards truly global regulation.

Practical Application: The PQS Must Cover PAC Management

TR68 explicitly requires that a company's PQS be extended to cover PAC planning and assessment, in particular:

  • How to perform risk assessment on changes
  • How to demonstrate risk control measures
  • Establishing a PAC Management Protocol

For a CDMO, this means your Change Control SOP must contain a clear PAC classification decision tree and connect with the client MAH's global filing strategy. The PIC/S document "How to Evaluate the Effectiveness of a Pharmaceutical Quality System in Risk-Based Change Management" provides a concrete evaluation framework and is recommended as a reference for procedure design.

Core Concept: Incentives for Regulators to Encourage Innovation

TR68 makes a forward-looking recommendation: health authorities should provide regulatory incentives to companies that adopt innovative technologies (to reduce risk and measurably prevent shortages). The innovative technologies named include:

  • Isolator technology replacing conventional open processes
  • Closed manufacturing systems
  • Rapid microbiology methods
  • Technologies that shorten manufacturing cycle time
  • Technologies that improve process capability

This incentive framework is still at the advocacy stage, but it represents an important direction in regulatory science, and is worth taking into account in a CDMO's technology investment decisions.

Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan

Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan: Overview

7.0 Overview — Plan Purpose and Scope

The risk assessment triage results in an assignment of a drug shortage risk priority level to each product based on the product's indication, availability of alternative therapies, and probability of a shortage. Section 7.0 describes how a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan should be developed based on the outcomes of the risk triage. While agencies are working on new guidances, such a plan might also be known as a Shortage Prevention Plan or Mitigation Plan in the future.

A Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan can provide a holistic framework at a product level to address both the proactive and reactive management of a drug shortage. This plan should be proactively developed for medically necessary, life supporting, or life sustaining products and shared with health authorities during inspections. Medium risk products used for short-term acute conditions or long-term chronic conditions can also benefit from a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan, especially if the risk triage has identified potential drug shortage risks at Risk Priority Level 1.

A Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan can be an effective mechanism for managing a product's continuously changing drug shortage risk profile. Drug shortages may present potential opportunities for product diversion and counterfeiting. Therefore, a company's surveillance program can leverage the Drug Shortage Risk Register as useful input into its process for managing product diversion and counterfeiting risks that may arise due to shortages.

Commentary: Why Is This Plan Needed?

Core Definition: Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan

This is an integrated, product-specific framework document that covers both:

  • Proactive prevention: identifying risks early and building resilience
  • Reactive response: emergency procedures when a shortage occurs

In the future it may be named a "Shortage Prevention Plan" or "Mitigation Plan"; the substance is the same.

Key Point: Which Products Must Have This Plan?

  • Should proactively develop: medically necessary products, life-sustaining drugs, and life-supporting drugs
  • Can also benefit: moderate-risk products and drugs for acute or chronic conditions, especially where the Risk Triage indicates Risk Priority Level 1

During health authority inspections, the plan should be proactively disclosed to demonstrate the manufacturer's risk management maturity.

Analogy: A Drug Shortage Plan Is Like a "Typhoon Response Plan"

Evacuation routes, supply checklists, and contact numbers must be ready in normal times (outside typhoon season) so that you can respond in an orderly way when a typhoon arrives. The Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan is the pharmaceutical company's "supply chain typhoon response plan": it is not something improvised after a crisis hits, but is built in advance, rehearsed regularly, and kept up to date.

The Special Role of the CDMO

The CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization) is a critical supply chain node in this plan. When the MAH (marketing authorization holder) develops the plan, the CDMO should proactively provide the following information:

  • Its own capacity status and bottlenecks (capacity bottlenecks)
  • Supply risks for raw materials and critical materials
  • Alternate process or supplier switching options

If the CDMO passively waits for instructions, the plan may contain "blind spots", leading to underestimation of shortage risk.

The 5-Component Structure of a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan

The Five Elements of the Prevention and Response Plan

Plan Components — English Source Text (p34–35)

A Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan consists of the following information:

  1. Identification of drug shortage hazards: An end-to-end risk assessment of the product's value chain until the first economic customer — its manufacturing operations, storage, and transportation — should identify potential sources of drug shortages. The Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan should address the management of the drug shortage risks listed in the Drug Shortage Risk Register. Typical visibility for a MAH ends after shipment to wholesale distributors.
  2. Assessment of the impact of a potential drug shortage: Based on the product indications, an assessment should be made and documented of which patient population would be affected by a drug shortage and what this effect would be. In addition, clinical risk assessments would be helpful in understanding the clinical impact of a drug shortage.
  3. Risk control plan (short-term and long-term): This section should describe the controls identified from the end-to-end risk assessments of the product, how the risk control strategy will proactively control drug shortage risks, what measures can be taken to ensure product supply resilience, and a plan to respond to a shortage and ensure a prompt return to normal operations in the event of a shortage.
  4. Communication plan: The Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan should include a communication plan for drug shortages. The AESGP, EFPIA, EGA, and PPTA collaborative document provides guidance on communications related to product shortages, including possible triggers for communication strategies.
  5. Risk review and monitoring: The Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan should be a living document that is reviewed and updated whenever a product's risk profile changes. Potential causes include approval of a new product or dosage form, a new indication, a new market, shutdown of a facility, increase in product usage, or new quality or manufacturing issues.

Commentary: The Five Elements in Detail

Element 1: Identify Hazards - Drug Shortage Risk Register

This is the starting point of the whole plan. Through an end-to-end (E2E) risk assessment covering manufacturing, storage, transportation, and other steps in the value chain, all potential sources of shortage risk are systematically listed to form the "Drug Shortage Risk Register".

The MAH's visibility typically ends once goods are sold to wholesalers, which means information on the last mile from retail pharmacies to patients often has to come from other channel partners.

Element 2: Assess Patient Impact - Patient Impact Assessment

Based on the drug's indications, assess "which patient populations" will be affected by a shortage and "to what degree". This assessment must be documented and linked to the Risk Priority Level in Section 6.2.

Clinical Risk Assessment is especially important here, particularly for critically ill patients or situations with no alternative therapy.

Element 3: Risk Control Plan - Short-term & Long-term

This has two levels: short-term emergency measures (e.g., emergency stock reallocation, priority scheduling) and long-term resilience building (e.g., dual-supplier strategy, qualification of a backup line).

This element must answer: "If a shortage occurs, how can we return to normal operations (Return to Normal Operations) as quickly as possible?"

Element 4: Communication Plan

Clearly define the "What (what to report), Who (to whom), When (at what time), and How (by what means)", as well as the conditions that trigger communication (Triggers). The joint document from AESGP, EFPIA, EGA, and PPTA provides guidance on a communication framework.

Element 5: Risk Review and Monitoring - Living Document

The plan is a "living document" and must be updated whenever the product's risk profile (Risk Profile) changes. Events that trigger a review include approval of a new indication, entry into a new market, facility closure, withdrawal of GMP certification, inventory falling below the safety stock level, and abnormal usage.

Key Point: How Does the CDMO Fit into the Five Elements?

  • Element 1: The CDMO must proactively disclose its own process bottlenecks and equipment single point of failure (SPOF) risks
  • Element 3: The CDMO and MAH jointly agree on plans to qualify backup capacity or alternate processes
  • Element 5: When any event affecting supply occurs at the CDMO (e.g., equipment failure, raw material stockout), a plan update must be triggered immediately

  • A disruption in the CDMO's process can directly affect 3 key nodes
  • When a problem occurs at the CDMO, the impact on the MAH's downstream supply (Wholesaler → Patient) may cascade
  • The CDMO must remain highly transparent to the MAH and provide real-time supply status reporting

The MAH's Visibility Boundary

According to the original text of TR68, "Typical visibility for a MAH ends after shipment to wholesale distributors."

This means the MAH usually cannot directly monitor inventory at retail pharmacies, hospital pharmacies, or the patient end. Information for the Pharmacy and Patient nodes therefore has to be fed back through wholesalers or regional affiliates (Affiliates), creating an information gap (Information Gap).

Analogy: The Supply Chain Is Like a "Plumbing System"

From raw materials to patients is like water flowing from the source to the users. The CDMO is a major pressure-boosting station in the supply network: if the station fails, no matter how well-built the downstream pipes are, the end users (patients) will still lose their water. The robustness of the CDMO therefore directly determines the resilience of the entire supply chain.

8.0

Stakeholder Management and Communications related to Drug Shortages

Stakeholder Management and Drug Shortage Communication: Regulatory Obligations and Collaborative Dialog

8.0 Legal Obligations and Collaborative Dialog

Multiple stakeholders are affected by, and need to be involved in, addressing a drug shortage — manufacturers, health authorities, distributors, pharmacies, healthcare providers and, sometimes, patients, depending on the cause(s) and level of the shortage.

The legal obligations for MAHs to notify the relevant health authorities of a potential drug shortage are described in:

  • Article 13 of Commission Directive 2003/94/EC — for the European Union
  • Section 506C of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act — for the United States

In addition, Part 1, Section 5.71 of the EU GMP Guidelines has been updated to provide guidance on notification of restrictions in supply.

A collaborative dialog between the company and the relevant health authorities is key to facilitating the control and/or prevention of a shortage and, ultimately, ensuring a continued supply of medicinal products for patients.

Companies need to consider many things when reacting to a current or impending shortage of one of their medicines in a given country or region. Developing a harmonized risk communication mechanism to alert health authorities of a potential or existing shortage is important to creating a shared understanding of the shortage.

The guidance paper Quality and Manufacturing Driven Supply Disruptions: Industry Communication Principles to Authorities provides communication principles and a framework to harmonize reporting and communications between MAHs and health authorities regarding drug shortages arising from quality and/or manufacturing disruptions. The paper proposes a harmonized trigger point, time point, and recipient(s) for communicating potential drug shortage events for nationally and centrally approved products in Europe.

Relevant health authorities, those where the product is marketed and where the shortage is expected to occur, must be notified when a meaningful disruption could occur in the supply of medicines due to manufacturing and quality issues, that is, when normal volumes of medicines cannot be delivered for further distribution.

A safety or clinical risk assessment should inform the risk communication strategy so that appropriate risk mitigation actions can be taken at the healthcare provider and/or patient level. For defective products (e.g., those with a potential lack of sterility assurance), a comparison might be needed of the clinical benefits and risks for patients of continued product use, especially when no alternative product or treatment is available.

Commentary: Regulatory Obligations and the Spirit of Collaboration

Analogy: Activating the Emergency Response Team (ERT): Collaboration, Not One-Way Notification

The traditional view is that "notifying the health authority" is a one-way compliance action. TR68 emphasizes "Collaborative Dialog", which is more like the scene after a plant's ERT is activated, when the fire brigade, facilities staff, and safety personnel command together: each role has its own expertise, but the shared goal is to minimize harm.

Building this kind of "partnership" with health authorities is far more efficient than responding passively after the fact.

Key Point: When Is Notification Required?

The notification trigger is: when a "meaningful disruption" in supply may occur due to a manufacturing or quality problem, i.e., the inability to distribute normal quantities as usual.

The risk matrix (Table 6.1.1-1🔍) tiers the urgency of notification (Risk Level A / B / C) according to the therapeutic classification and the availability of alternative therapies.

Special Situation of Defective Products: Clinical Benefit vs. Shortage Risk

When a product has a potential quality defect (e.g., inadequate sterility assurance) but there is no alternative therapy at all, TR68 requires a "Clinical Benefit vs. Risk Comparison":

  • Continue use: there may be a quality risk, but patients at least have a drug available
  • Stop supply: ensures no quality risk, but patients may face a more serious interruption of treatment

This decision must be jointly negotiated between the company and the health authority, not made unilaterally.

8.1

Key Risk-Indicating Information to Compile for Health Authorities

Key Risk-Indicating Information to Submit to the Health Authority

8.1 Information Framework

When anticipating a shortage may occur, companies should compile key pieces of information for health authorities when their involvement is needed. Knowing the reasons for the stock shortage or depletion not only facilitates the implementation of corrective and preventive actions (CAPA), but it can also help develop practical marketplace solutions for the shortage.

Based on product ownership, it is important to openly clarify communication accountabilities between the health authority and the (1) manufacturer or first distributor; (2) wholesaler, agent, trader, distributor, repackager, or relabeler; and (3) suppliers of the product to the patient (e.g., retail pharmacies, hospital pharmacies, physicians).

Patient-Related Information

  • Types of patients (e.g., by disease, demographics, age, genetics, origin) using the medicine. A good level of clinical understanding is important to assess whether any patient groups may be particularly affected (e.g., neonates, patients with cancer or diabetes).
  • Scale of the problem:
    • Likely number of patients treated with the product each month and the percentage of patients that may be affected
    • Current market share, usage profile, and monthly volume of prescriptions
    • Monthly sales volume of the product and the proportion affected
    • Possible local, regional, or global impact on product supplies

Distribution Information in the Country or Region

  • Name and address of the primary distributor of the product, if any
  • If other distributors (i.e., secondary wholesalers) supply the product
  • If the product is supplied only to hospitals or to both hospital and retail pharmacies
  • If the product is supplied through other channels, such as clinics and nursing homes
  • Opportunities for parallel import, repackaging, and relabeling, depending on regulations

Current Stock in Each Country or Region

  • Whether the product is currently out of stock in the country or region, or if another version of the product or process is available during the stock-out period
  • Whether the product stock is expected to be depleted in the near future (e.g., within the next few weeks or months) because of the supply issue
  • Whether the product is in stock but available quantities are insufficient to meet patient needs
  • Whether stockpiling is requested by the government, legislation, or guidance or as result of the Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan

Commentary: Information Checklist the CDMO Should Maintain

Communication Accountability

TR68 explicitly requires clarifying the notification accountability chain:

  1. Manufacturer / first distributor (i.e., the CDMO or the MAH's own plant)
  2. Wholesalers, agents, and repackagers
  3. End suppliers (retail pharmacies, hospital pharmacies, physicians)

The CDMO belongs to Tier 1 and must ensure that Tier 1 information is complete and accurate, as the basis for the MAH's notification to the health authority.

Three Categories of Information the CDMO Should Maintain

A. Patient-Related Information

  • Description of the product's main indications and target patient populations
  • Estimated Monthly Prescription Volume
  • Identification of particularly vulnerable populations (e.g., neonates, cancer patients, diabetic patients)

B. Distribution Information

  • Names and addresses of the main wholesalers
  • List of secondary wholesalers (if any)
  • Description of supply channels (hospital vs. retail vs. clinic)
  • Assessment of the possibility of parallel imports (regulations differ by market)

C. Current Stock

  • Current stock quantity (by market/warehouse)
  • Projected stock depletion date
  • Whether current stock is sufficient to meet patient demand
  • Whether a government-mandated emergency reserve exists

Formula: Calculating Days of Supply

Days of Supply
= Current Inventory
  ÷ Daily Consumption Rate

Where:
  Daily Consumption Rate = Monthly Sales Volume ÷ 30

Example:
  Current Inventory = 150,000 units
  Monthly Sales Volume = 30,000 units
  Daily Consumption Rate = 30,000 ÷ 30 = 1,000 units/day
  Days of Supply = 150,000 ÷ 1,000 = 150 days

The CDMO should regularly calculate Days of Supply for each product in its system and automatically trigger the notification process when the number of days falls below an alert level (e.g., 30 or 60 days).

8.2

Risk-Based Decision-Making by the Company and Health Authorities

Risk Decision Framework for the Company and the Health Authority

8.2 Four HA Decision Points

In the event of a shortage, having a product risk assessment developed proactively using the triage method and the risk matrices in Section 6.1, and the key risk-indicating information compiled in Section 8.1 becomes very useful in determining the appropriate actions to take. In addition to the manufacturer or MAHs, CAPAs to address the risks may need to be owned by other stakeholders in the supply chain. Therefore, communication between the stakeholders — including affiliates of the MAH in a country or region, distribution centers, or pharmacies as well as the press — is most important to detect unintended consequences of a corrective or preventive measure early in the system.

Several decisions may need to be made by health authorities in response to reports of actual or pending product shortages:

HA Decision Point 1: Formal Involvement

Whether to become formally involved in managing the shortage or in coordinating a response. The risk triage method will be useful in determining:

  • Whether health authorities need to become directly involved (e.g., by taking direct measures, coordinating responses, developing work plans and action items)
  • Whether becoming directly involved is not necessary at that time
  • Whether health authorities need to monitor the situation closely without becoming directly involved

HA Decision Point 2: Alternative Supply Sourcing

Whether to facilitate the sourcing of alternative supplies of the product, involving determining:

  • Whether the MAH should be allowed to import and supply a version of the product intended for a different market that may not be authorized in the country
  • Whether this product could be batch packaged for the other market and supplied in that country's packaging and delivery
  • Whether the imported product needs to be repackaged and, if so, the level of regulatory approval and oversight required
  • Whether healthcare professionals should be advised that an unlicensed version of the product or a clinically equivalent product is available

HA Decision Point 3: Expedite Regulatory Approvals

Whether to expedite approval of a request to use an alternate unlicensed facility or a post-approval change submitted by the MAH. A related decision is whether the approval should be conditional, temporary, or permanent.

HA Decision Point 4: Communication Strategy

What communication strategies will be required and how those strategies will ensure that companies, health authorities, healthcare professionals, and/or patients can take appropriate risk mitigation actions. After the clinical risk assessment, the health authority may decide to advise healthcare professionals to:

  • Give priority to certain groups of patients (e.g., patients already treated) for continued treatment
  • Use a different medicine or treatment for new (or all) patients
  • If a product is defective (e.g., loss of sterility assurance), weigh the clinical benefits and risks for patients of continued use when no alternative product or treatment is available

Any time a shortage situation is encountered it should be viewed as an opportunity for the organization to review their risk register and, where relevant, assess suitable updates to their risk control program to improve prevention practices.

Commentary: The Four HA Decision Points

Expanding CAPA Ownership

TR68 specifically notes: the owner of a CAPA should not be only the manufacturer or MAH; it may also need to include other stakeholders in the supply chain. For example:

  • Regional affiliates (Affiliates) own CAPA for local inventory allocation
  • Distribution centers own CAPA for logistics optimization
  • Pharmacies own CAPA for drug substitution counseling

The CDMO may also need to take on part of CAPA ownership, especially when the root cause of the shortage lies in a process problem at the CDMO.

Analogy: The Hard Choice Between "Defective Product vs. No Product"

Imagine an emergency room with only one batch of Epinephrine injection left, "labeled with a possible error", and no alternative. The physician's choice is: use this batch, with its labeling risk, to treat a patient in anaphylactic shock, or do nothing at all?

TR68's HA Decision Point 4 addresses exactly this situation: "whether the clinical benefit of using a defective product outweighs the harm caused by the shortage". This decision should not be made by the manufacturer alone; it must be the result of joint negotiation between the company and the health authority.

Practical Implications of HA Decision Point 3 for the CDMO

When the MAH asks the health authority for "expedited use of an Alternate Unlicensed Facility", the CDMO may be that "alternate facility". This means:

  • The CDMO must be prepared in advance to accept an urgent inspection or document review by the health authority
  • The contract between the CDMO and MAH should clearly specify the expedited authorization process for emergency situations
  • Approval may be "Conditional" or "Temporary", and the CDMO must understand the corresponding obligations

A Shortage Event Is an Opportunity for Improvement (Continuous Improvement)

The TR68 conclusion specifically emphasizes: every shortage event should be treated as an opportunity for the organization to review the risk register and update the risk control plan. Specific actions include:

  • Investigate in depth the true root cause of the shortage (True Root Cause)
  • Revise CAPA to prevent shortages of similar products from recurring
  • Identify and close the system gaps (Gaps) exposed by this shortage event

This principle fully aligns with the spirit of Continual Improvement in ICH Q10.

Section 4 Summary: Key Points Review

  • 7.0 Five-step cycle: The Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan consists of five steps: "identify hazards → assess patient impact → risk control plan (short-term + long-term) → communication plan → review and monitoring". It is a continuously cycling living document and should be proactively shared during health authority inspections.
  • 7.0 E2E value chain: From raw materials → drug substance → drug product → packaging → warehousing and distribution → affiliates → wholesalers → pharmacies → patients. The CDMO typically covers the first three to four nodes and is a critical risk concentration point in the supply chain; the MAH's direct visibility usually ends at the wholesaler.

9.0 Conclusions

Summary of the Core Arguments of the TR-68 Framework • p38-p39

9.0 Conclusions

The objective of ensuring an uninterrupted supply of safe, efficacious products to patients is shared by regulators, legislators, healthcare providers, and industry bringing them together to communicate for jointly developing solutions that serve the best interests of patients. As the risk to the patient population from a drug shortage rises, the level of rigor, effort, and cross-functional collaboration within an organization to address the risk should also increase. This effort should be coupled with effective communication among the organization, health authorities, and other relevant stakeholders to proactively manage drug shortage risks.

Although challenging, the industry should address the concerns associated with drug shortages, given the objective of protecting patients from harm. Proactively identifying relevant drug shortage hazards and robustly managing drug shortage risks in the end-to-end product value chain, as well as effectively and promptly communicating potential shortages to all stakeholders, is essential to reduce the risk of drug shortages.

TR-68 provides a practical framework that companies can use, a triage model to assess drug shortage risks, and templates for developing a Drug Shortage Risk Register and a Drug Shortage Prevention and Response Plan at a product level. It also describes ways that industry can engage more proactively with health authorities to request expedited review and approval of certain PACs by multiple health authorities to reduce the risks of drug shortages. This could be achieved by using agreed-upon PAC management plans and harmonized gCPs. Such protocols would be helpful for companies to address the risk of drug shortages due to aging facilities, processes, and analytical technologies by modernizing their facilities and implementing new technologies. This technical report also leverages the guidance developed by the AESPG, EFPIA, EGA, and PPTA collaboration for communications between MAHs and health authorities.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

Three Core Themes

The TR-68 conclusions present three mutually reinforcing themes:

  • Patient protection: ensuring uninterrupted supply is a shared mission of regulators, legislators, healthcare providers, and industry.
  • Risk-driven level of effort: the higher the risk, the greater the rigor and cross-functional collaboration should be.
  • Proactive communication: effective, timely communication with health authorities and all stakeholders is the core mechanism for preventing shortages.

The Three Tools Provided by TR-68

  • Triage Model: assesses the shortage risk level
  • Risk Register template: systematic product-level documentation
  • Prevention & Response Plan template: preparation beforehand + response afterward

In addition, the report stresses that PAC Management Plans and harmonized gCPs (good change practices) speed up review across multiple health authorities, reducing shortage risk from aging facilities and process updates.

Analogy: The Prevention and Response Plan Is Like an Emergency Response Manual

The Prevention and Response Plan is like a hospital's emergency evacuation manual: you hope never to need it, but when you do, it must already be on the shelf, complete, and up to date. If a shortage occurs without this plan, the organization is left in the chaos of reactive response; with the plan, everyone knows their role and timeline, and harm to patients can be minimized.

The seven parts (A-G) of TR-68 are the complete chapter structure of this "emergency response manual": from basic product data (A) → patient impact assessment (B) → priority level (C) → preventive measures (D) → response measures (E) → review triggers (F) → approval and sign-off (G).

Key Points for Completing Part A

Part A is the plan's "identity card". The ATC Code (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification code) is especially important because:

  • Products under the same ATC code are substitutes for one another → this directly affects the "substitutability" assessment in Part B
  • The EMA's drug shortage database is organized by ATC code → this lets health authorities quickly identify the scope of impact
  • The other information fields are to be filled in when the Response Plan is activated: affected countries and expected end date of the shortage

Part B - Impact to Patient (Risk Level A/B/C)

Mark the Risk Level as A, B, or C based on the product's therapeutic use and availability of alternative therapies using the criteria below.

Logic for Determining Risk Level

The core of Part B is a two-dimensional matrix of patient impact: therapeutic use (severity) × availability of alternatives.

Practical Meaning of the Three Risk Levels

  • Risk Level A: highest patient risk → requires the strictest controls, and any sign of a shortage must be reported to the health authority immediately. Typical examples: neonatal parenteral nutrition solutions, insulin, anticancer chemotherapy drugs.
  • Risk Level B: moderate patient risk → requires enhanced controls but allows some buffer time. Typical examples: chronic disease drugs with alternatives (e.g., antihypertensives for which other brands are available).
  • Risk Level C: lower patient risk → standard controls are sufficient. Typical examples: over-the-counter analgesics and laxatives, for which multiple alternatives exist.

Note: even for Risk Level C, the Part D prevention plan must still be completed; the control measures are simply less stringent.

Part C - Risk Priority Level (1/2/3)

Management Implications of the Priority Level

Part C Is a Resource Allocation Decision Matrix

The Risk Priority Level determines how much resource the company must invest in control. This logic is consistent with the "risk-based decision principles" of ICH Q9(R1): the higher the risk level, the more validation, monitoring, and mitigation is needed.

  • Priority 1: highest priority → requires proactive preventive measures (dual sourcing, high safety stock, PAC filings) + proactive communication with health authorities
  • Priority 2: medium priority → requires monitoring mechanisms and contingency plans, with periodic review
  • Priority 3: general priority → standard quality system controls, included in the annual risk review

Key insight: even for a Risk Level A product, if the likelihood of shortage is low (because of multiple preventive measures), the Priority can be 2 rather than 1. This encourages companies to proactively put safeguards in place to lower the priority level.

D

Part D - Risk Control Activities for Proactive Prevention

Based on the potential Risk Priority Level (1, 2, or 3), describe suggested controls to be established, the communication plan (in the event of a shortage), and other considerations.

CDMO Preventive Measures Implementation Checklist

Priority 1: Essential CDMO Lines of Defense

  • Qualify dual API suppliers (including RA filing preparation)
  • Safety stock target: at least 180 days (raw materials + intermediates + finished product)
  • PACMP (ICH Q12) preparation: a change management protocol agreed in advance with the health authority
  • Quarterly supply chain risk assessment report
  • Establish an early warning notification mechanism with the MAH (notify when a leading indicator reaches its alert limit)
  • Facility and equipment spare parts management plan (spare parts inventory for critical equipment)

Priority 2/3: CDMO Baseline Controls

  • Supplier qualification assessment (QAA covering all critical suppliers)
  • Monthly tracking report of inventory levels
  • Capacity utilization monitoring (avoid staying above the 85% alert line for long periods)
  • Annual review of the Drug Shortage Risk Register

Step 1: Identify the reason(s) for the shortage (check all that apply):

  • Reliability Issues: Off-label use, compassionate use, competitor sourcing; Environmental standards; Illegal product introduction, theft, counterfeits, natural disasters, health emergencies, pandemic
  • Others: ____________________________

Step 2: Assess risks to patients from the shortage per Section B (attach risk assessment), including the expected duration and the patient groups likely to be affected.

Step 3: Create a short-term action plan based on Section D and/or discussions with health authorities (e.g., when alternative therapies are available).

Step 4: Execute the communication plan based on shortage characteristics (e.g., Dear Health Care Provider letters, informed consent forms, patient letters).

Step 5: Create a longer-term action plan including actions and timelines needed to return product supply to normal.

The Five Steps of the Response Plan Explained

Step 1: The Diagnostic Significance of the Four Root Cause Categories

The four root cause categories (manufacturing, commercial, economic and geographic, reliability) correspond directly to the shortage root cause analysis in TR-68 Section 3. Only a correct diagnosis leads to the correct response: manufacturing problems need CAPA; commercial problems need supply chain restructuring; regulatory problems need an RA strategy; and multi-stakeholder problems such as pandemics need cross-functional collaboration.

Step 4: The Three Audience Tiers of the Communication Plan

  • Health authorities: formal shortage notification (FDA 21 USC 356c / EMA MAH guidance); includes the estimated duration and affected batches
  • Healthcare providers: Dear Health Care Provider Letter; provides information on alternative therapies and medication management advice
  • Patients: Patient Letter or updated informed consent; explains the cause of the shortage, the expected resolution time, and actions patients can take

Exercise: Defining the CDMO's Role

As a CDMO, where are your responsibility boundaries in the shortage response plan?

  • CDMO leads: Step 1 (manufacturing problem diagnosis), Step 3 (short-term manufacturing recovery plan), Step 5 (long-term capacity recovery timeline)
  • MAH leads: Step 2 (patient risk assessment), Step 4 (patient/HCP communication), formal notification to health authorities
  • Shared responsibility: developing the response plan, information sharing, timeline coordination

These responsibility boundaries should be clearly defined in advance in the signed Quality Technical Agreement (QTA).

Practical Commentary on Part F/G

The Management Logic of Trigger Conditions

The list of trigger conditions in Part F reflects the core philosophy of TR-68: a drug shortage plan is not a one-time document but a dynamic management tool. Any event that changes the supply-demand balance of the product should trigger a reassessment of the plan.

Trigger conditions of particular importance to a CDMO:

  • GMP certificate withdrawal: once a supplier's or the CDMO's own GMP certification is under threat of withdrawal, escalate immediately to the highest response status
  • Inventory below the safety level: this is the most common early warning trigger and requires an automatic alert to be set up in the ERP system
  • Shortage of an alternative product: a shortage of a similar product from another brand on the market may cause demand for this product to rise sharply

Part G - Compliance Significance of the Approval Matrix

Three-level approval (responsible person + QA decision-maker + senior management) ensures that the shortage prevention plan receives formal endorsement at the highest level of the organization. This is consistent with the ICH Q10 requirements for management responsibility and is an important document for demonstrating management commitment during audits.

Recommendation: include the annual review of the drug shortage prevention plan on the Management Review agenda, alongside the other key indicators of the quality system.
