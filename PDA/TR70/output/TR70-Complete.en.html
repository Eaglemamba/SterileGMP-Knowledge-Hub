1.0 Introduction

This technical report provides comprehensive information and suggested best practices as well as appropriate references to support guidance on cleaning and disinfection programs for aseptic manufacturing facilities.

1.1 Purpose

The purpose of this document is to identify systematic elements that are essential to assuring an appropriate and compliant cleaning and disinfection program for aseptic and bioburden controlled manufacturing facilities and classified environments.

1.2 Scope

The document covers cleaning and disinfection within controlled and noncontrolled environments using chemical agents that reduce or destroy microorganisms. It provides guidance for non-product-contact surface cleaning and disinfection.

This document is not intended to fully address product-contact surface cleaning from a CIP (clean-in-place) or COP (clean-out-of-place) system, which is specifically addressed in:

  • PDA TR No. 29 (Revised 2012): Points to Consider for Cleaning Validation
  • PDA TR No. 49: Points to Consider for Biotechnology Cleaning Validation
This document should be considered as technical guidance; it is not intended to establish any mandatory or implied standard.

The technical report team consisted of cleaning and disinfection experts from global pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical companies, academia, and manufacturers of disinfection agents.

Interpretation and Application

The Core Position of TR70

TR70 is one of the most complete practical guides to cleaning and disinfection in pharmaceutical aseptic facilities. It is not a regulation itself, but regulators worldwide treat its recommendations as a reference standard for "industry best practice" during inspections.

In a CDMO setting, whenever an auditor asks "How do you select your disinfectants?", being able to cite the TR70 framework and show systematic qualification documentation is the key to passing the audit.

Scope Boundary: Non-Product-Contact Surfaces

TR70 focuses on cleaning of non-product-contact surfaces such as floors, walls, ceilings and external equipment surfaces.

Product-contact surfaces (such as the inner walls of compounding tanks and piping) are covered separately by TR29 and TR49; keep this boundary in mind when studying TR70.

Analogy: Hospital vs. Operating Room

Think of TR70 as an "operating room cleaning SOP guide". It does not say how to wash the surgical instruments (that is a CIP/COP matter); it says how to disinfect the floors, door handles and carts of the operating room so that the environment stays within microbiologically controlled limits.

TR70 vs. TR29 vs. TR49: How the Three Reports Divide the Work

  • TR70: facility environment (walls, floors, external equipment surfaces)
  • TR29: cleaning validation of product-contact surfaces for small-molecule drugs
  • TR49: cleaning validation for biological (large-molecule) drugs

2.0 Glossary of Terms

Selected key terms from TR70. Full glossary covers all terms used throughout the report.

Key Terms Explained

The Three Levels of Agent: The Most Important Distinction

The core concept of TR70 is the difference in level among these three agents, and auditors often ask about it:

Sanitizer

Reduces bacterial counts to a safe level (typically a 3-log reduction). Does not kill spores. Suitable for lower-cleanliness areas or as a supporting step.

Disinfectant

Destroys vegetative organisms. Does not kill spores. Mainly used in Grade C/D areas, and also used in Grade B.

Sporicide

Destroys all vegetative microorganisms and spores. Must be used periodically in Grade A/B aseptic environments, because disinfectants cannot eliminate spores.

Log Reduction in Practice

If a surface carries 10,000 CFU (104):

  • 1-log reduction → 1,000 CFU remain
  • 3-log reduction → 10 CFU remain (sanitizer standard)
  • 6-log reduction → 0.01 CFU remain (sterilization standard, SAL = 10-6)

The 3-log reduction required by TR70 is the minimum threshold for a sanitizer; the requirement for a sporicide is usually higher.

Contact Time: Why You Cannot Wipe the Surface Dry Early

Think of a disinfectant as a "soaking insecticide": the agent must stay wet for a period of time to do its work. If the surface dries early, the contact time is insufficient, and even with the right agent the effect cannot be assured. This is why the operating SOP must specify a minimum contact time and why it must be confirmed during validation.

APA Zoning vs. Agent Selection Logic

The Aseptic Processing Area (APA) contains several cleanliness grades, which EU GMP defines as Grade A/B/C/D:

  • Grade A (filling zone): a sporicide must be used periodically; disinfectants are rotated
  • Grade B (background environment): sporicide and disinfectant used in rotation
  • Grade C/D (support areas): mainly disinfectant, with a sanitizer as a supplement

Transfer Disinfection

When materials, equipment or containers move from a lower-grade area into a higher-grade area, their surfaces must be disinfected first. This procedure needs to be validated to confirm the contact time and the effectiveness of the disinfectant. It is a common audit checkpoint.

The Special Status of VPHP (Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide)

VPHP is currently the main method of bio-decontamination for Grade A/B isolators and RABS. TR70 lists it in the glossary and discusses it in depth in later sections. Its mechanism of action includes spore killing, so it belongs to the sporicide level.

3.0 Sanitizer, Disinfectant, and Sporicide Claims and Classifications

Sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides are chemical agents that reduce, eliminate, or destroy microorganisms. Registration testing is performed to meet requirements of organizations such as the:

  • U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
  • EU Biocidal Directive
  • Australian Therapeutics Goods Administration (TGA)
  • Health Canada

Registration testing addresses high-bioburden environments (hospitals, food processors, clinical laboratories). Label claims on commercial products are approved through this registration process.

Testing for use of these chemical agents in GMP manufacturing also requires testing prior to use. However, this testing is significantly different — the bioburden load within a cleanroom is much lower.

GMP-context testing (internal qualification) is performed with:

  • Lower bioburden levels (reflecting actual cleanroom conditions)
  • Decreased wet contact time periods
  • On varying substrates (floor, wall, stainless steel, etc.)

This internal qualification is an expectation of regulatory agencies worldwide (FDA, EMEA, and others) and may be reviewed during inspections.

Classification of Antimicrobial Chemical Agents

Note: The term "disinfectant" is often used as a general term. In TR70, the term antimicrobial chemical agent is used when referring collectively to sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides.

Classification and Practical Application

Key Distinction: Commercial Registration Testing vs. Pharmaceutical In-House Qualification

The efficacy claims on commercial disinfectant labels (such as "kills 99.9% of bacteria") come from testing under high-bioburden conditions (hospitals, food processing plants).

The bioburden in a pharmaceutical aseptic cleanroom is far lower, so in-house qualification under actual GMP conditions must be performed separately. This is very important: supplier data cannot be cited directly as the GMP basis.

Quick Overview of Common Agents

  • IPA 70% (alcohol): disinfectant. Fast-acting, volatile, leaves no residue. Does not kill spores. The workhorse for routine disinfection in Grade B/C/D.
  • QAC (quaternary ammonium compounds): disinfectant. Long residual action, but there is a concern about cumulative resistance, so it must be rotated with other classes.
  • Sodium hypochlorite (bleach): has sporicidal capability, but is highly corrosive to metals, so concentration and contact time must be controlled.
  • Peracetic acid (PAA): potent sporicide. Widely used for surface disinfection in Grade A/B environments and isolators.
  • VPHP: sporicide, used for whole-enclosure bio-decontamination (isolators, RABS). Requires specialized equipment.

The Ecological Logic of a "Rotation" Strategy

If the same agent is used for a long time, resistant strains will gradually be selected in the environment, just like the problem of antibiotic resistance. TR70 recommends periodically rotating disinfectants with different mechanisms of action to prevent any single species from developing tolerance. In CDMO practice the rotation usually occurs every 1-3 months and is documented in the EM trend analysis report.

Substrate Compatibility Qualification

"An agent that works on stainless steel" does not necessarily work the same way on an epoxy floor or a plastic equipment housing, and it may also cause corrosion or degradation of the material.

The in-house qualification required by TR70 must be tested on the types of substrate actually in use, including stainless steel (SS316L), epoxy flooring, coated walls, glass and polycarbonate.

Audit Preparation Exercise

If the auditor asks: "Why did you choose disinfectant X for your Grade B environment?"

Framework for a standard answer:

  1. Explanation of the agent class (disinfectant / sporicide)
  2. Which target microorganisms it addresses (environmental isolates from EM history)
  3. Supporting in-house qualification data
  4. Explanation of the rotation strategy
  5. Change control records

4.2 Regulatory Inspections

Regulatory inspections review the cleaning and disinfection program as part of the broader GMP assessment. Firms must demonstrate documented rationale for agent selection, validated procedures, and ongoing monitoring data.

Regulatory Analysis and Audit Preparation

The Most Important Regulatory Principle: The Responsibility Lies with the Manufacturer

TR70 states clearly: "The responsibility for demonstrating the effectiveness of the agents used lies with the individual pharmaceutical manufacturer."

This means it is not enough to say "we use a commercial disinfectant and the supplier says it works." You must provide:

  • The basis for selection (written rationale)
  • Qualification data under actual cleanroom conditions
  • Confirmation of effectiveness against facility isolates
  • Ongoing monitoring trends (EM data)

Core Requirements of the FDA 2004 Aseptic Guidance

The guidance requires manufacturers to evaluate disinfectants on three aspects:

  1. Suitability: is the agent appropriate for the environment, the substrate and the target microorganisms?
  2. Efficacy: is there data showing it is effective under GMP conditions?
  3. Limitations: in what situations is it not applicable? Against which microorganisms is its effect limited?

During an audit, these three items are usually reflected in the disinfectant qualification report.

The Evolution of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022 Revision)

TR70 cites the old version of Annex 1, but the 2022 Annex 1 was substantially updated and is more specific about disinfection programs, including:

  • Explicit requirement for periodic use of sporicides (Grade A/B)
  • Requirement to document the rationale for disinfectant selection
  • Requirement to evaluate the susceptibility of environmental isolates

When applying TR70, cross-check it against the requirements of the latest version of Annex 1.

CDMO Audit Preparation Checklist

  • Written rationale for disinfectant selection
  • List of agents suitable for each cleanliness grade area
  • In-house qualification report for each agent
  • SOPs for contact time, concentration and method of use
  • Agent rotation schedule
  • EM trend analysis demonstrating the effectiveness of the disinfection program
  • Change control records (when an agent is changed)
  • Personnel training records (in-use testing including operators)

5.0 Qualification of New Suppliers and Agents

New suppliers and new antimicrobial chemical agents for use in the disinfection program should be qualified prior to use following established procedures. A satisfactory audit, qualification testing, and a clearly defined Certificate of Analysis (CoA) are important aspects to be considered as part of the qualification. If changes occur in the agent's formulation, packaging, or manufacturing site, an evaluation should be performed to determine if requalification is required.

When choosing a new antimicrobial chemical agent, the following supplier documentation should be evaluated:

  • Product literature and technical data
  • Material compatibility and storage conditions
  • Expiration dating and efficacy data
  • Material safety information and disposal requirements
  • Sterility and sterilization information (if the product is provided sterile)
  • Packaging presentations

Depending on the specific use and experience with the supplier, an audit may need to be performed. Key audit focus areas include:

  • Environmental control and cleaning of the manufacturing area and equipment
  • Control and disinfection/sterilization of packaging containers
  • Documentation of production processing activities
  • For aseptically filled agents: environmental monitoring (EM) program data, including alert and action levels, trending, corrective actions, and use of neutralizing agents in EM media
  • For sterile-labeled agents: sterility testing data and sterilization process qualification
  • Water systems and quality of water used in manufacturing
  • Package or container integrity studies
  • For double- and triple-bagged containers: disinfection of filled container and overwrap integrity; where sterility is claimed for inner bags, sterilization process qualification
  • Handling and storage of finished product containers
  • Change control: customer notification of ingredient, process, or packaging changes (e.g., wrapping, irradiation, sterilization)

Overview of Supplier Qualification

All new antimicrobial chemical agents must be qualified according to an established procedure before they are formally introduced into the disinfection program. There are three core elements: a satisfactory audit result, passing qualification testing, and a clearly defined Certificate of Analysis (CoA).

Situations That Trigger Requalification

If the supplier makes any of the following changes, you must assess whether requalification is needed:

  • Change in agent formulation
  • Change in packaging
  • Change in manufacturing site

For a CDMO this is a frequent GMP audit finding. If the supplier does not proactively notify a change and the facility has no change notification mechanism, this constitutes a compliance deficiency.

Audit Focus: Sterile-Filled Agents

If a purchased disinfectant is labeled sterile, the audit should specifically confirm:

  • Whether the supplier's EM program includes alert and action levels
  • Whether the EM media contain appropriate neutralizing agents, to avoid inhibiting colony growth
  • Whether the sterilization process qualification documentation is complete
  • Whether integrity studies of the multilayer packaging (double/triple bag) are in place

Analogy: The CoA Is Like a Food Label

The Certificate of Analysis (CoA) is like the ingredient label on food: it tells you "what is inside and at what concentration." But a CoA is only the supplier's own claim; qualification testing is the step in which you verify for yourself that the agent really works. You need both.

5.1 Qualification Testing

Qualification testing of a new antimicrobial chemical agent should include both laboratory and in-situ testing. Chemical analysis of the actives and microbial efficacy testing should be performed. Chemical analysis may be provided by the vendor or performed in-house by a qualified contract laboratory using the vendor's method.

Microbial efficacy testing — whether in suspension or in carrier studies — should be performed in-house or by a qualified contract testing laboratory. Critically, the antimicrobial agents used for testing should be close to or beyond their stated in-use expiration date. This must account for both ready-to-use and use-dilution prepared from concentrate expiry. Testing should be done in replicate on multiple lots of the agent where applicable.

Information supporting qualification includes:

  • Description of packaging, label, and container type
  • Description of ingredients and concentrations
  • Lot or batch number
  • Efficacy testing results
  • Irradiation or other sterilization verification certification
  • Safety data sheet (SDS) information
  • Disposal information

5.2 Efficacy Testing — Overview

Demonstrating antimicrobial kill is a function of: concentration of microorganisms, type of microorganisms, choice of agent, concentration of agent, porosity or texture of the surface, method of application, and contact time.

The agent should be effective against the normal microbial vegetative flora recovered from the facility. AOAC (Association of Official Analytical Chemists) guidelines suggest high inoculum levels; however, inoculum levels for facility testing should not exceed 105.

The three categories of antimicrobial agents are:

  • Sanitizers — minimal reduction in 30 seconds to 10 minutes; effective against low levels of vegetative microorganisms. Example: 70% isopropanol (IPA).
  • Disinfectants — higher efficacy than sanitizers; kill vegetative microorganisms but not spore-forming organisms. Examples: quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs), phenolics.
  • Sporicides — up to total kill of bacterial spore-formers and mold. Examples: bleach (sodium hypochlorite), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), mixtures of H2O2 and peracetic acid.

In general, contact times in qualification studies should not exceed 120 seconds for alcohols and 10 minutes for disinfectants and sporicides. Typically, only three agents total (one of each class) are qualified within a biopharmaceutical or pharmaceutical facility.

Overview of Qualification and Efficacy Testing

Key Requirement: Test with Agents Near or Beyond Expiry

The agents used in testing must be near or past their in-use expiration date. This represents the worst case:

  • If an agent still meets the log reduction criterion close to expiry, it will certainly do so in normal use
  • The expiration dating of both the "ready-to-use" form and the "use dilution" prepared from concentrate must be considered
  • Testing multiple lots confirms lot-to-lot consistency

Analogy: Three Lines of Defense

A disinfection program is like the three defensive lines in football: the sanitizer clears routine low-level contamination (the forwards pressing); the disinfectant eliminates normal vegetative organisms (the midfield intercepting); the sporicide deals with the most resistant spores (the last line of defense). If any one layer is missing, there is a risk of a breakthrough.

CDMO Practical Reminder: Only Three Agents Are Needed

Historically it was believed that multiple disinfectants needed to be rotated to prevent resistance from developing, but this is no longer the mainstream view (see TR70 Section 11.0). A typical aseptic pharmaceutical facility needs to qualify only one sanitizer, one disinfectant and one sporicide. This view helps simplify the validation strategy.

Core Differences Among the Three Classes of Antimicrobial Agents

A disinfectant cannot kill spore-formers; this is the core reason a sporicide must be used alongside it in a GMP disinfection program.

5.2.1 In-Suspension Studies

In-suspension studies may be used to quickly screen various chemical agents to determine which may be the most effective. However, these studies should not be considered a replacement for carrier/coupon surface studies in determining antimicrobial agent performance on clean room surfaces. They may also demonstrate an agent's efficacy in destroying suspended organisms in solutions (tanks, holding vessels, bioreactors).

A panel of six to ten microorganisms, including bacteria, yeast, and mold, should be used. Environmental isolates from the facility are preferred; ATCC cultures or internationally recognized culture collection cultures are acceptable until facility isolates can be obtained.

Method (one acceptable approach):

  1. Prepare a fresh culture of each organism to a known CFU/mL concentration.
  2. Transfer a small volume into a sterile preparation of the chemical agent and mix. Suggested inoculum: 103 to 104 CFU.
  3. Allow the mixture to sit for the specified contact (wetted) time.
  4. Filter the entire solution and rinse three times with an appropriate neutralizing agent. Plate the filter to a suitable medium (e.g., Trypticase Soy Agar, TSA) and incubate.
  5. Prepare a positive control: replace chemical agent with saline; serial dilutions to allow 10–300 CFU per plate.
  6. Prepare a negative control: follow the method but add no inoculum. No CFUs should be recovered.
  7. Calculate log reduction against each organism based on positive control CFUs versus recovered CFUs. Assess against pre-established acceptance criteria.

Neutralization agent validation must confirm: (1) organisms can grow in the presence of the neutralizing agent; (2) the neutralizing agent effectively neutralizes the chemical agent.

In-Suspension Efficacy Studies

The Role of In-Suspension Studies

In-suspension studies are a screening tool, not the final validation. Their advantage is that they are fast and simple to perform; their limitation is that a liquid environment cannot represent the killing effect on real cleanroom surfaces. A complete efficacy validation must include carrier surface studies.

Logic of Test Organism Selection

Order of priority for organism selection:

  1. Facility environmental isolates: the most representative of the real challenge
  2. ATCC reference strains: the alternative until facility isolates are obtained

The organisms selected should match the class of agent being tested:

  • Sanitizer → common vegetative organisms (such as Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 9027)
  • Disinfectant → a range of vegetative organisms + yeast + mold
  • Sporicide → must include spore-formers (such as Bacillus subtilis spores ATCC 6633)

Log Reduction Calculation

Log Reduction = log₁₀(N₀) - log₁₀(N)

N₀ = CFU of the positive control (initial inoculum)
N  = CFU recovered after exposure to the agent

Example:
  N₀ = 10,000 CFU (10⁴)
  N  = 10 CFU
  Log Reduction = 4 - 1 = 3-log

Acceptance criterion for a disinfectant: ≥ 3-log reduction

Neutralizer Validation: Both Directions Must Be Confirmed

The neutralizing agent acts as a "stop clock", inactivating the agent immediately after the specified contact time in the test. Validation must be confirmed in both directions:

  • Direction 1: neutralizing capability: confirm that the neutralizer effectively terminates the agent's activity (when organisms are added to the neutralizer + agent + organism mixture, the organisms can still grow)
  • Direction 2: organism compatibility: confirm that the neutralizer itself does not inhibit growth of the test organisms (neutralizer + organism vs. organism-only control; the recovered CFU should be comparable)

If only one direction is validated, the risk is that the neutralizer itself has an inhibitory effect, producing falsely low counts and wrongly suggesting the agent is highly effective.

5.2.2 Carrier Surface Studies

Carrier surface studies provide verification of the ability of the antimicrobial chemical agent to reduce microorganism levels on the types of material surfaces present within the facility.

A variety of surfaces commonly found in the facility should be selected, representing worst-case porosity or most difficult to clean. These may include: stainless steel, plastic, plastic bags, glass, vinyl curtains, polycarbonates, terrazzo, epoxy, vinyl, laminate flooring, painted epoxy and polysubstrate wall materials. Surface selection should be based on criticality and risk of harboring contamination impacting the final product. Carrier size should not exceed 1.5 in × 1.5 in (38 mm × 38 mm). Three or more replicates are recommended due to test method variability.

As with in-suspension studies, a panel of six to ten microorganisms (bacteria, yeast, mold) should be used. Environmental isolates are preferred.

Method 1 — Total Kill Method:

  1. Prepare a fresh culture of each organism to a known CFU/mL.
  2. Transfer a sufficient number of microorganisms (103–105) onto the carrier surface.
  3. Allow the inoculum to thoroughly air dry. Apply the antimicrobial agent by spraying, wiping, or submerging the carrier. Allow contact for a defined time (e.g., 5–10 minutes).
  4. Submerge the carrier in a vessel containing neutralizing agent and appropriate growth medium (e.g., Trypticase Soy Broth, TSB).
  5. After a set time, transfer the carrier to a second vessel containing the same growth medium without neutralizing agent.
  6. Incubate both vessels at appropriate temperature for a suitable time.
  7. No growth in both vessels is required to demonstrate the required log reduction.
  8. Positive control: carriers submerged in saline and gently sonicated or scrubbed; serial dilutions plated to allow 10–100 CFU per plate using the same media type.
  9. Negative control: sterile carrier used; no CFUs expected.
  10. Calculate log reduction using CFUs from positive controls versus test results.

Method 2 — Enumeration Method:

  1. After exposure to the antimicrobial agent, place the carrier in a vessel containing neutralizing solution and gently sonicate to remove organisms.
  2. Filter the entire solution; plate the filter to TSA and incubate to assess survival level.

Alternatively, serial dilution using saline, followed by filtration and plating, or a pour/spread plate method may be used.

Carrier Surface Efficacy Studies

Why Carrier Studies Matter More Than In-Suspension Studies

Disinfection in a real cleanroom acts on organisms that have dried and remain on a surface, not on organisms suspended in liquid. Air-dried organisms are more resistant to the agent, which is why carrier surface studies are the gold standard for efficacy confirmation.

Key difference: in a carrier study, the organisms are first air-dried onto the material surface to simulate a real contamination scenario, and only then is the agent sprayed or wiped on.

A Risk-Based Approach to Surface Selection

Choose the hardest-to-clean (worst-case) surfaces, usually the roughest or most porous materials. Although stainless steel is relatively easy to clean, it has the closest contact with manufacturing operations (highest criticality) and must therefore still be included in testing.

Common CDMO surfaces:

  • Stainless steel (filling machine, stands)
  • Plastic bags (material transfer bags)
  • Glass (isolator windows)
  • Epoxy floor
  • Vinyl curtains

Analogy: What the Ultrasonic Bath Is For

Sonication in the enumeration method is like using an ultrasonic toothbrush to remove dirt from crevices: the aim is to transfer organisms that have dried and adhered to the surface completely into the liquid so that the count is accurate, rather than leaving them on the surface and underestimating the result.

GMP Audit Perspective: CDMO Document Package (Applicable to TWi)

When a GMP auditor checks the qualification of disinfectants, they will ask to see the following documents:

  • Supplier audit report or questionnaire results
  • CoA review record for each lot
  • In-suspension study report (including the list of test organisms, acceptance criteria and actual results)
  • Carrier surface study report (including the list of surfaces and the results of three replicates)
  • Neutralizing agent validation report
  • Basis for in-use expiration dating (test records with agents near or past expiry)
  • Traceability records linking test lot numbers to production lot numbers

The absence of any one of these could become an observation in an FDA 483 or an EMA inspection.

Total Kill Method vs. Enumeration Method

The Total Kill Method uses a two-flask logic: the first flask contains neutralizer (organisms are transferred into it immediately after the agent's action is stopped); the second contains no neutralizer (to confirm that the carrier itself carries no residual active agent that could interfere). Both flasks must show no growth for the test to pass.

Section Summary

PDA TR70 Section 5 — Supplier Qualification & Efficacy Testing

Key Takeaways

  • Supplier qualification is a prerequisite, not optional. A CoA alone is insufficient — audit findings, qualification testing, and change control mechanisms must all be in place before a new agent is introduced to the disinfection program.
  • Requalification triggers are change-driven. Formulation, packaging, or manufacturing site changes all require evaluation for requalification.
  • In-suspension studies are a screening tool only. They provide rapid comparative data but cannot replace carrier surface studies as the definitive efficacy confirmation method.
  • Sporicide validation requires spore-forming test organisms. Bacillus subtilis spores (ATCC 6633) are the critical test organism — disinfectants do not kill spores, so this distinction is non-negotiable.
  • Log reduction criterion for disinfectants: ≥ 3-log. Testing must use agents close to or beyond in-use expiration date (worst-case scenario), in replicate, across multiple lots.
  • Neutralizing agent validation is bidirectional: confirm (1) it neutralizes the chemical agent, and (2) it does not inhibit organism growth.
  • Three agents is the standard qualification target: one sanitizer, one disinfectant, one sporicide for a typical pharmaceutical facility.

Key Takeaways

  • Supplier qualification is a mandatory prerequisite. A CoA alone is not enough: the audit result, qualification testing and a change control mechanism are all required before a new agent can be introduced.
  • Requalification is triggered by change. Any change in formulation, packaging or manufacturing site requires an assessment of whether requalification is needed.
  • In-suspension studies are a screening tool, not the final validation. They provide quick comparative data but cannot replace carrier surface studies as the final basis for efficacy confirmation.
  • Sporicide validation must include spore-formers. Bacillus subtilis spores (ATCC 6633) are the key test organism; a disinfectant cannot kill spores, and this distinction is non-negotiable in GMP.
  • Acceptance criterion for disinfectants: ≥ 3-log reduction. Testing must use agents near or past the in-use expiration date (worst case), with multiple replicates and multiple lots.
  • Neutralizer validation must be confirmed in both directions: (1) it effectively neutralizes the agent; (2) it does not inhibit growth of the test organisms.
  • The standard configuration is three agents: one each of sanitizer, disinfectant and sporicide meets the needs of a typical pharmaceutical facility.

6.0 In-Use Expiration Dating

6.0 In-Use Expiration Dating

Sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides should be assessed to ensure their performance throughout their assigned in-use period. They should be stored for use no longer than the predefined period as specified by written procedures.

The expiration dating provided by the manufacturer relates to the expiration of a closed or "primary" container. Once the container (ready-to-use or concentrate) has been opened, the manufacturer's expiration date is no longer valid for active ingredient potency and sterility.

The two important points surrounding in-use expiration are:

  • Antimicrobial effectiveness: The length of time the solution retains its ability to destroy microorganisms, as evaluated during efficacy testing.
  • Container bioburden: For controlled areas, how long the container and its contents maintain an appropriate bioburden level.

This is determined by performing bioburden testing on samples taken from containers (spray bottles, squeeze bottles, etc.) used in the disinfection process at the end of their in-use period.

Key Regulatory Implication

Failure to label prepared disinfectant containers with preparation date and in-use expiry is a common FDA 483 observation. Written SOPs must define the maximum in-use period for each type of container and agent.

6.0 In-Use Expiration Dating

Sanitizers, disinfectants and sporicides must be continuously evaluated for efficacy during use, and must not be used beyond the predefined in-use period specified in the written procedure.

The expiration date provided by the manufacturer applies only to the unopened primary container. Once a container is opened (whether ready-to-use or concentrate), the original expiration date no longer applies to the efficacy of the active ingredient or the assurance of sterility.

Two core considerations for in-use expiration dating:

  • Retention of disinfectant efficacy: how long the solution can still achieve the killing capability verified in efficacy testing.
  • Container bioburden control: how long the container and its contents can maintain an appropriate microbial load in a controlled area.

Method of determination: at the end of the in-use period, sample containers actually in use, such as spray bottles and squeeze bottles, and perform bioburden testing.

Practical Reminder for CDMO Audits

Failing to label prepared disinfectant containers with the preparation date and expiration date is one of the most common FDA 483 observations. The written SOP must clearly specify the maximum in-use period for each container and agent. All prepared disinfectant containers at TWi must be labeled accordingly.

7.0 Control of the Environment

7.0 Overview — Contamination Control Framework

An effective cleaning and disinfection system starts by limiting the introduction of contamination into the facility. Stopping both viable and nonviable contamination from entering controlled areas is critical to achieving the desired environmental conditions.

On the scale of importance, the control over the introduction of contamination is the most critical concern in the entire cleaning and disinfection process. This begins with the cleanliness of all items transferred into the facility — components, personnel, carts, tanks, tools, and instruments.

A comprehensive list of every item entering the controlled area should be constructed, and each item evaluated to determine whether it can be effectively cleaned and disinfected (or sterilized). Items that cannot be appropriately cleaned must be replaced.

Three high-risk time periods when contamination entry can impact operations:

  1. After cleaning/disinfection, before manufacturing begins: Personnel and components enter the area and potentially shed particulates and microbes during setup.
  2. During manufacturing interventions: Inappropriate clean room behavior and poor aseptic technique can contaminate disinfected surfaces.
  3. After manufacturing, before cleaning: Personnel must maintain aseptic awareness to preserve environmental cleanliness.

Control must also address proliferation of viable contaminants already present — molds can penetrate surfaces and equipment attachment points, making elimination difficult. Gram-negative organisms can generate endotoxins from product-contact surfaces if not cleaned appropriately.

7.1.3 Introduction of Cleaning Supplies and Equipment into the APA

Cleaning supplies and equipment represent potential bioburden sources. Prior sterilization or disinfection is standard practice.

For Grade A (ISO 5) and adjacent Grade B (ISO 5/6) areas: Cleaning equipment (buckets, mops, mop handles, mop heads, sprayers, wipes, extensions) must be thoroughly cleaned and rendered sterile prior to use. Methods: steam sterilization or sterile single-use disposable systems.

Sterility of disinfectant solutions: Sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides can harbor resistant microorganisms. For Grade A/B (ISO 5/6) areas, solutions must be sterile-filtered or sterilized before use. Approaches:

  • Aseptic filtration at 0.2 µm of final-use dilution into pre-sterilized containers in the Grade A/B area.
  • Mixing with sterile unit-dose containers certified as sterile, using sterile WFI.
  • Purchasing ready-to-use or ready-to-mix sterile solutions from a vendor with appropriate sterility documentation.
  • Autoclaving the mixed solution (if formulation permits).

Container type matters: Aspirating containers (squeeze bottles, trigger sprayers, bulk containers opened and closed) introduce environmental contamination into the solution — 70% IPA bottles that aspirate have been found to harbor mold. Non-aspirating containers prevent contamination ingress and may be used until their validated expiration period.

Grade cascade rule: A solution used in a Grade A area may subsequently be used in Grade B, then Grade C, in that descending order. A solution used in Grade A cannot return to Grade A use unless kept under positive pressure. Discard open bucket contents after each cleaning operation.

Sterility requirement for disinfectant solutions: disinfectants themselves may contain resistant microorganisms. Solutions used in Grade A/B areas must be sterile-filtered through 0.2 µm or otherwise sterilized before use. Methods include:

  • Outside the Grade A/B area, sterile-filter the final use-concentration solution through 0.2 µm into a presterilized container placed in the Grade A/B area.
  • Prepare the solution with sterile water for injection (WFI) in single-dose containers validated as sterile.
  • Purchase ready-to-use or ready-to-prepare sterile solutions from suppliers, with documentation supporting their sterility.
  • If the formulation allows, autoclave the prepared solution.

Choice of container type: containers that draw in air (squeeze bottles, trigger sprayers, large containers that are repeatedly opened) bring environmental contamination into the solution; there have been cases of 70% IPA bottles containing mold because of air intake. Non-aspirating containers avoid this problem and can be used up to the validated expiration date.

Principle of stepping down cleanliness grades: a solution used in Grade A may be stepped down for use in Grade B and then Grade C, but must not go back to Grade A unless the container is kept under continuous positive pressure to prevent backflow of contamination. Solutions in open bucket systems should be discarded after each cleaning operation.

7.2 Environmental Monitoring Data Analysis

Environmental monitoring (EM) data demonstrates the effectiveness of the microbial contamination control system, including the cleaning and disinfection program. Analysis considers:

  • Genus and species of organisms recovered
  • Number of isolates and their distribution within the facility
  • Trending history — comparison against historical performance

Normal pattern: When a facility is in control, data follows a predictable profile. Most common isolates are from personnel; fewer from air, soil, water, or liquid sources.

Analysis frequency: Formal documented trend analysis should be performed periodically. Evaluation of cleaning/disinfection program effectiveness and alert/action level adequacy should be performed at least annually. Results should be reported to site management and reviewed/documented by quality management.

Adverse trends and excursions: Any deviation from normal conditions triggers a signal. Actions are proportional to risk:

  • Alert level reached or atypical organism: heightened awareness notification.
  • Recurring action level exceedances: special cleaning/disinfection of the area.
  • Multiple sites or multiple occasions: full investigation.

Organism-specific responses: Vegetative organisms above action levels may require increased disinfection. Detection of spore-forming organisms above action level signals immediate response with sporicidal agents.

8.0 In-Situ Field Studies

8.0 In-Situ Field Studies — Overview

The true test of the effectiveness of a cleaning and disinfection program is the monitoring data collected from the manufacturing area. Evaluation of in-situ data from a robust environmental monitoring program will verify that the program is capable of attaining and maintaining a level of cleanliness that minimizes the probability of contamination.

In-situ data types include:

  • Nonviable (total particulate) data
  • Viable data for surfaces and ambient air
  • Personnel monitoring data
  • Microbial identification of representative environmental isolates
  • Residual testing of surfaces
  • Product quality data (in-process bioburden and sterility testing)

Two approaches to conducting in-situ monitoring are commonly used:

  1. Environmental monitoring before and after start-up of a facility or area (Section 8.1)
  2. Environmental monitoring before and after cleaning during routine operation (Section 8.2)

Laboratory vs. Real-World Performance

Coupon studies in the lab use controlled inoculums on polished stainless steel — clean, flat, and reproducible. Real clean rooms have epoxy floors, acrylic windows, irregular equipment surfaces, and personnel interactions. In-situ studies bridge this gap, confirming that the program works under actual operating conditions with real surfaces and real operators.

8.1 Environmental Monitoring Before and After Start-up of a Facility or Area

Applicable scenarios include: opening a new area, area shutdown due to adverse events, areas that have undergone significant modifications, or areas left idle for a significant period.

Facilities should have special start-up cleaning and disinfection programs for shutdowns or significant construction activities.

Standard approach — step-by-step EM after each stage:

  1. Initial cleaning: Remove gross soil (broom, vacuum) — e.g., after construction, to prepare the area for formal cleaning.
  2. Baseline monitoring: Increased viable monitoring of air and surfaces. Nonviable air monitoring. This establishes the pre-cleaning baseline for comparison.
  3. Cleaning and disinfection: Execute the full cleaning and disinfection program.
  4. Post-cleaning monitoring: After surfaces are dry, repeat increased viable surface monitoring and nonviable air monitoring.

Analysis: Compare before/after data. A robust program should demonstrate reduction of viable and nonviable counts and minimization of spore-forming or mold contaminants. If results do not demonstrate acceptable reduction, the program must be reviewed and modified.

8.1 Environmental Monitoring Before and After Facility or Area Start-Up

Applicable situations: opening of a new area, an area closed because of an abnormal event, an area that has undergone major renovation, or an area that has been idle for an extended period.

  1. Preliminary cleaning: remove gross dirt (brooms, vacuums), for example after construction is completed, in preparation for the subsequent formal cleaning procedure.

8.2 Environmental Monitoring Before and After Cleaning During Routine Operation

In this approach, a facility in routine manufacturing operation evaluates its ongoing cleaning and disinfection program effectiveness:

  1. Increased viable surface and air monitoring is performed after operations have occurred and just before cleaning and disinfection take place. Nonviable air monitoring is also performed.
  2. Cleaning and disinfection are performed.
  3. Increased monitoring is performed again after cleaning and disinfection are complete.

The data gathered before and after is analyzed. Expectations: reduction of viable and nonviable counts, minimization of spore-forming or mold contaminants. If results are unsatisfactory, the program must be reviewed and modified.

9.0 Cleaning and Disinfection — Why Cleaning Comes First

Cleaning is a critical step in the cleaning and disinfection process because the buildup of antimicrobial chemical agent residues, product residues, particulates, and other contaminants can inhibit an antimicrobial chemical agent's efficacy.

By lessening the level of particulates, microbes, and residues on the surface, cleaning prepares the surfaces for disinfection because:

  • There are fewer organisms to destroy, as most have been removed from the area.
  • Obstructions blocking the chemical agent from contacting the organism are minimized.
  • Chemical interference that would reduce the stability and effectiveness of the active agents is removed.
  • Residual that can interfere with future disinfection, or dry and flake off into the environment, is lessened.

The key to disinfection in the clean room is keeping the surface wetted for a sufficient period of time for the chemical to accomplish its mode of action. Drying time is a variable that must be carefully evaluated — especially in areas where unidirectional airflow (UDAF) is present, as air movement tends to dry surfaces quickly.

Cleaning operations should be performed routinely, with frequency based on area classification, usage, risk, and visible cleanliness.

Why must cleaning be performed before disinfection?

Cleaning is not a substitute for disinfection; it is a necessary prerequisite. Residual organic matter (product residues, particles) will:

  • Shield microorganisms from contact with the disinfectant
  • Interfere chemically with the active ingredient of the disinfectant, reducing its antimicrobial efficacy
  • Flake off once dry and be released back into the environment

Key operating point: contact time (wet time)

The disinfectant must keep the surface wet so that the active ingredient can penetrate the microbial cell wall. Grade A/B areas use unidirectional airflow (UDAF); the airflow velocity is high and surfaces dry extremely quickly. The SOP must specify the contact time explicitly and take into account the effect of airflow on the evaporation rate.

Factors that determine cleaning frequency

  • Area grade - the higher the grade, the higher the frequency
  • Intensity of use - number of batches, number of shift changes
  • Risk assessment - degree of product exposure
  • Visual cleanliness - visible contamination immediately triggers cleaning
A/B

Grade A / B Areas — Highest Rigor

Filling zones · LAF workstations · Background environments · Sterile tools and wipers

Grade A Areas — Most Critical Zones

Grade A is the highest classification, representing the local zone for high-risk operations such as filling, stoppering, and open product transfers. Grade A is typically provided by a Laminar Air Flow (LAF) unit or RABS/isolator unidirectional airflow workstation.

Cleaning requirements for Grade A:

  • Only sterile wipers (pre-wetted or dry) are used — non-woven, low-lint materials validated for use in the zone.
  • Cleaning tools (mop handles, buckets, squeegees) must be sterilized before entry into the Grade A/B suite.
  • Sporicide (e.g., hydrogen peroxide vapor, peracetic acid, chlorine dioxide) is routinely employed as the primary antimicrobial chemical agent. Sporicides eliminate bacterial and fungal spores, which neither sanitizers nor standard disinfectants achieve.
  • No floor mop is used directly within the Grade A zone itself; floor areas under LAF are typically wiped or cleaned by automated VHP/HPV cycles.
  • All cleaning actions follow unidirectional, overlapping stroke technique — clean to less-clean direction (top to bottom, inside to outside) to avoid spreading contamination.
  • Personnel performing Grade A cleaning must wear appropriate gowning (sterile gown, double gloves, face shield) and be qualified to work in the aseptic core.

Grade B Areas — Background to Grade A

Grade B is the background environment surrounding the Grade A zone during aseptic operations. Requirements are nearly as stringent as Grade A:

  • Sporicide used periodically (e.g., weekly or per campaign), with disinfectant rotation applied between sporicide applications.
  • Sterile or sanitized wipers required; low-lint materials.
  • Floors: mop system permitted, but mop heads must be dedicated to Grade B and either sterilized or replaced between uses.
  • Environmental monitoring (EM) frequency is highest — active air sampling every shift, settle plates during operations, surface contact plates after critical activities.
  • SOP must specify agent identity, concentration, contact time, wiper type, and cleaning sequence for each surface type.

Cleaning Requirements for Grade A / B Areas

Grade A - the core zone of the aseptic process

Grade A is the highest grade in EU Annex 1 and corresponds to ISO Class 5. Typical locations: filling zone, laminar air flow (LAF) hood, and the interior of RABS or isolators.

Six key elements of Grade A cleaning:

  1. Sterile wipers - sterilized, low-lint non-woven wipers must be used; ordinary paper towels or cotton cloths are not permitted
  2. Sterilized tools brought into the area - all cleaning implements (mop handles, buckets, squeegees) must be sterilized before use, and this must be recorded
  3. Sporicide - such as vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VHP), peracetic acid (PAA) or chlorine dioxide (ClO₂). General disinfectants cannot kill spores, so Grade A requires periodic use of a sporicide
  4. No direct mopping of floors - the floor beneath the LAF is usually treated with a VHP gassing cycle or manual sterile wiping
  5. Unidirectional overlapping wiping technique - from the inside out and from top to bottom, overlapping each stroke by 30% to avoid cross-contamination
  6. Personnel qualification - personnel performing Grade A cleaning must have completed aseptic gowning training and media fill qualification

Analogy: the surgical scrub procedure

Grade A cleaning is like a surgical scrub - every step and every direction is strictly prescribed, because any lapse directly affects patient safety (product sterility). "Close enough" does not exist at this grade.

CDMO practice: TWi sterile filling facility

TWi's Grade A/B areas (aseptic filling lines) are the highest-risk grades, and the cleaning SOP must clearly state: agent name, effective concentration, contact time, wiper type and lot number, cleaning sequence and operator qualification. One common audit finding is a disinfectant that does not match the area grade (for example, using only a general disinfectant in Grade A without periodically applying a sporicide).

C/D

Grade C / D Areas — Secondary and General Support

Preparation areas · General support corridors · Sanitizer and disinfectant programs

Grade C Areas — Secondary Support Zones

Grade C areas are less critical support areas where less critical stages of the manufacturing process are carried out. Examples include solution preparation, component preparation, and filling of products for further sterilization.

Cleaning program requirements for Grade C:

  • Disinfectant rotation is the standard approach — at least two chemically distinct disinfectants (e.g., a quaternary ammonium compound and an alcohol, or an oxidizing agent) are alternated on a defined schedule to prevent the development of resistant organisms.
  • Sporicide applied periodically — typically monthly or quarterly, or following any adverse EM trend. The sporicide cycle is less frequent than in Grade B.
  • Mops are acceptable for floors; mop heads must be disposable or laundered/sterilized on a defined schedule.
  • Wipers do not require sterilization but should be low-lint and compatible with the chemical agents used.
  • Environmental monitoring at Grade C occurs on a risk basis — typically twice weekly for air and surfaces, but frequency is adjusted based on EM data trends.
  • Written procedures must document agent identity, concentration, preparation method, contact time, and application sequence.

Grade D Areas — General Support Areas

Grade D represents the general support environment for aseptic manufacturing — packaging, storage of containers before cleaning/sterilization, and personnel corridors.

Cleaning requirements for Grade D:

  • A sanitizer (e.g., 70% isopropyl alcohol, quaternary ammonium solution) is generally sufficient as the primary antimicrobial agent.
  • Routine disinfectant use is standard; periodic sporicide may still be applied (e.g., biannually) to control environmental bioburden.
  • Standard mop-and-bucket systems are acceptable; dedicated equipment per zone is still best practice.
  • Environmental monitoring is performed on a risk basis — less frequent than Grade C, typically weekly or monthly depending on operations.
  • Operational particulate counts in Grade D are not defined by EU Annex 1 and depend on the specific operation.

Cleaning Requirements for Grade C / D Areas

Grade C - secondary support area

Grade C (equivalent to ISO Class 8 at rest) is typically used for solution preparation rooms, component preparation rooms and some pre-filling operations. Although it does not come into direct contact with the final sterile product, it is still a controlled environment and requires a systematic disinfection program.

Key points of a Grade C cleaning program:

  • Disinfectant rotation - periodically alternate two or more disinfectants with different chemical mechanisms to prevent the development of resistant microorganisms
  • Periodic sporicide use - usually monthly or quarterly, or triggered when environmental monitoring (EM) trends show an anomaly
  • Mop systems - may be used, but mop heads must be disposable or laundered/sterilized according to a plan
  • Wipers - need not be sterile, but must be low-lint and compatible with the agents used

Scientific basis for disinfectant rotation

With long-term use of a single disinfectant, tolerant strains in the environment may gradually accumulate. Rotating agents with different modes of action (for example, an oxidizing agent vs. a surfactant) effectively prevents this; it is a consensus requirement of EU GMP and FDA guidance.

Grade D - general support area

Grade D includes packaging rooms, holding areas for containers before sterilization, gowning anterooms, material transfer corridors and similar areas. The main objective at this grade is to maintain basic bioburden control and prevent contamination sources from entering higher-grade areas.

A general sanitizer is sufficient for routine needs, but a written SOP specifying frequency and method is still required.

Application Methods — How Agents Are Applied

Spray-and-wipe · Mop · Soak / Immersion · Proper wiping technique

Spray-and-Wipe — Most Common Method

The spray-and-wipe technique is the most widely used method for applying cleaning and disinfecting agents to walls, equipment surfaces, and work surfaces in classified areas.

Procedure:

  1. Apply the agent to the surface via spray bottle or pre-wetted wiper, ensuring full coverage of the target area.
  2. Allow contact time as specified in the SOP — the surface must remain visibly wet for the required duration.
  3. Wipe in a unidirectional, overlapping stroke pattern: strokes must overlap by approximately 30% to ensure no area is missed.
  4. Work in a systematic direction — top to bottom, inside to outside (highest risk zone cleaned first to avoid re-contamination from cleaner zone).
  5. Discard the wiper after each surface or after a defined number of strokes — never re-dip a used wiper into a clean agent container.

Mop Systems — Floor Cleaning

Mopping is the standard method for floor cleaning in Grade B, C, and D areas. Key requirements:

  • Dedicated mop heads per area grade — never use a Grade C mop in Grade B.
  • Mop heads should be disposable (single-use) or laundered at high temperature and validated for efficacy.
  • Mop head must be pre-wetted or wetted at the point of use from a freshly prepared agent solution — never from stagnant water.
  • Use the same unidirectional, overlapping stroke technique on floors as for walls.
  • In Grade B, mop handle and bucket must be sterilized before entry.

Soak / Immersion — Small Parts and Tools

Soaking is used for small equipment components, accessories, and tools that can be fully immersed in a disinfectant solution. Requirements:

  • Agent concentration and contact time must be pre-validated for the specific material/surface.
  • Items must be pre-cleaned before immersion — residual soil reduces disinfectant efficacy.
  • The immersion solution should be freshly prepared; reuse of solutions must be limited by defined time or contamination threshold.
  • After soaking, items are rinsed (if required) with sterile or WFI water before introduction into classified areas.

Application Methods

Three main application methods

  • Spray-and-wipe - the most common; suitable for walls, external equipment surfaces and work surfaces
  • Mop - used for floors; applicable to all grades, but specifications differ
  • Soak / immersion - suitable for small tools and parts; they must be completely submerged

Unidirectional overlapping stroke technique - the most critical operating detail

Random "back-and-forth wiping" carries microorganisms from contaminated areas back into cleaned areas and spreads contamination instead. The correct method:

  1. Start wiping from the highest-risk or cleanest area (the entrance of the Grade A filling zone first)
  2. Wipe in the same direction every time (for example, left to right or top to bottom)
  3. Overlap each new stroke with the previous one by about 30%
  4. On reaching a boundary, fold over or change the wiper; re-wiping in the reverse direction is prohibited
  5. Discard the wiper after each area or after a defined number of strokes

Analogy: painting a wall

A good painter uses overlapping horizontal strokes, covering evenly from top to bottom without leaving gaps - the principle of wiper technique is exactly the same. Wiping in random circles or back and forth is like using one brush loaded with old paint: the result is simply to spread the contamination evenly.

Sequence of Cleaning Procedure Steps

  1. Don protective equipment appropriate to the grade (PPE / gowning)
  2. Remove movable equipment or objects to extend the reachable cleaning area
  3. Initial cleaning - remove visible dust, debris and product residues (this step uses a cleaning agent, not a disinfectant)
  4. Apply the disinfectant - spray to ensure full wetting, and time the contact period
  5. Wipe or allow the agent to evaporate naturally, as specified in the SOP
  6. Apply the sporicide according to the plan, depending on the frequency for the grade
  7. Post-cleaning EM - confirm the effectiveness of cleaning
  8. Record and sign the cleaning log

Essential SOP Elements (Audit Checklist)

  • Agent name and manufacturer
  • Use concentration (dilution ratio)
  • Contact time
  • Wiper type and specification (whether sterilization is required)
  • Cleaning sequence (from higher grade to lower grade, from top to bottom)
  • Mop head replacement / sterilization frequency
  • Operator qualification requirements
  • Waste disposal method

Cleaning Materials — Mop Heads, Wipers, Buckets

Cleaning Materials: Mop Heads, Wipers, Buckets | TR70 §9.3–9.4

Cleaning Materials — Selection and Control

The choice of cleaning materials — mop heads, wipers, and buckets — directly affects the efficacy of a cleaning and disinfection program. Materials must be selected based on their physical properties, compatibility with chemical agents, and the cleanliness classification of the area in which they are used.

Wipers

Wipers are used throughout all cleanroom grades. Their primary function is to deliver mechanical action and carry chemical agents to the surface. Key selection criteria include:

  • Grade A/B (ISO 5): Sterile gamma-irradiated (γ-irradiated) wipers are required. Pre-saturated sterile wipers are preferred to ensure consistent chemical delivery and to eliminate the risk of contamination from the wiper itself.
  • Grade C/D: Sterile or non-sterile wipers may be used depending on risk assessment. Non-sterile wipers must still be low-particulate and low-linting.
  • Single-use vs. reusable: Single-use wipers eliminate cross-contamination risk between surfaces. Reusable wipers must be laundered and re-sterilized via a validated procedure before re-use.
  • Material: Polyester/cellulose blends are common. Fiber shedding and chemical compatibility with the intended cleaning agent must be evaluated.

Critical Note — Wiper Residues

Residues from chemical agents, loose fibers from wipers, and released wiper binders can become contamination sources on product-contact or near-product surfaces. Wiper type and chemistry must be part of the formal validation package.

Mop Heads

Mops are the primary tool for applying cleaning and disinfecting agents to walls and floors. Mop heads must be validated for compatibility with the disinfectants and sporicides used in the program. Key requirements include:

  • Mop heads used in Grade A/B areas must be sterile — typically γ-irradiated single-use heads or heads that have been laundered and re-sterilized by a validated process.
  • Reusable mop heads accumulate bioburden if not properly laundered. Validated laundering processes define wash temperature, detergent, rinse cycles, and re-sterilization method.
  • The wringing mechanism affects liquid retention: poor wringing leads to inadequate surface wetting and insufficient contact time for the disinfecting agent.
  • Mop handles and wringer buckets must also be included in the C&D program; they are potential sources of secondary contamination.

Buckets

Buckets used to prepare and carry cleaning solutions should be:

  • Dedicated to specific areas or grades to prevent cross-contamination.
  • Cleaned and disinfected after each use using the same validated procedures applied to other equipment in the area.
  • Inspected regularly for cracks, pitting, or residue buildup that can harbor microorganisms.
  • Properly labeled with the cleaning agent in use, concentration, and preparation date/time to ensure solution is not used beyond its validated in-use period.

Equipment Surfaces & HVAC / Air Handling Surfaces

Equipment Surfaces and HVAC Air-Handling System Surfaces | TR70 §9.4.1–9.4.3

9.4.1 Non-Product-Contact Equipment Surfaces

Non-product-contact equipment surfaces are those found in close proximity to product-contact surfaces. Because of their critical location, any cleaning chemical, sanitizer, disinfectant, sporicide, wiper, or other material used on these surfaces must not leave a residue that could be inadvertently transferred to a product-contact surface.

The cleaning sequence for non-product-contact surfaces is:

  1. Pre-cleaning: Remove past product spills, broken glass (from vials, syringes, ampoules), torn stoppers, damaged caps, and other foreign matter before attempting disinfection.
  2. Disinfection: Once pre-cleaning is complete, spray or wipe the surface with an efficacious disinfectant or sporicide and allow it to remain wetted for the specified contact time.
  3. IPA wipe-down: After the contact time, all surfaces should receive a wipe-down using 70% isopropyl alcohol (IPA) if a sporicide or disinfectant with residual properties was used.

9.4.2 Work Surfaces

Work surfaces — tables, carts, setup areas — may be near product or near components that come into contact with product. Precleaning before disinfection provides a higher level of disinfection efficacy. After cleaning, surfaces should be disinfected using either a disinfectant or a sporicide. A 70% IPA spray-down followed by a dry wipe may be required if the agent used leaves a residue. Frequency is normally daily but should be based on usage.

9.4.3 Nonstructural and Hard-to-Clean Surfaces

Structural surfaces (walls, ceilings, floors) along with filling equipment should be routinely cleaned and disinfected. Frequency should be based on environmental monitoring (EM) results and/or a risk-based analysis. Nonstructural surfaces require equal consideration, as they can contaminate the environment.

Surfaces are divided into two categories:

  • Routine nonstructural surfaces: Tanks, carts, countertops, racks, packaged supplies, storage bins, stairs, work surfaces, non-product-contact surfaces, monitors, gauges.
  • Hard-to-clean surfaces: Tops of doors, tracks, conveyors, phones, equipment feet and legs, underside of tanks/carts/equipment, wheels, exterior of tubing/pipes, incubators, refrigerators, cold rooms, tools (sterilization may be required).

HVAC and Air Handling Surfaces

HVAC components in cleanrooms — HEPA filters, air supply plenums, return air grilles, and ductwork — require a dedicated cleaning approach:

  • HEPA filters: Must not be wetted with cleaning or disinfecting agents under any circumstances. Accidental wetting creates a habitat for mold growth and can damage or compromise the filter matrix, leading to filter integrity failure.
  • Air supply plenums: Cleaned on a scheduled basis. Visual inspection for dust or particulate buildup informs frequency. Fogging or gassing agents may penetrate into plenum spaces but care must be taken to protect filter integrity.
  • Return air grilles: Wiped with a disinfectant-moistened wipe. Grilles collect particulates and must be cleaned with a frequency informed by EM trend data — adverse EM trends may trigger increased grille cleaning frequency.
  • Cleaning schedule tied to EM data: HVAC surface cleaning is not fixed at an arbitrary calendar interval. It is a dynamic schedule responsive to environmental monitoring trends, particularly elevated particle counts or microbial exceedances.

9.4.1 Non-Product-Contact Equipment Surfaces

Non-product-contact equipment surfaces are located in areas adjacent to product-contact surfaces. Because of this critical location, any cleaning agent, disinfectant, sporicide, wiper or other material used on these surfaces must not leave residues that could be transferred to product-contact surfaces.

Cleaning process for non-product-contact surfaces:

  1. Pre-cleaning: before attempting disinfection, first remove previous product spills, glass fragments (from vials, syringes, ampoules), broken rubber stoppers, damaged caps and other foreign matter.
  2. Disinfection: after pre-cleaning, spray or wipe the surface with an effective disinfectant or sporicide and keep the surface wet for the specified contact time.
  3. IPA wipe-down: after the contact time has elapsed, if a sporicide or disinfectant with residual properties was used, all surfaces should be wiped with 70% isopropyl alcohol (IPA) to remove the residue.

9.4.2 Work Surfaces

Work surfaces such as workbenches, carts and set-up areas may be adjacent to product or product-contact components. Pre-cleaning before disinfection improves disinfection effectiveness. After cleaning, disinfect with a disinfectant or sporicide; if the agent used leaves a residue, spray with 70% IPA and wipe with a dry cloth. The frequency is usually daily but should be adjusted to actual use.

9.4.3 Nonstructural and Hard-to-Clean Surfaces

Structural surfaces (walls, ceilings, floors) and filling equipment should be cleaned and disinfected periodically, at a frequency determined by environmental monitoring (EM) results and risk analysis. Nonstructural surfaces also need attention because they too can contaminate the cleanroom environment.

Surfaces fall into two categories:

  • Routine nonstructural surfaces: tanks, carts, countertops, racks, packaged materials, storage bins, stairs, work surfaces, non-product-contact surfaces, monitoring instruments, gauges.
  • Hard-to-clean surfaces: tops of doors, tracks, conveyors, telephones, equipment stands and legs, undersides of tanks/carts/equipment, wheels, exterior surfaces of tubing/pipes, incubators, refrigerators, cold rooms, tools (which may need to be sterilized).

HVAC Air-Handling System Surfaces

HVAC components in cleanrooms - HEPA filters, air supply plenums, return air grilles and ductwork - require dedicated cleaning approaches:

  • HEPA filters: must never be wetted with cleaning or disinfecting agents under any circumstances. Accidental wetting creates conditions for mold growth and can damage the filter media, compromising filter integrity.
  • Air supply plenums: clean periodically according to a plan. The cleaning frequency is determined by visual inspection for dust or particle accumulation. Fogging or gassing may penetrate the plenum space, but filter integrity must be protected.
  • Return air grilles: wipe with wipers saturated with disinfectant. Grilles accumulate particles, and the cleaning frequency should be based on EM trend data - if EM shows an adverse trend, increase the frequency of grille cleaning.
  • Linking the cleaning plan to EM data: cleaning of HVAC surfaces is not fixed to a calendar schedule but responds dynamically to environmental monitoring trends - especially elevated particle counts or microbial excursions.

Core concept: EM data-driven cleaning plans

A fixed "once a month" schedule as the sole basis is not sufficient. GMP requires the cleaning frequency to be responsive - when EM trends show rising particle or microbial counts, this is a signal to intensify cleaning of HVAC surfaces, rather than waiting for the next scheduled cycle.

Floors, Drains & Special Surfaces

Floors, Drains and Special Surfaces | TR70 §9.5

Floor Cleaning Principles

Floors in classified areas are among the highest-bioburden surfaces due to gravity settling of particles and personnel foot traffic. Effective floor cleaning requires:

  • Directional mopping: Always mop from the cleanest area toward the dirtiest area, and from the highest-grade room toward lower-grade rooms. This prevents reintroduction of contamination to cleaner zones.
  • Mechanical action: Mopping provides the mechanical action required to dislodge adhered microorganisms and particulates. Spraying alone is insufficient for floors.
  • Liquid removal: After mopping with a cleaner, excess liquid and suspended contaminants must be removed using a sterile dry mop, a sterile dry wipe, or a HEPA-filtered wet vacuum. This prepares the surface for the disinfecting agent.
  • Disinfection contact time: After the floor surface dries, it should be sufficiently wetted with a sterile (Grade A/B) or sterile/non-sterile (Grade C/D) disinfecting agent. The disinfectant must remain in contact with the surface for the validated contact time.
  • Zone boundaries: Physical entry mats, tacky mats, and step-over barriers define zone boundaries and reduce particulate transfer between areas. These items must also be included in the C&D program.

Drains

Floor drains present unique microbiological challenges in classified areas:

  • Drains are inherently high-bioburden areas. Standing water, biofilm, and organic matter accumulate within drain traps and surrounding surfaces.
  • Drains in Grade C and D areas should be cleaned and disinfected regularly. Sporicide application to drain surfaces and interior trap areas is strongly recommended.
  • Drains in Grade A or B areas are generally not present (open drains are incompatible with aseptic processing). Where present, they must be sealed or capped when aseptic operations are underway.
  • Drain cleaning must be documented with frequency and agent defined in the C&D SOP. Results should be correlated with environmental monitoring data from nearby monitoring points.

Curtains and Pass-Through Areas

Cleanroom curtains present particular challenges due to their soft surface finish. When viewed microscopically, curtain surfaces are rougher than wall surfaces and provide more protection for dirt and microorganisms against cleaning and disinfecting agents.

  • Curtains should be cleaned first using a high-surfactant-based cleaner or 70% IPA applied via a mechanical wipe or mop action.
  • After cleaning, curtains should be disinfected with a low-residue disinfectant or sporicide (e.g., hydrogen peroxide or peracetic acid). Contact time must meet validated requirements (minimum 5 minutes).
  • If a medium- to high-residue agent (phenolic, quaternary ammonium, or bleach) is used, curtains must be subsequently wiped with 70% IPA and a dry wipe to remove residue. Residue transfer to critical areas or filling equipment must be prevented.
  • Curtains should be cleaned with greater frequency than wall surfaces due to higher personnel contact frequency.

Principles of Floor Cleaning

Cleanroom floors are among the surfaces with the highest bioburden because of particles settling by gravity and personnel movement. Effective floor cleaning requires:

  • Directional mopping: always mop from the cleanest area to the dirtiest, and from higher-grade rooms to lower-grade rooms. This prevents contaminants from being carried back into cleaner areas.
  • Mechanical action: mopping provides the mechanical action needed to remove adherent microorganisms and particles; spraying alone is not sufficient for floors.
  • Liquid removal: after mopping with a cleaning agent, excess liquid and suspended contaminants must be removed with a sterile dry mop, sterile dry wipers or a HEPA-filtered wet vacuum, in preparation for the subsequent application of disinfectant.
  • Disinfectant contact time: once the floor surface is dry, apply a sterile (Grade A/B) or sterile/non-sterile (Grade C/D) disinfectant with thorough wetting, and the disinfectant must remain in contact with the surface for the validated contact time.
  • Area boundaries: tacky mats and step-over barriers define area boundaries and reduce particle transfer between areas. These items must also be included in the C&D procedure.

Drains

Drains present unique microbial challenges in cleanrooms:

  • The drain itself is a high-bioburden area. Standing water, biofilm and organic matter accumulate in drain traps and surrounding surfaces.
  • Drains in Grade C and D areas should be cleaned and disinfected regularly. Applying a sporicide to the drain surface and the inside of the trap is strongly recommended.
  • Grade A or B areas are generally not fitted with drains (open drains are incompatible with aseptic processing). If present, they must be sealed or capped while aseptic operations are in progress.
  • Drain cleaning must be documented; the SOP should define the frequency and agents used, and the results should be correlated with EM data from nearby monitoring locations.

Curtains and Pass-Through Areas

Cleanroom curtains present particular challenges because of their softer surface characteristics. Under a microscope, a curtain surface is rougher than a wall surface, providing more hiding places for dirt and microorganisms that are protected from cleaning and disinfecting agents.

  • Curtains should first be cleaned with a high-surfactant-based cleaner or 70% IPA, using mechanical wiping or mopping action.
  • After cleaning, disinfect with a low-residue disinfectant or sporicide (such as hydrogen peroxide or peracetic acid). The contact time must meet the validated requirement (minimum 5 minutes).
  • If a medium- to high-residue agent (phenolic, quaternary ammonium compound or bleach) is used, the curtain must subsequently be wiped with 70% IPA and a dry cloth to remove the residue, preventing transfer of residue to critical areas or filling equipment.
  • Because of the higher frequency of personnel contact, curtains should be cleaned more frequently than walls.

Analogy: the drain as a contamination "trap"

Imagine a drain as a "reservoir" that continually receives all kinds of contaminants - wastewater from every floor cleaning, microorganisms brought in by personnel and particles settling from the environment all end up concentrated in the drain trap. If it is not treated regularly with a sporicide, the drain can become a source of reverse contamination, carrying microorganisms back into the environment after the floor has been cleaned.

Lyophilizer Cleaning and Disinfection

Lyophilizers (freeze-dryers) represent one of the most critical pieces of equipment for C&D in aseptic fill-finish manufacturing. The interior chamber creates a complex environment for microbial contamination control due to its enclosed nature, condensation accumulation, and the use of cryogenic temperatures during operation.

Key C&D Requirements for Lyophilizers

  • Interior chamber cleaning after each cycle: The lyophilizer chamber must be cleaned after every production cycle. Product residues, stopper fragments, broken vials, and condensed moisture must be removed before the next loading.
  • Door gasket inspection: The door gasket (seal) must be inspected at each cleaning cycle for integrity. Cracks, cuts, or biological residue on the gasket can compromise chamber sterility and provide a harbor for contamination.
  • Shelf condensation management: Shelf surfaces accumulate condensation during the freeze and primary drying phases. After cycle completion, shelves must be wiped with a sterile wipe and cleaned before sporicide application. Standing moisture on shelf surfaces can dilute disinfectants and reduce efficacy.
  • Sporicide treatment: Sporicide treatment of the lyophilizer interior is often required — particularly following product campaigns, environmental monitoring excursions, or scheduled periodic decontamination. Common sporicides include 0.52% sodium hypochlorite, peracetic acid solutions, or vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VHP). VHP offers the advantage of gaseous penetration to all internal surfaces without manual wiping.
  • Condenser cleaning: The condenser accumulates ice during operation. After defrost, the condenser must be cleaned and disinfected. Condensate water must be drained and the condenser surface treated to prevent biofilm formation.

GMP Documentation Requirements

Lyophilizer C&D is a critical GMP activity. At manufacturing sites such as aseptic CDMO operations, all C&D activities on the lyophilizer must be:

  • Documented in the batch record (batch manufacturing record / BMR) for each production lot processed in that lyophilizer.
  • Linked to environmental monitoring results — EM samples from the lyophilizer chamber and loading area should be reviewed alongside the C&D record to confirm that cleaning was effective and the environment was in a state of control.
  • Executed according to approved SOPs with version control. Operators performing lyophilizer C&D must be trained and qualified on the procedure.
  • Subject to periodic validation review — lyophilizer C&D procedures require validation data demonstrating that sporicide contact time, agent concentration, and application method achieve log reduction targets for target organisms.

Regulatory Emphasis

FDA and EMA inspection findings frequently cite inadequate lyophilizer cleaning and disinfection as a contributor to environmental monitoring excursions and sterility assurance failures. The lyophilizer is a confined, low-temperature environment where moisture and residue can persist if C&D practices are not rigorously controlled.

Corrosion Prevention — Disinfectant Compatibility with Stainless Steel

Many disinfectants and sporicides used in aseptic manufacturing facilities are corrosive to stainless steel equipment surfaces when used incorrectly or in excess concentration. Understanding material compatibility is essential to protecting equipment longevity and preventing product contamination from corrosion byproducts.

High-Risk Disinfectants for Corrosion

  • Chlorine-based agents (sodium hypochlorite / bleach): These are highly effective sporicides but are among the most corrosive to 316L stainless steel, aluminum, and other metals. Pitting corrosion (localized corrosion creating small holes in the surface) is the primary failure mode. Risk increases with concentration, temperature, and contact time. Chlorine solutions must not be left on stainless steel surfaces beyond the validated contact time.
  • Peracetic acid (PAA): Effective sporicide; less corrosive than chlorine at typical use concentrations but still requires rinsing. Higher concentrations used in recirculating systems can accelerate corrosion of welds and surface imperfections.
  • Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂): Generally lower corrosion risk than chlorine at standard concentrations. VHP applications at high concentrations may affect certain polymers and elastomers rather than stainless steel.

Compatibility Data and Rinsing Protocols

Formal material compatibility data must be obtained before introducing any new disinfectant or sporicide into the C&D program:

  • Compatibility testing should cover the actual materials of construction in the facility: grade of stainless steel, polymers, gasket materials, painted surfaces, anodized aluminum, and floor coatings.
  • Validated rinsing protocols must define the rinse agent (sterile water for injection / WFI, or purified water), rinse volume, number of rinse cycles, and acceptable residual limits for the disinfecting agent on the surface post-rinse.
  • Contact time limits must be established and enforced. Operators must not allow sporicides or chlorine-based agents to dry on stainless steel surfaces before rinsing.
  • Corrosion inspection should be a routine part of equipment maintenance and cleaning verification — visible pitting, discoloration, or surface roughening should trigger investigation and potentially a change in C&D protocol.

Practical Application — Chlorine Rinsing at Fill-Finish Sites

At aseptic fill-finish CDMO operations, sodium hypochlorite is commonly used for periodic sporicide treatment of lyophilizer chambers and equipment surfaces. The validated procedure must specify that surfaces are rinsed with WFI within a defined time window after the sporicide contact time. Failure to rinse within this window has been the root cause of stainless steel pitting in filling line equipment, leading to surface qualification failures and unplanned equipment replacements.

10.0 Frequency for Cleaning and Disinfection

Selecting an appropriate cleaning and disinfection frequency for manufacturing facility surfaces — walls, ceilings, doors, non-product-contact equipment, and floors — is essential for maintaining effective contamination control. The industry has developed three main approaches:

Approach 1: Area Classification

Frequencies are set according to the stringency of the area classification. The most stringent area receives the most frequent cleaning. Example:

  • Grade A (ISO 5): Daily cleaning and disinfection
  • Grade C (ISO 7): Weekly
  • Grade D (ISO 8): Monthly

Limitation: Does not account for product contamination risk or type of manufacturing conducted.

Approach 2: Environmental Monitoring (EM) Data

Frequency is set based solely on trending EM data. This approach tends to be reactive and retrospective — it responds to existing contamination levels rather than proactively preventing them. More commonly used to justify reducing an already-established frequency based on sustained satisfactory results.

Approach 3: Risk-Based Model (Preferred)

Combines area classification and EM data while also considering product exposure risk, personnel intervention, and manufacturing type. This provides the greatest flexibility to tailor the program to the specific facility and process.

Key risk-based principles:

  • Frequency must be commensurate with the risk of product contamination. Open vs. closed processing and proximity to final sterilization steps are primary criteria.
  • Areas within the aseptic manufacturing boundary (Grade D to Grade C) should be cleaned more frequently than those outside.
  • ISO 8 areas adjacent and contiguous to open aseptic processing zones may need more frequent cleaning than standalone ISO 8 areas.
  • Product changeovers require cleaning per established procedures to prevent cross-contamination.
  • High-ingress locations (gowning airlocks, doors, floors) and areas supporting microbial growth warrant higher frequency.
  • Older buildings or difficult-to-clean layouts may warrant increased frequency.
  • Event-based triggers — such as EM action level excursions, HEPA filter failures, or facility shutdowns — require additional cleaning beyond routine schedules.
  • Routine EM data must be reviewed regularly to verify that the chosen frequency remains effective.

11.0 Resistance and Rotation

11.0 Resistance and Rotation

For many years, the industry debated whether microorganisms can develop resistance to sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides — analogous to antibiotic resistance. The current scientific consensus from TR70 and USP <1072> is as follows:

"The development of microbial resistance to antibiotics is a well-described phenomenon. The development of microbial resistance [to disinfectants] is less likely, as disinfectants are more powerful biocidal agents than antibiotics and are applied in high concentrations against low populations of microorganisms, so the selective pressure for the development of resistance is less profound." — USP <1072>

Key reasons why true disinfectant resistance is unlikely in cleanrooms:

  • Antibiotic resistance typically requires modification or acquisition of a single gene. Disinfectants have multiple, simultaneous mechanisms of action against cellular physiology — multiple simultaneous mutations would be required in a short contact time (e.g., 5 minutes).
  • Cleanrooms maintain inherently low cell numbers, reducing the statistical probability of a resistance mutation surviving.

Evolution of Rotation Strategy

Historically, firms rotated between two or more disinfectants (e.g., QAC alternate with phenolic). This led to:

  • High residue buildup on surfaces
  • Subordinate (reduced) efficacy performance

Current best practice: Rotate a disinfectant with a sporicide. This is superior because sporicides address spore-forming organisms that standard disinfectants cannot reliably eliminate.

Special considerations:

  • Using a sporicide alone (without a disinfectant) is possible if efficacy is proven — but is discouraged due to the inherent corrosive nature of most sporicidal agents.
  • All rotation systems must be evaluated using area classification, EM data, and/or formal risk assessment.

11.0 Resistance and Rotation Strategy

There has long been debate in the industry over whether microorganisms develop resistance to disinfectants similar to antibiotic resistance. The current scientific consensus in TR70 and USP <1072> is:

"Microorganisms are less likely to develop resistance to disinfectants than to antibiotics, because disinfectants are more potent biocides applied in low-bioburden environments, so the selection pressure for resistance is far lower than for antibiotics." - USP <1072>

The main reasons true resistance is unlikely to arise in a cleanroom environment:

  • Antibiotic resistance can arise from a single gene mutation; disinfectants act on cell physiology through multiple mechanisms, and within a short contact time (for example, 5 minutes) several simultaneous mutations would be needed to produce resistance, which is extremely improbable.
  • Microbial counts in a cleanroom are very low, making the statistical probability of an effective resistance mutation even lower.

Evolution of Rotation Strategy

Historically, plants alternated "two or more disinfectants" (for example, QAC rotated with a phenolic). The problems:

  • Serious buildup of surface residues
  • Decline in overall antimicrobial efficacy

Current best practice: rotate a disinfectant with a sporicide, because the latter can effectively kill spore-forming microorganisms that standard disinfectants cannot.

Analogy - the meaning of rotation

Imagine washing your hair with the same shampoo for a long time and it seems to work less well - this is not true "resistance" but a perception caused by surface residue or reduced performance.

The real value of disinfectant rotation is not to "prevent resistance" but to: (1) ensure that different types of microorganisms (including spores) are effectively covered; (2) prevent residue buildup; (3) verify the continued effectiveness of the rotation plan through EM data.

  • Efficacy validation data for each agent (including spore testing for the sporicide)

12.0 Return from a Shutdown

12.0 Return from a Shutdown

A shutdown is a planned or unplanned stoppage of operations that is likely to compromise the environmental conditions in a classified area. Types include:

  • Planned: Preventive maintenance, construction activities, scheduled facility holidays
  • Unplanned: Unexpected power outages, HVAC failures, emergency stops

The extent and duration of a shutdown determines the level of viable and non-viable contamination introduced. Regardless of type, defined actions must be taken before operations resume.

Return-to-Control Criteria

  1. Verify control systems: Confirm that all HVAC/air handlers, pressure differentials, and environmental controls are functioning properly before beginning any cleaning.
  2. Pre-clean with detergent or water: Remove dirt, dust, or construction debris before applying any disinfectant. Disinfectants cannot effectively penetrate gross contamination.
  3. Disinfect per established procedures: Apply disinfectant according to qualified procedures to reduce bioburden to acceptable levels. All surfaces — walls, floors, and equipment surfaces — must be included.
  4. Use qualified, in-situ data where possible: The multistep cleaning and disinfection regimen should be supported by evidence demonstrating it is capable of bringing the facility back to a state of control.
  5. Monitor before resuming operations: Sample for viable and non-viable particulates after cleaning and disinfection is complete. Evaluate results to verify the area is within control limits before returning to use.

Key Principle

The return-to-use decision must be data-driven. Environmental monitoring results — not just visual inspection or adherence to a cleaning schedule — should confirm the area is back in a state of control.

12.0 Return-to-Operation Procedure After a Shutdown

A shutdown is a planned or unplanned cessation of operations that may jeopardize the environmental state of the cleanroom. Types include:

  • Planned shutdowns: preventive maintenance (PM), construction activities, scheduled site holidays
  • Unplanned shutdowns: sudden power outage, HVAC failure, emergency line stoppage

The duration and scope of the shutdown determine the level of viable and non-viable contamination introduced. Whatever the type, a clear action procedure must be executed before returning to operation.

Return-to-Control Criteria

  1. Confirm that control systems are operating properly: before starting any cleaning work, confirm that HVAC / air conditioning, pressure differentials and all environmental control systems have been restored to normal.
  2. Pre-clean first with a cleaning agent or water: remove dust, dirt or construction debris accumulated during the shutdown. Disinfectants cannot effectively penetrate heavy organic contamination.
  3. Disinfect according to the established procedure: apply the validated disinfection procedure to reduce bioburden to an acceptable level. It must cover all surfaces - walls, floors and equipment surfaces.
  4. Support with in-situ data wherever possible: a multi-step cleaning and disinfection program should be backed by data demonstrating that it restores the facility to a state of control.
  5. Perform environmental monitoring before resuming production: after cleaning and disinfection are complete, sample for viable and non-viable particles. Production may resume only after the results are evaluated and confirmed to be within control limits.

Core principle

The decision to resume operations must be data-driven. Environmental monitoring results - not visual inspection or adherence to the cleaning schedule alone - confirm that the area has returned to a state of control.

CDMO Practice - TWi

At TWi, any unplanned shutdown (such as a power outage or HVAC failure) requires a deviation investigation to be initiated. 13.0 Hold Times for Cleaned Areas, Non-Product-Contact Equipment, and Utensils

13.0 Hold Times for Cleaned Areas

After an area or facility has been cleaned and disinfected, a maximum hold time must be established — the maximum allowable time between the completion of cleaning/disinfection and the resumption of production activities, during which the area is considered to remain in a "clean" state.

Hold Time Study Requirements

Studies must be performed to establish and validate the maximum hold time. Key elements:

  • Sampling timepoints: Viable and non-viable sampling is performed immediately after cleaning and disinfection, and again at or beyond the maximum proposed hold time limit (e.g., at 24h and 48h).
  • Controlled entry: Entrance into the area after cleaning should be limited to reduce artificial contamination during the study.
  • Representative conditions: The study should include the normal level of non-production activities that would typically occur in the area during the hold period (e.g., routine checks, maintenance pass-throughs).
  • Equipment and utensils: Hold times for non-product-contact equipment and utensils must also be established and validated. These materials must be stored in a manner that maintains their integrity for the duration of the established hold time.

Exceeding Hold Times

If the hold time is exceeded — for areas, non-product-contact equipment, or utensils — they must be cleaned and disinfected again before use. This is a non-negotiable requirement. Exceeding hold time without re-cleaning constitutes a potential GMP deviation.

Common Industry Hold Time Limits

While specific limits must be established for each facility based on its own validation data, common industry benchmarks include:

  • 24-hour hold time for Grade A/B areas after cleaning
  • 48-hour hold time for Grade C areas
  • 72-hour or weekly hold times for Grade D areas (depending on risk)

These are illustrative examples only — each site must validate its own limits.

13.0 Hold Time After Cleaning

After a facility or area has been cleaned and disinfected, a maximum hold time must be established - that is, the longest permitted time after completion of cleaning and disinfection during which the area is still considered "clean" before production resumes.

Hold Time Study Requirements

Studies must be performed to establish and validate the maximum hold time. Key elements:

  • Sampling time points: sample immediately after cleaning and disinfection, and again when the proposed maximum time limit is reached (or exceeded) (for example, at 24 h and 48 h).
  • Restricted entry: access to the cleaned area should be restricted during the study to avoid contamination introduced by personnel affecting the results.
  • Representative conditions: the study should simulate the non-production activities that normally occur during the hold period (such as routine inspections and maintenance access), so that the study conditions are representative.
  • Equipment and utensils: hold times for non-product-contact equipment and utensils also need to be established and validated. The storage method must ensure that the integrity of the clean state is maintained throughout the hold time.

Handling When the Hold Time Is Exceeded

If an area, non-product-contact equipment or utensils exceed the established hold time, they must be cleaned and disinfected again before use. This is a non-negotiable requirement. Continuing to use them without re-cleaning constitutes a potential GMP deviation.

Common Industry Hold Time Benchmarks

Although each facility must establish its own limits based on its own validation data, the following are common industry reference values:

  • Grade A/B areas: 24 hours after cleaning
  • Grade C areas: 48 hours after cleaning
  • Grade D areas: 72 hours or weekly (depending on risk)

The above are examples for reference only; each site must validate its own limits.

CDMO Practice - TWi

At TWi, exceeding a hold time directly triggers the deviation process. Operators must know the hold time limit for each area and each type of equipment, and record the cleaning time in the batch production record (BPR).

Key items auditors check:

  • Whether the BPR records the cleaning completion time and the pre-use verification

15.0 Overview: Linking EM Excursions to Cleaning Failures

If the cleaning and disinfection program is not carried out properly, microbial counts in specific areas or on equipment surfaces of the facility may exceed limits. Whenever there is an adverse environmental monitoring (EM) trend, an excursion, or a data shift, a review of the cleaning and disinfection program must be included as a required element of the investigation.

Quick Guide to Tracing Organisms

  • Gram-positive cocci / non-spore-forming rods: most likely from personnel (skin flakes, respiratory tract).
  • Gram-positive rods & fungi: most likely from the external environment, including air, soil, and facility interstitial spaces.
  • Gram-negative rods: most likely associated with water or liquid sources, such as drains, mop buckets, and spray containers.

CDMO Practical Tip

A positive EM result does not necessarily mean the product is contaminated, but it must trigger an investigation. Organism comparison is the first step in narrowing the root cause: if the isolate is Gram-negative, give priority to reviewing the water systems, spray bottle management, and wet-area cleaning procedures.

15.1 Investigation Areas — Cleaning Program Failures

When reviewing the cleaning and disinfection program as part of an investigation, areas that should be considered based on available data include but are not limited to:

Agent & Surface Factors
  • Antimicrobial chemical agent residue buildup and soil not adequately removed by cleaning, thus preventing adequate disinfection
  • Inappropriate application of antimicrobial chemical agents
  • Insufficient contact times on surfaces
  • Incorrect selection of agents in relation to the organisms found
  • Expired solution
  • Incorrectly prepared solutions
Equipment & Tool Factors
  • Inappropriate decontamination of components or bagging before transfer to the controlled area
  • Use of inadequate clean room tools
  • High bioburden or shedding from inappropriate cleaning apparatus
Procedural / Human Factors
  • Lack of adherence to established cleaning and disinfection procedures

15.1 Key Areas of Investigation: Causes of Cleaning Program Failure

When reviewing the cleaning and disinfection program during an investigation, consider the following aspects (not limited to these) based on the available data:

Disinfectant and Surface Factors
  • Accumulated disinfectant residue or surface soil that was not effectively removed, reducing the efficacy of subsequent disinfection
  • Improper application of the disinfectant (for example, insufficient dosage or uneven spraying)
  • Insufficient surface contact time, below the validated value
  • Disinfectant selection not matched to the isolated organism (for example, ineffective against a particular spore)
  • Use of expired solutions
  • Dilution errors (concentration too low or too high)
Equipment and Cleaning Tool Factors
  • Materials or packaging bags not properly decontaminated before transfer into controlled areas
  • Use of cleaning tools unsuitable for the cleanroom environment
  • Cleaning devices that themselves have a high bioburden or shed particles
Procedural / Human Factors
  • Failure to follow the established cleaning and disinfection SOP (Standard Operating Procedure)

Key Questions to Ask in Root Cause Analysis (RCA)

Wrong agent? Not enough contact time? Wrong technique? Or a missed area? Rule them out systematically one by one to find the true point of failure.

15.2 Investigation Checklist Questions

Equipment Related

  • For area disinfection (floors, walls, countertops), is the disinfection equipment clean and dry before each use? In Grade A/B (ISO 5/6) areas, is all equipment including tanks and tubing sterilized before each use?
  • Is equipment stored properly in a clean area and covered until use?
  • For transfer disinfection, are spray containers either single use or properly cleaned before reuse?
  • Are carts used for transport properly disinfected, including the wheels?

Materials and Solutions Related

  • Is cleaning and disinfection performed starting from the cleanest area, progressing to the dirtiest?
  • Are disinfection efficacy studies available at the site, including methods and expiry dating, as well as any predominant site isolates?
  • Are antimicrobial chemical agents adequately rotated with use of a sporicide to maintain low levels of spore-forming organisms without causing surface erosion from overuse?
  • Is the agent applied such that surfaces remain wet for the required contact time, yet not so wet that puddles remain — which may allow non-fermenting gram-negative organisms to proliferate?
  • Are in-situ studies available demonstrating effectiveness of the site's restart disinfection program (after construction, power outage, or prolonged shutdown)?
  • Do certificates of analysis on agents show any changes?
  • If the agent is sterile-filtered in-house, do sterile filtration records and bioburden data reveal any issues?

Personnel / Training

  • Are accurate and complete SOPs in place and available?
  • Are operators trained and qualified on agent application, including contact time and residual removal?
  • Have agents been prepared properly?
  • Are personnel instructed not to enter disinfected areas until after the contact time has been exceeded?
  • Are construction areas properly segregated from in-service areas to prevent cross-contamination?
  • Are clean room staff trained in and exhibiting consistently good aseptic technique?

Equipment-related

  • Before disinfecting floors, walls, work surfaces, and similar areas, is the disinfection equipment clean and dry? Are the canisters and tubing used in grade A/B (ISO 5/6) areas sterilized before each use?
  • Is equipment stored in a clean area and kept covered until use?

  • Are transport carts (including the wheels) properly disinfected?

Materials and solutions-related

  • Is cleaning and disinfection performed from the cleanest area toward the dirtiest area?
  • Are disinfectant efficacy study data available on site, covering the method, expiry period, and the principal site isolates?
  • Does the disinfectant rotation plan include a sporicide to control spore-forming organisms, while avoiding overuse that causes surface corrosion?
  • During application, is the surface kept wet for the validated contact time without pooling (pooled liquid can allow Gram-negative non-fermenting bacteria to proliferate)?
  • Are on-site efficacy study data available for the "restart disinfection program" (for example, after construction, a power outage, or a prolonged shutdown)?
  • Does the disinfectant lot Certificate of Analysis show any change in specification or composition?
  • If the site sterile-filters disinfectants in house, do the sterilizing filtration records and bioburden data show any abnormality?

Personnel / training-related

  • Is the SOP complete, correct, and easily accessible?
  • Have operators been trained and qualified in disinfectant application (including contact time and residue removal)?

  • Are personnel clearly told that, after disinfection, they must wait until the contact time has ended before entering?

Step 1 - Organism identification: Confirm the type of isolate and infer the likely direction of the contamination source (personnel / environment / water).

Step 2 - Timeline comparison: Compare the EM excursion date against cleaning records, personnel changes, construction activities, and material lots.

Step 3 - On-site verification: Perform enhanced EM sampling of the suspect areas to confirm that disinfectant efficacy and contact time meet requirements.

16.0 Conclusion

The systematic role and core objective of cleaning and disinfection  |  p48

16.0 Conclusion — The Role of Cleaning and Disinfection

A robust contamination control system starts with controlling contamination from entering classified areas. Such a system stages items from the exterior environment to be cleaned, disinfected, and sterilized prior to entry. Without a system for controlling contamination from entering, loss of control of environmental conditions is very likely.

Cleaning complements the control system as the blockage of all contamination from entering is extremely difficult. Cleaning with a non-abrasive action and subsequent lifting of dirtied soils, liquids, particulates, and microbes prepares the surface to be characteristically lower in soil/residue and bioburden, making disinfection of what remains a simpler and more successful process.

The disinfection of areas utilizing a validated agent corrects the errors that have occurred during the control and cleaning process.

"Cleaning and disinfection of classified areas is not a preventative measure but rather a corrective action procedure done to equalize the failures of the control system."

Many pertinent details defined in this technical report combine together to help provide the opportunity for success, which is measured as consistent acceptable environmental conditions. Consistent control is the ultimate goal.

This technical report is not intended to replace any existing requirements or standards; it is a best practice reference document regarding the fundamentals of cleaning and disinfection.

16.0 Conclusion: The Systemic Role of Cleaning and Disinfection

A sound contamination control system starts with preventing contamination from entering the classified areas. It requires that everything coming from the external environment go through staged cleaning, disinfection, and even sterilization before entry. Without entry controls, the risk of losing control of environmental conditions is very high.

Cleaning is a supplementary mechanism of contamination control, because completely preventing contamination from entering is almost impossible. Its role is to remove soil, liquids, particulates, and microorganisms in a non-abrasive way and reduce the surface bioburden, so that subsequent disinfection starts from a lower microbial count and has a higher chance of success.

Disinfection then uses a validated agent as the final correction (Corrective Action) for microorganisms remaining after cleaning.

Reframing the Core Concept

Many people mistakenly regard cleaning and disinfection as a "preventive measure". TR70 states clearly that it is a Corrective Action Procedure: a procedure used to remedy the situation after the contamination control system has already shown a failure.

The ultimate goal is therefore not "perfect cleaning and disinfection", but a "contamination control system that is consistent and sustained, with environmental conditions reliably meeting the requirements".

Appendix II — Regulatory Registration Overview

Disinfectants and sanitizers used in pharmaceutical and food-drug manufacturing facilities must comply with applicable registration requirements. These requirements differ significantly between the US and EU regulatory frameworks.

United States — US EPA Registration

In the United States, antimicrobial pesticide products — including disinfectants and sanitizers used in drug manufacturing facilities — are regulated under the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA), administered by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA).

  • All disinfectants used in pharmaceutical and food/drug manufacturing environments must hold a valid US EPA Registration Number.
  • The EPA registration process requires efficacy data demonstrating the product kills claimed organisms under labeled conditions, including contact time and concentration.
  • Manufacturers must provide the EPA-registered product label; claims must not exceed label specifications.
  • State-level registration may also be required in certain US states beyond federal EPA registration.

European Union — Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR)

In the EU, disinfectants used in aseptic manufacturing facilities are regulated under Regulation (EU) No 528/2012 — the Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR), administered at the EU level by the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) with national competent authorities.

  • All biocidal active substances must be approved at the EU level before products containing them can be authorized in Member States.
  • Product Authorization (PA) is required in each Member State where the product will be placed on the market, unless Mutual Recognition procedures are used.
  • Product type classification (e.g., PT2 — disinfectants for private and public health areas) determines the scope and use claims of the authorization.
  • Transitional measures apply for existing products while active substance reviews are ongoing.

Appendix II: Overview of Disinfectant Regulatory Registration Systems

Disinfectants and sanitizers used in pharmaceutical and food/drug manufacturing facilities must meet the registration requirements of each market. The regulatory frameworks of the US and the EU differ significantly.

United States: US EPA Pesticide Registration System

In the US, disinfectants used in drug manufacturing facilities are classed as "Antimicrobial Pesticide Products", governed by the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA) and enforced by the US Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA).

  • All disinfectants used in pharmaceutical and food/drug environments must hold a valid US EPA Registration Number.
  • The EPA registration process requires efficacy data showing that, under the conditions stated on the label (including contact time and concentration), the product kills the microorganisms it claims to kill.
  • The manufacturer must provide the EPA-registered label; no claimed use may go beyond the scope stated on the label.
  • Some US states require a separate state-level registration in addition to the federal EPA registration.

European Union: Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR)

In the EU, disinfectants used in sterile manufacturing facilities are governed by Regulation (EU) No 528/2012, the Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR), which is advanced at EU level by the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA), with the competent authorities of the Member States responsible for the actual authorizations.

  • Every biocidal active substance must first be approved at EU level before a product containing it can be submitted for authorization in a Member State.
  • Product Authorization (PA) must be obtained separately in each Member State where marketing is planned, or the process can be simplified through Mutual Recognition.
  • Product Type (PT) classification (for example, PT2: disinfectants for private and public health areas) determines the scope of authorization and the permitted claims.
  • Transitional measures apply to existing products while their active substances are under review.

In the United States, multiple federal agencies have overlapping but distinct authority over disinfectants and sanitizers used in pharmaceutical manufacturing settings. Understanding which agency regulates which product type is essential for compliance.

Appendix III: US Disinfectant Regulatory Framework

Several US federal agencies hold overlapping but distinct regulatory authority over disinfectants in pharmaceutical facilities. Knowing which agency governs which type of product is the basic prerequisite for compliance.

Key Compliance Points for Disinfectants in US Pharmaceutical Plants

  • EPA-registered products must be used according to the label instructions: concentration, contact time, and method of application must not deviate from the label.
  • FDA 21 CFR Part 211.56 (sanitation) requires facilities to establish written sanitation procedures that use EPA-registered disinfectants.
  • FDA expects disinfectant qualification studies (efficacy validation and in-use field testing) to demonstrate suitability in the specific facility environment.
  • Rotating the use of sporicides is industry best practice, to prevent adaptation by spore-forming organisms.

EPA vs. FDA: Who Governs What?

EPA governs "product registration": whether this disinfectant can be legally marketed and which germicidal claims it may make.

FDA governs "compliant use": the pharmaceutical manufacturer must be able to show, with documented records, that the disinfectant you use is effective in your facility and under your conditions.

Both must be satisfied; neither alone is enough.

The EU regulatory framework for disinfectants used in pharmaceutical aseptic manufacturing involves several overlapping regulatory instruments. EU regulation of disinfectants in sterile pharmaceutical facilities involves several overlapping regulatory frameworks. Unlike the US, where the responsibilities of the EPA and FDA are relatively clearly divided, the EU system requires compliance with both the product authorization pathway (BPR) and the GMP framework.

Key Compliance Points for Disinfectants in EU Pharmaceutical Plants

BPR governs "whether the product may be sold and used": the marketing authorization of the disinfectant itself, i.e. whether the active substance is approved and a product authorization number exists.

GMP Annex 1 governs "how it is used in a GMP environment": efficacy validation, rotation program, sterile preparation, and inclusion in the CCS.

Both sets of requirements must be met; having a BPR authorization does not automatically satisfy the GMP efficacy validation requirements.

Overview: How EPA Safety Labels Are Determined

Labeling requirements for antimicrobial products vary by country. For products registered in the United States, the EPA provides prescriptive precautionary label language. This language is determined by the results of six acute toxicity studies performed on the product formulation:

  • Acute oral toxicity — systemic toxicity via ingestion
  • Acute dermal toxicity — systemic toxicity via skin absorption
  • Acute inhalation toxicity (4-hour exposure) — systemic toxicity via breathing
  • Primary eye irritation — irritation or corrosion of ocular tissue
  • Primary skin irritation — tissue destruction or erythema
  • Dermal sensitization — potential for allergic contact dermatitis

With the exception of dermal sensitization, each study is assigned to a Toxicity Category (I–IV) based on study results. Category I is most hazardous; Category IV is least hazardous.

Overview of the EPA Safety Labeling System

The US EPA requires every registered antimicrobial product (including cleaners, disinfectants, and sporicides) to complete six acute toxicity studies before marketing, and assigns each study to a Toxicity Category from I to IV according to the results:

  • Acute oral toxicity
  • Acute dermal toxicity
  • Acute inhalation toxicity (4 hours)
  • Primary eye irritation
  • Primary skin irritation
  • Dermal sensitization

Except for sensitization, the other five studies are categorized by result. The category determines the signal words and PPE requirements on the label. Category I = most hazardous; Category IV = lowest hazard.

Practical Point

The supplier SDS (Safety Data Sheet) must reflect the EPA toxicity category. When purchasing, request the original EPA-registered label and confirm that the category and PPE specifications are consistent with the SOP.

Appendix VI: AOAC Protocol Testing for Disinfectant Registration (Section 22.0)

Overview: AOAC and EPA Registration

Firms registering antimicrobial products are required by the U.S. EPA to submit detailed information including:

  • Formula and chemical/physical characteristics of the formulation
  • Effectiveness data supporting claims against specific microorganisms
  • Safety / toxicity data

The AOAC International (Association of Official Analytical Collaboration) develops standardized methods accepted by the EPA for this testing. While EPA registration requires these methods, they should not be considered a mandatory requirement for GMP operations. GMP facilities do not utilize AOAC protocols for their own antimicrobial effectiveness programs; however, verifying that a supplier holds U.S. EPA approval is part of the disinfectant selection process.

See www.eoma.aoac.org for specific AOAC methods.

EPA Label Claim Categories

The general efficacy label claims recognized by EPA are:

  • Sporicide (Cold Sterilant) — eliminates all forms of microbial life including fungi, viruses, bacteria, and spores. Spores are the most resistant; sporicide = cold sterilant in EPA terminology.
  • Disinfectant — eliminates or irreversibly inactivates infectious bacteria (not necessarily spores). Divided into: Hospital Use and General Use categories.
  • Fungicide — reduces microorganisms on inanimate surfaces to safe levels per public health codes.
  • Tuberculocide — destroys or irreversibly inactivates Mycobacterium tuberculosis in the inanimate environment.
  • Virucide — destroys or irreversibly inactivates viruses in the inanimate environment.

Overview of the AOAC Test System

AOAC International (Association of Official Analytical Chemists) has developed a series of standardized microbicidal efficacy test methods, which are the core tools accepted by the US EPA for disinfectant registration.

AOAC Testing ≠ GMP Efficacy Validation

Important distinction: AOAC testing is the tool a supplier uses to obtain EPA marketing registration; it is not equivalent to a pharmaceutical plant's disinfectant validation. A GMP facility must still perform independent efficacy confirmation testing (using the suspension or carrier method) against its own environmental isolates (site-specific isolates).

EPA Efficacy Claim Categories (Explanation)

  • Sporicide / cold sterilant: kills all forms of microorganisms (including spores). In sterile pharmaceutical environments, sporicides are the highest level of disinfection requirement, used for sterilizing isolators, VHP cycles, and so on.
  • Disinfectant: kills vegetative bacteria but does not necessarily kill spores. Divided into Hospital Use and General Use. Hospital Use requires passing tests with three strains (S. choleraesuis, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa).
  • Fungicide: eliminates or reduces fungi to a safe level.
  • Tuberculocide: effective against Mycobacterium tuberculosis, indicating a degree of activity against a broad range of atypical mycobacteria (such as M. chelonae).
  • Virucide: effective against specific viruses; the scope is broad (such as adenovirus, norovirus).

AOAC Test Requirements by EPA Label Claim

Sterilizer (Sporicide) Claim

  • Method: AOAC Sporicidal Test
  • Carriers: 60 carriers each on two surfaces (porcelain penicylinders + silk suture loops)
  • Organisms: Bacillus subtilis ATCC 19659 + Clostridium sporogenes ATCC 3584
  • Requirement: Three samples from three lots (one lot 60 days old); must kill all 720 carriers. One lot independently tested required by EPA.

Disinfectant — Limited Efficacy Claim

  • Method: AOAC Use-Dilution Method or AOAC Germicidal Spray Products Test
  • Organisms: Salmonella choleraesuis ATCC 10708 or Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538
  • Requirement: 60 carriers, three lots (one 60 days old), killing 59 out of 60 carriers per set.

Disinfectant — Hospital/Medical Environment Claim

  • Method: AOAC Use-Dilution Method or AOAC Germicidal Spray Products Test
  • Organisms: S. choleraesuis, S. aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 15442
  • Requirement: 60 carriers, three lots (one 60 days old), killing 59 out of 60 carriers per set.

Fungicide Claim

  • Method: AOAC Fungicidal Test (or modified Use-Dilution / Germicidal Spray with AOAC Fungicidal elements)
  • Organism: Trichophyton mentagrophytes ATCC 9533
  • Requirement: 10 carriers, two lots, killing all fungal spores.

Virucide Claim

  • Method: Carrier modifications of AOAC Use-Dilution or Germicidal Spray
  • Requirement: Recoverable virus titer ≥104 from test surface; two lots, four dilutions — no detectable virus or ≥3-log reduction if cytotoxicity present.

Tuberculocide Claim

  • Method: Tuberculocidal Activity Method or AOAC Germicidal Spray modified per Tuberculocidal Activity Method
  • Organism: Mycobacterium tuberculosis var. bovis (BCG)
  • Requirement: Two lots; kill entire test organism on all carriers; no growth in inoculated media tubes. Alternative: Quantitative Tuberculocidal Activity Test (4-log kill required).

Non-food-contact Sanitizer Claim

  • Method: Guideline 91-30 Method No. 8
  • Organisms: S. aureus + K. pneumoniae or E. aerogenes on glass, metal, or ceramic surfaces
  • Requirement: ≥99.9% bacterial reduction within 5 minutes.

Food-contact Sanitizer Claim (Halide products)

  • Method: AOAC Available Chlorine Germicidal Equivalent Concentration Method
  • Organism: Salmonella typhi ATCC 6539
  • Requirement: Activity equivalent to 50, 100, and 200 ppm available chlorine; three lots (one ≥60 days old).

For QACs, chlorinated trisodium phosphate, anionic detergent-acids: AOAC Germicidal and Detergent Sanitizers Method against E. coli + S. aureus — 99.999% reduction within 30 seconds; three lots (one ≥60 days old).

AOAC Test Methods in Detail

AOAC Use-Dilution Method

One of the most commonly used EPA registration tests. Standard strains (S. aureus, S. choleraesuis, P. aeruginosa) are inoculated onto metal carriers (penicylinders), exposed to the disinfectant at use concentration for a set time, then transferred to culture medium for incubation and the kill rate is calculated.

Acceptance criterion: no growth on at least 59 of 60 carriers (≥ 98.3% kill rate).

AOAC Sporicidal Test

Designed for the most difficult-to-kill spore-forming organisms (B. subtilis, C. sporogenes). Two distinctly different carrier materials (porcelain, silk suture loops) are used to simulate the sporicidal effect on different surfaces.

Requirement: 720 carriers (60 per lot × 2 carrier types × 3 lots × 2 strains) must all show no growth, an extremely strict standard.

Three-Strain Requirement for Hospital-Grade Disinfectants

A hospital/medical environment claim requires passing tests with three representative strains at the same time:

  • S. choleraesuis (Gram-negative, representing Enterobacteriaceae)
  • S. aureus (Gram-positive, representing Staphylococcaceae)
  • P. aeruginosa (Gram-negative, the most difficult-to-kill non-fermenter)

These three strains cover the main pathogen types of clinical environments and are also organisms commonly recovered in pharmaceutical environmental monitoring.

Pharmaceutical Significance of Tuberculocides

In a pharmaceutical manufacturing environment, a product with a tuberculocidal claim is effective against a broad range of mycobacteria (Mycobacterium spp.). If atypical mycobacteria such as M. chelonae or M. gordonae are found in environmental monitoring, give priority to disinfectants carrying this claim.

Adapting AOAC for Pharmaceutical Environments

AOAC methods were originally designed for EPA registration, with an inoculum level typically of 106–108 CFU/mL.
Pharmaceutical/biotech companies typically reduce the inoculum to 104 CFU/mL (reflecting the lower bioburden of cleanrooms), and change the acceptance criteria to:

  • Vegetative bacteria: ≥ 3-log reduction
  • Spore-forming bacteria: ≥ 2-log reduction

This modification must be clearly documented in the disinfectant validation protocol, with the reason for deviating from the standard method.

Lot Stability Requirement

The EPA requires that, of the three lots tested, one must be at least 60 days "old", to simulate the worst case of product shelf stability in the supply chain. When reviewing supplier data, Purchasing and QA must confirm that the tested lots actually meet this requirement.

EU Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR) and EN Tests

The EU Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR 98/8/EC) requires approval for all biocide products — including clean room disinfectants — sold in EU markets. The BPR replaces individual country registration systems for active ingredients.

A key component of BPR dossiers is the efficacy assessment. The EU Commission places EN (European Norm) test methods at the top of its preferred efficacy data list. EN tests follow a three-phase evaluation framework:

Three-Phase EN Evaluation Framework

Phase 1: Basic suspension test to determine bactericidal, fungicidal, or sporicidal activity. No specific contact time; no interfering substances required.

Phase 2: Tests for defined applications, split into two steps:

  • Step 1: Suspension test with specified contact time. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) added as an interfering substance — 0.3% for clean conditions, 3.0% for dirty conditions.
  • Step 2: Tests simulating practical conditions, e.g., surface/carrier tests.

Phase 3: Field trial tests (in-situ field studies).

EU Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR) and the EN Test System

The EU Biocidal Products Regulation (BPR, Biocidal Products Directive 98/8/EC) requires all biocides (including cleanroom disinfectants) to obtain approval before being placed on the EU market. EN testing is the most widely recognized efficacy test system in the EU approval process.

EN Three-Phase Evaluation Structure

EN testing verifies product efficacy step by step, from the basic level to real-use conditions:

  • Phase 1 (basic suspension test): confirms basic bactericidal/fungicidal/sporicidal activity, with no specified contact time and no interfering substance added.
  • Phase 2 / Step 1 (quantitative suspension test): simulates actual use conditions, adding bovine serum albumin (BSA) as an interfering substance:
    – clean conditions: 0.3% BSA
    – dirty conditions: 3.0% BSA
  • Phase 2 / Step 2 (surface test): simulates the germicidal effect on real surfaces (such as stainless steel, glass).
  • Phase 3 (field trial): in-situ verification in the actual use environment.

Main EN Test Standards Explained

EN 1276: Quantitative Suspension Test for Bactericidal Activity (Phase 2, Step 1)

The most widely used EN bactericidal test. It quantitatively evaluates bactericidal efficacy in food, industrial, domestic, and institutional environments.
Acceptance criterion: 5-log (99.999%) reduction of vegetative bacteria within 5 minutes.

EN 1650: Quantitative Suspension Test for Fungicidal Activity (Phase 2, Step 1)

Designed in parallel with EN 1276, targeting efficacy against fungi.
Acceptance criterion: 4-log reduction within 15 minutes.

EN 13704: Quantitative Suspension Test for Sporicidal Activity (Phase 2, Step 1)

Targets efficacy against bacterial spores. Spores are harder to kill than vegetative bacteria, so the contact time is longer and the required log reduction is lower.
Acceptance criterion: 3-log reduction within 60 minutes.

EN 13697: Quantitative Non-Porous Surface Test (Phase 2, Step 2)

Evaluates bactericidal or fungicidal efficacy on non-porous hard surfaces such as stainless steel and glass (simulating cleanroom walls and tabletops), closer to actual operating conditions.
Acceptance criteria: bacteria, 4-log reduction in 5 minutes; fungi, 3-log reduction in 15 minutes.

Interpreting Table 23.0-1

This table is the core reference for quickly confirming whether a disinfectant meets EU regulatory requirements. Key observations:

Practical Implications for Cleanroom Operations

The BSA interfering substance in EN testing (0.3% clean / 3.0% dirty) simulates surfaces with organic residue. In an actual cleanroom environment:
– Disinfecting after a cleaning step = close to clean conditions (0.3% BSA)
– Skipping cleaning and disinfecting directly = dirty conditions (3.0% BSA)
This is one of the scientific bases for the GMP requirement to "clean first, then disinfect".

Whether under the AOAC or EN system, personal protection of operators is a core requirement:

  • When diluting disinfectants: rubber or nitrile gloves + chemically compatible protective clothing + rubber apron
  • When disinfecting ceilings: protective cap/gown + sealed goggles (to prevent liquid from entering the eyes)
  • Fogging/gassing operations: adequate ventilation or respiratory protection; return to the work area only after volatile components (alcohol, sporicides) fall below the PEL

AOAC vs. EN Methods: Comparative Overview

Side-by-side analysis for CDMO supplier qualification

AOAC vs. EN: Key Differences

AOAC vs. EN Comparison (Explanation)

Regulatory Authority and Applicable Market

AOAC methods serve the US EPA FIFRA regulatory framework; EN methods serve the EU BPR regulatory framework. Both are required for a product to be marketed in the respective market, and they are not interchangeable.

Differences in Test Design Logic

AOAC: based mainly on carrier tests, simulating the germicidal effect after the disinfectant contacts a contaminated surface (penicylinder = a model of a medical device), with a binary pass/fail result (59/60).

EN: based mainly on quantitative suspension tests, and also uses a standardized interfering substance (BSA), simulating the two real-world situations of "clean" and "dirty" more systematically.

Key Points for CDMO Supplier Qualification Audits

A CDMO (such as TWi) that supplies both US and EU customers should require the following when auditing disinfectant suppliers:

  • AOAC test data: confirm the EPA registration claims (sporicidal, disinfectant, bactericidal)
  • EN test data: confirm BPR regulatory compliance (EN 1276/EN 13697/EN 13704)
  • Both consistency of label claim scope: ensure the AOAC and EN claims cover the same types of microorganisms

Common supplier qualification gap: claiming "GMP compliance" but providing test data for only one of the two systems, or an AOAC claim (such as sporicidal) that does not match the EN test data (no EN 13704).

Analogy: AOAC vs. EN

AOAC is like a "US driver's license": it lets you drive legally in the US (EPA registration); EN is like an "EU driver's license": legal use in the EU (BPR registration). Holding a US license does not automatically allow use in the EU, and vice versa. A CDMO operating in a global supply chain must make sure the disinfectants it uses hold "both licenses".

Appendix VII: EN Testing

The EN test system simulates actual use conditions (including interfering substances and surface tests) more systematically than AOAC. EN 1276 (bactericidal), EN 1650 (fungicidal), EN 13697 (surface), and EN 13704 (sporicidal) together cover the full range of efficacy claim needs. When purchasing disinfectants for the European market, confirm that the supplier holds the relevant EN test data and complies with the BPR.

Global Market Challenges for CDMOs

A CDMO supplying both the US and EU markets:
US: require supplier EPA registration + AOAC test data
EU: require BPR approval + EN test data
Common: the plant's own disinfectant validation: perform suspension or carrier tests with site-specific isolates to meet FDA/EMA GMP requirements

Most common audit gap: for a product claiming "sporicidal efficacy", the supplier provides only bactericidal data (EN 1276/AOAC Use-Dilution) and lacks supporting sporicidal data (EN 13704/AOAC Sporicidal).

Appendix VIII: Large-Scale Gassing or Fogging of Clean Rooms (Section 24.0)

p. 62–65 | Decontamination technologies overview

Overview & Chemical Agents

Vaporization or fogging of disinfectants for large-scale decontamination is being used or considered by many firms in the pharmaceutical industry. A significant amount of published data show the efficacy of this type of disinfectant system against vegetative bacteria, fungi, and bacterial spores.

Chemical agents commonly used for large-scale gassing/fogging include:

  • Paraformaldehyde
  • Peracetic acid / hydrogen peroxide
  • Phenols
  • Bleach (sodium hypochlorite)
  • Quaternary ammonia
  • Vapor phased hydrogen peroxide (VPHP / VHP)
  • Gaseous chlorine dioxide
  • Ozone

Critical Principle: Gassing Does Not Replace Cleaning

While gassing and fogging systems can provide excellent destruction of microorganisms present in contaminated areas, such systems do not clean surfaces. Therefore, cleaning is considered a mandatory and routine step in addition to gassing or fogging. Residues, particulates, foreign matter, and pyrogens are not removed by any gaseous decontamination process.

Key Points

Large-Scale Gaseous Decontamination vs. Routine Cleaning/Disinfection

TR70 treats large-scale gaseous decontamination as a "special-event tool" rather than a substitute for routine procedures:

  • Trigger situations: return to use after facility renovation, a major microbial contamination event (positive EM event), restart after a plant-wide shutdown
  • Complementary relationship: decontamination can be performed before or after cleaning, but it cannot replace mechanical cleaning (wiping, mopping)
  • CDMO application: for client changeovers and room transitions between different product batches, large-scale VHP is sometimes needed as additional assurance

Analogy: Nuclear Weapons vs. Conventional Weapons

Large-scale VHP decontamination is like the "nuclear option" in military terms: powerful, but not a replacement for everyday routine protection. Daily wipe-down disinfection of the facility is the conventional weapon; VHP is the heavy weapon deployed only as a last resort. The two roles are different, and neither can do without the other.

Classification Logic of Gaseous Decontaminants

TR70 divides these technologies into two broad categories:

  • True gaseous: VHP, ClO₂, ozone, formaldehyde. Gas molecules distribute evenly and penetrate well
  • Wet fogging: micro-droplets (10–25 µm) physically coat surfaces. This relies on contact rather than gas penetration

Which one to choose depends on room construction, target organisms, residue requirements and personnel safety considerations.

P

Paraformaldehyde Gassing

p. 63 | Legacy technology — declining use

Mechanism & Historical Use

Paraformaldehyde gas has been used as a large-scale clean room decontamination methodology for many years. It is a white, crystalline powder that, when heated, releases formaldehyde gas — the actual decontaminant. The gas is created inside the room by a pan and heating element, then spread by portable fans set up prior to gas generation.

Paraformaldehyde gas microbial destruction claims through the U.S. EPA are limited to what would be termed a high-level disinfectant, sanitizer, or fungicide. However, sporicidal reduction has been obtained in clean rooms by many firms worldwide.

"Paraformaldehyde is an older methodology that has been replaced by most GMP firms with current, more modern chemistries and systems. It leaves concerning residuals on all surfaces (as defined by FDA) and requires the utmost safety concerns for human health. For these reasons, this methodology is declining in use."

Why GMP Facilities Are Phasing Out Formaldehyde Fumigation

Core Problems of Formaldehyde Fumigation

  • Surface residue: formaldehyde leaves residues on all contacted surfaces, and FDA has expressed clear concern about this
  • Personnel health risk: formaldehyde is a known carcinogen (IARC Group 1), and operating risk is very high
  • Regulatory pressure: EU and many national occupational safety regulations are setting increasingly strict formaldehyde exposure limits
  • Better alternatives already exist: VHP, ClO₂ and similar technologies are mature, and residue issues are easier to manage

CDMO Practical Reminder

If facility SOPs or historical records still contain a formaldehyde fumigation procedure, it should be listed as a priority item in the facility modernization improvement plan. Formaldehyde fumigation should not be included as an option at all in the design of new facilities.

W

Wet Droplet Fogging

p. 63–64 | Aerosol-based surface coating technology

Technology Description

Wet droplet fogging involves the generation of small liquid droplets (typically 10–25 microns in diameter) from a chemical agent placed into an airstream by a generator linked to a fogging device. Various fogging devices are placed strategically throughout the room; portable fans circulate the droplets.

Chemical agents commonly used include peracetic acid, hydrogen peroxide, sodium hypochlorite, phenols, and quaternary ammoniums — chosen based on the type of antimicrobial action required.

Key Operating Parameters

  • Goal: Lightly coat all surfaces with a thin but constant layer of chemical agent for an extended period
  • Fogging time: Typically 15 minutes to 1 hour
  • Release time: 15 minutes to 1 hour, or until all surfaces are dry
  • Efficacy range: Sanitization to sporicidal, depending on agent selected

This is considered a wet or aerosol process rather than a true gaseous process. Wetting of surfaces reduces decontamination times, dry times, and release times. With overuse and without manual cleaning procedures, residues can build up over time and corrosion can occur.

Practical Analysis of Wet Fogging

Analogy: Shower vs. Steam Bath

Wet fogging is like a "shower": a water mist wets the surface to achieve disinfection. VHP is like a "steam bath": the vapor gets everywhere and penetrates even crevices. The former is quick and convenient; the latter penetrates better but is more complex to set up.

CDMO Operating Considerations

When using wet fogging, pay particular attention to the following:

  • Operators must not enter the room until the mist concentration has dropped below the permissible exposure limit
  • Overuse without mechanical cleaning leads to accumulation of QAC residues (resistance selection pressure)
  • After each fogging cycle, all surfaces that are not completely dry should be wiped dry manually, with appropriate personal protective equipment worn
VHP

Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide (VPHP / VHP)

p. 64 | Gold standard for pharmaceutical gaseous decontamination

Technology & Mechanism

The vapor of hydrogen peroxide is noncarcinogenic and breaks down to water and oxygen, eliminating the corrosive residuals inherent with traditional methods such as paraformaldehyde.

VPHP systems generate a low level of vaporized 35% hydrogen peroxide (250–1,200 ppm) into manufacturing areas through portable or fixed distribution systems. In the vapor phase, disinfection may require longer times compared to traditional methods, depending on:

  • Number of VPHP generators
  • Size of the area to be treated
  • Required contact and clearance times

The distribution of vapor within the room can be verified with hydrogen peroxide sensors or chemical indicators.

Operational Considerations

  • The VPHP process is classified as a disinfection step, not sterilization
  • Leaks to the external environment and clearance time must be tested and assessed
  • Air-handling systems may need to be shut off; doorways and return vents sealed
  • Release times: 2–4 hours, depending on area size and gassing duration

Isolator vs. Large-Scale Application

The effectiveness of VPHP has been well documented in isolator operations for many years. However, isolator operations are smaller, sealed, and have evacuation systems that can remove and scrub the volatile gases. The use of VPHP in a large-scale, open manufacturing environment has also been very successful and — unlike UV — is effective in the presence of shadowing.

Conclusive validation studies for large-scale VPHP are specific to the operation and setup. Each area should be assessed for effectiveness in its own validation study.

VPHP in the C&D Program Sequence

  • Before cleaning: VPHP = sanitization step → must be followed by mechanical wipe/mop
  • After cleaning: VPHP = final disinfection step → the preferred sequence in most GMP programs

In-Depth Analysis of VHP

Core Concepts of VHP (Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide)

VHP / VPHP (Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide) is currently the most mainstream large-scale gaseous decontamination technology in pharmaceutical GMP environments.

  • Mechanism of action: H₂O₂ vapor releases free radicals (•OH) that damage cell membranes, DNA and proteins
  • Biocidal capability: can achieve a 6-log kill of spores (Geobacillus stearothermophilus), making it the most important decontamination tool for sterile manufacturing facilities
  • Residue advantage: ultimately breaks down into H₂O + O₂ with no toxic residue, meeting FDA expectations on residues
  • Concentration range: 250–1,200 ppm (depending on the target kill level)

The Four Phases of a VHP Cycle

1. Dehumidification
   → Lower the relative humidity to prevent premature condensation of H₂O₂

2. Conditioning
   → Begin injecting H₂O₂ vapor; concentration rises gradually

3. Exposure
   → Maintain the target concentration (ppm) × time (min)
   → Biological indicators (BI) placed at the most difficult-to-reach locations

4. Aeration
   → Introduce filtered air to break H₂O₂ down to a safe concentration
   → Typically takes 2–4 hours

CDMO Practice: How TR70 and PtC-12 Complement Each Other

TR70 (this document) provides the overall C&D framework and context: the position of VHP at room level, applicable scenarios, and its relationship to cleaning procedures.

PtC-12 (RABS and Isolators) provides specific guidance on isolator VHP decontamination: cycle development, biological indicator placement strategy, D-value calculation and parametric release.

In day-to-day CDMO operations, when designing an isolator VHP SOP, both documents should be consulted: TR70 provides the "why" and PtC-12 provides the "how".

G

Gaseous Chlorine Dioxide (ClO₂) & Ozone Gas

p. 64–65 | Emerging alternatives for large-scale decontamination

Gaseous Chlorine Dioxide (ClO₂)

Gaseous chlorine dioxide is another available alternative for gassing. Of the methods discussed, it is most similar to paraformaldehyde or VPHP rather than wet droplet fogging, as it is a true gas product. Application to the area to be decontaminated is accomplished in much the same way as the VPHP gas process. Basic differences between the products relate to corrosion, residual, safety, and setup.

To disinfect a room, gaseous ClO₂ is precipitated into the area via a generator and dispersion heads. These systems have been employed in many industry settings and are now being considered as a possible alternative for GMP operations.

Ozone Gas

Ozone is made by adding high voltage to oxygen. The system uses a high concentration of ozone gas, integrating a gas generator to emit ozone to the area to be decontaminated. Design specifications for typical systems include:

  • Ozone concentration: 200 ppm or more
  • Relative humidity: 80% or more
  • Treatment time: Determined by area size, inherent bioburden, and obstructions

These systems have been employed in many industry settings and are now being considered as a possible alternative for GMP operations.

Positioning of ClO₂ and Ozone

TR70 Perspective: A Framework for Technology Selection

TR70 describes ClO₂ and ozone more briefly than VHP, implying that in 2015 these two technologies were still "being considered". When selecting a large-scale decontamination technology, evaluate it along the following dimensions:

  • Corrosiveness (compatibility with stainless steel and electronic equipment)
  • Residue risk (product-contact surfaces, patient safety)
  • Personnel safety (permissible exposure limit, PEL)
  • Setup complexity (generators, distribution system)
  • Availability of validation data
S

Safety Requirements & Validation Obligations

p. 65 | Non-negotiable principles for any gassing program

Safety Imperative

Whenever chemical agents are used for large-scale gassing or fogging of clean rooms, safety concerns must be addressed. All of the agents discussed can result in injury or death of personnel if proper precautions are not taken to ensure the containment of the chemical agent to the intended areas. For many of the agents discussed, residues that are left behind on product-contact surfaces are also a significant concern and must be evaluated.

Validation Requirement

Although these methods of decontamination are effective, they should not be used to replace a routine program for cleaning and disinfecting the clean room areas. If they are used as the standard practice, they should be validated to demonstrate their ability to achieve an appropriate level of bioburden reduction. This should be performed taking into consideration the material of construction present in the clean room areas.

Source Identification

It is also important to consider the source of the organisms, for gassing will only remove what is present and may leave behind moisture, allowing for further proliferation if the causative agents (e.g., leaks, contamination sources) have not been removed from the area.

Safety and Validation: The Baseline a CDMO Must Meet

Minimum Validation Requirements for Large-Scale Decontamination

Although TR70 does not list a detailed validation program, the following elements are industry consensus:

  • Biological indicators (BI): Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores, ≥10⁶ initial population, targeting a 6-log kill
  • Exposure mapping: place BIs and chemical indicators at the most difficult-to-reach locations in the room (dead spots, behind equipment, corners)
  • Concentration verification: use H₂O₂ sensors or chemical indicators to confirm that the concentration is evenly distributed throughout the room
  • Material compatibility: confirm that room construction materials and equipment surfaces can withstand the decontaminant without damage
  • Residue testing: confirm that residue levels on product-contact surfaces after decontamination meet specifications
  • Leak testing: confirm that the decontaminant does not escape into other areas or corridors

Root Cause Analysis Before Decontamination

TR70 emphasizes that large-scale decontamination can only eliminate the "existing" microorganisms. If the source of contamination (such as facility defects, personnel practice problems or equipment damage) has not been eliminated, residual moisture after decontamination may instead lead to even greater microbial proliferation.

Correct sequence: identify and eliminate the contamination source → clean → decontaminate → confirm with environmental monitoring.

Exercise: Is Your Facility's VHP Program Complete?

  • Does the VHP cycle have a complete validation report (including BI results and exposure mapping)?
  • Does the operating SOP cover personnel safety, HVAC isolation procedures and release criteria?
  • Is there a confirmation step after VHP (EM sampling) to verify decontamination effectiveness?
  • Is mechanical cleaning before VHP documented?
  • Has the VHP compatibility of room construction materials been evaluated?
