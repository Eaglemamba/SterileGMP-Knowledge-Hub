1.0

Introduction

Background, revision history, and rationale for TR43

Original Text (English)

PDA Technical Report No. 43 (Revised 2023): Identification and Classification of Nonconformities in Moulded and Tubular Glass Containers for Pharmaceutical Manufacturing provides an updated approach to a quality decision-making process and represents best practices for the identification and classification of visual nonconformities for glass containers.

This technical report was originally published in 2007 and applied only to moulded bottles and tubular vials. The document was revised in 2013 to address industry feedback. The 2023 revision addresses evolving standards and container types, including:

  • Reduced reliance on limit samples; additional guidance on minimum sizes for nonconformities.
  • Harmonized risk levels for glass particle defects based on contemporary risk assessment.
  • Broader concepts on ready-to-use (RTU) vials and cartridges — RTU formats require different standards for particular types of defect.
  • Minor changes in descriptions to improve precise identification of nonconformities.
  • Standardized terminology, replacing "N/A" with "acceptable imperfection."
  • Condensed lexicons removing redundant or out-of-scope defects (e.g., Residual Stress eliminated).
Commentary

Core Concept: Why Do We Need TR43?

Imagine this: the glass manufacturer's sales rep says a defect is a "cosmetic issue", while the pharma company's QA says it is a "major defect". Each side talks past the other, incoming inspection stalls, and the lot nearly gets scrapped. This is exactly why TR43 was created: to establish a common language so that everyone understands and classifies the same glass container defect in the same way.

TR43 is not a mandatory standard but an industry-consensus best practice guide, helping pharmaceutical companies and glass suppliers establish consistent acceptance criteria.

Reminder: Key Points of the 2023 Revision

  • RTU containers (Ready-to-Use) are now formally included: RTU containers have already been washed, depyrogenated and sterilized and go straight onto the pharma company's filling line, so higher standards apply to certain defect types.
  • "N/A" → "Acceptable Imperfection": the terminology is standardized to state clearly that "this item does not constitute a nonconformity", avoiding misunderstandings during audits.
  • Reduced reliance on limit samples: minimum sizes are now described in text, which improves practicality and consistency between sites.

Analogy: Academic Grading Standards

TR43 is like a university "grading rubric". Without a common rubric, different professors might score the same assignment anywhere from 70 to 90. With TR43 as the "grading rubric for glass defects", both the glass manufacturer's outgoing QC and the pharma company's incoming IQC measure with the same yardstick, which greatly reduces disputes.

1.1

Purpose

Intended use of TR43 quality criteria and lexicons

Original Text (English)

The standardized quality criteria in TR-43 are intended as guidance for container manufacturers and for incoming container acceptance inspection at pharmaceutical companies. The attributes identified in this lexicon are not intended to represent the defect after a theoretical extrapolation of further damage during processing, rather just the defect when discovered.

Five detailed lexicons have been updated which visually illustrate glass nonconformities — one for moulded glass bottles and vials, and four for tubular glass vials, ampoules, cartridges, and syringes. Additionally, defects specific to RTU containers and their categorization are included.

Consulting with the appropriate regulatory authorities for agreement on the strategies employed for identification and classification of visual nonconformities of glass containers is always advisable.

The identification and classification of glass imperfections represents only a part of the overall acceptance criteria. Other aspects include adherence to dimensional standards, visual nonconformance inspection programs, acceptable quality limits, and reinspection.

Commentary

How TR43 Is Used

TR43 mainly serves two use cases:

  • Glass manufacturer outgoing quality control (Outgoing QC): setting process control criteria based on the TR43 classification.
  • Pharma company incoming inspection (IQC, Incoming Quality Control): establishing acceptance specifications based on the TR43 glossary and classification, and deciding whether to accept or reject a lot.

Important Limitation: The "State at the Time of Finding" Principle

TR43 states explicitly that classification is based on the condition of the defect at the time it is found, not on speculation about how the defect might worsen in later processing (such as washing, sterilization or filling).

For example, a tiny surface scratch may be Minor at inspection, but that does not mean it will not become a bigger problem after autoclaving. The pharma company must assess such risks itself in its acceptance specification.

Five Glossaries (Lexicons) for the Container Types

  • Moulded Glass Bottles and Vials
  • Tubular Glass Vials
  • Ampoules
  • Cartridges
  • Syringes

Because forming methods and uses differ between container types, the defect definitions and classification criteria differ as well.

1.2

Scope

What TR43 covers — and what it does not

Original Text (English)

These guidelines are not intended to establish mandatory standards for the classification and identification of glass nonconformities but, instead, are intended to provide an overview that complements existing guidelines, standards, and referenced materials.

TR-43 applies solely to empty glass containers. It does not address:

  • Drug product interaction with containers
  • Quality attributes introduced by pharmaceutical manufacturing processes
  • Container design dimensions or tolerances
  • Nonconformities in glass container secondary packaging materials

Pharmaceutical manufacturers are responsible for ensuring that container quality attributes are compatible with drug products and the subsequent processing of the containers such that finished drug product specifications are met.

Commentary

Analogy: TR43 Is the "Empty Cup Standard"

TR43 covers only the appearance defects of the empty glass container itself, much like checking an empty cup for cracks, bubbles or sharp edges. Compatibility between the drink (the drug product) and the cup is outside the scope of TR43.

This boundary is very important: topics such as container-drug compatibility and extractables must be addressed with other regulations and guidelines (such as ICH Q3D and USP 660).

The Pharma Company's Responsibility Boundary

Even if a glass container meets TR43, the pharma company must still confirm for itself:

  • Compatibility between the container and the drug product (chemical, physical, microbiological)
  • The quality state of the container after each process step (washing, sterilization, filling, vial capping)
  • Compliance with the final product specification

When a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization) helps a client establish an incoming inspection specification (IQC specification), it must take particular care to combine the TR43 framework with the special requirements of the client's drug product.

2.0

Glossary and Abbreviations

Key definitions from FDA, ANSI, ISO and TR43 itself

Original Text (English)

Wherever appropriate, terms are derived from current definitions of FDA, ANSI, ISO, and other regulatory or standards-setting organizations. Terms often differ from company to company and must be clearly defined in internal SOPs, specifications, and regulatory filings.

Commentary

Analogy: The Glossary Is a "Shared Code Book"

Pharma QA and glass manufacturer sales staff use words such as "defect", "nonconformity" and "lot" every day, but each company may define them differently. The glossary in Section 2.0 acts as a shared code book, making sure both sides mean the same thing by "lot" and "AQL" and avoiding talking past each other during supplier audits or deviation investigations.

Core Terms Explained

AQL (Acceptance Quality Limit)
AQL does not mean "zero defects". It is the maximum process average defect rate that is statistically allowed. For example, AQL = 0.65% means that across a series of consecutive lots, a process average defect rate of up to 0.65% can be tolerated; above this value the lot should be rejected.

Defect vs. Nonconformity
These two terms are often used interchangeably, but there is a subtle difference. A "defect" emphasizes impact on intended use; a "nonconformity" emphasizes failure to meet specification requirements. In practice, TR43 is mainly concerned with nonconformities (defects that do not meet the specification).

RTU containers: already washed, depyrogenated and sterilized and sent straight to the filling line, so stricter requirements apply to certain defect types (such as particulates and microorganisms).

Regulatory Basis for Incoming Inspection

Under 21 CFR 211.84 and 211.82, FDA requires drug manufacturers to sample, test and approve every incoming component lot (including glass containers). Even if the glass manufacturer is certified, the pharma company must still have a written incoming inspection procedure based on predetermined quality specifications. The glossary and classification framework in TR43 are exactly the tools needed to build those specifications.

Original Text (English)

Pharmaceutical/biopharmaceutical manufacturers should collaborate with their glass manufacturing vendors to develop a quality agreement that defines the responsibilities of both parties and includes a glass container conformance specification. Both parties should establish a container quality specification based on patient safety, product requirements, and process capabilities and create a suitable agreement that:

  • Develops the dimensional design of glass containers
  • Establishes the sampling plan the supplier uses for manufacturing control and release of finished lots
  • Defines glass container lots and acceptable quality limits (AQLs)
  • Determines the classification and disposition of nonconformities and the criteria for rework/reinspection
  • Decides on the criteria for documentation and training
Commentary

Core Elements of the Quality Agreement

The quality agreement between the pharma company (or CDMO) and the glass manufacturer must cover the following five areas:

  1. Dimensional design: tolerance ranges for container geometry (per ISO/ANSI standards)
  2. Sampling plan: the sampling method used for the supplier's process control (continuous sampling vs. lot sampling)
  3. Lot definition and AQL: the definition of a "lot" and the AQL value for each defect category
  4. Nonconformity disposition: criteria for return, reinspection and sorting
  5. Documentation and training: how both parties record and communicate defect findings and disposition results

The Special Role of the CDMO

A CDMO faces dual requirements: "upstream" (the glass manufacturer as supplier) and "downstream" (the client pharma company). When setting incoming inspection criteria, the CDMO should:

  • Confirm how sensitive the client's drug product is to container defects (for example, lyophilized products are particularly sensitive to bottom defects)
  • Translate client requirements into workable AQL specifications and write them into the purchasing specification for the glass manufacturer
  • Periodically review incoming inspection data to evaluate whether the AQL should be tightened or relaxed
3.1

Glass Container Dimensional Development

ISO standards as the foundation for dimensional design and control

Original Text (English)

Published standards are the foundation for the design and control of dimensional product quality for manufacturing moulded and tubular glass containers. The principal focus of these standards is to establish uniform dimensional design to ensure product safety and efficacy as a prerequisite to development of packaging specifications.

ISO establishes stringent dimensional requirements for glass containers (ISO 8362-1, 8362-4, 9187-1, 9187-2, 11040-1, 11040-4, 13926-1). These references provide universal dimensional standards for glass manufacturers to produce uniform containers supplied to pharmaceutical manufacturers.

Important limitation: These standards only cover the dimensional aspect and handling of glass containers — not the visual imperfections or nonconformities that can occur during glass manufacturing.

Commentary

ISO Dimensional Standards vs. TR43 Visual Defect Standard

This is the key to understanding where TR43 fits:

  • ISO standards (such as ISO 8362-1 for tubular glass vials): specify the geometric dimensions of the container, including tolerances for neck diameter, height and wall thickness. This answers the question "will it run on the filling machine?"
  • TR43: specifies the visual appearance defects of the container, such as bubbles, cracks and scratches. This answers the question "does the container quality meet pharmaceutical standards?"

The two are complementary, and both belong in the quality agreement. When accepting a lot of glass vials, you need to measure the dimensions and also inspect the appearance.

Analogy: Inspecting a House

It is like inspecting a new house: you confirm that the room sizes match the architectural drawings (the ISO dimensional standards), and you also check the walls for cracks and the tiles for damage (the TR43 visual defect standard). You cannot do without either, but they belong to different areas of assessment.

3.2

Glass Container Sampling

Lot definition, sampling plan types, and dPPM limits

Original Text (English)

The responsibility for glass container quality rests primarily with glass manufacturers. However, regulatory agencies hold pharmaceutical manufacturers responsible for ensuring the suitability of glass containers. Therefore, pharmaceutical manufacturers should have a process to confirm containers meet specified requirements — including sampling of incoming lots or procedures to verify acceptability according to a predetermined quality specification.

3.2.1 Definition of Lots

A glass container production lot is usually assumed to have a connotation of completeness and separateness. Many glass containers are manufactured in long runs (days or weeks), resulting in very large supplier batches that resemble continuously manufactured items. Incoming lots from large supplier batches are typically defined by pharmaceutical companies for the convenience of logistics and shipping, not based on uniqueness.

Since an incoming lot for a pharma company may not be the same as that of a glass manufacturer, it is important to establish an agreement on the definition of a lot. "Pocket conditions" can exist: some portions may exceed acceptance limits while adjacent portions remain within limits.

3.2.2 Sampling Plans

Two types: Variable (quantitative attributes — weight, height, diameter) and Attribute (pass/fail against specification). Subtypes include:

  • Continuous sampling: For inspection throughout glass manufacturing; designed for the manufacturer at time of production.
  • Lot-to-lot sampling: Each lot evaluated independently; applicable for pharmaceutical companies' incoming inspection (ASQ/ANSI Z1.4 or ISO 2859-1).
  • dPPM (Defective Parts Per Million): Alternative to Z1.4; agreed-upon metric between drug company and glass manufacturer; assumes high confidence in glass manufacturer's consistent quality capability.
Commentary

Special Considerations for RTU Containers

RTU (ready-to-use) containers bypass the washing and sterilization steps the pharma company would normally perform and go directly into the filling environment. Therefore:

  • Incoming inspection of RTU containers has stricter particulate and microbiological requirements than for standard containers.
  • Lot definition and sampling frequency need particular consideration, because any container defect directly affects the protective barrier of the aseptic process.
  • The 2023 revision of TR43 formally incorporates the special defect criteria for RTU containers into each glossary.

The Practical Difficulty of Defining a Lot

Glass containers are usually produced continuously over several days or even weeks, so one "manufacturing lot" may contain millions of units. Each truckload, pallet or carton the pharma company receives does not necessarily correspond to a complete lot at the glass manufacturer.

The quality agreement must therefore define clearly: "From the pharma company's point of view, what is an incoming lot?" Common definitions:

  • One purchase order (PO)
  • One pallet, or a fixed number of pallets
  • One manufacturing batch number at the glass manufacturer

"Pocket conditions" (localized conditions), where some cartons in a lot have a problem and others do not, show exactly why relying only on whole-lot AQL sampling is risky.

dPPM Calculation Formula

dPPM = (total number of nonconforming containers found in the lot / total number of containers in the lot) × 1,000,000

Example: in a lot of 50,000 glass vials, 15 nonconforming vials are found during processing:
dPPM = (15 / 50,000) × 1,000,000 = 300 dPPM

dPPM suits situations where the supplier is mature and the process is stable. If the glass manufacturer has not yet established stable quality capability, a conventional AQL sampling plan (ASQ/ANSI Z1.4) should be used first.

Analogy: The Sampling Dilemma of Continuous Manufacturing

Imagine the glass manufacturer as a conveyor belt that never stops, running continuously for weeks. What the pharma company receives is product from one stretch of that belt. Judging the product of a continuous process by a "whole lot accept/reject" concept is like judging a whole semester of learning by a single exam score: it has statistical limitations. This is the fundamental reason AQL sampling "can only provide a statistical upper bound and cannot guarantee zero defects".

3.3

AQLs

Setting and negotiating AQL values for glass nonconformity classifications

Original Text (English)

Separating the philosophy of zero defects from the practical reality of real-world manufacturing is important. Sampling plans generally enumerate nonconformities, their description, and based on severity, the corresponding AQLs.

The agreed-upon AQLs for glass containers are often a compromise between the needs of the pharmaceutical producer and the process capability of the glass manufacturer. AQL specifications require a supplier quality agreement to minimize risk.

AQL percentages may be established for each nonconformance classification. However, each company's process or product may have unique glass quality requirements. For example, a bottom nonconformance limit for a lyophilized product may be significantly different than that for a nonlyophilized product — in one case the nonconformity may have a functional impact, whereas the other may be a cosmetic nonconformance.

For nonconformities that are unacceptable at any level, "None Allowed" is the appropriate classification. Finding zero nonconformities in the sample does not ensure zero nonconformities in the batch — it can only provide a statistical upper bound. Therefore, it is not statistically possible to define AQL = 0.0%.

Commentary

The Nature of AQL: A Number That Compromises on Risk

Setting an AQL value essentially balances two realities:

  • The pharma company's expectation: the lower the defect rate the better, to protect patient safety
  • The glass manufacturer's process capability: glass forming is a high-temperature process, and completely zero defects is statistically impossible

An AQL value represents "the worst process average that is statistically acceptable". It is not a target value, and it does not mean the pharma company "accepts" the existence of that defect rate.

「None Allowed」vs. AQL = 0%

This is where many people get confused:

  • AQL = 0%: statistically impossible. A sampling plan can only provide a statistical upper bound and cannot guarantee absolutely zero defects in a lot.
  • "None Allowed": used to describe Critical defects. Finding even one in the sample means immediate rejection of the lot. This is an operational rule and is different from the statistical "AQL = 0%".

This is why TR43 uses "None Allowed" rather than "AQL = 0.0%" when describing Critical defects.

Analogy: Why AQL Must Be Customized

Take the same defect, a "slight bubble in the vial bottom". For a standard vial holding a liquid injectable it may be only Minor (AQL 4.0). For a lyophilized product, however, the freeze-drying process needs good contact at the vial bottom, so this defect may affect heat transfer efficiency and could be raised to Major B or even Major A. This is why AQL cannot be "one size fits all" and must be customized to the characteristics of the drug product and the process requirements.

Suppose you are pharma QA and are evaluating the following two AQL options for the glass vial "Finish Chip" defect:

  • Option A: AQL = 0.65% (Major A), using ASQ/ANSI Z1.4 Level II
  • Option B: AQL = 1.0% (Major B), using ASQ/ANSI Z1.4 Level II

Question: If each incoming lot is 5,000 units, which option requires more units to be inspected? What is the acceptance number for each? What is the potential patient safety impact of relaxing the AQL?

4.0

Glass Nonconformity Risk Levels

The five-tier classification framework — core of TR43

Original Text (English)

Sharing a common terminology of glass nonconformities between glass manufacturers and pharmaceutical manufacturers is recommended in order to identify and classify nonconformance attributes.

Nonconformities should be reviewed and classified (with rationale) using a quality risk management approach. Verification of nonconformity classifications may warrant additional testing.

The following list provides the rationale for each classification of nonconformance risk level:

  • Critical: A nonconformity that is likely to result in personal injury or potential hazard. Includes any nonconformity that compromises the integrity of the container, as well as risks of microbiological contamination of a sterile product.
  • Major A: A nonconformity leading to serious impairments, for example, a malfunction that makes the packaging unusable.
  • Major B: A nonconformity leading to impairments of a lesser degree, for example, reduced efficiency in production.
  • Minor: A nonconformity that does not impact product quality or process capability.
  • Acceptable Imperfection: An imperfection below the minimum nonconformity size or limit sample — considered not applicable. Therefore, it is acceptable.
Commentary

The Core Logic of Risk Classification

The five-level classification in TR43 is essentially a ladder of patient harm risk from high to low:

  • Critical: directly endangers patient life and safety (CCI breach, glass fragments, contamination)
  • Major A/B: affects container functionality and may indirectly endanger drug product quality
  • Minor: does not affect functionality but does not meet the specification
  • Acceptable Imperfection: does not constitute a nonconformity and does not require rejection

Every level should have a defined disposition, and the classification should have a documented risk assessment rationale.

Critical Defect = Immediate Lot Rejection, No Exceptions

If any Critical defect is found in the sample, the lot should be rejected immediately (Immediate Batch Rejection), with no negotiation or special dispensation.

Typical examples of Critical defects:

  • Cracks or breakage that compromise container closure integrity (CCI)
  • Sharp glass fragments (which could pierce a syringe or injure the patient)
  • Foreign contaminants (metal fragments, foreign matter)

Analogy: Traffic Lights

The five levels are like the tiers of a traffic signal:

  • Critical = red light (stop immediately, lot rejected)
  • Major A = dark orange light (AQL 0.65, strict control)
  • Major B = orange light (AQL 1.0, pass with caution)
  • Minor = yellow light (AQL 4.0, keep watching)
  • Acceptable Imperfection = green light (no limit, proceed normally)

Additional Context

Classification of glass nonconformities as critical, major A, major B, or minor (with associated AQLs) does not necessarily signify that this level of nonconformance would be considered acceptable as an outgoing quality level in pharmaceutical or diagnostic products.

However, using those classifications does allow for a distinction based on their potential impact on product quality and patient safety. The drug company and glass manufacturer need to establish the classifications and AQLs based on the drug product and process requirements.

Nonconformities should be reviewed and classified using a quality risk management approach (per ICH Q9). Verification of nonconformity classifications may warrant additional testing to establish their rationale.

Commentary

AQL ≠ Acceptable Defect Rate

TR43 states explicitly that setting an AQL does not mean the pharma company "accepts" the existence of that defect rate. An AQL is a statistical sampling tool that helps judge whether a whole shipment is likely to exceed a predetermined quality level; it is not an endorsement by the pharma company of the presence of defects.

The ideal goal is still zero defects; the AQL is a statistical safeguard under realistic process capability.

Exercise 2: Classifying Defects

Using the TR43 five-level classification framework, decide which level each of the following two defects belongs to, and explain why:

  1. Chip on the Vial Finish: an obvious chip on the sealing surface where the vial finish meets the stopper, about 0.5 mm deep, which may affect the sealing integrity of the aluminum cap.

5.0

Conclusion

Overall significance of TR43 and its lexicons for the industry

Original Text (English)

Acceptance or rejection of moulded and tubular glass containers is more than just the identification and classification of glass nonconformities. A larger process is involved that requires the drug manufacturer and glass manufacturer to create a glass-container quality specification together — employing existing standards for dimensional requirements and creating quality criteria including AQLs, nonconformity definitions, and reinspection.

The use of TR-43 lexicons should result in:

  • Minimizing confusion about quality terms by establishing a consensus lexicon created by glass and pharmaceutical/biopharmaceutical manufacturers
  • Identifying nonconformity classifications by severity based on potential impact on pharmaceutical/biopharmaceutical container application
  • Creating a common nomenclature that can be used throughout the industry for the definition and classification of glass container attributes
  • Serving as an important supplement to existing guidance documents for the evaluation and classification of nonconforming glass containers
Commentary

The Three Layers of Value of TR43

  1. Language layer: establishes common terminology between pharma company and glass manufacturer, avoiding communication errors that lead to wrong lot decisions.
  2. Risk layer: the five-level classification framework links directly to patient safety risk, so every acceptance decision has a risk management basis (consistent with ICH Q9).
  3. System layer: TR43 is only one part of a complete acceptance system; it must be combined with dimensional standards, AQL procedures, reinspection processes and a training program to form a complete glass container quality management system.

TR43 ≠ a Complete Acceptance Standard

TR43 is a supplementary guide, not a complete acceptance standard. In practice you also need:

  • An internal company SOP for glass container acceptance
  • A quality agreement signed with the glass manufacturer
  • Customized AQL specifications based on product characteristics
  • Personnel training and a visual inspection certification program
  • A deviation/NCR investigation process

Hierarchy of Reference Standards

The standards cited by TR43 form a hierarchy:

Section Summary

  • Core value of TR43: gives glass manufacturers and pharma companies a unified "language for identifying nonconformities". It is an industry best practice guide (not a mandatory standard) applicable to incoming inspection and process control of empty glass containers.
  • Key points of the 2023 revision: RTU containers formally included, reduced reliance on limit samples, standardized terminology ("N/A" → "Acceptable Imperfection"), and harmonized risk levels for glass particles.
  • Core terms: AQL (the worst process average defect rate statistically allowed by sampling), lot definition (to be agreed by both parties), limit sample, RTU containers, attribute sampling (ASQ/ANSI Z1.4, ISO 2859-1), dPPM.
  • Specification development process: the glass manufacturer and pharma company jointly establish a quality agreement covering five elements: dimensional design (ISO standards), sampling plan, lot definition and AQL setting, nonconformity disposition, and documentation and training.
  • Five-level risk classification framework (core): Critical (None Allowed) → Major A (AQL≤0.65) → Major B (AQL≤1.0/2.5) → Minor (AQL≤4.0) → Acceptable Imperfection (no limit). Classification must follow the ICH Q9 risk management approach and have a documented rationale.
  • Understanding AQL correctly: AQL is a statistical tool, not an endorsement of defects; Critical defects use "None Allowed" (not AQL = 0%); AQL values must be customized to the drug product characteristics and process requirements and cannot be one size fits all.

Table 8.0-1🔍 pertains to moulded glass containers, and Tables 8.0-2–8.0-5 describe tubular glass containers encompassing vials, ampoules, cartridges, and syringes.

These lexicons depict the most common glass nonconformance attributes in a slide format. Each lexicon slide contains the name of nonconformance or attribute, definition, location(s), method(s) of identification (where applicable), related attribute(s), and classification(s) of the nonconformance attribute as well as a photographic image(s) and/or drawing(s).

Column Definitions

  • Nonconformance — The primary name of the attribute.
  • a.k.a. — "Also known as" attribute name(s).
  • Definition — Description of the attribute.
  • Classification — Critical, Major A, Major B, Minor, or Acceptable Imperfection.
  • Related — Similar-looking attributes with slightly different characteristics.
  • Location — Where on the container the defect occurs (Finish, Body, Bottom, General, Neck, Heel, Shoulder, etc.).
  • Method — Potential method(s) for determination of acceptability.
  • Risk/Impact — Risk directly tied to the classification.
  • Source — Manufacturing root cause(s).

Inspection Methods Referenced

  • Calibrated measuring device with magnification (eye loupe with graduations, microscope with measurement software)
  • TAPPI Chart method (Test Method T564) — estimation of defect area
  • Identification/compositional verification (SEM EDS, SEM EDX, FTIR, ICP)
  • Hydrolytic resistance methods (USP <660>, Ph. Eur. 3.2.1 A)
  • Tactile method — probing with non-metallic instrument for depth and adhesion
  • Container Closure Integrity (CCI) Assessment
  • Calibrated dimensional measuring devices (comparator, calipers, Go/No Go gauges, runout/offset gauges)
  • Removable method (80°C WFI flush, swipe with gloved hand, water/air flush)
  • Flat surface method (footprint stamp test)
  • Visual with limit sample method
  • Visual method (unaided eye + standard lighting; table states "NA")

Risk / Impact Categories

  • CCI / Sterility — Likely to cause harm; Critical classification
  • User injury or harm — Critical; e.g., microbiological contamination, crack
  • Remote risk to user — Major A; e.g., check with potential to propagate into crack
  • Functional — Major A; e.g., package unusable, broken
  • Machinability — Major B; yield reduction, line stoppage
  • Negligible risk — Minor; particle removed by washing
  • Aesthetic — Minor or Acceptable Imperfection; cosmetic only

Manufacturing Sources

  • Material (Tube/Tank) — Glass melted in furnace; airlines, seeds, knots, stones
  • Moulding — Forming glass gobs into vials/bottles; wire edge, sunken side, rocker
  • Annealing — Controlled heating to remove thermal stress; belt mark, stuck mark, lehr sticker
  • Handling — Relative movement causing glass-to-glass or glass-to-metal contact; bruise, check, chip, scratch
  • Secondary Processing — Post-formation (washing, sterilization, RTU/RTF, coating); treatment bloom, water spot

Commentary — Appendix Methodology

🏭 Moulded vs. Tubular Glass: Core Differences

Moulded glass is formed by pressing a gob of molten glass directly into a metal mould. Production is fast and suits large-volume containers (50 mL–1000 mL injection bottles).

Tubular glass is first drawn from molten glass into tubing and then converted by secondary processing into small-volume vials, ampoules, cartridges and syringes.

  • Moulded: more uniform wall thickness, higher opacity, suitable for water for injection (WFI) containers
  • Tubular: thin wall, high transparency, suitable for lyophilized product vials and prefilled syringes
  • TR43 Table 8.0-1 covers moulded containers; Tables 8.0-2 to 8.0-5 cover the tubular types

🥦 Analogy: Glass Inspection Is Like Auditing a Restaurant Kitchen

Imagine you are auditing the kitchen of a Michelin-starred restaurant. The nonconformance lexicon is your audit checklist, and every defect has the following:

  • Name (Nonconformance): for example, "wood splinters from the cutting board"
  • Definition: makes sure auditors share the same understanding of the same phenomenon
  • Classification: food that directly poisons the diner = Critical; slows down service = Major B; merely looks unattractive = Minor
  • Location: a problem on a "food contact surface" (such as the Finish) is far more serious than one on the kitchen floor (the exterior of the Body)
  • Method: visual inspection, magnifier, chemical analysis; these are the auditor's different inspection tools

PDA TR43 is the lexicon that gives glass manufacturers and pharmaceutical companies worldwide a "common language", ensuring that suppliers and customers apply the same criteria to every defect.

⚠️ Key Point: Why Is the "Finish" Area So Critical?

The Finish is the container neck opening and sealing surface (land surface), the area the rubber stopper contacts. Any Finish defect that affects the seal endangers:

  • Container closure integrity (CCI)
  • The sterile barrier (sterility assurance)
  • Patient safety

This is why Crack, Split Finish, Chipped Finish, Line Over Finish, Soft Blister (in the Finish area) and similar defects are all classified as Critical.

📊 What AQL 0.65 Means in Practice

Major A defects usually correspond to AQL 0.65 (per ISO 2859-1 / ANSI/ASQ Z1.4).

Take a lot size of 10,000 vials as an example (General Inspection Level II):

  • Sample size n = 200 units
  • Acceptance number Ac = 1 unit (Major A defects)
  • Rejection number Re = 2 units

In other words, if 2 or more Major A defects are found in the sample, the whole lot must be rejected. AQL 0.65 allows a defect rate of only 0.65%, which is very strict and reflects the potential risk Major A defects pose to patients.

Commentary — Nonconformity Classification Logic

📈 Classification Decision Tree: How Is Severity Determined?

The TR43 classification logic follows a "strictest first" order of judgment:

  1. Does it affect CCI / sterility? → Critical
  2. Does it directly injure the user (patient / healthcare worker)? → Critical
  3. Is there a remote risk (such as crack propagation or particle shedding)? → Major A
  4. Is functionality lost (the container cannot be used)? → Major A
  5. Is it out of dimensional specification and does it reduce line efficiency? → Major B
  6. Are the particles removable by washing? → Minor (Negligible)
  7. Is it only an appearance issue? → Minor or Acceptable Imperfection

  • Crack: full-wall-penetrating crack (Always Critical)
  • Inside Overpress / Wire Edge: protrusion or unevenness ≥0.3 mm
  • Knot: if located on the sealing surface and endangering CCI
  • Line Over Finish: crosses 100% of the land surface
  • Malformed Finish: if seal integrity is compromised

  • Split Finish: Always Critical (vertical split through the Finish)
  • Stone: if located on the sealing surface and endangering CCI

Common theme: most Critical defects are directly related to the Finish (sealing area) or are full-wall-penetrating cracks in the glass wall.

🔧 Major B Defects: Dimensions and Machinability

Major B defects are usually dimensional problems that reduce the efficiency of downstream processes (filling, capping, labeling) but do not directly endanger the patient:

  • Bent Neck, Leaner, Offset Finish: affect the capping machine
  • Bulged Side, Sunken Side: affect the labeling machine and conveyor
  • Choked Neck: affects entry of the filling needle
  • Flared Bottom: causes vials to jam on the conveyor
  • Loose Glass (RTU, Exterior): may enter the filling environment
  • Rolled In Finish, Inside Overpress: hinder stopper insertion

📷 RTU vs. Bulk (Unwashed): Why Is the Classification Different?

The same defect (such as Loose Glass) is Minor in a Bulk (unwashed) container (washing removes it), but is raised to Major A in an RTU (Ready-to-Use, washed and sterilized) container (no downstream washing step protects against it).

This reflects the core philosophy of PDA TR43: risk classification must take into account the state of the container in the supply chain, not only the form of the defect itself.

RTU containers go directly onto the aseptic filling line, with no later process step able to remove any particle or contamination, so the risk level is raised.

🔍 Where the Four Main Measurement Methods Apply

  • Visual + magnification (eye loupe): most dimensional-type defects (Check, Scratch, Embedded Matter)
  • CCI assessment: all Finish-area defects (Crack, Line Over Finish, Soft Blister)
  • Chemical analysis (SEM EDS, FTIR): identifying the composition of contaminants (Contamination, Swabbing Stain, Water Spot)
  • Flat surface method: stability testing (Rocker, Leaner, Wavy Bottom)

When a defect cannot be judged by size, a "limit sample" is usually used: a physical boundary sample jointly agreed by the pharmaceutical company and the glass manufacturer.

🌟 Why Is the Moulded Glass Nonconformity Lexicon So Important?

The PDA TR43 lexicon is not just a defect classification tool; it is the common language between supplier, pharmaceutical company and regulator. Without a standardized lexicon, it is like three parties discussing quality criteria in different languages, with no way to reach agreement.

When an FDA inspector, EMA assessor or customer auditor visits the plant, being able to cite the specific defect names and classifications from TR43 is an important indicator of GMP maturity.

📚 The Role of the Limit Sample

For many Minor and Acceptable Imperfection defects, TR43 requires a limit sample to be used for judgment, because:

  • The boundaries of such defects are hard to describe precisely with numbers (such as the depth of Orange Peel)
  • Visual judgment varies from person to person, so a physical boundary sample is needed to unify the standard
  • A limit sample is a contractual agreement between the pharmaceutical company and the glass manufacturer and has legal force

📚 Educational Commentary

▶ What Is a Tubular Ampoule? How Does It Differ from a Glass Vial?

A tubular ampoule is a single-use, single-dose, flame-sealed container formed directly from glass tubing (glass tubing cane). Its characteristics are:

  • No rubber stopper and no aluminum cap: the seal relies entirely on the flame fusion of the glass itself (tip seal or pull seal), and does not need the rubber/metal combination on which container closure integrity (CCI) usually depends.
  • Single dose: once opened it cannot be resealed, and the whole content must be used at once or discarded.
  • Opening by breaking: the user must snap the ampoule neck (neck / constriction) to withdraw the drug, and this action itself generates glass particles.
  • Narrow neck design: the stem and constriction of an ampoule are the most fragile parts and the most defect-prone, and are the area to which the most defect definitions point.

By contrast, a moulded vial is formed by pressing molten glass in a mould, is closed with a rubber stopper and aluminum cap, and can be pierced repeatedly (although this is generally discouraged). The production processes and defect risks of the two are quite different.

Note on the TR43 structure: Table 8.0-1🔍 covers moulded glass containers; Table 8.0-2🔍 (this section) covers tubular ampoules; the following tables cover cartridges, prefilled syringes and vials. Each container type has its own separate nonconformity lexicon in TR43 because the risk mechanisms differ.

💡 Analogy: Opening an Ampoule Is Like Snapping a Piece of Chalk

Imagine holding a piece of whiteboard chalk and bending it downward with both hands. At the moment it snaps, fine powder flies off near the break. Opening an ampoule is similar, except that what flies off is glass particles rather than dust.

This is why the constriction and the color-break band of an ampoule must be so precise:

  • The color-break band or score guides the glass to break cleanly at the correct position, minimizing particles entering the solution.
  • If the color-break band is too high or too low (High/Low Color-Break Band, defect #25), the force and direction of the break change and the glass fractures more irregularly.
  • If the color-break band is incomplete (Incomplete Color-Break Band, defect #26) or the score is missing (Incomplete or Missing Score, defect #28), the user may apply excessive force, producing more particles and even cutting their fingers.

These three defects are therefore classified as Critical (when the color band lies entirely outside the constriction zone, or the score gap is ≥ 1 mm), corresponding directly to the "User Injury or Harm" risk.

⚠ Ampoule-Specific Critical Defects: Why Are They So Serious?

Of the 53 nonconformities in this table, the following can reach a Critical classification:

  • Adhered Glass (#1): if seal integrity is compromised, adhered glass fragments may break the hermetic seal of the ampoule, causing loss of sterility (CCI/Sterility).
  • Alkali or Glass Powder Spots (#3): if non-removable alkaline glass components are present on the inner surface and the hydrolytic resistance test of USP <660> is not passed, the glass surface has deteriorated and may interact chemically with the drug product, affecting efficacy or safety.
  • Contamination (#16): non-removable foreign matter on the inner surface (≥ 0.1 mm²) directly constitutes a User Injury risk.
  • Crack (#17): a full-wall-penetrating crack means a complete loss of CCI, with sterility and contamination risks together.
  • Fragile Head (#22, Closed Ampoule): if broken, glass fragments directly contaminate the sealed solution.
  • Haze (#24): like Alkali Spots, it is Critical when it is non-removable internal haze and the hydrolytic resistance test is not passed.
  • High/Low Color-Break Band (#25): when the color band lies entirely outside the constriction zone, the break position cannot be controlled when opening the ampoule, directly injuring the user or contaminating the product.
  • Incomplete Color-Break Band (#26) and Incomplete or Missing Score (#28): a gap ≥ 1 mm causes failure of the guided break, with the same Critical mechanism as above.

Summary of classification principles: the Critical defects of ampoules are concentrated in three risk domains: (1) loss of seal integrity (CCI/Sterility), (2) chemical contamination of the drug product (glass leaching), (3) direct injury to the user at opening (User Injury from Breakage / Particles).

🔧 Special Considerations for RTU Ampoules (Ready-to-Use)

RTU ampoules are ampoules delivered to the pharmaceutical company after the glass manufacturer has completed washing, depyrogenation and sterilization, so the pharmaceutical company can skip the washing step and fill the solution directly.

This has the following implications for defect classification:

  • Loose Glass (#32): for a standard ampoule, Loose Glass is Minor because "downstream washing can remove it" (Negligible Risk due to Downstream Processing). For an RTU ampoule this mitigation no longer exists, and the risk level of Loose Glass must be reassessed; it may be upgraded to Major A or Critical (if it remains inside).
  • Contamination (#16): removable foreign matter is Minor or Acceptable in the standard configuration, but in the RTU situation no internal contamination will be removed by later washing, so it should be assessed against a stricter standard.
  • Alkali or Glass Powder Spots (#3) and Haze (#24): the high-temperature washing of RTU ampoules (80 °C WFI) is performed by the manufacturer. If non-removable surface deterioration is still present on delivery, it indicates that washing at the manufacturer failed, and the risk is higher.

TR43 principle: when assessing defect classification, always consider the intended use of the final container and the downstream processing steps. RTU changes the risk mitigation matrix, so that some defects that would otherwise be Minor carry a higher actual risk in the RTU situation.

Teaching Notes — Tubular Cartridge Overview

What is a tubular cartridge?

A cartridge is a slender glass container used inside a pen injector. Common applications include:

  • Insulin (diabetes management)
  • GLP-1 receptor agonists (such as semaglutide and liraglutide, for diabetes and weight management)
  • Human growth hormone (pediatric and adult GHD)
  • Dual-chamber cartridges (lyophilized powder + diluent, mixed before injection)

The cartridge is a "two-component system": the glass barrel holds the drug solution, and a rubber plunger stopper is inserted from the cut end. The pen device drives a plunger rod that pushes the stopper forward and dispenses the drug precisely.

Analogy: cartridge vs syringe vs vial

Think of the three containers as three kinds of pens:

  • Vial = ink bottle — used for multiple draws; each draw needs a new needle and a fresh dose measurement
  • Prefilled syringe = ballpoint pen — pre-loaded, press to dispense, disposable
  • Tubular cartridge = fountain pen (with a replaceable ink cartridge) — loaded into a pen device; the device body is reused and only the cartridge is replaced

This analogy captures the core design philosophy of the cartridge: a reusable device plus a replaceable drug container, combining convenience with dose accuracy.

Key Teaching Points (Commentary)

A key cartridge dimension: bore diameter

The bore diameter (the inner diameter) is the critical dimension affecting dose accuracy. The pen injector uses a drive rod to push the stopper forward, and each fixed advance distance corresponds to a fixed dose.

If the bore is too large or too small (out of specification), the volume dispensed will be off even when the advance distance is correct. This is especially critical for insulin pens, where an error of 1 unit can affect glycemic control.

Related defect: Pulled ID (ovalization of the inner diameter) directly affects closure and stopper sealing, and is classified as Major A.

RTU cartridges: stricter criteria for ready-to-use containers

RTU (Ready-to-Use) cartridges have already been through:

  • Washing
  • Siliconization — a thin layer of silicone oil is applied to the inner wall to reduce stopper glide resistance
  • Sterilization
  • Sealed primary packaging

After receiving RTU cartridges, the pharmaceutical manufacturer proceeds directly to aseptic filling with no washing step. This means any contaminant inside the container goes straight into the final product, which is why the RTU classification thresholds are stricter.

For example, a removable contaminant in a Bulk container (Minor, ≥0.5 mm²) is upgraded directly to Critical in an RTU container, because there is no washing step left to remove it.

Analogy: the function of the bypass area

The "bypass area" of a dual-chamber cartridge is like a "waist narrowing" in the syringe barrel.

Before use, the lyophilized powder sits in the front chamber and the diluent in the rear chamber, separated by two stoppers (front and rear). When the patient turns the pen body to start mixing, the rear stopper advances; once it reaches the bypass area, the diluent can flow "around" the front stopper into the front chamber and mix with the powder.

If the bypass area is deformed (Bypass Area Malformed), the diluent cannot pass through smoothly and mixing fails. The patient may then inject undissolved powder, which is a Major A functional failure.

In-Depth Analysis — Cartridge-Specific Defects Compared with Other Containers

The importance of the glazed end (cut end)

The "glazed end" of a cartridge is the end of the glass tube that has been flame-polished (glazed) after cutting; the rubber stopper is inserted from this end.

If the cut end is uneven (Uneven Cut), not fully glazed (Unglazed) or has too much glass distribution (Excess Glaze), the consequences are:

  • Difficult stopper insertion, or damage to the stopper sealing surface
  • A sharp edge at the cut end that can cut the rubber stopper
  • Failure to fit the gauge of automated filling equipment
  • Non-compliance with the mechanical tolerances of the pen device

Severity of the three cut-end defects: Unglazed = Major A (functional failure); Uneven Cut & Excess Glaze = Major B (dimensions out of specification).

The "ring-off" risk of Neck Score

Neck Score is a series of frictive checks produced at the junction of the neck and shoulder. This area bears the mechanical torque of the pen device.

"Ring-off" means the neck breaks and separates along the line of damage, resulting in:

  • Glass fragments falling into the drug solution
  • Loss of container integrity
  • Device failure during injection by the patient

For this reason Neck Score is classified directly as Major A, with no size threshold and no downgrade possible.

The role of glass tubular syringes in pharmaceuticals

A tubular syringe is a syringe made from glass tubing and is the most common container format for the prefilled syringe (PFS). Compared with moulded glass, tubular syringes have more uniform wall thickness and a cleaner inner surface, making them particularly suitable for low-dose, high-potency biologics and small-molecule injectables.

Key anatomical regions of a syringe include:

  • Cone (luer): the interface for needle attachment or the needle cap, which directly affects CCI and sterility. Any cone defect (such as Chip, Cone Incomplete or Cone Clogged) can trigger a Critical classification.
  • Finger grip (flange): used for positioning in the autoinjector and for handling by the user. Dimensional deviations of the flange (Malformed, Uneven Cut, Not Perpendicular) affect device compatibility and function.
  • Barrel: holds the drug solution and provides the travel path for the plunger. Any barrel deformation (Bypass Area Malformed) can affect plunger glide force and dose accuracy.
  • Needle (staked needle type): some syringes are designed with a pre-staked needle. Needle quality directly affects patient safety and includes Needle Inverted, Needle Loose and Needle Deformed Point.

Analogy: the PFS is both a container and a delivery device

A traditional glass vial is only a container for the drug, which must be transferred into a syringe before use. The prefilled syringe (PFS), however, serves as both the primary packaging container and the drug delivery device.

It is like this: the vial is a "warehouse" where the drug is stored, while the PFS is "courier packaging plus the delivery vehicle", with the drug ready inside the package and deliverable directly to the patient.

It also means that any PFS defect, especially in the cone and needle areas, affects not only drug quality but also patient safety directly (User Injury or Harm). For this reason TR43 establishes several syringe-specific nonconformities (such as Needle Bent and Needle Inverted) that do not exist in the vial lexicon.

Key point: cone defects are the core of the Critical risk

The syringe cone (luer) is the most critical interface for container closure integrity (CCI) of the whole container, because it simultaneously carries:

  • The seal of the needle shield or luer cap, which prevents external contaminants from entering
  • The seal of the cone bore for the staked needle, which prevents leakage of the drug solution or loosening of the needle
  • Flow path integrity during administration, which ensures accurate dose delivery

For this reason the following cone nonconformities all have Critical classification triggers in TR43: Chip, Cone Incomplete, Adhered Glass, Crack, Airline (Open, on ID), Stone/Knot (if sealing is affected), Needle Loose, Needle Deformed Point (> 60 µm), Needle Inverted, Contamination RTU (interior ≥ 0.1 mm²).

The management approach for these defects is highly consistent with the CCI evaluation framework of PDA TR73 (Prefilled Syringe, Sections 12–18). Cross-referencing with TR73 Section 14 (CCI Methods) is recommended.

The dual impact of siliconization uniformity on drug product quality

Syringes are usually siliconized before shipment, with silicone oil applied evenly to the inner barrel wall so that the plunger has an appropriate glide force (glide force / break-loose force) when pushed.

Non-uniform siliconization (uneven coating) can cause two very different kinds of problems:

  • Functional problems: too little silicone leads to excessive plunger resistance, so the autoinjector may stall or under-deliver the dose; too much silicone leads to too little plunger resistance, which can cause unintended leakage.
  • Drug product stability problems: silicone oil droplets can enter the drug solution and induce protein aggregation or particle formation, with a particularly marked effect on biologics.

In the TR43 contamination lexicon, Contamination (Bulk and RTU) explicitly includes "silicone" as one of the contaminant sources. Defects related to the siliconization process (such as adhered silicone oil aggregates) should be classified in RTU syringes according to their CCI and functional impact.

Practical exercise: syringe inspection scenarios

Scenario 1: Chip at the cone

The inspector finds a 1.2 mm² chip on the cone of an RTU syringe. The needle cap can still be fitted but feels loose. How should it be classified?

  • Perform a CCI assessment first. If seal integrity is compromised: Critical
  • If seal integrity can still be maintained and the chip is ≥ 1 mm²: Major A
  • The "loose feel" itself signals a CCI risk and should be treated as Critical first, until CCI testing rules it out.

Scenario 2: Loose glass particles inside an RTU barrel

During 100% automated visual inspection (AVI) of an RTU syringe batch, visible glass fragments (confirmed as loose glass) are found inside the barrel.

  • RTU containers have already been washed, and the glass fragments are inside and not removable: Major A (interior)
  • If the fragments are confirmed to be external and removable: re-evaluate whether this is truly loose glass in the RTU state.
  • This type of defect is directly related to visible particulate inspection (VPI); refer to PDA TR85.

Scenario 3: Needle deflected by 3°

The needle of a staked-needle PFS shows a 3° deflection on the rotating template check (exceeding the 2° specification).

  • Needle Bent — > 2°：Major A
  • This defect can affect the alignment function of the autoinjector device and may cause unintended injury to the patient during needle insertion. The root cause should be investigated for the whole batch (secondary processing needle assembly vs handling damage).

Scenario 4: Bore Out of Round at the luer

Measurement of the luer bore shows an oval shape, but it is still within most standards. What is the effect on the automated filling line?

  • If the dimension exceeds the permitted luer tip range: Major B (affects machinability and the design tolerance of the filling machine)
  • If the dimension is within the permitted range: Acceptable Imperfection
  • The key is the calibration traceability of the measuring instrument: confirm with a calibrated micrometer or caliper.

Core Concept 1: What is a tubular vial?

The tubular vial is the most widely used glass primary packaging container in the pharmaceutical industry, commonly used for injectables, lyophilizates and other sterile drug products.

Manufacturing process (converting process):

  • Raw material: borosilicate glass tubing / cane, conforming to ISO 8536-3 / USP Type I specifications
  • Cutting: the glass tubing is cut to a specified length, and the cut end becomes the future vial bottom
  • Neck/finish forming: the top of the glass tubing is heated with flames and tooling to form the finish (the sealing area of the mouth) and the ring (neck ring)
  • Bottom forming: at the same time, the cut end is closed with flames to form the bottom
  • Annealing (lehr): the vials pass through an annealing lehr to relieve residual stress and prevent breakage defects such as Ring Off

Each process step can introduce specific defects, which is the core information in the Source column of TR43 Table 8.0-5🔍.

The following defects can all be upgraded to Critical in the finish area:

  • Chip: ≥ 50% of the sealing surface → Critical
  • Crack (through the wall): always Critical
  • Malformed Finish (severe deformation of the finish): CCI compromised → Critical
  • Unfilled Finish (incomplete finish contour): CCI compromised → Critical
  • Wavy Top (uneven sealing surface): CCI compromised → Critical
  • Knot / Stone (glass inclusions): located on the sealing surface → Critical

These defects directly affect stopper seating and drug product seal integrity. Once the seal fails, patient safety for sterile drug products is at risk.

⚠️ The core difference for RTU vials: why are the criteria stricter?

TR43 sets up separate nonconformity entries for RTU containers, for the following reasons:

  • Conventional containers: before filling at the pharmaceutical plant, they must go through washing and depyrogenation steps. Even if a small amount of loose glass particles or removable contamination is present on receipt, the downstream process can remove it.
  • RTU containers: the glass manufacturer has already completed washing, siliconization and sterilization, and ships them aseptically (usually in special trays or nests). The pharmaceutical plant, on receipt, performs no further washing.
  • The requirement for interior surface cleanliness of RTU containers is therefore equivalent to the cleanliness of a container that has already been washed with DI water / WFI. The risk of any residue to the drug product is no longer mitigated by a downstream washing step.

TR43 Table 8.0-5 explicitly establishes separate entries for Bulk/Unwashed and RTU to reflect the substantive difference in process flow.

💡 Analogy: an RTU vial = a hotel room that has been cleaned in advance

Imagine you book a high-end hotel room that is advertised as "already cleaned and disinfected":

  • When you enter, you find a hair on the floor and a drink stain on the table. The hotel says "it was already clean", but you cannot, and should not need to, clean it again.
  • This is the logic of RTU vials: the glass manufacturer is the hotel, which has made the promise of cleanliness.
  • The pharmaceutical plant is the guest. It trusts that the room is clean and "checks in" directly (fills the drug solution). If the room itself is not clean, the consequences fall directly on the end user (the patient).
  • This is why contamination and loose glass defects in RTU containers are upgraded from "acceptable (because it will be washed later)" to Major A or Critical.

📚 Educational Commentary — RTU-Specific Defects in Detail

RTU container life cycle: from glass tubing to sterile delivery

To understand why the RTU criteria are stricter, you must first understand its process flow:

  1. Glass tubing manufacture: molten glass is drawn into tubing (tubing cane)
  2. Forming/conversion: the tubing is cut and heat-formed into vials (Converting)
  3. Annealing: residual stress is relieved in the lehr (Annealing)
  4. Washing: ultrasonic washing with WFI (Washing — an RTU-specific step)
  5. Siliconization: silicone oil is applied to the inner wall (Siliconization — some RTU)
  6. Depyrogenation: high-temperature depyrogenation oven (Depyrogenation — 250°C+)
  7. Packaging: under ISO 5 laminar flow, the vials are placed in trays/nests and sealed
  8. Shipment to the pharmaceutical plant: goes directly onto the filling line (no washing step)

Steps 4–7 are performed by the glass manufacturer. The pharmaceutical plant performs step 8 → direct filling.

⚠️ Classification difference between Loose Glass RTU and Bulk

Loose Glass (Bulk): classified as Minor (removable)

Rationale: the downstream washing step can remove it, so the risk is negligible.

Loose Glass (RTU): classified as

  • Interior → Major A
  • Exterior → Major B

Rationale: RTU containers have already been washed, so any remaining loose glass particles indicate a failure of the washing process, and the pharmaceutical plant has no opportunity to remove them.

This dual-entry design is the most direct expression in TR43 of the concept of "process risk transfer".

💡 Haze vs Alkali Spots vs Treatment Bloom: how to tell them apart?

Three defects with a similar appearance have very different classifications:

  • Treatment Bloom: Acceptable — this is a normal by-product of dealkalization treatment (ammonium sulfate / dealkalization). It is a "good haze", indicating that the container surface has been modified to reduce chemical reactivity.
  • Alkali Spots: if not removable and failing USP<660> → Critical — glass constituents evaporate during forming and condense on the surface, indicating insufficient chemical stability of the glass.
  • Haze: if not removable and failing USP<660> → Critical — a larger-area deposit of volatilized glass constituents, likewise indicating a hydrolytic resistance problem.

Key to the judgement: perform the removability test first (rinse with 80°C WFI), then the USP<660> hydrolytic resistance test.

Broken Ringoff: a failure mode specific to tubular vials

Broken Ringoff is a defect specific to tubular vials, in which a horizontal circumferential crack appears near the shoulder or bottom and may eventually split the vial into two pieces.

Root cause: residual thermal stress formed during forming below the shoulder or above the heel. If annealing is insufficient, the stress cannot be relieved, creating a "time bomb".

Polarized light inspection: TR43 specifically notes that a polarimeter / strain viewer can be used on unbroken containers to visually confirm the residual thermal stress pattern, a unique non-destructive, preventive quality control tool.

📚 Integrating the Overall Knowledge of TR43

The core design philosophy of TR43: risk-based classification

TR43 is not just a dictionary of defect names but a risk-based classification framework. The severity classification of each defect (Critical / Major A / Major B / Minor / Acceptable) is based on the following five dimensions:

  1. CCI/Sterility (container closure integrity): any defect that breaks the seal → Critical
  2. User Injury or Harm: defects that cause patient injury → Critical or Major A
  3. Functional: renders the container/system unusable → Major A
  4. Machinability: causes losses on the production line → Major B
  5. Aesthetic: does not affect function → Minor or Acceptable

This framework is fully consistent with the ICH Q9 risk management approach and is the core reason TR43 has become an industry standard.

⚠️ Quick reference: unique defect concerns by container type

  • Moulded vials → focus on mould seams and wall thickness uniformity
  • Ampoules → focus on opening force / score ring and flame-seal quality
  • Cartridge tubes → focus on the stopper contact surface (By-pass) and finish-end sealing
  • Prefilled syringes → focus on luer cone integrity and needle assembly quality
  • Tubular vials → focus on RTU cleanliness, ring-off breakage and bottom forming (lyophilization)

Common exam question type: given a defect description, determine in which container type the severity is highest. The answer is often the container that includes RTU processing steps, or the container for which the sealing area is most critical.

📚 Practical Application: CDMO Incoming Inspection Strategy

How to apply TR43 in a CDMO QC plan

Step 1 — Establish Acceptance Criteria

  • Together with the glass supplier, set acceptance criteria for each defect entry based on the TR43 lexicon (whether permitted, and the permitted quantity)
  • Critical defects: permitted quantity = 0 (zero-defect policy)
  • Major A: typically AQL = 0.65% (per ISO 2859-1)
  • Major B: typically AQL = 1.0%
  • Minor: typically AQL = 4.0% (or per contractual agreement)
  • Acceptable Imperfection: no counting required

Step 2 — Separate RTU and Bulk acceptance plans

  • RTU containers need a separate SOP and acceptance criteria; the Bulk criteria cannot be applied directly
  • Key differing entries: Contamination (RTU) and Loose Glass (RTU); the RTU versions must be explicitly listed in the acceptance criteria
  • RTU acceptance is usually performed as skip-lot testing on the basis of the Certificate of Analysis (CoA) that the supplier has already executed

Step 3 — Confirm measurement methods

  • TR43 specifies a measurement method for each defect (visual, magnifier, caliper, gauge, CCI test, etc.)
  • The acceptance SOP should cite the TR43 measurement methods to ensure consistent QC personnel training
  • Establishing limit samples: many Minor entries in TR43 require physical limit samples to be established

Common CDMO scenario decision tree

Scenario: a batch of tubular vials is received and visual inspection finds an anomaly.

  1. Identify the defect → confirm the name against the TR43 Section 8.5 lexicon
  2. Confirm whether it is Bulk or RTU → check the supplier CoA and container specification
  3. Confirm by measurement → measure dimensions and confirm removability using the method specified in TR43
  4. Confirm the classification level (Critical/Major A/B/Minor/Acceptable)
  5. Compare the AQL sampling result → does it exceed the acceptance number
  6. Decide:
    — Critical finding → immediate quarantine, notify the supplier, reject
    — Major exceeds limit → nonconformance investigation, possible rejection
    — Minor exceeds limit → assess risk, possible conditional release or rejection
    — Acceptable → record and release
  7. If necessary, initiate a CAPA and require a root cause analysis from the supplier

The role of USP <660> / Ph. Eur. 3.2.1A in TR43

TR43 is one of the few guidelines that directly cites a chemical test standard in a visual defect classification.

For Alkali Spots and Haze, TR43 designs a three-tier decision logic:

  1. Removable? → if yes: Acceptable (or Minor); if no, go to the next tier
  2. Are the chemical constituents glass constituents only? → if it contains non-glass foreign material: reclassify as Contamination (higher severity)
  3. Does it pass the USP <660> hydrolytic resistance test? → if it passes: Minor; if it fails: Critical

This design reflects the core TR43 philosophy that "cosmetic defects must be combined with a functional risk assessment".

TR43 Complete Learning Summary — Consolidated Summary of the Nonconformity Lexicons for the Five Container Types

Review of core knowledge points

  • TR43 (Revised 2023) establishes a unified nonconformity lexicon for five glass primary packaging container types, covering definitions, classification, measurement methods, related defects and risk impact.
  • Classification level structure: Critical → Major A → Major B → Minor → Acceptable Imperfection, reflecting the risk hierarchy of CCI/user safety → function → machinability → appearance.
  • The dual-version entry design for RTU (Ready-to-Use) containers (Bulk vs RTU) reflects the fundamental influence of "downstream process risk mitigation capability" on the severity assessment of defects.
  • Crack is Critical in all five container types, the most consistent cross-container rule.
  • The USP <660>/Ph. Eur. 3.2.1A hydrolytic resistance test is integrated directly into the classification logic for Haze and Alkali Spots.

Summary of defects specific to tubular vials

  • Broken Ringoff: circumferential break, specific to tubular vials, arising from thermal stress during conversion and detectable preventively with polarized light.
  • Concave Bottom (Push Up): excessive inward bulging of the bottom, affecting the lyophilization process and filling needles.
  • Treatment Bloom: a normal by-product of dealkalization treatment and the only haze-like cosmetic defect defined as an Acceptable Imperfection.
  • Contamination RTU vs Bulk: the most important dual-version entry; RTU has no downstream washing mitigation, so the severity is raised.
  • Alkali Spots / Haze: three-step judgement (removability → composition identification → USP<660> test); the determination of Critical vs Minor depends on chemical analysis.
