1.0 Introduction

Original Text

Visual inspection of filled injectable products is an important part of the control of the manufacturing process. It offers a means to improve quality by removing noncompliant units and provides information to drive continuous process improvement.

Visual Inspection (VI) may be accomplished in several ways: manually (MVI) with the unaided eye under controlled conditions of lighting and time; semi-automated visual inspection (SAVI), which combines 1) automated handling of the containers to be inspected with human vision, and 2) a means to determine whether an individual unit should be accepted or rejected; and thirdly, automated visual inspection (AVI), which offers a validatable method using digital cameras to acquire images of the product and computational tools to analyze those images against preset criteria to determine if the unit is acceptable. While MVI is the reference method described in all the pharmacopoeias, all three categories are acceptable with appropriate qualification or validation.

For MVI, the visible range for particles in many filled injectable products is generally agreed to begin at 100 µm based on studies in clear glass vials (1). However, not all particles of this size can be reliably detected. Nominal probability of detection (PoD) for 100 µm particles is often 40-50% with PoD increasing with increasing particle size. Single particles greater than 200 µm in clear solution and in 10 mL clear vials typically achieve a PoD >95% (2).

To reliably meet visible particle acceptance criterion for filled products, careful control of the components used in the process is required. Criteria must generally be tighter than that of the finished product to compensate for the cumulative effects of multiple components (e.g., container and elastomeric seal), potential sources from the formulation and filling equipment, and the manufacturing environment. Today, most components are not subjected to tighter requirements as part of their incoming inspection and acceptance for use. However, specifications that meet stricter requirements are most critical for ready-to-sterilize (RTS) and ready-to-use (RTU) components since they do not undergo further particle removal steps.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Visual Inspection (VI): An indispensable quality control step in finished injectable manufacturing. It is not only about "picking out bad units"; it is also a source of information for continual process improvement.

Three inspection approaches:

  • MVI (manual visual inspection): Inspection with the unaided eye under controlled lighting and time conditions; the reference method in pharmacopeias worldwide
  • SAVI (semi-automated visual inspection): Automated container handling + human eye judgment + accept/reject decision
  • AVI (automated visual inspection): Uses digital cameras to capture images and computational tools to analyze whether they meet predefined criteria; can be validated

Probability of Detection (PoD): The likelihood that a specific particle will be seen and removed under a given inspection method and conditions. The PoD for a 100 µm particle is only about 40-50%, whereas for a 200 µm particle it can reach >95%.

Analogy

Imagine checking every dish for foreign matter before it leaves the restaurant kitchen. MVI is like the head chef looking at each plate with the naked eye; SAVI is like a conveyor belt that automatically brings each dish in front of the chef, who still judges by eye; AVI is like installing high-resolution cameras and an AI recognition system that detects abnormalities automatically.

The key point: no matter how sharp the chef's eyesight, a 100 µm foreign particle (finer than a human hair) has only about a 50% chance of being spotted. This is why we cannot rely on final inspection alone, and must control the cleanliness of raw materials and components at the source.

Key Notes

Why are RTS/RTU components especially important?

Ready-to-Sterilize (RTS) and Ready-to-Use (RTU) components do not go through any additional washing or particle removal step before use. This means the particulate level at the time of supplier delivery is the final level.

For a CDMO, this means:

  • Incoming inspection specifications must be tighter than finished product specifications
  • The Quality Agreement with the component supplier must explicitly include particulate control requirements
  • Cumulative effect: container + stopper + filling equipment + environment; the particles from every source add up

Formula

Practical implications of detection probability:

100 µm particle → PoD ≈ 40-50%
  → Of every 100 units containing such a particle, about 50-60 "slip through"

200 µm particle → PoD > 95%
  → Of every 100 units containing such a particle, only about 5 may go undetected

Takeaway: final inspection alone cannot achieve zero defects
      Particles must be controlled at the source, in components and processes

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Your client requires a visible particle rejection rate of no more than 0.1% for the finished injectable. If you use RTU stoppers and RTU glass vials, each of which can contribute particles, and then add particle sources from filling equipment and the environment, the specification at every step must be far tighter than the 0.1% finished-product target. This is the practical, commercial meaning of the "cumulative effect".

1.2 Scope

Original Text

The use of particle, particulate, and particulate matter all have the same meaning when used within this report. They are used in various global standards and compendia with equivalent meaning. In general, this term applies to foreign material which is unintended and either removable from the surface of a primary packaging component or freely moving within a filled and sealed pharmaceutical dosage form.

The test and inspection methods discussed within this report are the first step in a standard setting process. Once test and inspection methods are available, accepted, and implemented, they can be used to assess process capability and establish meaningful process control limits. Without reliable measurements, reliable control limits are not possible.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Particle / Particulate / Particulate Matter: The three terms are used interchangeably in this report. They refer to unintended foreign matter that may adhere to the surface of primary packaging or move freely within the sealed drug product dosage form.

Standard Setting Process: The methods in TR85 are the first step in this process. The logic chain is:

  • Step 1: Establish reliable test methods (the core of this report)
  • Step 2: Use these methods to evaluate process capability
  • Step 3: Based on process capability data, establish meaningful control limits

Analogy

It is like controlling indoor air quality in a factory. Saying "the air must be clean" does not set a standard. You first need a reliable air quality monitor (corresponding to the TR85 test methods), use it to measure current conditions (corresponding to process capability evaluation), and only then can you set a reasonable PM2.5 limit (corresponding to control limits). Without good measurement, every limit is empty talk.

Key Notes

「Without reliable measurements, reliable control limits are not possible.」

This statement is the core philosophy of the whole report. In the pharmaceutical industry, we often see specification limits based on tradition or experience rather than data. What TR85 promotes is data-driven quality control: method first, then data, and finally let the data speak.

This is also consistent with the spirit of ICH Q10: continual quality improvement must be built on objective measurement.

2.0 Glossary and Abbreviations

Original Text

Definitions have been provided to help clarify the concepts discussed in this technical report. While some of these definitions vary among companies, the definitions described below are consistent for use within this document.

Glossary Terms

Area
Measured surface area of a flat projection of a particle.

Aspect Ratio
The length of a particle or fiber divided by its width.

Extrinsic Particle or Fiber
Foreign material that comes from outside the primary process. Often these are from the manufacturing environment or incomplete cleaning of components. They are uncontrolled.

Feret Min and Max
Feret Min is the minimum distance between parallel tangents at opposing particle borders. Feret Max is the maximum distance between parallel tangents at opposing particle borders.

Intrinsic Particle
A particle that comes from within the primary process. These are qualified product contact materials and are often associated with the primary packaging components. They are unplanned but not unexpected.

Length
The measure of the distance along its longest axis.

Light obscuration (LO)
Analytical technique measuring the size and quantity of subvisible particles.

Membrane/microscopy (MM)
Analytical technique to collect particles from a liquid sample on a membrane filter followed by manual examination (sizing and counting) with a microscope.

Particle
Foreign material which is unintended and either removable from the surface of a primary packaging component or freely moving within a filled and sealed pharmaceutical dosage form. The term particle is used interchangeably with "particulate" and "particulate matter" without any intended difference in meaning.

Particulate
See Particle.

Perimeter
Measured length of the outer edges of a particle.

Probability of detection (PoD)
The likelihood that a given particle will be seen and removed with a specific inspection method and under specific conditions. Usually expressed as a percentage (0-100%) but may also be expressed as a probability (0-1).

Rect A
The longest side length when a rectangle is constructed around the particle.

Ready-to-sterilize (RTS)
Washed components supplied in a package suitable for sterilization prior to use with sterile injectable products.

Ready-to-use (RTU)
Washed and sterilized components supplied in a package suitable for transfer into an aseptic processing area and used with sterile injectable products without further processing.

Shape factor (SF)/Circularity
Provides information on the roundness of a particle. The SF value is between 0 and 1, where 1 represents a circle. A is the area of the particle and p is the perimeter.

Subvisible particle
A particle with a size <100 µm in diameter.

Visible fiber
A fiber-like particle with an aspect ratio ≥5 and a length >300 µm.

Visible particle
A particle with an aspect ratio of <5 and a length >100 µm.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Classification of particle sources: Extrinsic vs Intrinsic:

  • Extrinsic Particle: Originates from outside the process, for example fibers in ambient air or residues from incomplete cleaning. These are "uncontrolled".
  • Intrinsic Particle: Originates from qualified product-contact materials within the process, for example stopper fragments or glass particles. "Unplanned but not unexpected".

This classification is very important because particles from different sources require different control strategies. Extrinsic particles are controlled through environmental and cleaning controls; intrinsic particles are controlled through component quality and process parameters.

Key Concepts: Particle Morphological Parameters

Why are so many morphological parameters needed? Because particles are not regular spheres, and each measurement approach captures different information:

  • Aspect Ratio: The key to distinguishing fibers from particles: an aspect ratio ≥5 is a fiber, <5 is a particle
  • Feret Min/Max: The minimum/maximum distance measured between parallel tangents; commonly used in automated image analysis
  • Shape Factor/Circularity: A value between 0 and 1, where 1 is a perfect circle. Helps in determining the source of a particle
  • Area, Perimeter, Length, Rect A: Different ways of measuring size, each suited to different scenarios

Analogy

Think of particle classification as a detective solving a case. You find a suspicious object at the crime scene:

  • Extrinsic: Like debris that flew in after a window was broken; the source is outside the scene
  • Intrinsic: Like shavings that fell off the furniture in the room through normal wear; the source is inside the scene

The various morphological parameters are the detective's measuring tools: some measure length, some measure area, some analyze shape. Only together can they tell you "what this foreign particle is and where it came from".

Key Notes

The boundary between visible and subvisible particles:

  • Visible particle: Aspect ratio <5 and length >100 µm
  • Visible fiber: Aspect ratio ≥5 and length >300 µm
  • Subvisible particle: Size <100 µm

Note that there is a gray zone here: a fiber with an aspect ratio ≥5 and a length between 100 and 300 µm is neither a "visible fiber" nor a "subvisible particle". In practice, such borderline cases require particular attention in applying the classification criteria.

Key Notes: RTS vs RTU

Key difference:

  • RTS (Ready-to-Sterilize): Already washed; must be sterilized by the user before use
  • RTU (Ready-to-Use): Already washed and sterilized; can be transferred directly into the aseptic area

What the two have in common is that neither is washed again before use. The particulate level at supplier delivery is therefore the level you ultimately face. This is why TR85 is especially important for these two types of components.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: You receive a particulate test report for a batch of RTU stoppers, and the report uses Feret Max as the basis for size measurement. But your in-house AVI system uses Length (the longest axis) for its decisions. Because the same irregularly shaped particle can give different values for Feret Max and Length, you must confirm that the measurement definitions are consistent; otherwise the same batch of stoppers could "pass" at the supplier and "fail" in your system.

This is why TR85 devotes so much space to harmonizing terminology and definitions.

3.1 Patient Risk Assessment

Original Text

All parenteral drug preparations contain particulate matter. Particles ultimately administered to a patient may originate from the primary packaging components or may enter through the manufacturing process and during administration from the syringe or infusion set apparatus. Therefore, the potential patient particulate exposure burden derives from the sum of these sources.

The clinical implications of visible particulate matter in injectables have been reviewed in several recent publications (7,8). Historically the presence of unintended visible particulate matter has been regarded as a quality defect which can impact safety resulting in additional batch-related actions, including batch reinspection, rejection, or recall. Visible product defects such as cracked or leaking containers or product discoloration often point to a product quality and possible safety issue. While the presence of a rare or isolated visible particle induces a similar instinctive response, the potential safety implication requires a comprehensive and contextually-based evaluation.

Patient exposure to particles occurs across a size continuum from submicron to millimeters, while risk assessments are generally conducted on particles reported as visible or subvisible components and not the aggregated risk of both subgroups. Assessing the potential risk that patient exposure to particles might present requires identifying the source of the particle; determining particle-specific attributes such as size, number, and composition; and ascertaining the intended use including the route and frequency of administration and relevant patient population characteristics (9). Any impact to the final product's benefit-risk profile that might result from the presence of particulate matter arises from the combination of these elements and calculated once the reference product and characteristics of the intended recipient are known.

In all instances of injections, the size of the particle is a restricting attribute. The particle must be small enough (or nonrigid) to pass through the bore of the needle (i.e., the particle must be less than the internal diameter of the needle in at least two dimensions). Detection probability increases with size such that larger particles are more likely to be identified with a detection probability (under ideal conditions) typically exceeding 95% for particles that are 200 µm and larger (2). Consequently, the product is either rejected during manufacture or found during dose preparation and discarded. There have been no reports identifying patient harm from randomly administered visible particulate matter in pharmaceutical preparations (8). Therefore, particles that are ultimately injected will be at or below the limit of detection of the qualified manufacturing environment with most of those particles being subvisible.

In most instances, the greatest risk to patients receiving injectable medicines is related to a lack of product sterility. For this reason alone, a robust inspection program is not sufficient; but rather, a broad range of other visible attributes is assessed along with laboratory testing of microbiological, chemical, and physical parameters. These are based on statistical sampling plans to assess the risk of elevated bioburden or other related harms in the batch. The risks to product sterility may be mitigated through processing decisions such as qualified component and product terminal sterilization processes (e.g., moist or dry heat, irradiation, or gaseous or vapor treatments). While the presence of a single particle is unlikely to provide a sufficient substrate to support microbiologic proliferation, multiple units identified with extrinsic particles should raise an alarm since the source is likely from a nonsterile environment. Appropriately qualified depyrogenation and autoclaving processes preclude the introduction of bioburden through primary packaging components.

Inert particles such as those from elastomers or glass generally pose little concern when introduced into a patient's subcutaneous tissue or muscle because they are typically sterile or sanitized. However, the particles could potentially stimulate a local immunologic reaction consisting of macrophages and foreign body giant cells at the interface of the particulate material (e.g., a granuloma), but it is likely that such a reaction would remain asymptomatic (10). More commonly, complications from subcutaneous and intramuscular medications arise from the irritating properties of the drug product and are often drug-specific but generally have a minimal impact (11).

Intravenous (IV) infusion of particles may induce phlebitis due to the particles causing direct traumatic irritation to the vein or from chemical damage by undissolved particles (12). Recent observational studies have also described instances in select patient cohorts where morbidity has been reduced through the use of infusion filters, suggesting that particles in the infusate were responsible for the difference in outcomes (13). Other studies have failed to corroborate these findings in additional patient populations (14).

A particle introduced intravenously that exceeds the internal diameter of a pulmonary capillary (7–12 µm) will lodge in that capillary or a more proximal pulmonary arteriole. While clinical outcomes are generally benign, such matter introduced into the systemic circulation might stimulate a local immunologic reaction (e.g., a granuloma) in the lung (15). These isolated, small parenchymal lesions generally remain asymptomatic. In addition, as demonstrated in animal studies, the massive infusion of particulates has been accompanied by histologic evidence of injury to pulmonary capillary endothelial cells (16), microscopic thrombi in the pulmonary capillaries (17), pulmonary microscopic granulomata (18), and hepatic inflammatory effects (19). An important distinction to note is that most of the studies above infused massive amounts of subvisible particles. While an all-inclusive review of the clinical implications of particulate infusions is beyond the intended scope of this technical report, the potential exists for the intravascular infusion of particulate matter to cause pathophysiologic injury depending upon the size and quantity of the particles, and, therefore, should be included as an element in any clinical risk assessment.

"In summary, particulate matter will be present in every injectable dose. The risk of visible particles in injections to a patient is low from products manufactured and inspected under appropriately qualified current Good Manufacturing Practices conditions (20,21). Their origin, composition, size, numbers, and characteristics of the intended patient population are all important factors to weigh when assessing the clinical import from exposure."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Particulate Exposure Burden: The total amount of particulates the patient ultimately receives, from sources including packaging components (glass vials, rubber stoppers), the manufacturing process, and the syringe or infusion set used at administration. Risk assessment must consider the cumulative effect of all these sources.

Benefit-Risk Profile: When assessing the effect of particulates on product safety, the source, size, number and composition of the particles must be considered together with the route, frequency of administration and characteristics of the target patient population. A judgment cannot be made on any single factor alone.

Detection Probability: The probability that a particle is found by visual inspection is positively correlated with its size. Under ideal conditions, the detection probability for particles of 200 µm and above generally exceeds 95%. This means that most of the particles that end up being injected into the patient are subvisible particles below the detection threshold.

Extrinsic Particles: Particles from the external environment rather than from the product or packaging itself. If extrinsic particles are found in multiple units, this should be treated as a serious warning sign, because the source is likely a non-sterile environment, suggesting a gap in sterility assurance.

Analogy

Particulate risk assessment is like assessing the risk of foreign matter in food. A hair falling into soup (an inert particle in a subcutaneous injection) is unhygienic but usually causes no serious health problem. But if broken glass gets into the food (a large particle in an intravenous injection), it can cause direct physical injury. And if foreign matter turns up in several dishes in the kitchen (extrinsic particles in multiple units), it is no longer an isolated incident but a failure of the hygiene control system. This is exactly why TR85 stresses the importance of distinguishing random single particles from systemic contamination.

Key Notes

Key conclusion: No report has confirmed that "randomly administered visible particles" have ever harmed a patient. This important fact provides the foundation for the whole risk assessment framework: particulate risk exists, but for products manufactured and inspected under qualified cGMP conditions, the risk that visible particles pose to patients is low.

Risk grading by route of administration:

  • Subcutaneous/intramuscular injection: Inert particles (such as glass or rubber) have usually been sterilized and the risk is lower; at most they may cause a local granulomatous reaction, which is usually asymptomatic
  • Intravenous infusion: The highest risk. Particles may cause phlebitis or block pulmonary capillaries (internal diameter 7-12 µm), and animal studies show that large numbers of particles can cause lung and liver damage

Sterility assurance remains the first priority: The report stresses again that for injectable patients the greatest risk is still the sterility of the product, not the particulates themselves. This is consistent with the "hierarchy of decisions" that runs through the PDA series of reports.

Formula

Patient particulate exposure risk = f(source, size, number, composition, route of administration, frequency of administration, patient population)

Key size thresholds:
  - 200 µm: visual detection probability > 95% (ideal conditions)
  - 100 µm: boundary between visible and subvisible particles
  - 7-12 µm: internal diameter of pulmonary capillaries, the key retention size for IV administration
  - 10 µm: starting size for pharmacopeial subvisible particle tests

Injection needle internal diameter limit:
  A particle must be < the needle's internal diameter in at least two dimensions to pass through

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Your client asks you to fill a biologic that requires frequent intravenous infusion. Based on the risk assessment framework in this section, you should:

  • Raise the stringency of the particle control strategy to the highest level (IV administration + high frequency = highest cumulative exposure risk)
  • Give priority to low-particle-release packaging components (for example, surface-treated rubber stoppers, glass vials with low delamination)
  • Build a full particle source identification capability (forensic particle characterization) so that root causes can be traced quickly when a particulate problem occurs
  • Complete the particulate risk assessment together with the client and incorporate the results into the product's benefit-risk file

3.2 Existing Test Methods and Limitations

Original Text

Visual inspection of primary packaging components prior to use is hindered by four factors: the lack of a standardized industry inspection method for particle detection; the diversity of potential particle defects introduced through the manufacturing process; decreased visual detection capabilities on isolated or empty components; and the probabilistic nature of particle detection (3). Visual inspection of the packaging components is generally conducted by sampling and not inspection of every piece (100% inspection), thereby limiting the information necessary to construct an adequate library of the type and number of particles the process produces. Detection of particles by MVI, SAVI, or AVI is a cumulative function of the particles' visible attributes including particle size, shape, color, density, and reflectivity. Particle detection can also be more difficult in the empty components because the particles cannot be put in motion as with particles in a liquid product (22).

The absence of standardized methods or visible particle acceptance criteria leaves it to each supplier and pharmaceutical manufacturer to establish these requirements for each product supplied. While regulatory guidance documents and industry publications may provide useful perspectives, the absence of a clearly articulated standard results in manufacturers subsequently adopting a hybrid approach using various portions of the published pharmacopeial (6,23) and International Standards Organization (ISO) methods (24). Identifying the limitations of test methods adapted for the evaluation of primary packaging components provides the opportunity to develop component-specific methods that would serve as a common standard for supplier-customer quality consideration.

Understanding the particle attributes is essential for each manufacturer when developing and adopting appropriate test methods, characterizing particle defect attributes, defining achievable detection capabilities, and assessing the potential impact of particles to the product's benefit-risk profile. The use of standard forensic methods of particle characterization (22) can be beneficial to inform the clinical risk assessment and direct allocation of resources to critical visual inspection and process control strategies. Additionally, the forensic datasets are important components to identify potential sources of particle ingress or generation, variability in raw material components, and monitor the stability of manufacturing processes (4).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

MVI / SAVI / AVI (the three categories of visual inspection methods):

  • MVI (Manual Visual Inspection): Inspection by qualified inspectors with the unaided eye under standardized conditions
  • SAVI (Semi-Automated Visual Inspection): The machine assists with spinning/illumination, but the final decision is made by a person
  • AVI (Automated Visual Inspection): Inspection and decision made entirely by a machine vision system

Forensic Particle Characterization: Using microscopy, spectroscopy and similar methods to identify the source, composition and morphology of particles. This is a fundamental tool for building a particle knowledge base and tracing the root cause of contamination.

Probabilistic Nature of Detection: Particle detection is not a deterministic "yes or no" outcome but a probabilistic event influenced by multiple attributes such as particle size, shape, color, density and reflectivity. The same particle may be detected or missed under different conditions.

Analogy

Detecting particles on packaging components is like looking for dust in an empty room, which is much harder than looking for suspended matter in a glass of water. Particles in liquid can be swirled and their motion tracked, but particles on an empty glass vial or rubber stopper are stationary and may be stuck to the surface, which greatly increases the difficulty of detection. This is why existing pharmacopeial methods (designed for filled liquid products) are of limited effectiveness when applied to empty packaging components.

Key Notes

Four major obstacles to inspecting packaging components:

  1. Lack of a standardized method: there is no unified industry standard for particle inspection of packaging components
  2. Diversity of defects: the manufacturing process can produce many different types of particle defects
  3. Difficulty of inspecting empty components: particles cannot be set in motion as they can in a liquid
  4. Probabilistic nature of detection itself: a 100% detection rate cannot be guaranteed

These four obstacles are the core reason TR85 exists: it is precisely because existing methods are inadequate that enhanced test methods need to be developed.

3.2.1 ISO Test Method 8871-3

Original Text

ISO 8871-3 Elastomeric parts for parenterals and for devices for pharmaceutical use — Part 3: Determination of released-particle count (24) provides analytical methods for the extraction of loose particulates from the surface of elastomeric components used in pharmaceutical packaging applications. The ISO standard is split into subvisible particle extraction using light extinction (or light obscuration (LO)) and visible particle extraction followed by membrane filtration and microscopic examination (MM) for particle detection. Once particle extraction is complete, quantification is performed with an optical microscope and a calibrated reticle at approximately 50x magnification. Particle counts can be conducted either manually or via a validated automated microscopic method. Both the particle extraction and the counting technique are considered optimized in evaluating elastomer-associated visible particles. However, the method would require some level of enhancement for visible particles (i.e., particles >100 µm and fibers >300 µm as defined in this report) through the design of a reticle capable of differentiating particles from fibers and identifying a shaker for use in Europe that is comparable to that specified in ISO 8871-3. Additionally, further concern exists regarding the sensitivity of the method based on the surface area/number of stoppers sampled. Currently, there is no similar ISO method for particle extraction or characterization for parenteral container inspection.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

ISO 8871-3: A method for the quantitative analysis of particles released from the surface of elastomeric components for injectables (such as stoppers and plungers). It contains two main analytical paths:

  • LO (Light Obscuration): Used for subvisible particles (generally ≥10 µm); particles are counted by sensing the obscuration of light with a laser
  • MM (Microscopic Method): Used for visible particles; after collection by membrane filtration, particles are counted under an optical microscope at 50× magnification using a calibrated reticle

Calibrated Reticle: A graduated scale placed in the microscope eyepiece, used to measure particle size. TR85 points out that its design needs to be improved so that it can distinguish particles from fibers.

Analogy

The ISO 8871-3 method is like "checking the wash water after washing clothes": first the rubber stoppers are placed in a solution and shaken (extraction) to rinse off loose particles from the surface, then the solution is filtered through a membrane (membrane filtration), and finally the particles on the filter are counted under magnification (microscopic counting). This method works well for rubber components, but there is currently no similar "standard wash-and-inspect method" for glass vials.

Key Notes

Three key limitations of ISO 8871-3:

  1. Need for enhancement for visible particles: For particles >100 µm and fibers >300 µm, an improved reticle design is needed to distinguish them effectively
  2. Regional equipment differences: In Europe, equipment equivalent to the shaker specified in ISO 8871-3 needs to be found
  3. Sampling sensitivity: For rubber stoppers with different surface areas/quantities, there are still doubts about whether the sensitivity of the sampling strategy is sufficient

Biggest gap: There is currently no similar ISO method at all for the extraction and identification of particles from glass containers. This is one of the core problems TR85 sets out to solve.

4.1 Enhanced Closures Testing Method Development

Original Text

Currently, specific but different guidance requirements exist for visible and subvisible particulates in parenterals. For final product inspection, the distinction between visible and subvisible is established by what can be seen by the unaided human eye when performing the compendial method inspection of approximately 10 sec per container in front of black and white backgrounds under controlled lighting. If a particle is observed in a final-filled container by the unaided eye of a qualified inspector, it is classified as a visible particulate defect and considered against the visible particle requirements such as those defined in USP <790> (6).

In the case of packaging components for injectable products, suppliers of these components often perform an inspection for particulate matter at the time of release. Likewise, pharmaceutical manufacturing companies often perform a particulate test at incoming receipt. The testing of these components is intended to demonstrate that they meet specification and are appropriate for their intended use. As with final product testing, component testing should be capable of classifying any particulate on the components as either visible or subvisible. In cases where the packaging components are inspected by the unaided human eye for particulate, the same principles for classification apply. Namely, any particulate observed by the unaided human eye as part of packaging component testing will be considered visible.

In some cases, testing for particulate in packaging components will use analytical methods that exceed the ability of the unaided human eye. The most common example of such testing is a method that involves rinsing packaging components, filtering this rinsate, and then examining the filter under a microscope to measure and count any observed particulates. Microscopic examination will detect a wide range of particles including some which are not visible to the unaided human eye (i.e., subvisible) in addition to particles that are visible to the unaided eye. For this reason, the microscope confounds the ability of the test method to make the important distinction of visible versus subvisible. This challenge is common to any analytical or inspection method that exceeds human detection capability. Examples include current AVI systems, artificial intelligence applications, and numerous chemical and microbial assays.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Visible vs. Subvisible Particles: This is the core classification in injectable quality control. The regulatory definition of "visible" is very specific: what a qualified inspector can see with the unaided eye in about 10 seconds against a black and white background under controlled lighting is "visible".

USP <790> (USP Chapter 790): Specifically sets the standard for visible particles in finished injectable products, requiring the product to be "essentially free" of visible particles.

AVI Systems (automated visual inspection systems): AVI stands for Automated Visual Inspection; these systems use machine vision to detect particles in containers, and their detection capability generally exceeds that of the human eye.

Analogy

This is like the difference in security screening: eye inspection is a guard looking at your luggage, while using a microscope is like going through an X-ray machine, which can see things the human eye cannot (such as small objects hidden in the lining of clothing). The problem is that the X-ray machine is "too sensitive" and flags even things that do not need to be worried about. Dual testing by supplier and pharmaceutical company: The closure supplier tests at shipment and the pharmaceutical company also tests at receipt. Both sides must use consistent methods and standards, otherwise contradictory data will arise.

4.1.1 Size Threshold

Original Text

To allow for differences between visible and subvisible particles when using instrumental methods with capability that exceeds the unaided human eye, a size threshold is required to determine a visible particulate. The particulate size which is visible during inspection in filled product containers is the basis for the size thresholds. This approach ensures that particulate classification (i.e., visible and subvisible) in packaging components will more clearly connect to the regulations for visible and subvisible particulates in filled product containers.

Multiple studies (2) have been performed on the detectability of particulate matter of various sizes in the final containers of parenteral products when inspected by qualified inspectors following the compendial methods (6,26,28). These studies have demonstrated that under controlled conditions, the smallest size standard sphere that could be reliably detected (i.e., average PoD >70%) is 150 μm (2). The same studies demonstrated negligible detection of 50 μm particulates and variable detection for 100 μm particulates.

Based on this understanding of detection as a function of particle size in parenterals, the value of 100 μm was selected as the threshold above which particles would be classified as visible when using an analytical method that exceeds the capability of the unaided human eye. The 100 μm threshold was conservatively selected to ensure that most particulate matter, even those with a moderate chance of being seen by the unaided human eye in a filled container, would be classified as "visible" by the analytical method using this threshold value. The 100 μm threshold is considered conservative because it is derived from the best case inspection conditions (i.e., clear solution, clear 10 mL vial, compendial lighting, background, and inspection time) and because it is lower than the 150 μm value established as the lower limit where detection in final containers is considered reliable (i.e., average PoD >70%).

When establishing a threshold defining a particle as visible, the particle geometry must also be considered. The 100 μm threshold was derived from studies of the detectability of standard spheres in liquid filled containers. Therefore, the 100 μm value is most applicable for an equant particulate which has an approximate 1:1:1 aspect ratio (length:width:thickness). This is similar to the ratio of a circle when considering the profile of the particulate in two dimensions. However, fibrous particles can have much higher aspect ratios. Such fiber-like particles need to be much longer in length as compared to an equivalent spherical diameter to be reliably detected in final product containers (1). The lower ability to detect a fiber-like particle versus that of a spherical particle is related to the two-dimensional profile of the two shapes and how this profile presents to the inspector. For example, a single 100 μm sphere has nearly eight times the two-dimensional surface area as a 100 μm fiber-like particle with an aspect ratio of 10:1 (length:width).

Based on the reduced detectability of fiber-like particles, a second threshold size to classify a particulate as visible was established for particulate with fiber-like geometries (i.e., those with a length to width aspect ratios >5). The size threshold to consider a fiber-like particulate visible was derived by calculating the length of a fiber with an equivalent two-dimensional surface area as the 150 μm spheres which were established as the lower limit where detection in final containers is considered reliable. For this calculation, an aspect ratio of 5 was used as a conservative limit such that higher aspect ratios would result in the classification of even longer fiber lengths as subvisible. Based on this approach, a particulate with fiber-like geometry would need to be greater than 300 μm to be considered visible when observed using analytical methods more capable than the unaided human eye. L = √(17,671 × 5) ≈ 297 μm → rounded to 300 μm

100 μm sphere vs. 100 μm fiber (10:1):
Sphere area = π × (50)² ≈ 7,854 μm²
Fiber area = 100 × 10 = 1,000 μm²
Ratio ≈ 7.85 times → the text says "nearly eight times"

Key Notes

100 μm is a conservative value: Studies show that 150 μm is the lower limit for reliable detection (PoD >70%), but TR85 chose the lower value of 100 μm as the threshold. This is a conservative "better to catch too many than to miss any" strategy: it ensures that even particles that are only barely visible under the best inspection conditions are classified as "visible".

Strict limits on scope of application:

  • These thresholds apply only when testing packaging components with analytical methods that exceed the capability of the human eye
  • They do not apply to final visual inspection of finished product containers (which still uses the human eye as the standard)
  • If a particle has already been seen with the naked eye, it is classified directly as "visible" and no threshold needs to be applied

Table Analysis

Interpretation of Table 4.1.1-1: This table is the core reference tool of the whole method.

4.1.2 Novel Particle Size Measurement Tool

Original Text

The assessment of particles in elastomer components traditionally involves rinsing the components, then filtering the rinsate, and finally examining the filter under a microscope. No thresholds to differentiate visible from subvisible, as discussed above, have been established. To consistently apply the principles of the visible particle size thresholds defined in Section 4.1, a novel measurement tool was developed for use in elastomer particle testing involving microscopic analysis of the filtrate.

Figure 4.1.2-1 depicts a novel reticle design that enables an analyst to readily determine whether particles observed microscopically on a filter should be considered visible or subvisible. The reticle has two shapes in the view field: a circle, and a rectangle. The circle is to be sized such that for a given microscope under the intended magnification, the circle is equivalent to a diameter of 100 μm. Similarly, the rectangle is 300 μm × 60 μm to address both size and aspect ratio thresholds for fiber-like particles.

The reticle design shown in Figure 4.1.2-1 allows for rapid and consistent application of the particle size and aspect ratio thresholds established for this test. When an analyst examines the filter through a microscope, they simply compare any particle observed against the rectangle and circle templates in the reticle. Any particle that fits entirely within either of the two shapes is considered subvisible and is not to be counted as visible. Those that extend beyond the boundaries of either template are counted as visible, and depending on the aspect ratio as a particle or fiber.

4.2 Summary for Performing the Enhanced Closures Testing Method

Original Text

A summary of the Enhanced Closure Testing Method for counting visible particles on elastomeric closures follows. Refer to 8.1 Appendix A for a detailed discussion on the development of this method.

A number of parts (normalized for surface area) are placed in an Erlenmeyer Flask, and 50 ml of surfactant solution is dispensed and added to the flask. The flask is then placed on an orbital shaker to agitate the parts and remove visible particles from the surface. After 20 seconds of shaking the flask is removed from the shaker and the surfactant solution is immediately filtered through a membrane filter (a gray filter was used to enhance contrast for both light and dark colored particles and fibers). The rinsing process is repeated and the filter is allowed to dry. Once the filter is dry, any visible particles present are counted by using a specialized reticle and an optical stereomicroscope.

A thorough discussion of the sample preparation process and the counting methodology used to evaluate within and between operator replicate samples can also be found in 8.2 Appendix B.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Summary of method steps:

  1. Sampling: Place a surface-area-normalized quantity of closures into an Erlenmeyer flask
  2. Rinsing: Add 50 mL of surfactant solution
  3. Shaking: Shake on an orbital shaker for 20 seconds to dislodge surface particles
  4. Filtration: Immediately filter the rinse solution through a gray membrane filter
  5. Repeat: Repeat the rinsing process
  6. Drying: Allow the filter to dry
  7. Counting: Count the visible particles using the dedicated reticle and an optical stereomicroscope

Analogy

The whole procedure is like washing clothes and then examining the dirt that came out: put the stoppers (clothes) into the flask (washing machine), add surfactant (detergent), shake for 20 seconds (wash), then filter the wash water (drain and filter) and see what is left on the filter. The choice of a gray membrane is especially clever: like using a gray tablecloth to look at dust, both light and dark particles can be seen clearly.

Key Notes

Some key method design details:

  • Surface-area normalization: Closures of different sizes are tested in different quantities, to keep the comparison fair
  • Gray membrane filter: Gray was chosen deliberately over white or black, because a gray background improves the contrast of both light and dark particles
  • 20-second shaking: This time was optimized: too short may not dislodge the particles completely, too long may break up particles and make the count inaccurate
  • Immediate filtration: Filtration must follow immediately after shaking, to prevent particles from redepositing on the closure surface
  • Repeat rinse: Done twice to ensure adequate recovery

Practical Application

Practical considerations when implementing this method in a CDMO laboratory:

  • Equipment needs: An orbital shaker (with settable speed), a stereomicroscope (equipped with the TR85 dedicated reticle), and gray membrane filters
  • Personnel training: Analysts must be trained in the use of the reticle and pass a qualification
  • SOP integration: The method can be integrated into the existing incoming quality control SOP
  • Supplier communication: It is advisable to require in the quality agreement that the supplier also use this method, so that the data are comparable

Practice Questions

  1. Why choose a "gray" membrane rather than "white" or "black"? What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?
  2. If the shaking time were changed from 20 seconds to 60 seconds, what effect might it have on the results?
4.3 Method Qualification Strategy and Lessons Learned

Original Text

Preliminary studies for attribute agreement revealed that rigorous training and easy-to-follow instructions reduces the subjectivity of the analyst's counting technique. Detailed instructions and the use of the unique counting reticle described in Section 4.1 are key to this training. Repeatability of the particle and fiber count data was proven when multiple analysts inspected each other's filter samples. The material found to be best suited for training analysts and qualification of the method was to select stopper populations prior to their pharmaceutical washing step. These stoppers provided particle counts that were sufficiently high to yield meaningful statistical evaluation.

In attempting to measure repeatability when performing multiple sample extractions from a single stopper bag or across multiple stopper bags, significant variability was observed. Specific factors contributing to this variability in the particle counts were identified through the multiple design of experiments (DOE) based investigations and subsequent review of the findings with the operators involved.

The inherent lack of homogeneity of particle and fiber deposition on the surface of elastomeric components that are packaged within a bag or collectively transported in multiple cartons from the same batch was one of the underlying root causes for the inconsistent initial results observed early in the development of this test method. Repetitive handling, sampling, and shipping greatly increases variability and localization of particles.

Another challenge identified during development and qualification of this method was to determine the number of stoppers needed for training and qualification. Because it is common for commercial stopper batches to have particle counts near zero, these batches are not ideal to train analysts or qualify the method. There were several attempts made to purposely seed particles into stopper batches, but these were not successful. Generating appropriate particulate loading through various attempts at artificially seeding with shredded fragments of rubber and aliquots of a cellulose emulsion proved to be ineffective due to inconsistent distribution within the bag, which in turn resulted in highly variable recovery.

Additional detail on the development of this method and the evaluation of contributions to method variability can be found in 8.1 Appendix A.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Attribute Agreement: A statistical method used to assess whether different analysts reach consistent decisions on the same sample. In particle counting, it confirms whether results counted by different people using the same method are comparable.

Repeatability: The degree of agreement among results when the same analyst repeatedly tests the same sample under the same conditions.

DOE (Design of Experiments): A statistical approach that systematically varies experimental conditions to identify sources of variation. The TR85 team used DOE to identify the specific factors causing count variability.

Homogeneity: The uniformity of the distribution of particles on the closure surface. This is one of the biggest challenges for this method: the distribution of particles within a package bag is inherently non-uniform.

Analogy

Imagine you need to train a group of sommeliers to judge wine quality. Ideally, you would give them wines of every quality grade to practice on. But if almost all the wine you have is top grade (particle counts close to zero), the sommeliers cannot learn to tell the difference. You try to "make" poor wine yourself (artificially spiking particles), but the quality of each bottle is inconsistent. In the end you find that the best training material is wine that has "not yet been through the final refining step" (stoppers before washing), because it naturally contains enough impurities for the sommeliers to practice discriminating.

Key Notes

Three major lessons learned:

  1. Training is key: Rigorous training + clear SOPs + a dedicated reticle = greatly reduced subjectivity. This echoes the GMP principle that "personnel qualification" is always the cornerstone of the quality system.
  2. Non-uniform particle distribution is natural: Even among stoppers in the same package bag, the particle distribution is itself non-uniform. Handling, sampling and transport all aggravate this non-uniformity. This means a single sampling result has limited representativeness, and an appropriate sampling strategy is needed.
  3. Artificial spiking failed: Attempts to simulate the particle burden with rubber fragments and cellulose emulsion produced highly variable recovery because of non-uniform distribution. This tells us that the real-world particle distribution is more complex than we imagine.

Key Notes

Strategy for choosing training material: Using "pre-wash" stoppers as training and qualification material is a pragmatic solution. These stoppers have not yet gone through the pharmaceutical washing step after manufacture and naturally carry enough particles to give meaningful counts for statistical evaluation. This is more reliable than artificial spiking, because the particles are naturally present.

Practical Application

If your CDMO is going to implement the TR85 method, here are practical recommendations for method qualification:

  • Training material: Ask the stopper supplier for "pre-wash" samples for training; do not use commercial batches (counts are too low)
  • Personnel qualification: Design an attribute agreement test, in which several analysts review each other's membrane filter samples
  • Sources of variation: When establishing the sampling SOP, consider the problem of non-uniform particle distribution; it may be necessary to sample from different positions in the package bag
  • DOE verification: Use DOE to systematically evaluate the effect of operating variables (shaking speed, number of rinses, etc.) on the results

Practice Questions

  1. Why are particle counts in commercial stopper batches usually close to zero? What challenge does this pose for method qualification?
  2. "Handling, sampling and transport aggravate the non-uniformity of particle distribution": explain the physical mechanism behind this phenomenon.
  3. If you were responsible for designing the personnel qualification program for this method, what assessment criteria would you set?

Section Summary

  • Need for standardization: TR85 developed an Enhanced Closures Testing Method that integrates the strengths of USP <788> and ISO 8871-3, allowing suppliers and pharmaceutical companies to compare particle data on the same basis
  • Dual size thresholds: The visible threshold is 100 μm for equant particles (aspect ratio <5) and 300 μm for fibrous particles (aspect ratio ≥5). 100 μm is a conservative choice, below the 150 μm lower limit of reliable detection
  • Innovative reticle design: A reticle with a circle (100 μm) + a rectangle (300 μm × 60 μm) simplifies particle size "measurement" to "comparison", improving speed and consistency
  • Standardized method workflow: Surface-area-normalized sampling → surfactant rinse → 20-second orbital shaking → gray membrane filtration → repeat → drying → reticle counting
  • Lessons learned in method qualification: Non-uniform particle distribution is the main source of variability; artificial spiking is impractical; pre-wash stoppers are the best training material; rigorous training + reticle = reduced subjectivity
  • Note on scope: The size thresholds apply only to component testing where the analytical method exceeds the capability of the human eye, not to visual inspection of finished products

5.1.1 Operator Training for the Enhanced Container Inspection Method

Original Text

To achieve reliable and consistent results, inspectors must be trained on the inspection method before performing routine inspection and their inspection performance verified by testing both initially and then every two years (minimum) thereafter. Key Concepts

Requalification Interval: Inspectors of empty containers are requalified at least once every two years, a lower frequency than for inspectors of filled containers. The reason is that after filling, the containers still undergo 100% inspection and AQL sampling, creating multiple lines of defense.

Analogy

Qualification is like a driving test: you need to pass the initial test and also renew your license periodically (requalification). The text specifically explains why the requalification interval for inspectors of empty containers can be more relaxed than for filled containers: there is a downstream double safeguard (100% inspection + AQL sampling). 5.1.3 Magnification Requirements To Inspect Glass Containers

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

USP <790> (general chapter): Specifies that routine inspection must be performed without magnification. This is a chapter with regulatory enforceability.

Analogy

This is like the vision test in a medical checkup: in a normal checkup you use a standard eye chart (naked eye or corrected), and a telescope is not allowed. Key Notes

The focus of this subsection is to prevent scope creep. If magnifiers are allowed in investigations without strict control guidance, they can easily become part of routine inspection over time, which would:

  • Deviate from pharmacopeial requirements, creating regulatory compliance risk
  • Change the detection baseline, causing the false reject rate to rise
  • Make inspection standards inconsistent from batch to batch

It is recommended that the SOP clearly define the use cases, approval process, and record requirements for magnifiers.

5.2 Method Qualification Strategy and Detection Capability

Original Text

To set realistic targets for human inspection performance, the detection capability of the method being used for inspection must be understood. A significant portion of the work reported here centered on performing inspections on seeded containers assembled into test kits to better assess the detection capability of the method described.

For all observations reported in this study, the light intensity was confirmed as ≥3,000 lux at the point of verification which is at the upper end of the inspection conditions defined in the EP (26), USP (6), and JP (28), of 2,000-3,750 lux.

Three equivalent test kits were created representing a wide range of visible particles types and sizes commonly found in glass containers. Eight different particulate types (glass, metal, cellulose, hair, plastic, and rubber [black, grey, and orange]), were defined and used when preparing the kits. Each container was checked for particles prior to seeding, each unit was seeded with a single particle and then sealed to prevent further contamination. For these studies, each kit contained 60 each of vials, syringes, and cartridges, making a total of 180 pieces. Key Concepts

Detection Capability: The ability of the method, under specific inspection conditions, to detect particles of a given size and type. This is the prerequisite for setting realistic inspector performance standards: you cannot ask people to exceed the limits of the method itself.

Seeded Container: A container confirmed to be clean into which particles of known size and type are deliberately placed, used to test the performance of the detection method. The key is that the adhesive must not be visible, otherwise it introduces bias.

Feret Max (maximum Feret diameter): The maximum distance between two parallel tangent lines of a particle, commonly used to characterize the size of irregularly shaped particles. It suits irregular particles such as glass and metal.

Rect A: The length of the longer side of the rectangle enclosing the particle, suited to more regularly shaped particles such as rubber.

Analogy

Building a test kit is like preparing a standardized exam: you need to design a paper covering different levels of difficulty (particle size) and different question types (particle material), and then have examinees (inspectors) at different test centers (companies) answer under the same conditions. Only then can you objectively compare the performance of different people and different companies and establish a statistically meaningful baseline.

The way particle size is measured (Feret Max vs. Length vs. Rect A) is like using different yardsticks to measure "question difficulty": length is most suitable for long thin fibers, while Feret Max best reflects the size seen by the human eye for irregular fragments.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Probability of Detection, PoD: The probability that a particle of a given size and type is successfully detected under given inspection conditions. This is the core metric for evaluating the performance of an inspection method. PoD rises as particle size increases, but particles of different materials/shapes have different PoD at the same size.

Aspect Ratio: The ratio of a fiber's length to its width. A fiber with a high aspect ratio (such as a hair or plastic strand) is long, but because it is very narrow, it presents a small "area" to the human eye and is therefore harder to detect than a particle of the same length.

Analogy

Imagine looking for something on a white tablecloth: a 300 μm black bead (particle) is much easier to see than a 300 μm long thin thread (fiber), because the bead occupies more "visual area". This is why a 300 μm particle has a 95% PoD while a 300 μm fiber has only a 70% PoD; the difference lies in the aspect ratio.

5.3.1 Normalizing the Particle Size

Original Text

To better understand the difference in detectability between fibers and particles the impact of the aspect ratio on detectability (which is the relationship between the width and length of the fiber) must be considered. To address the impact of the aspect ratio the data was normalized by multiplying the Key Dimension of each particle by its SF. This conversion was applied to both particles and fibers to create the approximate area that would be seen by the inspector's unaided eye.

Normalized Particle Size = Key Dimension x Shape Factor (SF)

Using this transformation, the PoD for fibers and particles is similar to the PoD seen in Figure 5.3.1-1. The combined PoD then becomes 80% for 100 μm particulate and 95% for particulates of 200 μm. The diameter of the circles at specific x-y locations is proportional to the number of data points which occurred at that location.

In summary, a core element of the study was the creation of sample kits containing realistic defect examples where the PoD was not compromised or biased by the sample preparation (e.g., if the glue holding the particle is visible). By preparing these kits with defects that appear as those found during routine inspection the performance of the inspector using this method could be verified in a controlled way.

The study produced an extensive database with 49,360 validated data points, thereby providing a high degree of statistical confidence in the conclusions drawn on inspection performance. This study found that using the compendial inspection conditions the PoD for visible particles ranged from 70% for 125 μm particles to 95% for particles >300 μm. Due to their aspect ratios, fibers are more difficult to detect and their corresponding PoD are 80% for 300 μm fibers to 90% for fibers >800 μm.

These results align well with detectability studies performed on filled containers following the compendial methods (6,26,28). These earlier studies demonstrated that under controlled conditions, the smallest size standard sphere that could be reliably detected (i.e., average PoD >70%) was 150 μm.

In conclusion, from this study with empty containers and the previous work on filled containers, this method can be used successfully for the inspection of unfilled RTU containers by both the pharmaceutical manufacturing companies and the glass component suppliers. Inspectors using this method should be trained and qualified using test kits with well characterized and representative samples that accurately simulate routine defects and defect free samples. Additional review and discussion of the data analysis on inspection performance can be found in Section 8.3 Appendix C.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Shape Factor, SF: A coefficient used to convert the "key dimension" of particles of different shapes into a "visual equivalent area". The SF of a round particle is close to 1, while that of a long thin fiber is far less than 1 (because its width is much smaller than its length).

Normalized Particle Size: = Key Dimension x Shape Factor. This conversion allows particles and fibers to be compared on the same baseline for detection rate, eliminating the effect of shape differences.

Apparent Area: The "effective area" of the particle as seen by the inspector's naked eye. This, rather than length or diameter in a single dimension, is the real factor that determines whether it can be seen.

Formula

Normalized particle size formula:

Normalized Particle Size = Key Dimension x Shape Factor (SF)

Examples:
- Round particle (SF ≈ 1.0): 300 μm x 1.0 = 300 μm (unchanged after normalization)
- Long thin fiber (SF ≈ 0.1): 300 μm x 0.1 = 30 μm (greatly reduced after normalization)

PoD baselines after normalization:
- 100 μm (normalized) → 80% PoD
- 200 μm (normalized) → 95% PoD

Compared with the non-normalized data:
- Particles: 125 μm → 70% PoD; 300 μm → 95% PoD
- Fibers: 300 μm → 70% PoD; 800-1,000 μm → 90% PoD

Analogy

Normalization is like currency exchange rate conversion: a 300 μm fiber and a 300 μm particle have the same "price tag", but their "purchasing power" (visibility) to the human eye is completely different. Using the shape factor, we convert both into the same "currency" (apparent visual area), so they can be compared fairly. After conversion, the detection rates of fibers and particles turn out to be about the same at the same "visual area"; the problem is only that the fiber's "exchange rate" is low (small area).

6.0 Conclusion

Original Text

Visual inspection continues to be an important step in the manufacture and delivery of safe and effective injectable medicines. Current inspection methods, manual and machine assisted, can provide reliable monitoring and control of the visual characteristics of injectable products, including visible particles. The work described in this technical report is intended to further the development of methods to increase reliable supply and reduced rejection of injectable drug products. Reliable detection and control of visible particles in and on the components used to contain these products is essential to meeting this goal.

This technical report provides an improved method to assess visible particle burden on elastomeric closures. This method uses much that is known and available but provides standardization of key parameters including extraction, agitation, and size thresholds (100 μm for particles and 300 μm for fibers) to aid in consistent enumeration. This is expected to assist both the manufacturers of such closures and the pharmaceutical companies who use them in packaging their drug products. A standard method to assess visible particles will hopefully lead to a practical working standard which incorporates risk-based limits suitable for the intended application.

This report also describes a study to better assess the ability to inspect empty glass containers. While several studies have looked at PoD in finished, filled containers, little has been done with empty containers to-date. This study shows similar inspection performance between empty and filled containers and supports the use of standard compendial visual inspection conditions to perform inspection of empty glass containers. An average PoD of 70% was achieved for 125 μm particles and 300 μm fibers. These agree with and support the thresholds chosen for the Enhanced Closure Testing Method. Further analysis of the study data shows that PoD of particles and fibers can be related to each other through assessment of the apparent visible area.

Continued advancement in visual inspection and particle control will require broad industry support. Ongoing engagement with additional companies and regulatory organizations would further the work that was described herein and lead to acceptance of common methods and acceptance limits for visible particles on or in primary packaging components.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Enhanced Closure Testing Method: The core contribution of TR85. This is not a brand-new invention; it "standardizes" existing extraction, agitation, and counting techniques so that different laboratories and companies can measure the visible particle burden on closures with the same yardstick.

Size Thresholds: particles ≥ 100 μm, fibers ≥ 300 μm. These numbers were not set arbitrarily; they were cross-validated against the PoD (Probability of Detection) data from the empty container inspection study.

Probability of Detection, PoD: The probability of successfully finding a particle of a given size under specific inspection conditions. In this report, the empty container study achieved an average PoD of 70% for 125 μm particles and 300 μm fibers, which provides a scientific basis for the threshold setting.

Apparent Visible Area: The key concept that brings the PoD of particles and fibers onto the same dimension. Contaminants of different shapes (particles vs. fibers) can be compared for ease of detection through their projected area.

Analogy

Imagine you run a restaurant chain in which every branch must check that its tableware is clean. In the past each branch used its own standard: some by eye, some with a magnifier, and each shook the glasses with a different force. TR85's approach is like drawing up one unified "tableware cleanliness testing SOP": it specifies how much water to use, how long to shake, and how large a residue counts as a failure. Only then can headquarters truly compare the cleanliness of each branch, and negotiate meaningful quality specifications with the tableware supplier.

Key Notes

The value of standardization is commercial as well as technical: When closure manufacturers and pharmaceutical companies use the same test method and thresholds, both sides can set specifications, evaluate deviations, and compare suppliers in a common language. This directly reduces communication costs and the risk of disputes in the supply chain.

Inspection performance is similar for empty and filled containers: This is an important finding. It means a pharmaceutical company can carry out effective visual inspection before filling (at the empty container stage), intercepting nonconforming containers early and reducing losses after filling. This is especially significant economically for high-value biologics.

Risk-based limit setting: The report states clearly that the ideal goal is a "practical working standard which incorporates risk-based limits". This is consistent with the spirit of ICH Q9 quality risk management: not a one-size-fits-all zero tolerance, but reasonable acceptance limits for visible particles set according to product characteristics and clinical risk.

Key Concepts

Variable Data vs. Attribute Data:

  • Variable Data: Data that can be measured on a continuous scale, such as dimensions (mm) or potency. Validating this kind of data is relatively straightforward because it can be expressed in precise numbers.
  • Attribute Data: Data based on categorical judgments, such as "pass/fail" or "particles present/absent". Visual inspection produces attribute data, which depends on human subjective judgment.

Analogy

Imagine training chefs at three different restaurants to taste the saltiness of the same soup.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Repeatability vs. Reproducibility:

  • Repeatability: Whether results are consistent when the same operator tests the same sample repeatedly under the same conditions. This report assesses it with a paired t-test, i.e. whether the counts obtained by different operators examining the same filter agree.
  • Reproducibility: Whether test results are consistent across different operators or laboratories on different extracted samples. This report assesses it with ANOVA, i.e. whether the particle counts obtained after each operator's own extraction are comparable.

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance): Compares whether the means of three or more groups differ significantly. A p-value > 0.05 indicates no significant difference between the groups (i.e. the method results are comparable).

Paired t-test: Tests whether the differences between paired observations are significant. It is suited to the situation where "the same filter is counted by two different operators".

Analogy

Think of "extraction + counting" as "brewing coffee + tasting":

  • Repeatability: Do three tea tasters tasting the same cup of coffee give consistent scores? → Tested with a paired t-test.
  • Reproducibility: Do three baristas who each brew a cup and then taste their own get consistent results? → Tested with ANOVA.

The Supplier 1 results show that the tasting ability (counting repeatability) is very good (p > 0.05), but the brewing process (extraction reproducibility) differs for fibers (p = 0.022 < 0.05). This indicates that the problem lies in the extraction, not the counting.

Key Notes

Interpreting the Supplier 1 results:

  • Particles >100 μm: extraction reproducibility passed (ANOVA p = 0.590) and counting repeatability passed (paired t-test p = 0.078-0.761).
  • Fibers >300 μm: extraction reproducibility failed (ANOVA p = 0.022), but counting repeatability still passed (paired t-test p = 0.391-1.000).

Formula

Interpreting the ANOVA hypothesis test:

H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃  (all operator means are equal)
H₁: at least one group mean is different

Decision rule:
  p-value > 0.05  →  do not reject H₀  →  no significant difference between groups  →  pass
  p-value ≤ 0.05  →  reject H₀    →  significant difference between groups  →  fail

Supplier 1 example:
  Particles >100μm: p = 0.590 > 0.05  →  pass ✓
  Fibers >300μm: p = 0.022 < 0.05  →  fail ✗

Conclusion

The original strategy for confirming the robustness of the test method for extracting and counting the particulate present on the resulting membrane filter was intended to demonstrate quantitatively the repeatability and reproducibility of both components of the test method. Preliminary studies for attribute agreement revealed that rigorous training and easy to follow instructions are key to reducing the subjectivity of the analyst's counting techniques. Once this foundation of objectivity was formed for operators, the validity of their results was not in question. Repeatability of the particle and fiber count data was confirmed not only when extracted samples were independently reinspected, but also when all operators inspected each other's filter samples. This is evident in the hypothesis test results (well above a p-value of 0.05) that were obtained throughout the studies and the multiple qualification cohorts. On the other hand, reproducibility of output data with replicate extractions or between each operator's sample sets was not easily demonstrated.

Through multiple investigatory design-of-experiments and review of the findings with the operators involved, specific factors that contributed to providing what was initially thought to be inexplicable variability in the particle counts were identified. The inherent lack of homogeneity of particle and fiber deposition on the surface of elastomeric components that are packaged within a bag or collectively transported in multiple cartons from the same batch was one of the underlying root causes for the inconsistencies of the initial data gathered. As a result, managing sample bag to sample bag variability proved to be one of the largest factors in obtaining sufficiently low CV values. Repetitive handling, sampling, and shipping greatly increases variability and localization of particles within the bags.

Results from the final transportation and qualification study demonstrated that seeding particles was not necessary to generate counts that are sufficiently high to yield meaningful statistical evaluation if unwashed samples are used instead of RTS product.

Although several studies revealed relatively good comparability within replicate extractions for individual operators within a supplier's testing team, consistent agreement within the range of values for particles or fibers was not demonstrated across all organizations. Counting technique for particles and fibers applied to sets of replicate extractions yielded good comparability between local operators for all three suppliers. This comparability between local operators for all three suppliers demonstrates that the newly developed method is a robust and repeatable procedure.

Key Concepts

Attribute Agreement Analysis: A statistical method for evaluating whether multiple inspectors make consistent categorical judgments (such as "pass/fail") on the same set of samples. Design of Experiments (DOE): Systematically varying multiple factors to find out which have the greatest effect on the result. Through DOE, the study team identified uneven particle deposition and shipping vibration as the main sources of variation.

RTS (Ready-to-Sterilize): Stoppers that have been washed but not yet sterilized. Because RTS products have already been washed, their particle load is low, which makes it hard to generate enough counts for statistical analysis.

  • The mystery: Why do particle counts differ so much between operators/suppliers?
  • First round of investigation: Manual seeding of particles was tried → and the seeding process itself was found to introduce variation
  • Second round of investigation: Switching to unwashed stoppers plus serial sampling from a single bag → eliminated bag-to-bag differences

Four conclusions of Appendix A:

  2. Extraction reproducibility is the bottleneck: The distribution of particles on the stopper surface and inside the bag is naturally uneven, and shipping aggravates this effect.
  3. Training is key: Rigorous training and easy-to-follow operating instructions can effectively reduce subjectivity.
  4. Unwashed samples are better than manual seeding: Using unwashed stoppers with a naturally higher particle load is more practical and better reflects real conditions than adding standard particles manually.

8.2 Appendix B. Method: Determination of Visible Particles and Fibers on Elastomeric Components by Membrane Filtration and Microscopic Examination

Original Text

Introduction

The purpose of this method is to provide a procedure to quantify the number of visible particles present on elastomeric closures used for injectable pharmaceutical products. The procedure describes how the elastomeric closures are to be sampled from the fully assembled package, then rinsed to release any particles present, and subsequently sized and counted using a stereomicroscope.

Scope

This method is applicable to all RTU and RTS elastomeric closures but only applies to visible particles. The method requires that such closures be tested from fully assembled final packaging to reflect how they are received and used by the pharmaceutical manufacturer.

Fiber-like particles (i.e., length to width aspect ratio of ≥5) have significantly less surface area than a sphere of equivalent diameter to the fiber length. This results in significantly less detection capability for a 100 μm long fiber as compared to 100 μm diameter sphere. To ensure this analytical method is detecting visible particles only, fiber-like particles have been given a larger visible size threshold of 300 μm.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

RTU (Ready-to-Use): Stoppers that have been washed and sterilized and can be used directly for aseptic filling.

RTS (Ready-to-Sterilize): Stoppers that have been washed but not sterilized; the pharmaceutical manufacturer must sterilize them after receipt.

Fully Assembled Package: The method requires sampling from the final packaged state. This is very important because it reflects the true particle state of the product as the pharmaceutical manufacturer actually receives it.

The 300 μm threshold for fibers: The surface area of a fiber-like particle (aspect ratio ≥5) is much smaller than that of a spherical particle of the same length. A fiber 100 μm long is visually harder to detect than a sphere 100 μm in diameter. The "visible" threshold for fibers is therefore set at 300 μm, more lenient than the 100 μm for particles.

Analogy

Imagine placing on a sheet of white paper a lead bead 1 mm in diameter and a piece of thread 1 mm long but only 0.2 mm wide. The bead is easy to see because of its larger area, while the thread, although equally long, is too thin to be noticed easily. A fiber therefore needs to be "longer" to reach the same visibility as a particle, which is why the fiber threshold is set at 300 μm rather than 100 μm.

Key Notes

Two key limitations on the scope of the method:

  • Applies only to visible particles; it does not cover subvisible particles (<100 μm)
  • Applies only to elastomeric closures; glass containers have a separate method (see the other appendices of this report)

Preparation for the test

  1. Using cleaned forceps, rinse gridded, then un-gridded side of filter for 20 sec per side and place on filter holder.
  2. Place clean funnel on filter holder.
  3. Fill 50 mL of Polysorbate 80 solution (as defined as Reagent 1 above) in a clean graduated cylinder.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Blank Testing: Running the complete test procedure without adding any stopper samples. The purpose is to confirm that the entire test system (rinse liquid, equipment, environment) does not introduce extraneous particles. A blank must be run both before and after the sample tests.

In-line Filtration: Before the prepared Polysorbate 80 solution is dispensed into the test vessels, it passes through a filter of ≤1 μm to ensure that the rinse liquid itself is free of particles.

Blank acceptance criterion: No particles or fibers >100 μm may be observed. This is a "zero tolerance" criterion: no background particles are acceptable.

Analogy

A blank test is like running the dishwasher empty before real cooking: with no dishes, you run one full wash cycle and then check whether the water that comes out is clean. If impurities are found in the water after the empty run, the dishwasher itself needs cleaning, and in that case the data from the dishes you wash cannot be trusted either.

Key Notes

The devil in the operating details:

  • Wiping direction: "overlapping straight lines, away from the operator". Wipe in a deliberate outward direction rather than at random, so that particles are not carried back toward the operator's working area.

  • Double rinse: Steps 3-6 must be repeated once (2 × 50 mL = 100 mL of rinse liquid in total) to ensure complete extraction.
  • Subsequent blank fails → everything invalid: If the blank run after completion of the sample series does not pass, all sample results are considered invalid and must be repeated.

Sample Testing Procedure

Original Text

Sample testing procedure

The use of the terms clean or cleaned below refer to tools that have been rinsed with clean and filtered water prior to use.

  1. Using cleaned forceps, rinse unprinted, then printed side of filter for 20 sec per side and place on filter holder.
  2. Place clean funnel on filter holder.
  3. Open the sample bag carefully and transfer 100 cm² of parts to a clean Erlenmeyer flask.
  4. Fill 50 mL of Polysorbate 80 solution into a graduated cylinder.
  5. Pour the 50 mL of Polysorbate 80 solution into a clean Erlenmeyer flask or accurately add 50 mL of Polysorbate 80 solution directly to the Erlenmeyer flask through dosing.
  6. Shake the flask on the orbital shaker at the appropriate RPM for 20 sec.
  7. Decant the solution from the flask into the funnel.
  8. Repeat steps 4-7.
  9. Discard stoppers.
  10. Rinse the inside of the Erlenmeyer flask thoroughly with water (as defined above as Reagent 2) and pour rinsate into the funnel.
  11. Rinse the inside of the funnel thoroughly with water (as defined above as Reagent 2).
  12. Remove the funnel and with clean forceps, place the filter in a clean petri slide.
  13. Dry the filter by placing in the laminar air stream with lid ajar at least until the filters have dried.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

100 cm² of parts: The sample quantity is defined by surface area rather than count, because the amount of adhering particles is proportional to the contact area. Stoppers of different sizes come in different numbers, but the total surface area stays the same, so results can be compared across different sizes.

Differences from the blank procedure:

  • Blank: no stoppers are added; all other steps are the same
  • Sample: step 3 adds the placement of the stoppers, and step 9 adds the discarding of the stoppers
  • The rinse is likewise 2 × 50 mL (steps 4-7 repeated once)

Analogy

This procedure is like "washing dishes" in a cleanroom:

  1. First prepare a clean "strainer" (steps 1-2)
  2. Put the "dishes" (stoppers) into the "washing basin" (Erlenmeyer flask), add the "dish soap" (PS80 solution) and shake for 20 seconds (steps 3-7)
  3. Wash a second time (step 8)
  4. Throw away the "dishes" (step 9): what we want is not the dishes but the "dirty water" that was washed off
  5. Rinse the "washing basin" and the "funnel" with clean water to make sure all particles are transferred to the filter (steps 10-11)
  6. Remove the filter, air-dry it and wait for examination (steps 12-13)

Key Notes

Key differences and pitfalls in operation:

  • The order of filter rinsing sides differs: The blank procedure rinses the gridded side first and then the un-gridded side; the sample procedure rinses the unprinted side first and then the printed side. These subtle differences must be recorded accurately in the SOP.
  • Open the bag "carefully": Rough opening of the bag may generate extra particles (such as plastic debris), or cause particles on the stoppers in the bag to be dislodged to places they should not go.
  • The final rinse uses pure water (Reagent 2), not the PS80 solution: The rinse in steps 10-11 is meant to recover particles remaining on the equipment, so pure water is sufficient.

Filter Evaluation

The calibration of the stereomicroscope should be verified daily prior to use using a micrometer that is calibrated at the operational magnification. Use suitable, direct illumination (e.g., from a goose-neck lamp, light emitting diode [LED] light source recommended) with angles set between 0 and 10° from the vertical viewing axis.   • Fibers: >300 μm in length (to be determined using the rectangle template)

Calculations

Use the following equation to calculate the number of visible particles per 10 cm² for both particles and fibers.

visible particles / 10 cm² = (visible particles counted on filter × 10) / (number of parts used × surface area per part)

visible fibers / 10 cm² = (visible fibers counted on filter × 10) / (number of parts used × surface area per part)

Report the number of visible particles/10cm² and visible fibers/10cm².

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reticle: A calibrated template mounted in the microscope eyepiece, containing circular and rectangular shapes. The operator compares the observed particles with the templates:

  • A particle must exceed the boundary of the circular template to be counted as >100 μm
  • A fiber must exceed the boundary of the rectangular template to be counted as >300 μm

Aspect Ratio: Length divided by width. A particle with an aspect ratio ≥5 is classified as a fiber and uses the 300 μm threshold; one with an aspect ratio <5 is classified as a particle and uses the 100 μm threshold.

Illumination angle 0-10°: Nearly vertical illumination gives the best contrast for particles on the grey filter and reduces interference from shadows.

Formula

Interpreting the result reporting formula:

Visible particles/10 cm² = (number of visible particles counted on the filter × 10) ÷ (number of parts used × surface area of each part)

Worked example:
  Assume: 20 mm stoppers, each with a surface area of about 5 cm²
  Sampling: 100 cm² ÷ 5 cm²/stopper = 20 stoppers
  Counted on the filter: 15 visible particles

  Visible particles/10 cm² = (15 × 10) ÷ (20 × 5)
                  = 150 ÷ 100
                  = 1.5 particles/10 cm²

Note: the numerator is multiplied by 10 to normalize the result
to the unit "per 10 square centimeters", which makes it easier to
compare stoppers of different sizes.

Analogy

The reticle works like the "size reference card" in a customs officer's hand: baggage (a particle) is "stopped" (counted) only if it is larger than the frame on the card. Anything smaller than the frame is waved through (not counted as a visible particle). The circular frame corresponds to ordinary particles and the rectangular frame to fibers, each with its own "interception threshold".

Key Notes

Significance of standardized result reporting:

  • Reporting per 10 cm² allows results for stoppers of different sizes (13 mm, 20 mm, 28 mm, etc.) to be compared directly.
  • This differs from the pharmacopoeial particle tests, which report per container or per mL. For closure particles, a surface-area basis makes more sense because particles adhere to the surface.
  • Microscope calibration must be verified before use every day. This is a basic GMP requirement that ensures the accuracy and traceability of the measurements.

Practical Application

CDMO incoming inspection scenario: Your company has just received a batch of 20 mm RTU stoppers, and the client requires visible particle testing according to the TR85 method. Your QC team needs to:

  1. Confirm that the stereomicroscope has completed its calibration for the day (using a calibration slide micrometer)
  2. Complete the pre-test blank (pass with zero particles)
  3. Take 100 cm² of stoppers from the fully assembled package (about 20 stoppers of 20 mm)
  4. Perform the two 50 mL PS80 rinses + filtration
  5. Count particles (>100 μm) and fibers (>300 μm) at 50x under the microscope using the reticle
  6. Calculate the result as "X particles/10 cm²"
  7. Complete the post-test blank to confirm that the system is clean

The whole process takes about 45-60 minutes and is an important part of routine incoming quality verification.

Key Concepts

Probability of Detection (PoD): The probability that an inspector correctly identifies and reports a known particle under defined conditions. PoD = 1.0 means a 100% detection rate, and PoD = 0 means no detection at all. PoD is affected by multiple factors, including particle size, material contrast, container type and inspector experience.

High-contrast particles (such as black rubber particles in a clear container) are easier to detect, while low-contrast particles (such as clear glass fragments in a glass container) are extremely hard to see. This is the most fundamental physical limitation of visual inspection.

Analogy

Key Notes

This indicates that the basic skills of visual inspection transfer across settings, provided the inspection environment is standardized.

Practical significance of the material effect:

  • Clear glass fragments (100-200 µm) in glass containers have the lowest PoD, which makes this the most dangerous combination
  • Gray rubber and metal particles also have low contrast
  • Black or orange rubber particles are relatively easy to detect
  • White cellulose fibers have a much lower PoD than brown hair or white plastic fibers

Practical Application

Consider the following strategies:

  • Extend the inspection time per unit for large-volume containers
  • Increase the frequency of inspector breaks to avoid eye fatigue
  • Add supplementary automated inspection for high-risk material combinations (clear glass fragments + glass containers)

Main Effects - Statistical Analysis

Original Text

Main Effects. Using the Boruta method (33) the data were analyzed for the significance that each variable has on the resulting PoD. This method is based on a machine learning technique called Random Forest. Random Forest computes the importance of predictor variables vs a response variable. It repeats this process and generates a distribution of importance for each predictor variable. It then sorts the predictor variables based on their importance and statistically compares them to random (shadow) variables to categorize them as:

  • Rejected: variable has significantly less predictive importance than random variables.
  • Confirmed: variable has significantly more predictive importance.
  • Tentative: variable cannot be categorized as either rejected or confirmed when computation stops.

Figure C10 Boruta Plot of inspection study results demonstrating factors significant to reliable inspection performance.

As a result, important variables are those that can play a significant role in predicting the response variable. This is similar to significant predictors in Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) but fails to satisfy usual assumptions of parametric models.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Boruta Method: A feature selection algorithm based on Random Forest. Its distinctive feature is the use of "shadow variables" as a benchmark: each real variable is shuffled to create a random version, and the predictive importance of the real variables is then compared with that of the random ones. If a variable's importance is consistently higher than that of the shadow variables, it is classed as Confirmed, a meaningful predictor.

Random Forest: A machine learning method that makes predictions by building a large number of decision trees and averaging them. It does not require assumptions such as normally distributed data (a non-parametric method), so it is better suited than traditional ANOVA to handling complex multi-factor interactions.

Analogy

The Boruta method is like the blind audition format of a talent show. Each contestant (variable) is compared not only with the other contestants but also with a group of "random performers" (shadow variables). Only contestants who consistently outperform the random performers advance (Confirmed). Those who do worse than the random performers are eliminated (Rejected), and those in between are "undecided" (Tentative). This ensures that the factors finally selected genuinely have predictive power rather than being statistical artifacts.

Key Notes

Why not use traditional ANOVA? ANOVA assumes conditions such as normally distributed data and homogeneity of variance. Visual inspection data, however, are usually binary (detected/not detected) and involve complex interactions. As a non-parametric method, Boruta/Random Forest is not bound by these assumptions and can identify the truly influential factors more accurately.

This is also one of the more advanced statistical applications in PDA technical reports, and shows that the industry is bringing machine learning tools into traditional quality analysis.

Original Text

Conclusion

Based on statistical analysis of the valid data from the empty container inspection study, the main variables influencing the PoD were determined to be particle size, inspector, container, and particulate type. Further, particle size is a significant factor as well, with lower PoD observed for smaller particles.

Material providing poorer contrast against the backgrounds produced lower PoD values. This was more significant when examining fibers where cellulose had a statistically lower PoD than brown hair and plastic. These results are consistent with previous visual inspection studies with filled containers.

Finally, the container size influenced the PoD, with larger containers (with a greater surface area) effectively reducing the time per unit surface area available to inspect the product and resulting in reduced detection rates for smaller particles.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four key variables affecting PoD:

  • Particle Size: The smaller the particle, the harder it is to detect. This is the most intuitive influencing factor.
  • Inspector: Individual differences remain even under standardized conditions.
  • Container: The size and type of the container affect the inspection time available per unit area.
  • Particulate Type: The optical contrast of the material determines its visibility.

Key Notes

Consistency with filled-container studies: The conclusions of this study (empty containers) are consistent with the results of earlier visual inspection studies on filled containers. This means that the basic physical and human factors affecting PoD are universal and do not change depending on whether the container is filled.

Implications for specification setting: Acceptance criteria for visible particles in empty containers should take these inherent detection limits into account. For low-contrast materials (such as glass fragments) or large-volume containers, tighter process controls may be needed, because the reliability of visual inspection itself is lower.

Analogy

In short, visual inspection is like playing "spot the difference" under varying conditions: the smaller the object, the harder it is to find; the larger the area to search, the easier it is to miss something; the more the object resembles its background, the harder it is to notice; and everyone's eyesight is different. These four factors together determine your probability of "finding" it. Understanding these limitations is what allows you to design a more effective quality control strategy.

8.4 Appendix D. Method: Visual Inspection of Empty Glass Containers

Original Text

Introduction. The purpose of this method is to provide a procedure to identify and quantify the number of visible particles present on empty glass containers used for injectable pharmaceutical products. The procedure describes the inspection conditions and how the containers should be presented to the inspector.

Scope. This method is applicable to all empty glass containers. Inspection times may need to be increased for larger (>100 mL) containers.

Equipment and materials.

  1. Inspection cabinet with matte black and white backgrounds with diffuse lighting of 2,000-3,750 lux at the point of inspection.

Glass container inspection procedure.

  1. Remove a container from the uninspected tray.
  2. Hold the sample approximately 10 cm directly below the lamp at a distance approximately 30 cm from the inspector's eyes.
  3. Inspect the sample for 5-10 sec against the black background. Rotate and invert the container to ensure all areas are examined adjusting your grip on the container as needed.
  4. Inspect the sample for 5-10 sec against the white background. Rotate and invert the container to ensure all areas are examined adjusting your grip on the container as needed.
  5. Place each piece examined into the either the reject tray or passing tray as required.
  6. Complete any necessary records documenting the inspection of each piece before moving to the next inspection.

The time spent per container may be controlled using a pacing device. Recording the time spent inspecting each batch and then calculating the nominal inspection rate is a reliable method to confirm that the rate of inspection was maintained within the defined limits.

Information recorded during or after inspection may include details such as the type and location of any particles observed, and any other defects observed. On completion of the inspection, the data should be evaluated against the established acceptance criteria to determine the disposition of the batch. During inspection, the operator should avoid direct viewing of the light source and take regular eye breaks to avoid eye strain which may adversely influence the results of the inspection.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Diffuse Lighting: Illumination that is spread evenly and produces no harsh reflections or shadows. The range of 2,000-3,750 lux is the verified optimal inspection illuminance: too dim and particles cannot be seen clearly, too bright and glare and eye fatigue result.

Matte Black and White Backgrounds: The black background is used to observe light-colored or transparent particles (such as glass fragments and white fibers), and the white background is used to observe dark particles (such as black rubber and metal fragments). The matte surface avoids reflective interference.

Pacing Device: A tool that controls the inspection rate and ensures that each container receives enough inspection time. This can be a simple timer, a metronome, or an indicator light built into the inspection station.

Analogy

Imagine you are a gemologist: you have a dedicated appraisal station (inspection booth) with steady lighting (diffuse lighting), you view each gem once on black velvet and once on white paper (black and white backgrounds), you hold the gem at a specific distance and angle (10 cm below the light, 30 cm from the eyes), and you keep rotating and inverting it. You spend at least 10-20 seconds on each gem and record everything you find. Visual inspection is essentially this kind of precise observation work.

Key Notes

Key operating details:

  • Standardized distances: 10 cm from the light and 30 cm from the eyes ensure consistent illuminance and viewing angle.
  • Inspection against both backgrounds: Each container must be inspected once against the black background and once against the white background, because the visibility of particles of different colors differs greatly between backgrounds.
  • Rotation and inversion: Every surface of the container must be inspected, including the bottom and the neck/mouth.
  • Eye rest: Avoid looking directly at the light source and take regular breaks so that eye fatigue does not impair judgment. This is an important human factors consideration.

Large containers: For containers larger than 100 mL (>100 mL), the inspection time needs to be extended. This is fully consistent with the findings of Appendix C: a larger surface area needs more time.

Practical Application

Practical recommendations for establishing an SOP:

  • Illuminance verification: Verify with a light meter, at regular intervals (at least monthly), that the illuminance at the inspection point is within the range of 2,000-3,750 lux.
  • Operator qualification: All inspectors need to pass a vision test (such as the Jaeger or Snellen test) and be retested periodically.
  • Pacing control: Use a timer or conveyor speed to control the inspection time for each container, to prevent quality from dropping because of schedule pressure.
  • Environmental control: The inspection area should be free of interference from external light sources, with noise distractions kept to a minimum.
  • Rotation schedule: Rotate inspectors every 30-60 minutes, or at least provide an eye rest.
  • Data tracking: Record the inspection time, reject rate and particle type distribution for each batch as the basis for trend analysis.

The flowchart describes the following decision process for filter-mounted samples:

  1. Use defined magnification and reticle to locate particulate on filter.
  2. Determine if the particle is Fiber-Like:

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reticle: A measuring graticule mounted in the microscope eyepiece. For particle detection, the reticle contains a red circle (to assess particle size) and a red box (to assess fiber size). A particle that is completely enclosed by the circle or box (not touching the boundary) is regarded as "smaller than" that size and is not counted.

Filter-Mounted Sample: A method in which the rinse liquid is filtered and the membrane is then mounted on a glass slide for microscopic examination. It is one of the standard methods for quantitatively assessing the number and type of particles in a container.

Analogy

Imagine measuring height: if a person stands under a door frame without touching the top of the frame (contained inside), he is shorter than the frame (not counted). But if his head touches or exceeds the frame (touching the border), he has reached or exceeded that height (counted). The red circle and box of the reticle are this "door frame", used to screen which particles are large enough to need recording.

Key Notes

The "Fiber-Like" decision in the flowchart is the first key decision. Fibers and particles use differently shaped measuring tools (box vs. circle) because their geometries are completely different. Misclassification leads to the wrong measurement criterion being applied, which directly affects the count.

Section Summary

  • Test kit design: Particles of known size and type are fixed in containers, and three equivalent kits are made to eliminate bias. Outliers are removed before use.
  • Study scale: 11 companies, 55 inspectors and 59,400 data points (of which 49,360 were valid), making it one of the largest capability studies of visual inspection of empty containers in the industry.
  • Four influencing factors: Particle size > inspector variation > container type/size > particulate material type, all confirmed by Boruta/Random Forest statistical analysis.
  • Optical contrast is the key: Clear glass fragments in glass containers have the lowest PoD, and white cellulose fibers are also clearly hard to detect. High-contrast materials (black/orange rubber) are relatively easy to detect.
  • Container size effect: The surface area of a 30 mL vial is about three times that of a 1 mL syringe, which reduces the inspection time per unit area and lowers PoD. Large containers need longer inspection times.
  • Standard operating procedure (Appendix D): Diffuse lighting at 2,000-3,750 lux, 5-10 seconds each against the black and white backgrounds, 10 cm from the light and 30 cm from the eyes, and regular eye rest.
  • Reticle assessment flow (Figure B1): First decide whether the item is a fiber or a particle, then use the corresponding red box/circle to assess its size; items that do not touch the boundary are not counted.
