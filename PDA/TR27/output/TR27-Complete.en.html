Preface Introduction Integrity Through Product Life

Preface

This report reviews issues of pharmaceutical product package integrity and provides guidance for evaluating the barrier qualities of a pharmaceutical package. It supersedes the previously issued "PDA Technical Information Bulletin No. 4, Aspects of Container/Closure Integrity."

In this updated guideline, the term "product package" is used, rather than the term "container/closure system," as it encompasses any nonporous barrier system that protects a pharmaceutical product from the environment. Similarly, the term "package seal" is used generically to mean all types of seals or closure systems.

Although it is written to reflect the complexity of all pharmaceutical products and packages, the emphasis throughout the document is clearly on packaging intended for sterile products.

"This document should be considered as a guide; it is not intended to establish any mandatory or implied standard."

Commentary

The Historical Significance of TR27

PDA TR27, published in 1998, is one of the earliest systematic guidance documents on pharmaceutical package integrity. It replaced the earlier "Technical Information Bulletin No. 4" and marked an important milestone as the industry moved from scattered guidance to a mature framework.

Although TR27 was published in 1998, its core concepts (container closure integrity, CCI, and assessment across the product life cycle) remain the basis of regulatory expectations from the FDA, EMA, USP and others today.

Analogy: Why "product package" rather than "container/closure system"?

Picture a glass vial of sterile injectable: the vial body (container) + the rubber stopper (closure) + the aluminum cap (seal) together form one complete barrier system. TR27 uses the broader term "product package" because some products (such as prefilled syringes and flexible bags) have no traditional container/closure boundary but still need their barrier quality evaluated.

Key Point: Not a Mandatory Standard

TR27 states explicitly: "This document is a guide and is not intended to establish any mandatory or implied standard."

This matters: TR27 is industry best practice (GMP guidance), not regulatory text. However, its methodology has been cited in FDA 483 observations and warning letters, so in practice it carries considerable weight.

Core Terminology

  • Package Integrity: the ability of a package to maintain its barrier function
  • Container/Closure Integrity (CCI): the ability of the container and closure system together to prevent leakage or microbial ingress
  • Seal: broadly, any closure mechanism, including rubber stoppers, heat-sealed films, aluminum caps and so on
  • Sterile Product: the primary focus of this document

Section 2: Introduction

Package integrity definition, container functions, and regulatory context

2. Introduction

Product quality can be defined in terms of the ability of a product to perform its desired functions despite the stresses to which it has been exposed before and during its intended use.

Functions of Pharmaceutical Container Systems

Pharmaceutical container systems may have a variety of functions, including:

  • Facilitation of product use
  • Prevention of improper use
  • Light protection
  • Containment of the product
  • Maintenance of a microbial barrier
  • Maintenance of a rarefied product environment (e.g., vacuum or inert gas headspace)

Assurance of package integrity is primarily concerned with:

  • Product containment
  • Maintenance of an effective barrier against ingress of microorganisms
  • Maintenance of a barrier against potentially reactive gases such as oxygen or water vapor
  • In some cases, maintenance of a vacuum
"The information provided in this guideline is intended to assist users in developing integrity assessment strategies for use during the phases of product life."

Commentary

What Is "Package Integrity"?

Package integrity is the ability of a package system to continuously maintain its intended barrier function throughout the product life cycle. This definition has several key dimensions:

  • Barrier direction: two-way. It prevents the contents from leaking out (containment) and prevents external contaminants from entering (ingress barrier).
  • Time dimension: the barrier function must be maintained from manufacture to the end of shelf life.
  • Stress tolerance: this includes stresses such as shipping vibration, temperature cycling and sterilization processes.

Regulatory Drivers: Why Is CCI Essential?

For sterile products, a leak or seal failure directly threatens product sterility. The main regulatory requirements include:

  • 21 CFR 211.94: container closure systems must protect the drug product and be compatible with it
  • USP <1207>: package integrity testing of sterile products (updated in 2016, citing the TR27 methodology)
  • EU GMP Annex 1 (2022): explicitly requires CCI testing as part of sterility assurance
  • ICH Q8/Q9: package system selection should be based on risk assessment

Analogy: CCI Is Like a Building's Waterproofing Layer

The waterproofing membrane of a building is invisible and goes unnoticed day to day, but if it fails, the whole building is damaged. In the same way, container closure integrity is the invisible gatekeeper of sterile product quality: the package may look intact, yet a microscopic leak path could let microorganisms in and endanger patient safety.

3. Integrity Assurance Through Product Development And Product Life

Product package integrity assessment begins during a product's development phase and continues throughout its life cycle, encompassing routine manufacturing and shelf-life stability assessments.

The Phases of a Product's Life Cycle

  • 3.1 Package design, process development, and durability assessment
  • 3.2 In-process control
  • 3.3 Product testing

Further reconfirmation of product package integrity should occur when there are major changes in the package system that may affect its integrity, including:

  • Package design changes
  • Package materials changes
  • Changes in manufacturing processing conditions, including sterilization

The following subsections contain information on the critical factors for assessing a product's package integrity at each phase of development and production.

Change Management Triggers Re-confirmation

TR27 states explicitly that the following changes require CCI to be re-confirmed:

  • Package design changes (such as switching to a different vial type or changing the stopper specification)
  • Packaging material changes (such as changing the rubber formulation or glass material)
  • Process condition changes (especially sterilization conditions, since the thermal stress of steam sterilization may affect seal integrity)

This corresponds directly to the modern regulatory requirement for change control: any change that could affect CCI must be supported by a complete revalidation plan.

Analogy: Life Cycle Assessment Is Like Automotive Safety Testing

A car's safety is not tested only when it leaves the factory. It is verified at three stages: the design stage (crash simulation), the manufacturing process (weld quality inspection) and the finished product (full-vehicle crash test). CCI assessment follows exactly the same logic: the earlier a seal problem is found, the lower the cost of correcting it.

3.1

Package Design, Process Development, and Durability Assessment

Package Design, Process Development and Durability Evaluation

Original Text

Package integrity quality assurance involves the selection of appropriate packaging materials, the design of the seal or closure system, and the verification that the incoming components meet specifications. Establishing leakage rate specifications may be desirable early during product development based on the particular product and package performance characteristics to ensure that the product will reflect these limits.

Packages may be closed via either seals or closures. By definition, a seal is the result of the bonding of two similar or dissimilar surfaces. Examples of seals include:

  1. Contiguous containers formed from a single material (e.g., glass or plastic ampoules);
  2. The direct fusion of two material surfaces, usually by heat (e.g., heat-sealed plastic bags or unit-dose blister packaging);
  3. The bonding of two material surfaces with an intermediate adhesive agent.

A closure, on the other hand, is the physical mating of two surfaces. In the strictest sense, all closures are tortuous path barriers. The degree to which a closure can limit leakage is a function of the closure design, the viscoelastic nature of the package components, and the extent of package component fit. For instance, an elastomeric rubber-stoppered sterile vial is capable of achieving a very low gaseous leakage rate due to the viscoelastic nature of the rubber and its ability to conform to the vial surface upon compression.

There are critical factors that impact the quality of each package seal. Such factors typically include package component variables of composition, dimension, and critical defects; and sealing operation variables of time, temperature, pressure, and torque. The impact of processing on package integrity must also be characterized. Package component washing, drying, siliconization and sterilization, and product processing such as terminal sterilization may all impact the ability of the package seal to maintain its effectiveness.

During the package design and process development phases, the acceptable ranges for each critical variable should be established based on package performance criteria that incorporate integrity testing. Wherever feasible, the variables of design and processing should be exaggerated and their impact on package integrity evaluated to help define the margin of safety. Stability studies extending through shelf-life must also include integrity tests of the packaged product.

The assurance of product quality depends on the ability of the product to maintain integrity throughout the stresses anticipated during storage, distribution, and use. Relevant types of stress may include temperature, pressure, and relative humidity extremes, shock and vibrational stress, and exposure to microorganisms. The durability assessment may include exposure of the product to simulated conditions coupled with appropriate leakage detection methodology.

"When greater time and effort are spent on package integrity verification during the design and process development phases, fewer problems will likely be encountered after commercialization."

Commentary

Package Integrity Lifecycle Phases

TR27 divides package integrity quality assurance into three sequential phases that complement one another:

  • Phase 1: Package design and process development — material selection, seal design, specification setting, durability evaluation
  • Phase 2: In-process control — monitoring of critical process parameters, in-line integrity verification
  • Phase 3: Product testing — final product sampling and testing, indirect integrity indicators

The more effort invested at the design stage, the fewer problems after commercialization. This is the core philosophy of TR27.

Seal vs. Closure

Seal: the joining of two surfaces (fusion or adhesion) so that they become one continuous whole, for example ampoules, heat-sealed pouches and blister packs.

Closure: the physical fit of two surfaces, which is essentially a "tortuous path barrier", for example stoppered vials and screw-cap bottles.

A rubber stopper can achieve a very low leak rate because the viscoelastic nature of rubber lets it conform tightly to the vial neck under compression, automatically filling microscopic gaps.

Critical Process Variables

Key variables that affect package seal quality include:

  • Component variables: material composition, dimensions, critical defects
  • Sealing operation variables: time, temperature, pressure, torque
  • Processing effects: washing, drying, siliconization, sterilization (including terminal sterilization)

In validation studies, each critical variable should be deliberately pushed to its extremes to define the margin of safety.

Real-World Analogy

A rubber stopper closure works like the rubber gasket on a refrigerator door: when the door closes, the rubber is compressed and deforms to fill the gap around the frame, creating a near-airtight enclosure. If the rubber ages (material degradation) or the door frame warps (dimensional deviation), the seal fails. Package integrity works on the same principle.

3.2

In-Process Control

In-Process Control

Original Text

The processes that have been determined during product development to most critically impact package integrity should be those that are targeted for in-process control. Performance criteria for in-process controls are typically based on the performance of a specific unit operation rather than the performance of the final product. For example, in-process controls and tests may be used to statistically verify that the time, temperature, and pressure variables of a sealing operation are "in control" as opposed to final product testing used to demonstrate that microbial barrier or rarefied internal atmosphere criteria have been met.

During validation studies it must be demonstrated that a process "in control" yields only products that meet the established leakage rate specifications.

For a limited number of products, instrumentation exists that permits high-speed 100% on-line verification of package integrity at the established leakage rate specifications. But for many sterile products, in-process leak test instruments have limitations that preclude high-speed, on-line integrity verification at the required product leakage rate specifications. In-process leak test instruments are designed to check for intermittent defective packages leaking at a gross rate that circumvented the various in-process controls.

There are a variety of logical, validatable in-process integrity tests that can help verify product/package integrity. Examples include:

  • Vision systems to cull out defective components;
  • Gas flow rate meters installed on ampoule sealing lines;
  • Time/temperature/pressure controls on thermal sealing equipment;
  • Torque monitors on screw-capping equipment.

These on-line in-process controls, in combination with well-designed product/process validation studies, are an essential part of the total package integrity quality assurance. However, no amount of in-process or final product testing can replace the need for the control and monitoring of various critical package and sealing parameters.

Commentary

Core Logic of In-Process Control

The goal of in-process control is to verify that the "process is in control", rather than to verify final product quality directly. The logic chain is:

Critical process variables under control
(Time, Temp, Pressure, Torque)
↓
Validation studies confirm "controlled process = conforming product"
↓
Product meets the leak rate specification

Limitations of In-Line Integrity Testing

For many sterile products, in-line leak test instruments cannot reach the sensitivity needed for the product's leak rate specification. These instruments can only detect gross leaks and are used to reject obviously defective units, not to provide quantitative integrity verification.

Key concept: no test can replace the monitoring and control of critical process parameters. Testing is the last line of defense, not the first.

Shop-Floor Analogy

In-process control is like a real-time temperature alarm on a production line: even if you cannot measure the seal strength of every finished unit in-line, as long as the heat-seal temperature is always held at 180±5°C, the validation data have already shown that product made under this condition will meet specification.

3.3

Product Testing

Product Testing

Original Text

A product that has been properly and carefully designed with critical production processes well characterized and controlled should have little need for final product release testing for package integrity. Certainly the quality of a pharmaceutical product cannot merely be based on the results of a product integrity test, especially when such tests are usually performed on a fraction of the production lot.

Sterility tests have historically been considered an indicator of package integrity; however, due to the insensitive nature of sterility tests, the small number of samples tested, and the possibility of false positives, these results are no longer considered reliable as a final indicator of package integrity.

Generally, integrity test methods selected for final product testing are designed to verify the absence of gross leaks well above permeation rate levels. For those package systems that can be tested 100% on-line with instruments shown to measure near or below the leakage rate specification, there is no need for additional product integrity testing. For other products, a statistically established percentage of the batch may be integrity tested for in-house process control purposes.

Indirect tests of package integrity may also be employed on production lot samples. Examples of indirect measures include:

  • Visual inspection;
  • Residual seal force of a serum vial with elastomeric closure;
  • Seal strength of an adhesive or thermal seal.

The acceptance criteria for such tests should be based on the critical variable acceptance ranges established during the development phases of package design and process development.

Commentary

Sterility Testing Is Not Integrity Verification

TR27 states clearly that the sterility test was historically regarded as an indicator of package integrity but is no longer considered a reliable final indicator, for the following reasons:

  • Insufficient sensitivity: a sterility test can only detect microorganisms and cannot quantify leak rate
  • Too few samples: the sampling rate is far too small to represent the whole batch
  • Risk of false positives: handling contamination may lead to incorrect results

Indirect Integrity Tests

Indirect tests do not measure leakage directly; they infer seal quality from surrogate indicators:

  • Residual Seal Force (RSF): measures the compressive force the stopper exerts on the vial neck, reflecting seal integrity
  • Seal Strength: peel or tensile testing, suitable for heat seals or adhesive seals
  • Visual inspection: detects visible defects; the least sensitive method but the most commonly used

The acceptance criteria for these indirect tests must be traceable back to the acceptable ranges of critical variables established during the design stage.

Package Integrity and Leak Rate Specifications

Package Integrity and Leak Rate Specifications

Original Text

A leak is an unintended crack, hole, or porosity in a package capable of passing a liquid or gas through the package. Leakage is the movement of the liquid or gas through the leak. Leakage is defined in units of mass or volume flow of a gas or liquid over time under defined conditions of temperature and pressure.

Leakage is often described in units of standard cubic centimeters of gas (air) that can move through an orifice per second (std cc/s or scc/s) under defined conditions of temperature and differential pressure. Similarly, leakage units of atm cc/s are also used to define leakage measured at local atmospheric pressure and temperature. While the two are not identical, units of scc/s and atm cc/s are often used interchangeably. Another common unit of measure is the SI unit of Pa m³/s (Pascal cubic meters per second). One Pa m³/s is equal to 9.87 scc/s or roughly 10 scc/s.

Leak Rate Scale Reference

Strictly speaking, all packages demonstrate leakage to some extent, although such leakage may not affect product quality. Therefore it is important to establish an allowable leakage rate specification for a given product/package system early during product development.

For sterile liquid products: containers must prevent minute liquid product leakage and microbial ingress. The leakage rate specification limit should be the same as the cut-off for liquid passage, as microorganisms are believed incapable of penetrating a leak smaller in size than can be penetrated by liquids. Water is generally thought to be incapable of penetrating pores that allow less than approximately 10−3 scc/s of airflow at a differential pressure of approximately 1 atmosphere. This relationship between gaseous flow, liquid leakage, and liquid-borne microbial ingress was demonstrated by L. Kirsch et al. and Morton, 1989.

Leakage rate may also be expressed in alternative terms: product weight loss or gain, rise of percent oxygen in inert gas-flushed headspace, or volume of dye that has migrated through a package seal. Such descriptions must clearly and meaningfully define mass or volume flow over time, and are as acceptable as gas leak rate units.

Commentary

Leak Rate Unit Conversion

Three common leak rate units and how they relate:

1 Pa m³/s ≈ 9.87 scc/s ≈ 10 scc/s

std cc/s (scc/s)
  = volume of gas (cm³) passing through the opening
    per second under standard conditions (101.3 kPa, 20°C)

atm cc/s
  = measured at local atmospheric pressure
  ≈ scc/s (often used interchangeably, though strictly different)

Pa m³/s (SI unit)
  = international standard unit; converts exactly to 9.87 scc/s

Rule of thumb: Pa m³/s ≈ 10 × scc/s, which can be used directly for engineering estimates.

Leak Rates and Physical Phenomena

TR27 provides an intuitive leak rate scale to help build a sense of magnitude:

  • 1 scc/s: audible sound — a gross leak visible to the naked eye
  • 0.1 scc/s: visible liquid flow — detectable by visual inspection
  • 10−3 scc/s: critical leak size — the boundary below which water cannot pass, and also the boundary below which microorganisms cannot pass
  • 10−11 scc/s: detection limit of the helium mass spectrometer — currently the most sensitive leak test method

Critical Leak Size

10−3 scc/s is the most important benchmark value in package integrity:

  • Water (liquid) cannot pass through a pore that allows an air flow of <10−3 scc/s (at a pressure differential of about 1 atm)
  • Microorganisms (carried in liquid) also cannot pass through a defect smaller than the critical size for liquid penetration
  • Therefore, the upper limit of the leak rate specification for sterile liquid products, sterile powders and lyophilized products is usually set at or below 10−3 scc/s

Scientific basis: Kirsch et al. and Morton (1989) experimentally established the relationship between gas flow, liquid leakage and liquid-borne microbial ingress.

Alternative Ways to Express Leak Rate

Not every leak has to be expressed as gas flow; the following alternatives are equally acceptable:

  • Weight loss/gain: for example, "product loses X grams per year when stored at 35°C for 2 years"
  • Rise in headspace oxygen concentration: used for inert gas-filled packages
  • Volume of dye migration: used for dye ingress tests

Key requirement: the relationship between mass or volumetric flow and time must be clearly stated, and the test conditions must be specified.

5.1

Test Method Sensitivity

Test Method Sensitivity

Original Text

In selecting a leak test method it is important to choose a test which is capable of detecting the leakage rates of concern. The leakage rate of concern will depend on the product/package system as well as the phase of the product life cycle in which the test will be utilized.

Most physical leak test methods have an optimum sensitivity range, meaning that a method capable of detecting leaks between 10−6 scc/s and 10−7 scc/s may not be appropriate for gross leaks of 100 scc/s. Some physical test methods have a "gross" and a "fine" leak test mode in order to expand this sensitivity window.

However, it is inappropriate and can be quite costly to select a test method that is too sensitive. For instance, a helium leak test capable of detecting leaks as low as 10−11 scc/s would be an inappropriate test for a high density polyethylene screw-capped bottle merely intended to retain tablets and capsules. But the converse is also true: an airborne microbial challenge test would be meaningless for a product with a gaseous leakage rate specification of 10−7 scc/s.

Because leak test methods have a range of sensitivity, and because the leakage rate of concern may differ depending on the stage of the product's life cycle, it may be necessary to test a given product using more than one leak test method, each designed to detect leaks of varying magnitude. A package containing a lyophilized powder undergoing shipping simulation studies may need to be tested for larger leaks resulting from shock or vibration damage, while long-term stability samples of the same product may need to be checked for leaks indicative of gaseous leakage and permeation.

A test method's sensitivity can be expressed in various units of measure. Optimum sensitivity of a method can only be realized through careful selection of the test parameter conditions. Examples:

  • Oxygen analyzer: sensitivity expressed as smallest detectable change in headspace oxygen content;
  • Dye test: sensitivity expressed as smallest volume of dye detectable under stated conditions;
  • Helium mass spectrometry: sensitivity expressed as smallest measurable leak rate in scc of helium per second.

Commentary

Excessive Sensitivity Is Also a Problem

TR27 explicitly warns that an overly sensitive method is likewise inappropriate and costly.

  • Using a helium mass spectrometer on an HDPE screw-cap bottle (for tablets) = using a sledgehammer to crack a nut, a waste of resources
  • Using a microbial challenge test for a product with a leak rate specification of 10−7 scc/s = meaningless (the sensitivity of microbial tests is far lower than this specification requires)

Principle: when selecting a method, its sensitivity should match the product's leak rate specification; neither too high nor too low is correct.

Analogy: Thermometer vs. MRI

Choosing an overly sensitive test is like using an MRI for routine temperature measurement: powerful, but completely unsuited to the occasion and extremely costly. Likewise, test sensitivity must match the actual risk level of the product.

5.2

Other Test Method Selection Considerations

Other Considerations in Test Method Selection

Original Text

A wide variety of leak test methods are available for the evaluation of pharmaceutical packages. The usefulness of a physical method must be determined on a product-by-product basis by evaluating:

  • The ability of a method to test the seal of interest;
  • The sensitivity of the instrument or method;
  • Any relevant sources of measurement variation.

In some cases, factors such as data storage, analysis and display functions, statistical manipulation of data, practicality, speed, safety, on-line integration feasibility, and expense may play important roles in the selection of the most appropriate leak test method.

Sterile package integrity has traditionally been verified by airborne or liquid immersion microbial challenge tests. Microbial challenge tests have a long history of use and may be an indicator of microbial package integrity. Section 6 reviews both the microbial challenge approach and the alternate physical testing approach for verifying a package's microbial barrier properties.

Section 8 provides summaries of several of the most commonly used physical methods for package integrity testing.

Commentary

A Comprehensive Framework for Test Method Selection

Besides sensitivity, TR27 lists the following factors to consider when selecting a leak test method:

  • Technical suitability: whether the method can effectively detect leaks in that seal type
  • Sources of measurement variability: including operator, environment and instrument precision
  • Data management: whether it supports data storage, statistical analysis and display functions
  • Practicality and speed: whether it suits production line needs (high-speed vs. off-line)
  • Safety: toxicity of reagents, safety of high-pressure equipment
  • Feasibility of in-line integration: whether it can be integrated into existing production equipment
  • Cost: equipment investment, consumables, validation cost

Physical Tests vs. Microbial Challenge Tests

Traditionally, sterile package integrity was verified by microbial challenge tests (aerosol or liquid immersion methods). The modern trend, however, is to replace probabilistic microbial challenges with deterministic physical tests:

  • Microbial challenge test: has a history of use and indirectly reflects microbial barrier effectiveness; however, its sensitivity is limited and results are affected by variables such as microbial concentration and exposure time
  • Physical test: quantifies leak rate, is repeatable and verifiable, and integrates well into the quality system; FDA and USP <1207> tend to support replacing microbial challenge with physical testing

Practice Question 1

A sterile lyophilized powder for injection (vial with rubber stopper and aluminum cap) is scheduled for package integrity validation. At which stage is a high-sensitivity physical test method capable of detecting down to the leak rate specification most needed?

  • A. Rejecting gross leakers on the commercial production line
  • B. Package design verification and stability studies
  • C. Routine visual inspection sampling
  • D. Incoming inspection of supplier components

Answer: B — Design verification and stability studies must confirm that the packaging system stays below the leak rate specification, so a high-sensitivity test method that reaches the specification level is required. Commercial production lines (A) can usually rely on gross leak detection.

Practice Question 2

A product has a leak rate specification of 10−3 scc/s (sterile liquid product). Which of the following statements is correct?

  • A. For a package with a leak rate below 10−3 scc/s, liquid water can still pass through and carry in microorganisms
  • B. For a package with a leak rate above 10−3 scc/s, liquid water cannot pass through and microorganisms cannot enter
  • C. At a leak rate below 10−3 scc/s, water cannot pass through and microorganisms cannot enter via liquid
  • D. 10−3 scc/s is the detection limit of the helium mass spectrometer

Answer: C — Based on the Kirsch et al. study cited in TR27: water cannot pass through a pore that allows an air flow of <10−3 scc/s (pressure differential ~1 atm), and microorganisms (carried in liquid) cannot pass either. Therefore 10−3 scc/s is the critical leak size for both the liquid barrier and the microbial barrier. The detection limit of the helium mass spectrometer (D) is 10−11 scc/s, and options A and B are both incorrect.

All sterile pharmaceutical packages must prevent the ingress of microorganisms. This section focuses on liquid immersion and airborne microbial challenge test methods and alternate physical test methods useful for establishing a package's microbial barrier properties.

In subsection 6.1 the rationale for selecting a microbial challenge test versus a physical test method is discussed. Subsections 6.2 and 6.3 describe how one may directly verify a package's microbial integrity through either liquid immersion or airborne microbial challenge tests.

In subsection 6.4 an outline is presented describing how physical leak tests may be validated as an alternative indicator of package microbial integrity. Commentary

Key Question for This Section

A sterile package has only one fundamental purpose: to prevent microbial ingress. Section 6 offers three routes to demonstrate this:

  • Liquid immersion microbial challenge - directly simulates ingress from a liquid environment
  • Airborne microbial challenge - directly simulates ingress from a gaseous environment
  • Physical leak test (with correlation established) - an indirect, surrogate method

Analogy: Why Are Three Methods Needed?

Imagine you want to confirm that a door is truly sealed. The most direct way is to put pests outside the door and see whether they can crawl in (= microbial challenge test). But that is time-consuming and labor-intensive. The alternative is to use smoke to check the size of the gap around the door (= physical test), provided you already know how big a gap lets pests through (= correlation).

Key Terms Preview

  • Direct Correlation: the same batch of samples is subjected to both microbial and physical testing to identify the critical leak rate
  • Indirect Correlation: a critical leak rate known from the literature is cited, and the physical method is verified to have adequate sensitivity

6.1 Microbial Challenge vs. Physical Test Methods

Criteria for Choosing Between Microbial Challenge Tests and Physical Test Methods

Original Text

For most sterile pharmaceutical packages, the leakage rate specification is that which will prevent the ingress of microorganisms. Such packages must therefore be validated as providing an effective barrier against either airborne and/or liquid-borne microbial ingress. The microbial barrier properties of a package may be demonstrated using either microbial challenge tests or physical tests that have been correlated to microbial ingress.

Reasons to Choose Microbial Challenge Tests

  1. No reasonable alternative: Certain closures or packages that rely solely upon a tortuous path to prevent airborne microbial ingress may not be reliably tested by a physical method.
  2. Package design incompatibility: A package may not tolerate the relatively high differential pressures required for testing by vacuum decay or tracer gas techniques.
  3. Limited resources or expertise: The manufacturer does not have the resources or expertise required for developing and validating an alternate physical test method.
  4. Limited distribution product: An extensive physical test method validation program cannot be justified.

Reasons to Choose Physical Tests

Conversely, a physical test may be the preferred approach for microbial barrier package validation. If a sufficiently sensitive physical test method is available that is compatible with the product-package system, it is often more economical in the long run.

Physical tests can be selected that yield qualitative or quantitative results or that can pinpoint the location of leaks. Microbial challenge tests are relatively insensitive, yield inconsistent results, are labor intensive, necessitate extensive storage space for sample incubation, and require long test times.

Another disadvantage is that microbial challenge tests must often be done using media-filled packages rather than packages containing the intended product.

Physical tests are the more logical choice when the leakage rate specification for a product/package system is significantly lower than the limit for microbial ingress. For example, a given sterile glass vial closed with an elastomeric stopper may require a leakage rate specification of 10‑7 std cc/s to maintain an inert gas headspace. The gaseous leakage rate that correlates to the ingress of liquid-borne microorganisms for this package system has been shown to be approximately 10‑5 std cc/s when tested by helium mass spectrometry (Ref. Kirsch, et al).

Commentary

Analogy: Leak Rate Specification vs. Microbial Ingress Rate

Take a glass vial with a rubber stopper as an example:

  • Leak rate specification needed to maintain an inert gas headspace: 10⁻⁷ std cc/s
  • Critical leak rate for liquid-borne microbial ingress: 10⁻⁵ std cc/s (Kirsch et al.)

Because the specification is already 100 times tighter than the microbial critical value, passing the physical test automatically establishes the microbial barrier, and no additional microbial challenge test is needed.

Decision Logic for Selection

Prerequisites for choosing a physical method: (1) the method has sufficient sensitivity; (2) it is compatible with the product-package system; (3) a correlation to microbial ingress (direct or indirect) has been established. If all three cannot be met at the same time, fall back to the microbial challenge test.

6.2 Liquid Immersion Microbial Challenge Tests

Liquid Immersion Microbial Challenge Test

Original Text

There is no standard liquid immersion microbial challenge test, but the following guidelines are suggested for the establishment of a meaningful and useful method.

  • Select a small bacterium that is demonstrated to yield a high population of viable organisms at the end of the study.
  • Whenever possible, positive controls that demonstrate growth must be included. These may consist of defective packages or packages prepared outside the package assembly process control limits.
  • A statistically representative number of samples should be tested.
  • When packages vary in size, but not in design or package component materials of construction, worst case container sizes are to be evaluated, or, preferably, sizes should be bracketed.
  • If more than one seal is relied upon for package integrity, each seal should be challenged.
  • The position of the container during the integrity test should ensure that the seal is exposed to the challenge microorganisms.
  • The media, incubation, and challenge conditions of time and pressure must be logically chosen keeping in mind critical integrity variables such as package component materials, assembly, processing parameters, and challenge organism viability and morphology.
  • Whenever reasonable, the product itself should be challenged, as it is possible the product may influence seal integrity. However, this may not be feasible based on the product's inability to support microbial growth.

Commentary

Key Point: Why Is a Positive Control Needed?

The positive control confirms that the test method is valid, i.e., that the challenge organism can actually grow in the culture medium and that the test conditions are able to detect a defective package. If the positive control fails to show growth, the entire test result is invalid.

6.3 Airborne Microbial Challenge Tests

Airborne Microbial Challenge Test

Original Text

There are many references describing airborne microbial challenge test procedures published by the food and medical device industries. Several of these are listed in the references at the end of the Microbial Challenge Aerosolization Test Method summary. It is recommended that such references be utilized in establishing an airborne microbial challenge test for pharmaceutical packages.

Very briefly, some critical factors in the design of such tests include:

  • Location and position of the microbial challenge source versus the test articles
  • Methodology used to aerosolize the microbial challenge
  • Size and morphology of the challenge organisms
  • The size, shape, and configuration of the test chamber

There are many variables that affect the outcome of airborne microbial challenge tests. The key to performing consistent airborne challenges is design of the aerosolization apparatus with sufficient fine adjustments and system monitors (for example, pressure gauges, flow meters) so that variation between runs and/or operators is minimized.

Commentary

Analogy: Airborne vs. Liquid Challenge

Liquid immersion test = soaking the container in a bacterial suspension to see whether the suspension penetrates the seal interface. Airborne test = placing the container in a space filled with suspended microorganisms to see whether airborne organisms can pass through the seal path into the container. The latter simulates an exposure scenario that is more common in actual storage and use environments.

Key Point: Variability Control Is Critical

The biggest challenge of the airborne test is reproducibility. Aerosol concentration, particle size, and chamber airflow all affect the results. A precise monitoring system (pressure gauges, flow meters) is therefore needed, and batch-to-batch consistency must be demonstrated during method validation.

Key Design Points of the Airborne Challenge Test

The airborne challenge test simulates ingress of airborne microorganisms and is particularly suitable for tortuous path closures, such as crimped aluminum tubes or screw caps.

6.4

6.4 Physical Leak Test Detection Methods

Overview of Physical Leak Test Methods

Original Text

Unlike microbial challenge tests, physical leak test methods do not directly measure microbial ingress, but rather, they measure some physical property of the leak that may be related to microbial failure. The relationship between the sensitivity of a physical leak test method to the probability of microbial failure can be either directly or indirectly determined.

Commentary

The Nature of Physical Test Methods

Physical leak tests measure the "physical characteristics of the leak" (such as gas flow rate, pressure decay, or tracer permeation) rather than counting microorganisms directly. Therefore, when a physical test is used as a surrogate indicator of the microbial barrier, a correlation to microbial ingress must be established.

This correlation can be established in two ways: 6.4.1 Direct Correlation and 6.4.2 Indirect Correlation.

6.4.1

6.4.1 Direct Correlation to Microbial Ingress

How to Establish Direct Correlation

Original Text

Typically the development of a direct correlation between a physical integrity test and microbial ingress first requires the creation of representative acceptable and defective packages that can be tested by both leak test methods. Then it must be experimentally demonstrated that the measured leak rate by the physical method can be quantitatively correlated to the probability of microbial failure.

To illustrate, the following results would need to be demonstrated by direct correlation of a liquid chemical tracer test to a microbial immersion test:

  1. The presence of tracer in all test units that show microbial ingress,
  2. The presence of tracer in some test units that fail to show microbial ingress,
  3. The absence of tracer in some test units that fail to show microbial ingress.
  4. The absence of microbial ingress in all of the test units that do not show tracer.

A direct correlation of tracer to microbial ingress would need to take into consideration the potential interference of the tracer with microbial growth, and the potential interference of microbial growth on tracer detection.

Liquid chemical tracer tests provide simple pass-fail results; either the tracer is present or it is not. If a physical test method is used that provides a range of leakage rate results, correlation to microbial ingress is based on the rate of leakage determined by the physical method versus the probability of microbial failure.

Commentary

Rationale for Establishing Direct Correlation

Direct correlation requires that the same batch of package samples undergo both of the following:

  1. Physical testing (e.g., a liquid chemical tracer test)
  2. Microbial challenge testing (e.g., an immersion test)

By cross-comparing the two sets of results, the quantitative relationship between the leak rate measured by the physical test and microbial ingress is confirmed.

Interfering Factors to Watch For

  • Tracer interfering with microbial growth: some chemical tracers have bacteriostatic activity, which may cause false negatives
  • Microorganisms interfering with tracer detection: microbial growth may block pores or alter the physical properties of the liquid

Four Outcomes That Must Be Demonstrated (Tracer vs. Microorganisms as an Example)

Note: Scenario 4 (no tracer detected but microbial ingress occurs) is the greatest risk to establishing a direct correlation and must be shown by testing not to occur.

6.4.2

6.4.2 Indirect Correlation to Microbial Ingress

How to Establish Indirect Correlation

Original Text

A physical test method can be indirectly correlated to microbial ingress based on documented evidence of microbial ingress within the physical method's leakage rate detection range. Again, for a sterile glass vial with elastomeric stopper, it has been demonstrated that the helium leak rate (under specific testing conditions) that corresponds to microbial ingress as determined by rigorous immersion challenge testing is 10‑5 std cc/s (Ref. Kirsch, et al). This information can be used to verify the microbial ingress leak detection capability of another physical method by demonstrating that the second method can reliably detect leakage at or below the documented leakage rate of about 10‑5 std cc/s.

It is important to keep in mind that the leak test gas and the test conditions of temperature and differential pressure used for the referee test (in this case the helium test) may differ significantly from those employed by the alternative physical method. Therefore it may be necessary to calculate an equivalent leakage rate against which the alternative physical leak test method must be compared.

Indirect correlation of a physical test method to liquid-borne microbial challenge tests can also be made by demonstrating that the physical method is capable of detecting microscopic liquid passageways near the limit of microbial ingress. This relies on the assumption that without a passageway large enough to permit liquid flow, microbial ingress cannot occur. For example, a dye or liquid chemical tracer can be used as an indirect indication of microbial package integrity. In order to rely on such results, the sensitivity of the dye or liquid chemical tracer test must be optimized in order to approach the leakage limit for liquid ingress.

Commentary

Direct Correlation (6.4.1)

Approach: perform physical and microbial tests on the same batch of samples

  • Acceptable and defective samples must be manufactured
  • Directly identifies the critical leak rate
  • Heavy workload and high cost

Advantage: directly demonstrates the correlation, with no reliance on literature assumptions

Indirect Correlation (6.4.2)

Approach: cite a known critical leak rate and verify that the new method can detect that rate

  • Cite Kirsch et al.: 10⁻⁵ std cc/s
  • The new method must demonstrate adequate sensitivity
  • Equivalent leak rate must be calculated

Advantage: saves the effort of repeating microbial testing

The Concept of Equivalent Leakage Rate

Different physical methods use different test gases, temperatures, and pressure differentials. For example:

  • Reference method (helium mass spectrometry): uses helium at a specific temperature and pressure differential
  • Alternative method (e.g., vacuum decay): uses air under different temperature/pressure differential conditions

The numbers therefore cannot be compared directly. The equivalent leakage rate must be calculated from the gas molecular weight, viscosity, and test conditions, and it must then be confirmed that the sensitivity of the alternative method covers that equivalent value.

The Second Route to Indirect Correlation: Liquid Pathways

If it is assumed that "no microbial ingress occurs without a liquid pathway of sufficient size," then detecting the presence of a liquid pathway (using dye or a liquid tracer) indirectly demonstrates that the microbial barrier has failed. However, this assumption must hold, and the sensitivity of the tracer must approach the lower limit of liquid ingress; otherwise leaks will be missed.

8.1

Bubble Test

Qualitative — Visual detection of gaseous leakage

8.1 Bubble Test

Description: The package is submerged in water or other suitable clear, colorless solvent. A vacuum is exerted on the test system, and the package is examined visually for evidence of gaseous leakage. Alternatively, surfactant may be added to the seal in question, a differential pressure is applied across the seal, and evidence of gaseous leakage is detected by visible inspection.

Validation

This method determines the presence and location of leaks that allow sufficient gaseous flow to permit the formation of visually detectable bubbles in the submersion fluid or in the applied surfactant. This method can be validated for the detection or location of larger leaks or package imperfections. Validation will depend on the test method parameters and the reliability and sensitivity of visual detection.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity of the method is based on the ability of gas to move out of a leak to form visible bubbles in the submersion fluid or applied surfactant, and the ability to identify positive leak controls.

Quantitative/Qualitative: Qualitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Differential pressure exposure
  • Differential pressure rate of exposure
  • Test time
  • Submersion fluid or surfactant surface tension
  • Cleanliness of package
  • Visual inspection parameters: light intensity, magnification, background, time for inspection, visual inspector training and experience

Time to Complete

Up to several minutes.

Equipment Required

  • Vacuum or pressure source
  • Submersion fluid or surfactant
  • Submersion vacuum vessel for submersion testing
  • Pressure controls and monitors
  • Visual inspection: appropriate lighting, light meters, magnification, and color background

Stress on Package

  • Pressure differential
  • Wetting of seal or entire package

Product/Package Types

Applicable to all pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier seal, and that can tolerate wetting and pressure differentials.

Literature References: ASNT 1982; ASTM D 3078; ASTM D 4991; ASTM E515; Elder 1995; Guazzo 1996; Morton 1987; Morton et al, May-June 1989

8.1 Bubble Test — Commentary

Core Concept

The bubble test is the most intuitive way to detect a leak: the package is immersed in a clear liquid, vacuum or differential pressure is applied, and the operator watches for bubbles. A stream of bubbles indicates a gas leak path.

An alternative is to apply a surfactant (such as soap solution) over the seal area, apply differential pressure, and watch for foaming.

Real-Life Analogy

It is like finding a leak in a bicycle tire with a basin of water: submerge the inflated tire, and wherever bubbles appear is where the hole is. The principle is exactly the same.

Key Point: Why Qualitative and Not Quantitative?

The bubble test can only tell you "whether it leaks", not "how much it leaks". It is therefore classified as qualitative. The biggest variables affecting reliability are the training and experience of the visual inspector and the lighting conditions.

Limitations of Use

  • The package must be able to withstand wetting and differential pressure
  • Not suitable for packages that rely on a tortuous path or a barrier seal
  • Better suited to detecting larger leaks; sensitivity is insufficient for small defects
8.2

Helium Mass Spectrometry

Quantitative / Semi-Quantitative — Most sensitive physical leak test

8.2 Helium Mass Spectrometry

Description: Helium is used as a tracer gas for detection and measurement of leakage across a package seal. Helium is detected by mass spectrometry. There are four testing modes:

  • Tracer Probe: The package is placed in-line with the mass spectrometry unit and helium is sprayed around the outside. The mass spectrometer vacuum pump applies vacuum to the package and helium leakage into the package is measured.
  • Hood Method: The package is enclosed in a "hood" or chamber into which helium is introduced. The mass spectrometer vacuum pump applies vacuum to the package and helium leakage into the package is measured.
  • Detector Probe: The package is flooded with helium and a detector probe connected to the mass spectrometer is used to sample the atmosphere around the package perimeter. The package is not under vacuum in this case.
  • Inside-Out: The package is flooded with helium (by penetrating the package, flooding prior to sealing, or "bombing" — exposing the sealed package to pressurized helium). The package is then placed in a test chamber directly connected to the mass spectrometer and subjected to vacuum.

Validation

Helium mass spectrometry methods can be validated for the detection, location, and measurement of gaseous leaks and helium permeation. Instrument sensitivity can be demonstrated using calibrated helium leaks. Positive control package leaks can be used in some cases. Validation studies should examine the ability of the leak test method to accurately measure gas leakage in the package system.

Sensitivity

Helium leak detection methods are potentially the most sensitive indicators of package leakage and permeation. Sensitivity is based on the ability to accurately detect and measure helium leakage/permeation rate. General mass spectrometry system parameters that impact sensitivity include:

  • Volume of vacuum system
  • Pressure in the vacuum system
  • Pressure in the sensing element of the mass spectrometer
  • Response time of the mass spectrometer
  • Effective pump speed of vacuum pump system
  • Ratio of the total gas flow of the vacuum pump system to the total gas flow of the mass spectrophotometer leak detector

Quantitative/Qualitative:

  • Quantitative: Hood method and Inside-Out method
  • Qualitative or Semi-Quantitative: Tracer Probe method and the Detector Probe method

Critical Test Parameters

All methods:

  • Package testing mode; test time duration; temperature of package
  • Percentage of tracer gas surrounding the test boundary
  • Location, length and size of connection between tester and vacuum system
  • Helium background within the vacuum system
  • Cleanliness, surface area, surface finish and material composition of test boundary
  • Stability of electrical supply to the mass spectrometer

Hood and Inside-Out methods additionally:

  • Concentration of the tracer gas in the package or hood
  • Bombing time; bombing pressure
  • Degassing of the package surface (post bombing dwell time or air wash)

Tracer and Detector Probe methods additionally:

  • Specialized operator training, experience, technique
  • Surface scanning speed
  • Differential pressure across the package
  • Helium gas concentration within the test boundary
  • Distance the probe or sniffer is held from the surface
  • Length and diameter of sniffer probe lines
  • Build-up of helium in the background atmosphere

Time to Complete

Method dependent. Several hours may be required for bombing. Once helium challenge conditions are established, the actual test time is a few seconds.

Equipment Required

  • Helium mass spectrometer with vacuum system
  • Package test fixture or probe
  • Helium source
  • Vacuum (Tracer Probe, Hood and Inside-Out methods)

Stress on Package

Vacuum is applied to packages in Tracer Probe, Hood, and Inside-Out methods.

Product/Package Types

  • Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier material and that are required to maintain a very low leak rate specification.
  • Inside-Out: Hermetically sealed containers with a defined internal volume that can withstand external vacuum.
  • Tracer Probe: Items that can withstand a vacuum of 0.1 Pascal (10⁻⁵ torr).
  • Detector Probe: Items where the effluent side of the leak is accessible with the probe.
  • Hood method: Packages that can be evacuated.

Literature References: ASNT 1982; ASTM E 493, E 499, E 908, E 948, E 1603; Elder 1995; Gilchrist et al 1985, 1989; Guazzo 1994; Hoffmann 1996; Kirsch et al 1997; Rhea et al 1984; Varian Vacuum Products 1995

8.2 Helium Mass Spectrometry — Commentary

Why Use Helium (He)?

  • Helium is one of the smallest inert gas molecules and has extremely high penetrating ability
  • The helium content of the atmosphere is very low (about 5 ppm), so background noise is minimal
  • It is non-toxic, non-flammable, and does not react with the package or product
  • Mass spectrometers have very high detection sensitivity for helium

Explaining the Helium Leak Rate

Helium mass spectrometry can quantify the "leak rate", usually expressed in:

atm·cc/sec (atmosphere cubic centimeters per second)
or Pa·m³/s (pascal cubic meters per second)

For example, 1×10⁻⁷ atm·cc/sec is a very small leak, meaning only a tiny amount of helium passes through the defect each second. This value can serve as the basis for a seal specification limit.

Explaining Bombing (Helium Pressurization)

The "bombing" technique is commonly used in the Inside-Out method: the sealed package is exposed to a high-pressure helium environment for several hours so that helium penetrates into the package through any defect. The package is then removed and placed in a vacuum chamber, and the amount of helium escaping from the package is measured, which gives the leak rate. Bombing time and pressure are critical parameters.

Sensitivity Ranking

Among all physical leak tests, helium mass spectrometry has the highest sensitivity. It can detect tiny defects that are completely invisible to the naked eye, and it is one of the gold standards in the pharmaceutical industry for the most demanding seal specifications (such as ampoules, prefilled syringes and freeze-dried vials).

8.3

High Voltage Leak Detection (HVLD)

Qualitative — Near-instantaneous detection for conductive liquid-filled containers

8.3 High Voltage Leak Detection (HVLD)

Description: Containers are exposed to a high frequency, high-voltage current near the area of the package to be detected for leaks. The presence of a leak will result in a flow of current above base noise detected by the HVLD system as a reject. This test method is only feasible for containers constructed of relatively nonconductive materials and for product that is significantly more conductive. The product must be at or very near any potential leaks to allow conductance to occur.

Validation

HVLD methods can be validated for the detection of leaks in product/package systems meeting the conductivity requirements stated above. Validation studies should examine the ability of HVLD to detect control leaks as a function of:

  • Voltage level and detection sensitivity settings
  • HVLD probe positioning
  • Package wall thickness and geometry
  • Product formulation variations

Sensitivity

HVLD can detect leaks large enough to permit liquid penetration or wetting. HVLD sensitivity must be demonstrated during validation studies for each product/package system to ensure the detection of all control leaks with a minimum number of false positives.

Quantitative/Qualitative: Qualitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • High voltage setting
  • Product conductivity
  • Sensitivity setting (Gain)
  • Test area cleanliness
  • HVLD probe positioning
  • Cleanliness of the package
  • Package geometry
  • Access of product to the leak
  • Package conductivity

Time to Complete

Test time is almost instantaneous.

Rapid, short exposure to a voltage.

8.3 High Voltage Leak Detection (HVLD) — Commentary

How HVLD Works

HVLD detects package leaks using a high-frequency, high-voltage electric field. It works on the following premises:

  • The package material (such as glass or plastic) has low electrical conductivity
  • The product inside the package (such as a saline-containing liquid) has high electrical conductivity
  • If there is a hole, the conductive liquid contacts the electric field and produces a current spike, which the system identifies as a defect

HVLD Special Note — Liquid-Filled Containers

HVLD is the only near-line or in-line leak test method designed specifically for liquid-filled containers. Its greatest advantages are:

  • Testing is almost instantaneous and can be integrated into a high-speed filling line (100% in-line inspection)
  • No need to destroy the package or use a tracer gas
  • Best suited to injectable solutions (containing salts) in glass or plastic containers

Limitation: HVLD is not applicable if the product is an oily or low-conductivity liquid. During validation, the effect of formulation changes on sensitivity must be confirmed.

Analogy

Imagine the package is an insulating shell filled with conductive salt water. The HVLD probe applies an electric field "net" around the outside. Normally current cannot pass through the shell; but if there is a crack and the salt water touches it, it "short-circuits", the current surges, and the alarm sounds.

Voltage and Product Stability Considerations

TR27 specifically reminds users to evaluate the effect of high voltage on product stability during validation (for example, protein drugs or charge-sensitive molecules), to ensure that the test itself does not damage product quality. This is a required item in an HVLD validation protocol.

Lid Deflection

Qualitative — Monitors flexible lidding movement under differential pressure

8.4 Lid Deflection

Description: To perform lid deflection tests a pressure differential is applied to a package and the resultant movement of the package's flexible lidding material is monitored and used as an indication of the absence or presence of unacceptable levels of leakage. Lid deflection sensors can work by any of a variety of methods, including:

  • Capacitance sensors
  • Inductance sensors

  • Photoelectric sensors
  • Ultrasonic pulse echo sensors

Validation

Lid deflection methods can be validated for the detection of the absence of lidding material or for the detection of gaseous leaks large enough to result in minimal or no flexible lidding material displacement when a package is subjected to a reference differential pressure. Sensitivity

The sensitivity of lid deflection methods is based on the ability to accurately detect flexible lidding material movement when the package is subjected to a standard differential pressure. Sensitivity will largely depend on the method for monitoring lidding movement and the capabilities of the particular sensor device selected. Generally, lid deflection tests are used as an indicator of larger leaks. This method is sometimes coupled with pressure/vacuum decay leak tests in order to provide both a "gross" and a "fine" leak test detection range.

Quantitative/Qualitative: Qualitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Lidding movement sensor accuracy and sensitivity
  • Differential pressure applied to package
  • Package headspace
  • Package component geometry

Time to Complete

A few seconds.

Equipment Required

Differential pressure.

Product/Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier seal and that can tolerate a differential pressure applied to the seal area. The test can often be used instead of vacuum/pressure decay for packages that tend to deform under pressure.

8.4 Lid Deflection Test — Commentary

Core Concept

Lid Deflection Test: differential pressure is applied to packages with a flexible lid (such as Petri dishes, heat-sealed trays and flexible packaging). On a properly sealed package, the lid shows a measurable deflection under pressure; if there is a leak, gas escapes through the defect, the lid cannot establish the normal pressure differential, and the deflection is insufficient → the package is judged defective.

Variety of Sensor Technologies

Several physical principles are available for lid deflection sensors:

  • Strain gauge: measures minute deformation of the material
  • Capacitive / inductive: non-contact measurement of distance change
  • Ultrasonic pulse-echo: the most precise; can detect very small displacements
  • Photoelectric: measures displacement using light interruption or reflection

Sensor selection directly affects sensitivity, so the validation protocol must be specific to the sensor used.

Analogy

Imagine a sealed balloon: when you press it lightly with a finger (applying differential pressure), a well-sealed balloon bulges evenly; if the balloon has a hole, the pressure dissipates and you do not feel the expected counter-pressure. Lid deflection follows the same logic: it measures the "response force" to judge the seal condition.

Use in Combination with Pressure/Vacuum Decay

The lid deflection test is sensitive to gross leaks and is usually combined with pressure decay or vacuum decay to provide two-tier screening ("gross leak + fine leak"). It is especially suitable for packages that deform easily under pressure (on which conventional pressure decay tests give false positives).

8.5 Liquid Tracer Test — Commentary

Analogy

It is like smearing a colored liquid on a pipe joint: if the joint leaks, the color seeps through to the other side. The liquid tracer test makes this concept precise: choose a tracer substance that can be detected in trace amounts, and quantify the amount that penetrates.

Why Is It Usually Qualitative Rather Than Quantitative?

Visual observation of dye penetration can only establish "presence or absence", but if a spectrophotometer is used to quantify the concentration of tracer that has entered, the result becomes semi-quantitative or quantitative. TR27 therefore says "generally qualitative" to express this flexibility: it depends on the precision of the detection method.

Key Considerations in Tracer Selection

  • Compatibility: the tracer must not react with the package material or the product
  • Surface tension / viscosity: affect the tracer's ability to penetrate a defect
  • Photostability: for fluorescent dyes, consider whether they fade under strong light
  • Cleaning effectiveness: after testing, ensure the tracer is completely removed and does not contaminate the product

Microbial Challenge, Aerosolization

Qualitative — Airborne microbial challenge for tortuous path / barrier seals

8.6 Microbial Challenge, Aerosolization

Description: Microbial challenge testing by aerosolized microorganisms involves placing product-filled or media-filled package units into a specially designed aerosolization chamber and charging the atmosphere within the chamber with a nebulized cloud or aerosol of microorganisms for a specified period of time. During the challenge test, the packages may be subjected to differential pressure challenges. The atmosphere within the chamber must be kept within defined humidity and temperature ranges and well circulated.

After the challenge, the units are removed from the chamber. If media-filled product is used (or product which supports microbial growth), the units are then incubated and examined for microbial growth. Otherwise, the package contents are removed and exposed to media which is subsequently incubated and examined for growth.

Validation

Microbial aerosolization challenge methods determine the presence of microorganisms that have penetrated a package exposed to aerosolized microorganisms. Critical validation parameters include:

  • Concentration and uniformity of aerosolized microorganisms
  • Chamber temperature, pressure, and humidity settings and controls
  • Ability of the media or product to support microbial growth
  • Positive controls should demonstrate growth

Sensitivity

The sensitivity of the method is based on the incidence of ingress of aerosolized microorganisms into positive control package defects and the ability to detect such ingress.

Quantitative/Qualitative: Qualitative

Detection limit: There must be sufficient bacterial growth for media to visibly or analytically demonstrate growth.

Critical Test Parameters

  • Media or product microbial growth promotion
  • Stable microbial concentration in the aerosol (level and uniformity)
  • Size of challenge organism
  • Electrical charge on aerosolized particle
  • Extent of microbial drying during test
  • Time of exposure to the aerosol
  • Chamber air flow patterns and speed
  • Chamber temperature and humidity conditions
  • Differential pressure exposure (optional)

Time to Complete

Exposure time: typically a few hours. Incubation time: several days, depending on the microbial challenge and incubation conditions.

Equipment Required

  • Standard microbiological laboratory equipment
  • Microbial growth support media or product
  • Airtight test chamber
  • Microbial spore nebulization unit with impinger, motor and fan
  • Temperature, pressure, and humidity controls and monitors
  • Chamber sterilization equipment
  • Air samplers

Stress on Package

  • Pressure differential (optional)
  • Challenge Organism: Typically, B. subtilis spores

Product/Package Types

Applicable to sterile pharmaceutical packages that cannot tolerate liquid immersion microbial challenge tests and that rely on tortuous path or barrier seals to ensure package integrity.

8.6 Microbial Challenge (Aerosolization) — Commentary

Core Concept: Where Aerosol Challenge Is Used

The aerosol microbial challenge method is used specifically for sterile packages that cannot be immersed in water, for example:

  • Foil pouches containing desiccant
  • Sterilization pouches made of breathable porous material (Tyvek)
  • Packages that rely on a tortuous path seal rather than a liquid-tight seal

The integrity of these packages cannot be assessed by liquid immersion tests; a gaseous (aerosol) microbial challenge must be used.

Challenge Organism: Bacillus subtilis spores

Reasons for using spores rather than vegetative bacteria:

  • Spores have a higher survival rate in a dry aerosol environment
  • Their smaller particle size better simulates the worst case
  • They resist drying well, so the concentration is more stable throughout the test

Challenge concentration and uniformity are critical to validation: the distribution of microorganisms in the chamber must be uniform, and the positive controls must show growth.

Why Does the Test Take Several Days?

The test procedure has two stages:
(1) Aerosol exposure: several hours
(2) Incubation in culture medium: 7–14 days
The incubation period gives any microorganisms that have entered the package enough time to grow to a detectable level. The overall test cycle is therefore long, and the method is not suitable as a rapid release test.

Comparison with the Liquid Immersion Method

Aerosol challenge vs liquid immersion (8.7):
The aerosol method is like putting the package in a room filled with a bacterial mist; the liquid immersion method is like dropping the package into a bacterial broth. The two have completely different applications, depending on whether the package can tolerate wetting.

8.7 Microbial Challenge (Liquid Immersion) — Commentary

Core Concept

The liquid microbial challenge test is one of the "gold standard" methods for sterile package integrity testing (especially for single-use sterile packages, vials, ampoules and other containers that can tolerate liquid immersion). A package filled with culture medium is immersed in a high-concentration bacterial suspension; if the package has a defect, bacteria enter, and a turbid medium after incubation means failure.

Basis of Detection: Turbid Medium

The acceptance criterion is very intuitive: after incubation, if the medium inside the package is turbid, bacteria have entered and multiplied → the package fails. If it is clear → the package is intact. This is an extension of the concept of the sterility test.

Basis for Selecting the Challenge Organism

The challenge organism concentration must be maintained at ≥10⁷ CFU/mL at the end of the test to ensure the challenge is sufficiently stringent.

Package Headspace Analysis

8.8 Package Headspace Analysis

8.8 Headspace Analysis — Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is Headspace Analysis Needed?

Headspace analysis: many pharmaceutical packages are filled with an inert gas (such as nitrogen N₂ or carbon dioxide CO₂) in place of the air in the container to prevent oxidative degradation. If the package leaks or permeation occurs, oxygen (O₂) gradually enters, the inert gas escapes, and the headspace composition changes.

Headspace analysis measures the gas composition in the container (especially O₂ and CO₂ concentrations) to determine whether leakage or permeation has occurred.

Common Detection Techniques

  • Laser Headspace Analysis: non-destructive; near-infrared light measures O₂ content directly through the glass vial; the most commonly used modern technique
  • Puncture sampling: the sample is analyzed by gas chromatography or an oxygen analyzer; destructive
  • Pressure measurement: indirectly infers the headspace condition

Why Is It Classified as Quantitative?

Headspace analysis can precisely measure the percentage concentration of O₂ (or other gases), compare it with a specification limit (such as O₂ < 1%), and track the trend over time. It is therefore one of the few of the nine methods classified as quantitative.

Use in Accelerated Testing

TR27 notes that packages may first be placed in a "challenge environment" (a high concentration of a rare gas, or vacuum) to accelerate the leak/permeation rate and shorten the test time. This follows the same logic as accelerated stability testing: trading pressure for time.

8.9 Particulate Transmission Test — Commentary

Core Concept

Particulate Transmission Test: the package is placed in a sealed chamber filled with an aerosol of dust (such as carbon black or talc) and removed after several hours, and the inside of the package is checked for dust ingress. It is a challenge method for packages sealed by a tortuous path or porous barrier materials (such as Tyvek).

Electrical Charge on Dust

Electrostatic charge on dust particles affects their movement: charged particles may be attracted to oppositely charged surfaces, or repel one another. The test design must consider the charge state of the dust to ensure the challenge is representative. This is an often overlooked but important critical parameter of this method.

Analogy with Microbial Challenge (8.6)

The particulate transmission test can be seen as the "non-biological version" of the microbial aerosol challenge: in both, small particles are sprayed into a sealed chamber, both apply to tortuous path packages, and both give a qualitative result (whether anything got in). The difference is that dust is cheaper and carries no biosafety risk, but the sensitivity may not match a real microbial challenge.

8.10

Residual Gas Ionization

High-voltage field ionizes headspace gas in vacuum-sealed packages — qualitative, non-destructive

Description

A high voltage, high frequency field is applied to packages sealed under vacuum. The field causes residual headspace gas to ionize. A conducting plate located near the package detects the level of ionization current. Ionization above an established limit triggers rejection of the package.

Validation

The residual gas ionization method can be validated for the detection of insufficient vacuum in packages designed to maintain a minimal vacuum level. Validation studies should examine the ability of the ionization sensors to differentiate between packages containing various known levels of vacuum as a function of voltage, frequency, or package headspace.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity is based on the ability to accurately detect pressure exceeding a desired acceptance limit in evacuated packages. Instrument sensitivity can be determined using control packages containing known pressure levels. It is possible to detect smaller leaks and permeation rates as the time interval from when the package was originally evacuated to the test time is lengthened.

Quantitative / Qualitative

Qualitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Voltage and frequency
  • Conducting plate and voltage field positioning
  • Package component ionization potential
  • Package headspace volume
  • Gaseous content of the residual headspace
  • Package component geometry

Time to Complete

Less than a second.

Equipment Required

  • Gas ionization instruments
  • Package handling systems

Stress on Package

High frequency high voltage spark. Impact of the voltage spark on product stability should be considered.

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages designed to maintain a partial vacuum in the headspace. The package tested must be relatively non-ionizing.

Literature References

Wu, 1989 • Crist, 1994

Commentary

Principle: Residual Gas Ionization

A high-voltage, high-frequency electric field is applied to a container sealed under vacuum. If residual gas is present in the package (indicating a leak or insufficient vacuum), the gas is ionized and the resulting ionization current is picked up by a nearby conductive plate. When the current exceeds the allowable limit, the package is rejected.

Analogy: Much like a fluorescent tube glowing at low pressure, gas is excited into glowing/conducting under a high electric field. The higher the vacuum (the less gas), the weaker the ionization; if there is a leak (more gas), the ionization becomes stronger.

Key Points

  • Qualitative method: gives only pass/reject; cannot quantify the leak rate
  • Non-destructive: the package remains intact and can be returned to the line
  • Applies only to vacuum-sealed packages; not suitable for positive-pressure packages
  • Very short test time (<1 second), suitable for 100% in-line inspection on the production line
  • The package material itself must not have too high an ionization potential (i.e. the material must not be too conductive)

Time-Dependent Sensitivity

Sensitivity increases with time: if a package is stored for a longer period after sealing before testing, even a tiny leak will have allowed more gas to accumulate, making it easier to detect. This means testing can be deferred for a period after packaging to improve sensitivity.

Practical Application

Commonly used for container closure integrity checks of glass vials containing lyophilized injectables. After freeze-drying the vial is under vacuum, and residual gas ionization can quickly screen out vials with insufficient vacuum.

Analytical detection of tracer gas permeating through package — quantitative, non-destructive

Description

A tracer gas is placed on one side of the package seal. An inert carrier gas is passed across the opposite seal side. Tracer gas is detected by appropriate analytical methods. Examples of analytical methods include coulometric detectors for oxygen detection and photoelectric sensors for carbon dioxide and water vapor detection.

This test is useful for the measurement of leakage and permeation from packages that require a specific gas headspace to ensure long term product stability, or for products that are susceptible to degradation upon exposure to environmental gases or vapors. While oxygen, carbon dioxide, and water vapor are typically selected for testing pharmaceutical packages, a wide variety of other tracer gases can be used as well.

Validation

This method can be validated for the detection, location, and measurement of gaseous leaks and permeation large enough to result in an unacceptable shift in headspace content over time or in product degradation. Validation studies should examine the ability of the analytical method to accurately measure gas leakage/permeation rate in the package system.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity of tracer gas detection methods is based on the ability to accurately measure tracer gas leakage/permeation rate in the package system. Instrument sensitivity can be established using reference gas mixtures of known tracer concentration. Test method sensitivity is largely dependent on the design of the test fixture.

  • Package test fixture design and dimensions
  • Temperature and humidity of the testing environment
  • Inert carrier gas temperature and humidity
  • Concentration of the tracer gas in the package

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that are required to maintain a specified leakage/permeation rate of gas through the package to ensure product stability.

Literature References

Amini, 1981 • ASNT, 1982 • ASTM E 427 • ASTM E 498 • ASTM E 1603 • Demorest, 1988, 1992, 1996 • Elder, 1995 • Gnanasekharan, 1994 • Guazzo, 1996

Commentary

Principle: Tracer Gas Leak Detection

A tracer gas is placed on one side of the seal, the other side is swept with an inert carrier gas, and an analytical instrument checks whether the carrier contains tracer gas. Typical tracer gas choices are:

  • Oxygen (O₂): coulometric detector
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂) / water vapor: photoelectric sensor
  • Other inert gases (helium, He, etc.): mass spectrometer

Key Points

  • Quantitative method: can measure the actual leakage/permeation rate
  • Non-destructive: no stress applied to the package
  • Long test time (hours to days), so not suitable for high-throughput 100% in-line inspection
  • The design of the test fixture is a key variable for sensitivity
  • Suitable for evaluating packages that require a specific headspace gas (e.g. N₂-filled lyophilized products, CO₂-filled liquids)

When to Use?

The tracer gas method is best suited when you need to confirm the gas barrier performance of the package over the entire shelf life. For example, if a lyophilized injectable requires an oxygen content of <1%, you need to verify that the permeation rate of the package over a 2-year shelf life is acceptable.

vs. Other Methods

The tracer gas method measures both leakage and permeation, whereas the pressure decay method measures leakage only. For a complete evaluation of package barrier performance, tracer gas is therefore more comprehensive, but it takes longer.

8.12

Ultrasonic Detection of Gas Flow

Detects turbulent gas escaping from pressurized packages — qualitative, non-destructive

Description

Containers under pressure are placed near an ultrasonic sensor capable of detecting acoustic energy in the ultrasonic range of 20–100 kHz. Ultrasonic sensors will detect movement of air or gasses out of package leaks large enough to create turbulent gas flow.

Smaller leaks may be detected by the application of a special surfactant to the seal. Bursting of surfactant bubbles is detected ultrasonically. There must be enough package headspace to permit detectable gas flow out of the package.

Validation

Ultrasonic gas flow detection methods can be validated for the detection and location of leaks in package systems sealed under pressure or in packages which can be "squeezed" or otherwise manipulated to create pressure inside the package. Validation studies should examine the ability of the ultrasonic sensor to detect control leaks as a function of instrument sensitivity settings, internal package pressure, package-sensor positioning, background noise, and line speeds.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity of the method is limited by the presence of turbulent flow gas leakage and the ability to detect such flow using positive control package leaks or standard gaseous reference leaks.

Qualitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Sensitivity settings
  • Background noise
  • Internal package pressure
  • Line speeds
  • Package-sensor positioning

Time to Complete

Test time is almost instantaneous.

Equipment Required

  • Ultrasonic detection instrumentation
  • Package handling system for squeezing package and/or positioning near the sensor
  • Surfactant for ultrasonic detection of bubble breakage

Possible squeezing of flexible packages. Possible surfactant wetting of seal surface.

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier seal, that contain a headspace volume, that are sealed under pressure or that are flexible enough to permit pressurization by squeezing.

Literature References

ASNT, 1982 • ASTM E 1002 • Bandes, 1988 • Elder, 1995 • Guazzo, 1997

Commentary

Principle: Ultrasonic Detection of Gas Flow

When a pressurized package has a leak, gas escaping through the leak site creates turbulent flow, which generates ultrasonic acoustic energy at 20–100 kHz. An ultrasonic sensor detects this acoustic energy to determine whether a leak is present.

For smaller leaks, a surfactant can be applied to the seal area; the bursting of leak bubbles also generates an ultrasonic signal.

Key Points

  • Qualitative method: pass/reject determination
  • Non-destructive
  • The test is almost instantaneous, suitable for in-line inspection on the production line
  • Limitation: it detects only larger leaks that produce turbulent flow; very small leaks may be missed
  • Background noise is the main source of interference and must be evaluated during validation
  • The package needs sufficient headspace, or must be able to be squeezed to pressurize it

Comparison: 8.12 vs. 8.13 Ultrasonic Methods

8.12 (gas flow detection): detects the sound of gas escaping from the leak site; requires a pressure differential and is suited to pressure-sealed packages.

8.13 (ultrasonic imaging): scans the seal interface with pulsed ultrasound to image seal defects; no gas flow is needed and it is suited to evaluating seal quality.

Applicable Packages

Flexible bags (IV bags), gas-filled blister packs, and ampoules with a positive-pressure headspace. Not suitable for containers that rely on a tortuous path seal.

8.13

Ultrasonic Imaging

High-frequency pulse imaging of seal integrity — qualitative, non-destructive

Description

Very short ultrasound pulses are generated at frequencies up to several hundred MHz. These ultrasound frequencies are focused on the container seal by acoustic lenses. Once inside the package the ultrasonic pulses may be absorbed, scattered or reflected depending on the change in the material. The reflected ultrasound echoes are used to create an image that can be analyzed for the presence of improper sealing, delamination, gaps, or product-contaminated seals.

Alternate ultrasonic inspection methods work by contact transmission of ultrasonic energy through the package seal using an array of transmitters and receivers in direct contact with the seal region.

Validation

Ultrasonic imaging methods can be validated for the detection and location of seal imperfections in flat thermal or adhesive bonded seals, for instance a pouch. Validation studies should examine the ability of the ultrasonic sensor to detect control leaks as a function of ultrasound pulse generation and transmission variables, instrument sensor sensitivity settings, transmission and detection sensor positioning, package materials of construction, and package geometry. Validation studies should reflect the range of acceptable package sealing parameters.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity is based on the ability to detect positive control package defects such as gaps, seal contaminants, package material defects, and other seal inconsistencies.

Quantitative / Qualitative

Qualitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Sensor sensitivity settings
  • Quantity of transmitters and sensors per seal surface area
  • Package-sensor and transmission positionings
  • Ultrasound frequency
  • Ultrasound pulse amplitude
  • Package materials of construction
  • Package geometry
  • Dry package testing vs. water immersion testing

Time to Complete

Test time is less than one second.

Equipment Required

  • Ultrasonic detection and imaging instrumentation
  • Package handling system for positioning packages in the sensor

Stress on Package

None.

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier seal, that contain a headspace volume, that are sealed under pressure or that are flexible enough to permit pressurization by squeezing.

Literature References

Adams, 1995

Commentary

Principle: Ultrasonic Imaging

Very short pulses of high-frequency ultrasound (hundreds of MHz) scan the seal interface. The ultrasound undergoes reflection, scattering and absorption at material interfaces (such as between the seal surface and air), and the reflected echoes form an image. Defects (such as delamination, contaminants and gaps) show abnormal reflection characteristics.

Two modes are available:

  • Reflection mode: analyzes the image of the reflected echoes
  • Transmission mode: a transmitter and a receiver sandwich the seal area, and the transmitted intensity is measured

Key Points

  • Qualitative method: pass/reject based on image interpretation
  • Non-destructive: no stress applied to the package
  • Very short test time (<1 second), suitable for the production line
  • Particularly good at detecting product-contaminated seals, which other methods find difficult to detect
  • Validation should cover both the dry test and the water-immersion test modes
  • The higher the frequency, the better the resolution, but the shallower the penetration depth

Practical Advantage

Ultrasonic imaging is one of the few methods that can visualize the location of a seal defect. It can not only say that a defect exists but also pinpoint where in the seal area it lies, which makes it especially useful for sealing process development and root cause investigation.

Applicable Packages

Pouches with thermal seals and adhesive-bonded seals. Commonly used to validate the range of heat-sealing process parameters (e.g. boundary-condition testing of temperature, pressure and time).

8.14

Vacuum/Pressure Decay (Differential)

Measures pressure change vs. reference — qualitative or quantitative, non-destructive

Description

A pressure differential is applied to a package and the change in pressure or vacuum is measured either inside the package itself using a test fixture, or outside in a sealed package test chamber. The change in test chamber pressure or vacuum can be determined by absolute pressure measurement, or it can be determined using a differential pressure manometer in which the test chamber pressure change is compared to that of a control package.

Validation

Vacuum/pressure decay methods can be validated for the detection or measurement of gaseous leaks large enough to allow a measurable change in pressure in the package or the test chamber over the duration of the test. Validation studies should examine the ability of the instrument to detect control leaks as a function of test chamber geometry, instrument sensor capabilities, differential pressure testing conditions, test time, and environmental temperature conditions.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity is based on the ability to detect and/or measure a change in pressure or vacuum resulting from gaseous leaks. Generally, pressure decay is considered a more sensitive test than vacuum rise, and differential pressure is potentially more sensitive than absolute pressure sensors.

Quantitative / Qualitative

Qualitative or quantitative, depending on the instruments and test conditions used.

Critical Test Parameters

  • Pressure/vacuum sensor mechanism of operation and capabilities
  • Volume of space being tested (test chamber or package headspace)
  • Test chamber geometry
  • Time allowed for measured pressure change to occur
  • Stabilization time
  • Differential pressure applied to package
  • Water vapor pressure inside package
  • Atmospheric pressure and temperature
  • Package creep effects when exposed to differential pressure

Time to Complete

Test time may range from less than a second to a few minutes.

Equipment Required

  • Leak test instrumentation including vacuum or pressure sensors in absolute or differential pressure sensing mode
  • Differential pressure source, monitors and controls
  • Temperature and humidity controls and monitors

Stress on Package

Differential pressure applied to package. Consider: headspace volume impact, liquids plugging leaks under vacuum, and package tolerance to differential pressure (vacuum rise tests limited to 1 atm; pressure decay may be much greater).

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier seal and that can tolerate a differential pressure applied to the seal area.

Literature References

ASNT, 1982 • Blakistone, 1995 • Chrai et al., 1994 • Elder, 1995 • Gnanasekharan et al., 1992, 1994 • Guazzo, 1994, 1996 • Morton, 1987, 1989 • Snow, 1992 • The Parenteral Society, 1985

Commentary

Principle: Vacuum/Pressure Decay (Differential)

A pressure differential is applied to the package, and the change in pressure/vacuum is measured over time. There are two measurement modes:

  • Absolute mode: directly measures the pressure change in the test chamber
  • Differential mode: compares the pressure change of the test package with that of a reference (control) package, eliminating the effect of ambient temperature fluctuations

There are four test configurations:

  • Vacuum rise: the outer chamber is evacuated while the package interior is at pressure; if there is a leak, gas enters the outer chamber and the pressure rises
  • Pressure decay: positive pressure is applied to the package; if there is a leak, the pressure falls

Key Points

  • Can be qualitative or quantitative, depending on the instrument and conditions
  • Non-destructive
  • Sensitivity ranking: differential > absolute pressure; pressure decay > vacuum rise
  • The maximum pressure differential for vacuum rise is limited to 1 atm; pressure decay can apply a larger differential
  • Liquid (or semi-solid) may plug the leak site under vacuum, causing false negatives
  • Package creep (especially in flexible packaging) can interfere with the pressure reading

Key Distinction: 8.14 vs. 8.15

8.14 (differential/decay method): applies a pressure differential from the outside and measures the pressure/vacuum change in the chamber or package. More sensitive; suited to general seal integrity checks.

8.15 (direct measurement/retention method): uses a piercing device to enter the package headspace directly and read the absolute pressure value. Suited to packages that must maintain a vacuum or positive pressure (e.g. lyophilized products).

Selection Considerations

For liquid-filled injectable vials under vacuum testing, the liquid may be drawn into the seal and block the leak, so the pressure decay method is preferable. For powders or lyophilized products, either method is applicable. The smaller the test chamber volume, the higher the sensitivity.

8.15

Vacuum/Pressure Retention (Absolute)

Direct headspace pressure measurement via piercing — qualitative or quantitative, destructive

Description

Vacuum/pressure retention tests consist of directly measuring the vacuum or pressure within a package headspace by typically using a package piercing device and an absolute pressure gauge. Vacuum/pressure retention tests are used for packages that are designed to maintain either a vacuum or positive pressure within the package as a function of product stability.

Validation

Vacuum/pressure retention methods can be validated for the detection or measurement of gaseous leaks large enough to allow a measurable change in pressure in the test chamber over the duration of the test. Validation studies should examine the ability to access the package headspace and accurately measure headspace pressure.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity is based on the ability to detect and/or measure a change in pressure or vacuum resulting from gaseous leaks. Test method sensitivity is dependent on the ability to access the package headspace without creating a loss of pressure or vacuum.

Quantitative / Qualitative

Qualitative or quantitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Pressure/vacuum sensor mechanism of operation and capabilities
  • Volume of space being tested (test chamber or package headspace)
  • Time allowed for measured pressure change to occur
  • Vacuum or pressure initially introduced to the package
  • Water or other vapor pressure inside package
  • Temperature of package and test system
  • Technique for piercing or accessing package headspace

Time to Complete

Test time is a few seconds.

Equipment Required

  • Absolute pressure gauge
  • Temperature controls and monitors

Stress on Package

Pressure or vacuum applied to access headspace.

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that are designed to maintain a positive pressure or a vacuum in the headspace over product shelf-life.

Literature References

ASNT, 1982 • Morton, 1987 • Chrai et al., 1994 • PDA, 1981 • Crist, 1994 • Rhea et al., 1984 • Gnanasekharan et al., 1992 • Wang et al., 1997

Commentary

Principle: Vacuum/Pressure Retention (Absolute)

A piercing device is inserted into the package headspace and connected to an absolute pressure gauge to read the internal package pressure directly. If the package is designed to be vacuum-sealed (e.g. lyophilized products), the measured value should be close to 0 (vacuum); if it is nitrogen-filled (e.g. a positive-pressure package), the specified pressure value should be read.

Core differences between 8.14 and 8.15:

  • 8.14: applies a pressure differential from the outside and measures the chamber change (no piercing)
  • 8.15: pierces into the package headspace and reads the absolute pressure directly

Key Points

  • Because the package must be pierced, this is generally regarded as a destructive test (some designs can be resealed, but in general it is destructive)
  • Can be qualitative or quantitative
  • Fast test (a few seconds)
  • Key challenge: the piercing process must not alter the pressure inside the package, so the design of the piercing tool is critical
  • The water vapor pressure inside the package affects the reading and must be corrected for when interpreting results

Typical Application

Confirming the vacuum quality of lyophilized injectables. For example, after freeze-drying, samples are taken and pierced to confirm that the vial vacuum is within specification (e.g. <200 mTorr), as a way of evaluating the consistency of the lyophilization process.

Caution

Because it involves piercing, this method is not suitable for 100% in-line inspection on the production line; it is usually used as a sampling test or during process development.

8.16

Visual Inspection

Human or electronic detection of product leakage — qualitative, non-destructive

Description

The package is examined visually by human or by electronic inspection devices for evidence of product leakage. The test may be accompanied by additional package challenges of temperature or pressure extremes to increase the sensitivity of the test.

Validation

Visual inspection for leakage determines the presence or location of leaks penetrated by the contained product. This method can be validated for the detection of larger leaks or package imperfections. Validation will depend on the reliability and sensitivity of the mode of leakage detection.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity is based on the incidence of product migration through positive control package defects and the ability to detect such migration. There must be sufficient product or contents leakage to permit visible or electronic detection.

Quantitative / Qualitative

Qualitative test

Critical Test Parameters

  • Package cleanliness
  • Differential pressure and temperature exposure (optional)
  • Light intensity, magnification, time, background, angle of inspection
  • Visual inspector training and experience
  • Electronic inspection parameters dependent on mode of inspection

Time to Complete

From less than a second for electronic inspection to several minutes by visual inspection.

Equipment Required

  • Temperature and/or pressure controls for optional stress testing
  • Appropriate lighting, light meters, magnification, and color background
  • Electronic inspection equipment and instrumentation

Stress on Package

Pressure or temperature differential (optional).

Product / Package Types

Applicable to all pharmaceutical packages and closure/seal types.

Literature References

ASNT, 1982 • ASTM E 165 • Chrai et al., 1994 • Gnanasekharan et al., 1992 • Hansen et al., 1995 • Jones et al., 1995 • Lin et al., 1982 • Shaw, 1995

Commentary

Principle: Visual Inspection

The most traditional package integrity method. Through human visual inspection or an electronic/machine vision system, the outside of the package is examined for signs of product leakage (such as liquid traces, color change or package deformation).

It can be combined with temperature or pressure stress to force potential leak sites to show themselves, increasing sensitivity.

Key Points

  • Qualitative method, non-destructive
  • The only method applicable to all package types (no restrictions)
  • Low sensitivity: detects only larger leaks where visible product leakage has already occurred
  • Manual visual inspection is affected by subjective factors such as operator training, lighting and viewing angle, so repeatability is low
  • Automated visual inspection (AVI) systems are fast but still rely on image contrast and are not sensitive to very small leaks
  • It is the minimum baseline method required by regulators (FDA, EMA) and is needed for almost all products

Limitations

Visual inspection can only see "traces of a leak that has already occurred"; it cannot predict or detect small leaks that have not yet become visible. It is like the oil warning light in a car: it tells you the oil is already low, not that a seal has a tiny crack and is about to fail.

Regulatory Context

The FDA aseptic processing guidance (2004) requires 100% visual inspection of all injectable products (21 CFR 211.167). It also recommends supplementing this with more sensitive deterministic methods, such as pressure decay, to ensure that small leaks are detected as well.

Volumetric Gas Flow

Description

A pressure differential is applied to a package and the outward flow rate of gas is measured.

Volumetric gas flow methods can be validated for the measurement of gaseous leaks large enough to allow a measurable flow of gas out of the package over the duration of the test. Validation studies should examine the ability of the volumetric flow rate instrument to measure control leaks as a function of instrument sensor capabilities, differential pressure testing conditions, and test time.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity is based on the ability to accurately measure gaseous flow out of a package under positive pressure. Instrument sensitivity can be verified using gaseous reference leaks or positive control package defects. Test method sensitivity is influenced primarily by flow meter accuracy, test time, and the differential pressure applied to the package. Generally, volumetric gas flow is not as sensitive as vacuum/pressure decay methods.

Quantitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Time of test
  • Differential pressure applied to package

A few seconds.

Equipment Required

  • Leak test instrumentation equipped with flow meters

Differential pressure.

Product / Package Types

Applicable to pharmaceutical packages that do not rely on a tortuous path or barrier seal and that can tolerate a differential pressure applied to the seal area. Literature References

ASNT, 1982 • Guazzo, 1996 • Gnanasekharan et al., 1992, 1994 • Hoffmann, 1996 • Snow, 1992

Commentary

Principle: Volumetric Gas Flow

Positive pressure is applied to the package, and a flow meter measures the flow of gas escaping from the package leak (units: mL/min or sccm). The larger the flow, the more severe the leak.

Difference from the pressure decay method:

  • Volumetric gas flow: measures flow (flow rate) directly
  • Pressure decay (8.14): measures the rate of pressure drop (flow is calculated indirectly)

Key Points

  • Quantitative method: can measure the actual gas flow
  • Non-destructive
  • Sensitivity is lower than the pressure decay method (limited by flow meter accuracy)
  • Short test time (a few seconds)
  • Particularly suitable for packages that deform easily under pressure: with a deformable package, pressure decay can give false positives because the package expands/contracts, whereas the flow method is not affected
  • Suitable for testing a specified orifice size (critical orifice), such as the nozzle of an inhaler

When to Choose Volumetric vs. Pressure Decay?

If the package is a flexible bag (such as an infusion bag) or another container that deforms readily when pressurized, pressure decay readings will be distorted by the deformation. In that case the volumetric gas flow method is more reliable, because it measures the amount of escaping gas directly and is not affected by container deformation.

8.18

Weight Loss / Gain

Gravimetric measurement of volatile loss or hygroscopic gain — quantitative, non-destructive

Description

The container is filled with either a volatile liquid or solid, or a hygroscopic material such as a desiccant. The container is stored in an environment intended to promote migration of the substances out of the package (volatile materials), or to promote migration of water vapor into the package (hygroscopic materials). Leakage plus permeation are measured in units of package weight loss or weight gain.

For greater accuracy, it is recommended that control packages filled with inert materials be tested at the same time. Each time the test units are weighed, the control packages' weight change should be subtracted from the change in test unit weights.

Validation

Weight loss or weight gain methods can be validated for the measurement of leaks of a wide variety of sizes, as well as for permeation measurement. Validation will depend on the test method parameters and the sensitivity of the balance used to monitor weight change. Acceptance limits are generally established as a function of the product-package system.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity is dependent on the extent of weight loss or gain. The weight change of the test units must vary significantly from the control units, and should be above the limit of balance sensitivity.

Quantitative / Qualitative

Quantitative

Critical Test Parameters

  • Volatility (weight loss) or hygroscopicity (weight gain) of the packaged material
  • Time allowed for weight change
  • Challenge environment: temperature, and either pressure (weight loss) or relative humidity (weight loss or gain)
  • Handling of samples and weighing techniques

Time to Complete

A few minutes may be required for highly volatile materials (e.g., dry ice). Otherwise, several days or weeks may be required.

Equipment Required

  • Balance
  • For weight gain: relative humidity storage chamber; temperature and humidity monitors
  • For weight loss: appropriate package storage environment; optional elevated temperature, differential pressure with monitors

Stress on Package

Environmental extremes of temperature, pressure, humidity.

Product / Package Types

Applicable to all pharmaceutical packages and seal types intended to contain products susceptible to weight change over shelf-life.

Literature References

Amini, 1981 • Cilento, 1977 • Guazzo, 1996 • Morton, 1987 • USP <661> • USP <671>

Commentary

Principle: Weight Loss/Gain

An analytical balance is used to monitor the change in package weight under defined storage conditions:

  • Weight loss: the package contains a volatile substance (such as an organic solvent or dry ice) that evaporates and escapes under pressure/temperature conditions, so the weight decreases
  • Weight gain: the package contains a hygroscopic substance such as a desiccant, and under high humidity water vapor enters, so the weight increases

Control packages (filled with an inert material) are tested in parallel to subtract the effect of environmental factors.

Key Points

  • Quantitative method: can measure the total of leakage + permeation
  • Non-destructive: after weighing, the package can continue to be used/observed
  • Broad applicability: suitable for all package types and seal designs
  • The test time is very long (days to weeks), so it is not suitable for in-line inspection
  • Sensitivity depends on balance accuracy and the amount of weight change
  • It is a reference method in USP <661> and <671> and is widely accepted by regulators
  • It measures leakage and permeation together, and the contribution of each cannot be distinguished

Best Use Case

In package shelf-life validation and shelf-life studies, to confirm that the barrier performance of the package meets specification throughout the shelf life. For example, confirming that a capsule bottle containing a desiccant gains no more than the limit of moisture over 2 years at 25°C/60%RH.

USP Reference

USP <671> (Containers - Permeation) specifies test methods for water vapor permeation, and the gravimetric method is one of its core methods. USP <661> (Containers for injections) also cites the gravimetric method as a tool for evaluating container closure tightness.

Vacuum/Pressure Decay Types Explained

Method 8.14 can be implemented in four distinct configurations. Understanding the differences is critical for method selection and validation:

"Generally, pressure decay is considered a more sensitive test than vacuum rise, and differential pressure is potentially more sensitive than absolute pressure sensors." — PDA TR27, Section 8.14

Four Configurations Explained

Memory aid: differential > absolute; decay > rise

Sensitivity ranking (high to low):

  1. Pressure decay + differential sensing (Pressure Decay Differential): highest
  2. Pressure decay + absolute sensing (Pressure Decay Absolute)
  3. Vacuum rise + differential sensing (Vacuum Rise Differential)
  4. Vacuum rise + absolute sensing (Vacuum Rise Absolute): lowest

Why Is Differential More Sensitive?

In differential mode, the test package and a reference package (with no leak) are pressurized/evacuated at the same time. Ambient temperature fluctuations affect the two chambers almost equally, so they cancel out in the differential reading. This greatly reduces false signals from temperature drift and lets the instrument detect smaller real pressure changes.

Special Considerations for Liquid Packages

In a vacuum rise test, liquid in a liquid-filled package (such as an injectable) may be drawn into the seal point and temporarily block the leak path, causing a false negative. In this case the pressure decay method is preferable, as it pressurizes from inside the package outward and the liquid is not drawn toward the seal.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

TR27's Overall Contribution to the CCI Field

PDA TR27 (1998) is a landmark document on pharmaceutical package integrity testing. Before USP <1207> was formally adopted into the pharmacopeia, it systematically consolidated all the major container closure integrity (CCI) test methods of the time, including liquid ingress, microbial challenge, helium leak, residual seal force (RSF) and headspace analysis, and established a scientific framework for evaluating them.

TR27's core contribution is that it clearly distinguishes the complementary roles of deterministic physical tests and probabilistic microbiological tests, and it provided the industry's first technical basis for comparing methods against each other, directly laying the groundwork for the later USP <1207>.

Under the TR27 framework, they correspond to two levels: process control (RSF) and product release verification (physical/microbiological testing).

TR27's Historical Position and Legacy

When TR27 (1998) was published, USP <1207> was still in draft (PF 1997) and FDA had no complete CCI guidance. TR27's 56 references span a full 25 years of research, from 1972 to 1997, and form the industry's first systematic technical framework for CCI.

Looking back from today: TR27 directly gave rise to the structure of the formal 2016 USP <1207> chapter. The classification into deterministic vs. probabilistic methods, helium leak rate as a benchmark method, and the standing of headspace analysis can all be traced to TR27's references and technical discussion.

Implication for pharmaceutical CDMOs: understanding TR27's references means understanding the intellectual history behind modern CCI regulatory requirements, which helps provide scientific traceability for method selection in validation plans.
