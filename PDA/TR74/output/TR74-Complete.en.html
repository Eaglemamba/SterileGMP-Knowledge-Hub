1.0 Introduction

Original Text

Reprocessing and reworking are tools that biological manufacturers can use when a manufacturing process step has operated outside of its validated operating ranges. For reprocessing, rather than proceeding with the next manufacturing process step, the product is rerun through the previous manufacturing process step operated within the acceptable operating ranges. For reworking, the product is subjected to one or more processing steps that are different from the established manufacturing process to obtain acceptable product.

In biological manufacturing, however, reprocessing, and especially reworking, should be considered only in exceptional circumstances. Reworking can be considered during events such as critical equipment failures, out of specifications of critical quality attributes of drug substance or drug product, and/or deviation from in-process controls that impact quality of the drug substance or drug product (further conditions to consider are stated in Section 5.0).

Reprocessing and reworking are deviations that are managed within the corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) of the manufacturer's quality management system. An essential prerequisite for the acceptance of a reprocessing or reworking step is a clear indication of the root cause. Due to the size and complexity of biological products, including the limitations in testing methods (especially measurement of functional biological activity, i.e., potency), reprocessing, and especially reworking, are limited for biologicals. Thus, they should be entered into with caution so as not to impact the product's critical quality attributes (CQA), and the costs and risks should be carefully evaluated and documented.

Effective reprocessing and reworking strategies should integrate supply chain resilience considerations, including forecasting, inventory buffers, and manufacturing scheduling. Combining quality risk management assessments (e.g., a FMEA assessment) with supply risk scoring can support balanced decisions that protect both compliance and patient access.

Reprocessing and reworking also require regulatory authority approval before the manufactured product can be released for human use. In limited cases, regulatory authority approval for reprocessing can be acquired ahead of time by presenting a comprehensive change management plan (proactive approach detailed in Section 3.6). This plan should explain the reason for reprocessing, such as refiltration or reconcentration due to technical failures or mechanical breakdown of a chromatography column (1). It must also specify the frequency and nature of the reprocessing procedures for potential events, as well as detail the steps for validation and ensuring product comparability.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reprocessing: repeating the same process step after it has been run outside its validated operating range (returning to the previous step and re-running it within acceptable ranges). The step itself does not change; it is simply "done again".

Reworking: applying one or more steps that are not part of the originally defined process in order to obtain acceptable product. This is a "new, unplanned step" and carries much higher risk than Reprocessing.

Key difference: Reprocessing = re-running a known step; Reworking = introducing an unvalidated new step. The former can be built into a Change Management Plan in advance; the latter can hardly ever be fully predicted beforehand.

Potency: the indicator of a biological product's functional biological activity. Because it is difficult to measure, the effect of Reworking on Potency often cannot be fully detected. This is the fundamental scientific reason for the limits placed on reworking biologicals.

Analogy

Reprocessing = re-straining a pot of soup: you cook the soup by the established recipe, but high pressure leaves the straining incomplete. You pour the soup back and strain it again through the same cloth. The step has not changed; it is simply repeated. That is Reprocessing.

Reworking = a chef improvising an extra step to "rescue" the dish: but if the soup has been overheated and some of the protein has denatured, you must add a "clarification step" that is not in the original recipe to repair it. That is Reworking: you have introduced an operation outside the recipe, and you must revalidate whether it affects final quality.

Why are biologicals more at risk? A biological is like a delicate soufflé: once its structure is disturbed, even if it looks normal again, its internal structure (tertiary structure, glycosylation pattern) may have quietly changed, and current test methods cannot detect it.

Key Notes

Decision Hierarchy: however great the process pressure, every Reprocessing / Reworking decision must follow this order:

  1. Sterility Assurance - non-negotiable
  2. Product CQAs (critical quality attributes) - patient safety first
  3. Business & Flexibility - considered last

Root cause is a prerequisite, not an option: TR 74 states clearly that a clear root cause is a necessary precondition for accepting Reprocessing / Reworking. Without a root cause there is no basis for any remedial action; this is the minimum GMP threshold.

Key addition in 2026: this edition explicitly adds a "Supply Chain Resilience" framework that integrates FMEA with supply risk scoring, a significant upgrade over the previous edition (which focused only on technical aspects). It also means that in future audits, documentation evaluating Reprocessing must cover supply chain risk considerations.

Practice Questions

  1. Suppose your UF/DF step runs at a flow rate outside the validated range because of equipment misoperation. Decide: should this follow the Reprocessing or the Reworking pathway? What is your reasoning?
  2. Why do the limitations in measuring Potency make Reworking of biologicals more complex than for small-molecule drugs? Give a specific example.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: your company is manufacturing a clinical batch for a Phase II mAb client. A loose line connector in the Protein A affinity chromatography step causes a pressure deviation, and yield falls below the expected lower limit.

You must make three decisions immediately:

  • Does the event meet the conditions for Reprocessing (is the root cause clear? can the same chromatography step be re-run)?
  • Does the client's CMC dossier and IND/IMPD already include Reprocessing provisions (proactive approach)? If not, which regulatory pathway must follow?
  • Supply chain impact assessment: the effect of this batch delay on the clinical trial timeline, and whether backup inventory is available as a buffer.

The assessment and decision documentation for these three points is exactly how the complete management framework described in TR 74 Section 1.0 is put into practice.

1.1 Purpose

Original Text

PDA Technical Report No. 74 (Revised 2026): Reprocessing and Reworking of Biologicals (TR 74) presents a strategy based on the experiences and practices of a PDA Technical Report Team representing a cross-section of industry professionals. The intent is to provide guidance on the design, development, controls, procedures, validation, regulatory submission, and implementation of reprocessing and reworking procedures for the manufacturing of biologicals.

The information discussed represents what the authors consider a balance of scientific, regulatory, and business considerations for the reprocessing of biologicals in particular but can also be applied to the reworking of biologicals in general. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Positioning of TR 74: this technical report is not a mandatory standard but a "single-source overview" that supplements existing guidance. Its core is to integrate a balanced view across scientific, regulatory and business considerations.

Lifecycle coverage: TR 74 starts from the earliest stages of design and development and runs through to regulatory submission and implementation. This means it applies across the whole product lifecycle, not only to emergency handling after commercialization.

Triple-balance perspective (Scientific × Regulatory × Business): this is the methodological core of TR 74. Any Reprocessing / Reworking strategy must satisfy all three dimensions at once; being merely "scientifically feasible" or "needed by the business" is not sufficient justification.

Analogy

TR 74 as an "emergency response manual": think of an airline's Abnormal Procedures Checklist. It does not force pilots to follow fixed steps; it provides a best-practice path based on industry consensus. The pilot (the manufacturer) still has to judge the situation, but the manual ensures nobody starts from scratch.

The importance of "consulting regulatory authorities": just as a major diversion requires notifying ATC (Air Traffic Control) first, early communication with the competent authority (FDA, EMA, TFDA) before implementing any Reprocessing / Reworking strategy is the most effective way to reduce the risk of batch rejection. This is not confirmation of a regulatory obligation but risk management best practice.

This is especially true for a clinical CDMO: the client's IND strategy and your manufacturing strategy must be aligned.

Special responsibility of a CDMO: as a contract manufacturer, your technical reports and CAPA records are not only for your own use; they are important supporting material for the client's CMC submissions to regulators. The TR 74 framework gives you a basis for standard terminology when communicating with the client.

1.2 Scope

Original Text

The previous version of TR 74 included only such biopharmaceutical products as recombinant proteins and polypeptides. Because the principles of reprocessing and reworking are applicable across a wider array of manufacturing processes, the scope has been expanded to include guidance on biological products. In addition to recombinant proteins and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (both as innovators/novel and biosimilars), biological products (2–4) encompass a wide range of other products such as vaccines and blood-derived proteins.

The quality of biologicals is defined by the chosen production and manufacturing process. Minor changes in the process can affect the quality of the drug product; therefore, the development of the manufacturing process is of paramount importance for biologicals, including the implementation of any reprocessing or reworking. Incorporating the topic of reworking further expands the scope of this technical report, but because the reworking of biologicals is far more challenging than reprocessing, the authors emphasize that extra caution should be taken.

The guidance in this technical report is general in nature, and the case studies illustrate how the general principles may be applied. No attempt is made to encompass all types of unit operations for which reprocessing or reworking could be considered.

This technical report is not intended to establish mandatory standards but serves as a single-source overview that complements existing guidance documents listed in Section 8.0.

The principles discussed in TR 74 are applicable to both clinical and commercial manufacturing processes, but the authors recognize that reprocessing and reworking during the clinical manufacturing of biologicals is often more challenging than during commercial manufacturing. This is primarily because the understanding of the product and the process is very limited, so extra caution is emphasized.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Significance of the expanded scope (2026 update): the previous TR 74 covered only recombinant proteins and polypeptides. The 2026 revision expands this substantially to all biological products, including:

  • mAbs (Monoclonal Antibodies): both innovator products and biosimilars
  • Vaccines
  • Blood-derived Proteins

What this means for a CDMO: you may once have thought "we do not make vaccines, so TR 74 is not relevant to us"; that reasoning no longer holds. Any biological product manufacturer should include TR 74 in its QMS reference document system.

The core concept "Process IS the Product": "The quality of biologicals is defined by the chosen production and manufacturing process." This sentence is the foundational principle of biologicals CMC. A biological has no fixed chemical structural formula: your process is the definition of your product. This is why any process deviation (including Reprocessing / Reworking) must be rigorously assessed for its impact on CQAs.

Analogy

An intuitive view of "Process IS the Product": a small-molecule drug is like a standard screw mass-produced in a factory: every unit has the same specification and is acceptable as long as it meets the dimensional tolerance. A biological is like a hand-crafted beer: with the same recipe, a different brewer, temperature or stirring method produces a completely different flavor profile. Changing any process step is changing "the product itself".

The gap between clinical and commercial manufacturing: picture a chef (the manufacturer) practicing a new dish at home (clinical stage). He is still feeling his way through every step and has limited understanding of the ingredients. If something goes wrong, he has even less room to adjust, and every operation builds knowledge and process understanding. At the commercial stage, the chef has already made two thousand portions in the restaurant and knows every variable well; even so, reworking a biological still demands caution.

Key Notes

The dual challenge of clinical manufacturing (for our own work setting): TR 74 states explicitly that Reprocessing / Reworking at the clinical stage is more difficult than in commercial manufacturing, for these reasons:

  1. Limited Process Understanding: the Design Space has not been fully established and process boundaries are unclear.
  2. Limited Product Understanding: the CQA list may not yet be final, and the baseline of comparability data is still thin.
  3. Uncertain regulatory pathway: clinical-stage IND/IMPDs usually lack pre-approved Reprocessing provisions, so every deviation needs a case-by-case assessment.

Applying the decision hierarchy here: the time pressure of a clinical trial (enrolled patients are waiting) is real business pressure. But the TR 74 position is clear: even at the clinical stage, Sterility Assurance and CQA integrity must take priority over trial timeline needs.

Practical Application

Our position (a clinical-stage CDMO): the passage at the end of TR 74 (2026) Section 1.2 is written directly for us: "clinical manufacturing is often more challenging than commercial manufacturing... extra caution is emphasized."

What specific actions does this imply?

  • When assessing a new client for Tech Transfer, proactively confirm whether the client's CMC strategy already addresses Reprocessing scenarios (proactive approach). If not, we recommend adding it to the Risk Register.
  • When designing Process Characterization Studies, consider including an assessment of "Excursion Scenarios" to lay the groundwork for any future Reprocessing application.
  • When agreeing the Quality Agreement with the client, define the notification procedure and decision authority levels for Reprocessing / Reworking; a framework agreed in advance saves time and effort compared with disputes after the fact.

Section Summary

  • Core definitions: Reprocessing = re-running the same step; Reworking = introducing a new, unplanned step. Both require a clear root cause, and both require regulatory approval before release.
  • Why biologicals are limited: molecular complexity + difficulty measuring Potency = the effect of Reworking on CQAs cannot be fully detected, so it must be entered "with caution".
  • Proactive vs. reactive approach: a proactive approach (building it into the Change Management Plan in advance) is the most effective way to reduce regulatory uncertainty, but it applies only to Reprocessing; Reworking can hardly ever be fully predicted.
  • Key additions in 2026: scope extended to vaccines and blood-derived proteins; a framework integrating Supply Chain Resilience and FMEA has been added.
  • Special challenges for a clinical-stage CDMO: limited process understanding + insufficient product knowledge make Reprocessing / Reworking more difficult than in commercial manufacturing. TR 74 stresses "extra caution" twice, which describes our working environment exactly.
  • The decision hierarchy is unchanged: Sterility Assurance > Product CQAs > Business & Flexibility. However great the supply chain pressure, this order must never be inverted.

Key Notes - Most Critical Terms

Reprocessing vs. Reworking - the watershed for the regulatory pathway

Reprocessing: reintroducing an intermediate or API into the established process and repeating the same step (such as re-filtration or re-concentration). For biologicals, this is usually limited to a small number of cases caused by technical equipment failure (such as a mechanical failure of a chromatography column).

Reworking: subjecting a non-conforming intermediate or API to steps different from the originally defined process in order to achieve acceptable quality (such as recrystallization with a different solvent, or an additional chromatographic purification step). This carries higher risk and usually requires a pre-approved protocol.

→ Decision principle: if a problem can be solved by Reprocessing, avoid Reworking; going the Reworking route requires full comparability data to support it.

Design Space: operating within the approved range does not constitute a change; the post-approval change process is triggered only when operating outside the Design Space.

Prior Approval:

  • Clinical-stage products: EMA requires prior approval of substantial modifications; FDA requires an information amendment to be submitted.
  • Marketed commercial products: EMA requires a Type II variation; FDA requires a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) to the BLA.

3.0 Reprocessing Considerations — Introduction

Original Text

Reprocessing (3, 9, 11–15), or repetition of an existing biopharmaceutical unit operation, requires a well-designed process, supportive data, and validation to augment the existing manufacturing process. Importantly, the supportive data must provide the manufacturer with a high degree of confidence (assurance) that the drug substance or drug product produced by reprocessing meets the predefined quality standards for the drug substance or drug product and is comparable to a nonreprocessed batch. The reprocessed and reworked biological drug substances and sterile drug products should adhere to all CQAs associated (including bioburden and endotoxins) to normal batch disposition and release. Enhanced testing protocols are often required to assess the quality attributes of the reprocessed material, ensuring that it remains consistent with the established manufacturing process.

Reprocessing in pharmaceutical manufacturing can occur under various scenarios that can be broadly categorized as proactive and reactive approaches.

In GMP, a risk-based approach (6), to reprocessing is incorporated by systematically assessing and managing potential risks associated with reactive reprocessing activities. This begins with identifying and evaluating the nature of deviations and their potential impact on product quality and patient safety. By prioritizing areas of greatest concern, manufacturers can focus resources and controls where they are most needed. Decisions regarding reprocessing are made based on these risk assessments, ensuring that only safe and effective practices are implemented.

For any event that requires reprocessing, the manufacturer must perform a formal root-cause investigation of the unit operation excursion if the excursion is not within the design space. Additional development studies may be required to supplement the root-cause investigation. Consideration of any proposed reprocessing by regulators will likely be based on the root-cause analysis and the impact of reprocessing on product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reprocessing: repeating an existing unit operation of the process in order to remedy a nonconforming intermediate or drug substance, while remaining within the validated design space.

Fundamental difference from reworking: reprocessing means "performing the same step one more time"; reworking means "introducing a new step outside the original process design." TR74 treats the two separately, and the regulatory burden differs markedly.

CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes): these include purity, potency, aggregates, HCP/DNA residuals, bioburden, and endotoxin. After reprocessing, all CQAs must be comparable to those of normal batches.

Analogy

Imagine a chef in the kitchen of a Michelin-starred restaurant. If the saltiness of a main course is slightly over the limit (a deviation), the chef can dilute it once more. That is "reprocessing": the same cooking step, simply performed one more time. But if entirely new ingredients must be added or the cooking method changed, it becomes "reworking," and the quality of the whole dish must be re-evaluated.

Regulators have the same expectations: for reprocessing the risk boundary is narrower and the procedure clearer, whereas reworking needs more supporting data and stricter approval.

Key Notes

Decision hierarchy reminder: the ultimate purpose of reprocessing is always to protect patient safety. TR74 explicitly requires that reprocessed and non-reprocessed batches show a "high degree of confidence of comparability." This is not a matter of saying that "the results look about the same"; it requires a systematic data package and validation.

Under the TFDA and PIC/S frameworks, if reprocessing has not been included in the CMC submission beforehand, the batch may not be released even if QA considers the quality acceptable.

3.1 Proactive Approach

Original Text

In a proactive scenario, the potential need for reprocessing is identified in advance through predetermined criteria. The execution of the reprocessing activities occurs through a predefined concurrent validation protocol with clear acceptance criteria or through previously validated procedures may be reviewed and approved by applicable regulatory agencies. This approach includes:

  1. Predefined Criteria: Manufacturers outline specific conditions or triggers that would necessitate reprocessing, allowing for a structured response to an event that may require reprocessing. Post reprocessing, there should be sufficient data to support a conclusion that any given reprocessed batch of a drug substance or drug product is uniform and meets the defined acceptance criteria.
  2. Validated Procedures: Reprocessing is executed according to a validated procedure that has undergone detailed review by the manufacturer's quality unit. This concurrent validation ensures that the process is capable of consistently producing a product that meets established quality standards. The validation protocol should include the scope of the manufacturing process that is being reprocessed, number of batches required, tests to be performed, and acceptance criteria. The proposed acceptance criteria for all tests should be appropriately justified. The concurrent validation approach should be described within the Validation Master Plan. An example of reprocessing that might be specifically covered by a Biological License Application (BLA) would be repeating viral filtration following a failed filter-integrity test. Because viral filters occasionally fail, manufacturers may be approved for refiltration of the protein drug substance or drug product (following successful validation). The approach to validation should be consistent with a lifecycle approach to validation, as discussed in the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Guidance for Industry: Process Validation: General Principles and Practices and PDA Technical Report No. 60 (Revised 2026) Process Validation: A Lifecycle Approach (10).

When reprocessing has been prospectively validated, it is appropriate to provide the full validation protocol and report in a regulatory submission and to follow the validated process where indicated. When reprocessing is anticipated to undergo concurrent validation, the protocol—inclusive of initial starting conditions and appropriate acceptance criteria to determine whether successful completion of a reprocessing step has been achieved—should be provided.

  1. Regulatory Guidance derived from Established Protocols: An essential prerequisite for the acceptance of a reprocessing step is a clear identification of the root cause. For biological products, these situations are usually restricted to some refiltration, reconcentration steps upon technical failure of equipment, or mechanical breakdown of a chromatography column. These steps should be appropriately described, validated, and included in the regulatory submission. Such documentation should include the description of conditions for which reprocessing could be applied and a demonstration that the reprocessing step(s) does not impact the quality of the drug substance and/or drug product.

The sequence of steps detailed in Section 3.6 provides a roadmap for implementing proactive reprocessing, ensuring that all necessary evaluations and approvals are addressed before action is taken.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Core definition of the proactive approach: at the time of the marketing application (BLA/MAA/NDA), the triggers, validation plan, and acceptance criteria for the reprocessing step are all written into the CMC package in advance. If the event actually occurs in the future, the approved procedure is simply followed.

Three key elements:

  • Predefined criteria (triggers): which process failures initiate reprocessing? (Example: a viral filter integrity test failure.)
  • Validated procedures: the reprocessing step itself must be validated, either prospectively or concurrently.
  • Regulatory approval (pre-approval): the reprocessing plan must be described in Module 3.2.S.2.2 / 3.2.S.2.5 / 3.2.S.2.6 and be agreed by the regulatory authority before it may be executed.

Analogy

The proactive approach is like the standard emergency checklist of an aircraft: before takeoff, the pilot already knows "if an engine fails before V1, carry out steps A-B-C." Because it has been rehearsed and approved in advance, when the event occurs the crew only needs to follow the checklist and does not have to invent a solution on the spot.

By contrast, the reactive approach is when the engine really does fail but the manual has no such scenario: the risk must be assessed in real time, and the regulator must be notified and give approval before the situation can be handled.

Key Notes

Practical point for CDMOs: for a clinical-phase CDMO, a proactive plan means the possibility of reprocessing must already be discussed at the IND/CTA stage. This requires early involvement of Process Development and Regulatory Affairs, and is usually most critical at the Phase 2/3 transition.

Example of viral refiltration in a BLA (accepted by FDA): when a viral filter must be replaced because of an occasional integrity failure, and the refiltration plan was validated and submitted beforehand, it counts as proactive reprocessing from a regulatory standpoint and does not need to be reported batch by batch.

Conditions for using concurrent validation: if prospective validation has not been completed, validation data may be collected concurrently during commercial production, provided that the validation plan has been approved in advance and the acceptance criteria are predefined.

Practical Application — CDMO Scenario

A CDMO client is preparing a BLA for a mAb. Historical data show that in about 3% of batches the UF/DF step shows unstable transmembrane pressure (TMP), so the concentration endpoint is not reached.

Proactive strategy: write into Module 3.2.S.2.5 of the BLA: "If TMP is low and the target concentration is not reached, the recirculation time may be extended by no more than X hours and additional DF cycles performed, with at least 2 validation batches and acceptance criteria of purity ≥ Y% and aggregates ≤ Z%."

Once approved, if a similar event occurs again, the approved procedure can be followed directly with no deviation report or supplement, which greatly shortens batch release time and avoids supply interruption.

3.2 Reactive Approach

Original Text

Reactive reprocessing occurs in response to unforeseen circumstances during the manufacturing process. The same categories of data and decisions required for a proactive reprocessing approach apply to this approach, except that less time and information may be available for performing assessments. As a result, reactive reprocessing is better suited for steps that are simple (such as microbial or virus filtration after failure of filter-integrity testing) and have a low likelihood of affecting product quality. The sequence of steps discussed in Section 3.6 may be used to guide the approach to reactive reprocessing, starting with the design of a reprocessing unit operation (see Section 3.6, Step 6).

Two distinct reactive reprocessing scenarios are described as follows:

  1. Nonprospectively Validated Reprocessing at an In-Process Hold Point
    1. In this scenario, a need arises for a nonprospectively validated reprocessing unit operation while a product is held at an in-process stage. This may involve scenarios such as freezing the product for an extended period.
    2. During the hold period, manufacturers can develop and execute a plan to validate the reprocessing step. This provides assurance that the product meets all the required quality attributes subsequent to the product concurrent release.
  2. Immediate Reprocessing During a Manufacturing Run
    1. This scenario occurs when a reprocessing need emerges unexpectedly during a manufacturing run. In such cases, the reprocessing step may need to be implemented before its validation.
    2. If a rapid design and implementation of the reprocessing option is feasible, it may be executed "at risk." This means that the reprocessing can occur with the understanding that supportive data must be generated and reviewed to ensure product quality before the batch is released to the market.

Of the two reactive reprocessing scenarios described above, the second scenario (#2) carries the highest degree of risk and is likely to be implemented only with relatively simple unit operations that affect a limited number of CQAs, such as dead-end filtration for drug substance or drug product. Release of the reprocessed batch using a reactive scenario should only be performed upon receiving regulatory approval. (See Section 4.0 for strategies on regulatory submissions).

A proactive process design approach that accounts for reprocessing could apply to more complex (such as ultrafiltration, diafiltration, concentration, chromatography, viral removal steps, and polishing) steps with significant benefit to the manufacturer.

In principle, almost any unit operation may be considered for potential reprocessing as long as it is demonstrated to consistently deliver a drug substance or drug product that meets the same specified quality commitments of the standard manufacturing process. Reprocessing simple unit operations is relatively well accepted and understood by regulators and the industry. Reprocessing a sterile drug substance and reprocessing and reworking of sterile-biological batches requires critical attention to maintaining sterility, endotoxin, and particulate controls. Reprocessing of nonsterile drug substances manufactured in low bioburden facilities also requires special attention to be paid to microbial and particulate controls.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reactive reprocessing: deciding whether and how to reprocess only after an unexpected process deviation has occurred. The key limitations are heavy time pressure and insufficient information.

Risk differences between the two reactive scenarios:

  • Scenario 1 (hold point): the product can be frozen or held, and the hold period is used to generate additional validation data before release. The risk is relatively manageable.
  • Scenario 2 (immediate in-process reprocessing): the highest risk. Validation is not yet complete at the time of execution, so this is an "at risk" operation. Regulatory approval must be obtained before release.

Meaning of executing "at risk": a CDMO must understand that "at risk" does not mean operations can be done freely. It means the company bears the risk of executing first, but must complete the validation data afterwards, and batch release must wait for regulatory approval.

Analogy

The reactive approach is like a late-night restaurant kitchen suddenly running out of a key ingredient, forcing the chef to improvise the recipe. Scenario 1 is "stop serving, go buy new ingredients, test the dish, and then serve it" (hold point strategy); Scenario 2 is "the dish must go out now, the chef substitutes ingredients on the fly, and the owner supervises and notifies the food safety authority" (at-risk strategy).

Clearly the risk of Scenario 2 is far higher than that of Scenario 1, so TR74 recommends giving priority to a proactive plan for complex steps (such as UF/DF and chromatography).

Key Notes

Complex operations are not suited to the reactive approach: UF/DF, chromatography, viral removal, and polishing steps affect many CQAs in complex ways and should not rely on a reactive plan. The reprocessing design for these steps should be planned as proactive during product development.

Additional requirements for sterile batches: whether proactive or reactive, once reprocessing of a sterile drug substance or drug product is involved, sterility assurance, endotoxin control, and particulate control must be explicitly evaluated. This is the subject of Section 3.8.

TFDA/PIC/S framework points: release of a reactively reprocessed batch requires prior approval from the regulatory authority (whether TFDA, EMA, or FDA); the decision cannot rest with in-house QA alone.

  1. For a clinical-trial sterile drug substance batch whose final sterilizing filter (0.2 µm) fails its filter integrity test, how would you decide whether Scenario 1 or Scenario 2 applies?
  2. Which functions should jointly take part in the decision to execute "at risk"? When a CDMO makes this decision on behalf of a client, what compliance risks need to be communicated beforehand?
3.3 Reprocessing Frequency

Original Text

A manufacturing process that requires frequent reprocessing is considered to be poorly controlled and not in a validated state. While there is no formal definition of "frequent," it would be reasonable to question the state-of-control for a unit operation that requires reprocessing as often as 10% of the time (16). (Ten percent is given here as an illustrative example and is not intended to be a prescriptive limit.)

It is important to distinguish between two types of reprocessing frequency:

  • Number of reprocessed unit operations within a batch
  • Number of batches that require reprocessing over a defined time period

Given that the Annual Product Review/Product Quality Review provides a one-year performance summary, it may be appropriate to evaluate reprocessing trends within that annual timeframe. Under circumstances where reprocessing becomes recurring or trends upward, a reassessment of the process design should be strongly considered. If reprocessing is carried out frequently or reaches a specified percentage of manufactured batches, it must be considered part of the normal process, described as such in the application, and receives approval.

3.3.1 Reprocessing Frequency per Batch

The frequency of reprocessing for the same batch at commercial-scale reprocessing should be restricted to a maximum number of reprocessing events according to process development data for small-scale reprocessing.

Validation of the reprocessing step should be supported by a scientifically justified number of demonstration runs. While no fixed minimum is mandated by regulatory agencies, performing at least two independent runs is common practice to demonstrate reproducibility. The exact number should be based on process complexity, risk assessment, and historical data to ensure the process consistently achieves predefined acceptance criteria.

The reprocessing frequency is established by the manufacturer based on appropriate studies at either small scale or same scale. Small-scale validation studies are commonly used to establish frequency for a reprocessing event for a particular unit operation. These studies may be conducted before implementation of a reprocessing approach at manufacturing scale. In a reprocessing event, product quality can change after repeated run exposures during filtration or chromatography processing steps and may impact the stability of long-term storage. There is no rule-of-thumb for frequency determination; the manufacturer must establish it for each project based on its impact on quality attributes. For example, a simple dead-end filtration, typically a maximum of three reprocessings, can be included in a small-scale reprocessing study. Some protein molecules may not be stable for a second reprocessing; in that case, maximum reprocessing may be restricted to only one reprocessing event.

These studies are used to obtain data on product quality attributes (e.g., aggregation) resulting from exposure to physical/shear stress during repeated unit operations. Small-scale validation-study design should be predictive of the commercial process. This will ensure that the data generated is relevant to large-scale. Small-scale reprocessing studies must use the same protein solution for the repeated number of runs and the samples to be withdrawn after each run. Product quality attributes of each run should be compared against the initial and intermediate feed-material to understand the impact of multiple exposures and to determine the frequency for the large-scale reprocessing event. All reprocessing events, including repeated runs, need to be completed within the allowed chemical and microbial hold time for intermediate pools or scientifically justified if there is any excursion from validated hold time.

When developing a proactive reprocessing step, a review of the development data and history may identify process steps with inherent variability that may, on rare occasions, lead to a need for reprocessing (e.g., failing in-process control (IPC) assay results, filter integrity failure, difficult separation step). The variability must be scientifically understood.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two frequency dimensions must be monitored together:

  • Dimension A: number of repeats within a batch (per batch): how many times may the same step be repeated at most within one batch? The upper limit must be established through small-scale validation studies.
  • Dimension B: frequency across batches: what percentage of batches in a year require reprocessing? If the trend rises, the process is out of control and the design must be re-evaluated.

10% is an illustrative threshold, not a mandatory regulatory red line: TR74 states specifically that 10% is only an example. The actual threshold should be defined according to product risk and process characteristics, and incorporated into APR/PQR trend analysis.

Formula and Calculation

Suggested metric for monitoring reprocessing frequency:

Batch frequency =
  number of batches requiring reprocessing / total batches manufactured in the same period × 100%

Suggested evaluation trigger thresholds (examples):
  > 5% → initiate a process design review
  > 10% → treat as process not in control; submit CAPA and assess regulatory notification

Maximum reprocessing repeats per batch (examples):
  Dead-end filtration  → max 3 times
  UF/DF               → defined by validation study (usually max 1-2)
  Chromatography      → defined by resin performance data

Analogy

Reprocessing frequency is like the starting failure rate of a car. If a car occasionally needs "one more try" to start (sporadic failure), that is acceptable normal variation. But if one start attempt in ten fails, the manufacturer has to redesign the engine; it cannot rely on the manual workaround of "just try again." The regulatory logic is exactly the same.

Key Notes

Hold time is the key constraint: all reprocessing operations (including repeated executions) must be completed within the permitted intermediate hold time. If the validated hold time is exceeded, additional scientific justification is required; it must not be quietly extended.

APR/PQR is the main tool for trend monitoring: both TFDA and PIC/S require the Annual Product Review to include an evaluation of reprocessing trends. If the frequency rises, it should trigger a re-review of the process design rather than simply being recorded.

Small-scale studies must be predictive: small-scale study designs must be representative of commercial scale, including the same shear stress and the same protein concentration range; otherwise the data cannot be extrapolated to the commercial process.

3.4 Continuation vs. Reprocessing (Repetition) of a Unit Operation

Original Text

Most unit operations include some sort of in-process control measurement to determine whether the step meets the objective (e.g., a concentration, pH). An adjustment to bring a product within acceptable limits is not considered reprocessing and, instead, should be designed as a process "continuation." Procedures should account for continuation to perform an adjustment within defined limits. Without appropriate instructions, continuations in processing may be erroneously considered as reprocessing and lead to procedural deviations.

For example, during a step in the ultrafiltration-diafiltration operation, concentration is measured at different volumes to check for the desired target concentration. If the concentration does not meet the target specification, the process can be continued until the target specification is met, and it is considered a continuation of the process, not reprocessing. Similarly, in the titration process, the addition of titrant to achieve the desired pH or conductivity set point may be considered a continuation of the process. Likewise, adding a buffer dilution to achieve the target product concentration is considered a continuation of the process, not a reprocessing activity.

In the case of drug product manufacturing, when large-volume containers are thawed and only a partial amount is used for filling, the remaining formulated drug substance may need to be refrozen for subsequent use. This practice may be considered reprocessing if not included in an approved application. Supporting freeze-thaw stability data from reduced- or at-scale studies can help justify the impact on drug product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Principles for deciding between continuation and reprocessing:

  • Continuation: extending or adjusting an operation, within the framework already designed into the batch record, to reach the target value. It is part of the normal process and does not require a deviation report.
  • Reprocessing: after a process step has been completed, the result fails specification and the step must be "started over." It is a corrective action triggered by a deviation.

The key question: "Was this adjustment written in advance in an SOP or the batch record?" If yes, and within the defined limits, it is continuation; if not, or if it exceeds the design range, it is reprocessing.

Analogy

When baking a cake, the recipe says "bake for 25-35 minutes until a toothpick inserted comes out clean." Continuing to bake within that time range is continuation. But if the cake has already come out and cooled, the center turns out to be undercooked, and it has to be put back in the oven, that is reprocessing, because it goes beyond the original designed procedure.

The difference lies in "whether it occurs within the boundaries of the original process design."

Key Notes

The cost of misclassification: misclassifying continuation as reprocessing triggers unnecessary deviation investigations and regulatory submissions; conversely, misclassifying reprocessing as continuation and not reporting it is a serious GMP violation.

Special risk of freeze-thaw cycles: when a large-volume container is partially used and must be refrozen, this is a regulatory "gray area." Freeze-thaw stability data must be included in the application beforehand and approved before this can be treated as an approved process rather than reprocessing.

CDMO practical reminder: the batch record should be designed to state clearly which out-of-specification IPC results may trigger continuation and which must start the deviation procedure. This is the key document design that prevents operators from making wrong calls on the floor.

Practice Questions

  1. A UF/DF step has a target concentration of 20 mg/mL (range 18-22 mg/mL), and the batch record allows "continue ultrafiltration until 20 mg/mL is reached," but the validated maximum filtration time limit has already been exceeded. Is this continuation or reprocessing?
  2. A DS batch is held after sterile filtration (0.22 µm), and QA later decides to add another 0.22 µm refiltration as an extra safeguard. Which case is this, and why?
3.5 Quality Concerns During the Reprocessing of Biologicals

Original Text

Protein aggregation is a key concern during manufacturing and quality assessment, particularly in the context of reprocessing. Most therapeutic proteins are only marginally stable in solutions and are susceptible to chemical instabilities (e.g., deamidation, oxidation, hydrolysis) as well as physical instabilities (e.g., denaturation and noncovalent aggregation).

During reprocessing, proteins may be exposed to various stress conditions. Process controls play a critical role in mitigating the effects of destabilizing factors, such as pH, buffer components, and denaturants, that can induce protein unfolding and subsequent aggregation.

Several stress factors during reprocessing can contribute to protein aggregation and instability. Key considerations include:

  • Increased Viscosity During Reprocessing: Higher protein concentration can increase viscosity, affecting subcutaneous formulation. Evaluate using rheology or dynamic light-scattering.
  • Thermal Stress: Elevated temperatures (e.g., during viral inactivation) can promote protein denaturation and aggregation. Effects are difficult to quantify and should be controlled.
  • High Ionic Strength: Use of high ionic strength eluents (e.g., in ion exchange or hydrophobic interaction chromatography) may induce aggregation.
  • pH Sensitivity: Eluents with pH near or far from the protein's isoelectric point can promote aggregation.
  • Filtration Stress: Filtration processes may lead to aggregation due to membrane contact, air bubbles, or shear stress. Ultrafiltration at high protein concentrations adds risk, especially before filling or during buffer mixing.
  • Formulation Handling: Aggregation can be triggered by agitation, freeze-thaw cycles, and prolonged storage of bulk solutions before formulation.
  • Air-Water Interfaces: Exposure to air-water interfaces during formulation can reduce protein stability and initiate aggregation pathways. Visual inspection may be required.

Note: For factors affecting reworking, refer to Section 5.9.

Failure to manage these factors may result in out-of-specification (OOS) drug substance or drug product, visible particulates, reduced storage stability, loss of efficacy, and potentially increased immunogenicity risk (17, 18). Therefore, before performing any reprocessing, evaluating and understanding the potential impact on product quality is essential. If a reactive reprocessing strategy is used, the sampling plan should include specific analytical tests to demonstrate that reprocessing does not adversely affect the quality, safety, or efficacy of the drug substance or drug product.

"Note: For reprocessing, product release may occur in parallel with ongoing stability studies, since the process remains within the validated manufacturing design space. For reworking, stability data must be available prior to release to define retesting intervals, as the introduction of new steps is equivalent to a modified synthesis with potentially higher risk (see Section 5.0)."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Protein aggregation is the primary quality risk in reprocessing biologicals: therapeutic proteins (mAbs, fusion proteins, enzymes, etc.) are inherently marginally stable. Every additional process step is a potential protein stress exposure event.

Two types of instability mechanisms:

  • Chemical instability: deamidation, oxidation, hydrolysis; these usually become apparent only at a stage where the analytical methods can detect them.
  • Physical instability: protein denaturation and noncovalent aggregation; these are directly related to processing operations and are affected by shear, temperature, interfaces, and so on.

The patient safety link of immunogenicity: aggregates are one of the main triggers of immunogenicity. This links directly to patient safety, and FDA/EMA have clear regulatory requirements for the control of aggregates in therapeutic proteins.

Analogy

Think of a therapeutic protein as a delicate piece of origami. Each fold (process step) can make the crease sharper (quality improves), but it can also slightly tear or deform the paper (quality damage). In a normal process every step is precisely controlled and damage is minimal. But reprocessing means "folding it one more time"; if the protein molecule already shows slight structural fatigue, a second folding may make the damage more pronounced.

Key Notes

Difference in stability data requirements between reprocessing and reworking (critical!):

  • Reprocessing: product may be released in parallel with ongoing stability studies, because the step is still within the design space and the risk is relatively well known.
  • Reworking: stability data must be available before release and cannot be generated in parallel, because introducing a new step is equivalent to modifying the synthetic route and requires upfront data for confirmation.

This difference directly affects a CDMO's batch release timeline: release of a reworked batch will inevitably take longer.

Hidden risk of the air-water interface: many operators underestimate the effect of the air-liquid interface (for example, tubing filling/emptying and foam formation) on protein stability. During reprocessing in particular, vigorous agitation and introduction of air bubbles should be avoided.

Practical Application

A high-concentration mAb (150 mg/mL) is reprocessed after the UF concentration step. During the second UF cycle, viscosity rises markedly and slight visible particles appear (found at visual inspection).

Response steps:

  1. Stop the operation immediately and notify QA
  2. Sample for subvisible particles and aggregates (SE-HPLC/DLS) analysis
  3. Evaluate whether the reprocessing limit set in the batch record has been exceeded
  4. If aggregates are out of specification, trigger an OOS investigation and evaluate rejecting the batch
  5. Record the event as an input to the design of small-scale reprocessing studies (the studies must cover this high-viscosity scenario)

Practice Questions

  1. After reprocessing, SEC-HPLC for a mAb shows aggregates rising from 0.8% to 1.5% (specification ≤ 2%). Although still within specification, should you release this batch? What additional evaluations need to be considered?
  2. How does the link between immunogenicity risk and aggregates affect reprocessing decisions for clinical trial material? How should the CDMO communicate this risk to the Sponsor?

Step 1: Identifying and Evaluating Reprocessing Need
The need to reprocess may arise when a biological intermediate/drug substance/product does not conform to specifications or acceptable ranges. Review the design basis and development history for the existing unit operation to understand the designed purpose of the proposed reprocessing steps, especially the control of CQAs. Identify the in-process testing or monitoring triggers that would indicate a need for reprocessing at a given unit operation. The detection method for a failure mode that leads to reprocessing is critical to the reliability of the overall process and to the success of a reprocessing step. The method must be rapid enough to allow for timely decision-making before the opportunity to reprocess has passed. For example, integrity testing of a 0.2 µm filter would be a better detection method to support reprocessing because it can be completed in a few minutes or hours compared to microbial testing, which can take several days to complete.

Step 5: Design Reprocessing Procedure
Begin the initial design with the proposed manufacturing-scale reprocessing procedure including all equipment, process parameters, linkages to adjacent unit operations, pre-use/post-use procedures (e.g., equilibration/cleaning), and process controls. Give special attention to any procedure required to return the process intermediate and equipment to an appropriate starting condition so that the reprocessing step can be performed. This restoration step is designed to replicate the intermediate input feed-stream conditions (e.g., concentration, volume, pH, conductivity) and is not considered to be a rework step.

Step 6: Design Assessment
Consider the broader effect of the reprocessing design outside the scope of its specific unit operation; for example, facility design, pre-/post-viral segregation, linkages to adjacent unit operations, draw on utilities, and scheduling.

Step 7: Perform Quality Attribute Impact Assessment
Perform a risk assessment (6), to identify all quality attributes that may be affected by the proposed reprocessing step. In particular, consider such issues as modes of degradation, CPPs, exposure to product-contact surfaces, leachables from filters/single-use tubing, and adsorptive losses that may be impacted by an additional processing step, intermediate hold point, or freeze-thaw cycle. It is not expected that any new CQAs will be identified, but CQAs may take on a higher or lower overall risk following reprocessing (8, 9, 20, 21). Consider whether the stability of the reprocessed batch should be formally evaluated, especially upon the first instance of reprocessing.

Step 8: Reassess Criticality
Review existing criticality assessments conducted for process parameters, raw materials, equipment, in-process controls, and other factors based on the standard process to determine whether any changes or additions are required. The criticality may be influenced by the addition of a reprocessing step.

Step 9: Identify or Develop Analytical Methods
Identify or develop the analytical methods to monitor all relevant CQAs in intermediate feed-streams and/or drug substance/product. Even though no new CQAs are likely to be identified, additional analytical methods capable of monitoring all potentially impacted CQAs in the relevant matrix may be needed. Additional development and qualification may be needed to adapt methods to the reprocessed product. The risk assessment (Step 7) should determine whether additional characterization methods are required to establish comparability and acceptable product quality.

Step 10: Establish Comparability Criteria
Establish appropriate comparability criteria for all CQAs. Once CQAs and their corresponding analytical methods are identified, it may be possible to develop the key comparability criteria to assess product quality during the development of the reprocessing unit operation. All of the original drug substance or drug product specifications must continue to be met following reprocessing. Verify that product quality at intermediate points in the process meets original expectations or consider new criteria as appropriate.

Step 11: Design Scaled-Down Model
Design and qualify a scaled-down model, if applicable. To generate additional data, a scaled-down unit operation model can be adapted from the previously qualified model of the standard process. The requirements for a laboratory-scale model of a reprocessing step are the same as those used in the development of a standard manufacturing process (i.e., design a scaled-down model that is representative of the manufacturing-scale reprocessing unit operation).

Step 12: Obtain Representative Materials
Obtain representative materials for performing reprocessing at the reduced scale. If representative intermediate feed-streams from manufacturing cannot be obtained, then it may be necessary to generate the materials using a qualified scaled-down model of all, or a section of, the manufacturing-scale process. It should also be possible to recreate the failure mode in the feed-stream in a representative manner (e.g., spiking with impurities). If the failure mode is based on operational checks, such as integrity tests, rather than a product-quality excursion, then use of a standard intermediate feed-stream may be justified.

Step 13: Execute Developmental Studies
Plan and execute reduced-scale development studies, if required once the process design elements described above in Steps 4–12 are completed. The design of reprocessing development studies (e.g., using a design-of-experiments – methodology or worst-case conditions) should result in an understanding of robustness across appropriate critical and noncritical process parameter ranges. Evaluate any effects of the reprocessing step on quality attributes using the selected analytical methods. The design and scope of these studies should take into consideration all elements of the original development studies related to the unit operation, including intermediate and drug substance/product stability. Determine which prior development studies should be repeated and what additional studies (e.g., testing for new degradants) are needed.

Step 14: Assess Development Data
Upon completion of the development studies, review the generated data to evaluate whether the scaled-down process studies indicate the following:

  • All predetermined comparability criteria are met by the reprocessing procedure.
  • Process parameter operating ranges are confirmed.
  • Process intermediate, drug substance, and product stability are potentially impacted.

Step 15: Finalize Designs
Adapt GMP manufacturing-scale reprocessing procedures and equipment based upon the results of scaled-down studies. For example, if the development studies described in Step 13 indicate that process parameter ranges must be narrowed or shifted to ensure that all quality and comparability criteria are met, then it may be necessary to modify the manufacturing-scale process design and control strategy to reflect this new process understanding.

Step 16: Transfer Technology to Manufacturing
Execute technology transfer according to company policies and procedures. Transfer of the reprocessing step to manufacturing should consider scale-up issues and implementation of associated GMP procedures. For more complex reprocessing activities, an intermediate pilot-scale process or non-GMP "engineering run" may also be beneficial to ensure a successful transfer into the GMP manufacturing environment (see Section 3.8 for more information on contamination control strategies).

Step 17: Validation, Comparability and Stability Protocols
Write formal prospective process validation, comparability, and stability protocols for manufacturing-scale reprocessing. Validation considerations include the number of lots of resin or other raw materials required, the number of runs needed, and the additional nonroutine sampling and testing, beginning with consideration of the original validation of the standard process. Additional validation and comparability run(s) at manufacturing scale may be supported by the reduced-scale data generated during development. Real-time stability studies are considered to be a holistic test of recombinant proteins and, therefore, are often used to document that no changes to the protein have occurred. After validation of the reprocessed unit operation and subsequent license approval, it may not be necessary for reprocessed lots to undergo further stability testing.

Step 18: Regulatory Submission
For approval of the reprocessing step, include the supportive scaled-down development data and prospective validation/comparability/stability protocols in the submission (3, 8, 9). If using the proactive approach, file these elements in the initial marketing application:

  • Output of the quality attribute impact assessment (Step 7), criticality reassessment (Step 8), and results of development studies (Step 13) in Module 3.2.S.2.6
  • Manufacturing-scale reprocessing protocols in Module 3.2.S.2.5, including triggers, tests/acceptance criteria, and stability commitments
  • Any other agency commitments or strategies for implementation
  • Description of the validated reprocessing step in Module 3.2.S.2.2

Step 19: Monitor and Investigate Triggering Event
Monitor failure modes for signs that the manufacturing process might need reprocessing or, alternatively, decide to proactively carry out reprocessing to collect data for a submission. Regardless of whether the reprocessing is prospectively designed, it is essential to thoroughly investigate the root cause of any triggering event and its impact on product quality following GMP procedures and the principles of ICH Q9(R1): Quality Risk Management (6). The investigation outcome, along with CAPAs, should be considered in relation to process validation, regulatory commitments (Step 20), and reprocessing design (Steps 5 and 6) (23).

Step 20: Validate and Release
For each reprocessing batch, follow established GMP procedures for executing validation, comparability, and stability protocols, and ensuring all acceptance criteria, including release-testing, are met. Document validation and comparability results in a final report, completing change control and regulatory commitments before releasing the reprocessed batch.

Key Concepts

Design logic of the 20-step framework: the steps are not a mandatory linear sequence; TR74 explicitly states that it does not prescribe a fixed order. This means:

  • in early development, one can proceed in sequence from Step 1;
  • when a reprocessing plan is added after commercialization, one can start from Step 6;
  • a reactive scenario can also borrow some steps of this framework (but is limited by time).

The concept of the restoration step: the restoration step mentioned in Step 5 means returning the system to appropriate starting conditions before the reprocessing is performed (for example, re-equilibrating a chromatography column). This step does not itself count as rework, because it is within the design scope of the reprocessing step.

CTD module correspondence (key point of Step 18):

  • Module 3.2.S.2.2 → process description (including the description of the reprocessing procedure)
  • Module 3.2.S.2.5 → process validation/evaluation (including the reprocessing validation plan)
  • Module 3.2.S.2.6 → process development data (including the results of the QA impact assessment)

Analogy

These 20 steps are like the standard process for constructing a new building: first the geological assessment (Steps 1-3), drawing the design (Steps 4-6), testing building materials (Steps 7-13), revising the design (Steps 14-15), construction handover (Step 16), fire safety acceptance (Step 17), applying to the authority for the occupancy permit (Step 18), and then moving into routine maintenance and emergency response (Steps 19-20). Each stage has its own logical order, but it can be adjusted flexibly according to the project.

Key Notes

The timeliness principle of Step 4 (trigger detection speed): the trigger detection method must be fast enough. A filter integrity test (minutes to hours) is better than a microbial test (days). This principle directly affects the choice of IPC methods in a proactive design.

Discussion focus on stability studies in Step 17: TR74 allows that, once validation is complete and regulatory approval has been obtained, reprocessed batches need not undergo stability testing every time, but the first reprocessed batch is usually recommended for inclusion in stability. The scope of the stability commitment should be confirmed with FDA/TFDA before submission.

Special reminder for CDMOs: if the CDMO carries out, as an outsourced service for the Sponsor, the development studies for the reprocessing step, the division of documentation responsibilities in technology transfer (Step 16) (who retains the master batch record, and who provides the technical data package) must be clearly defined in the contract.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Core differences between reactive and proactive submissions:

  • Proactive: the reprocessing plan is included in the initial marketing application in advance and approved beforehand.
  • Reactive: a supplemental application is submitted after the fact, and there must be parallel direct communication with FDA/the competent authority to obtain "permission to use the specific batch."

The dual track of reactive submissions: the supplemental application (amending the CMC package) and the batch use approval are two independent but parallel procedures; it is not enough to do only one of them.

Key Notes

Practical risk for CDMOs: the release timeline of a reactively reprocessed batch cannot be predicted, because the response of the regulatory authority must be awaited. For products with tight supply (orphan drugs, breakthrough therapies), a proactive plan should be established first, so that regulatory timelines do not cause supply interruption.

Points to note under the TFDA framework: TFDA's review timelines for supplemental applications may differ from FDA's. A CDMO manufacturing in Taiwan and supplying several markets at the same time needs to know in advance the type of supplemental application in each market (for example CBE-30, PAS) and its review timeline, so that supply planning can be coordinated overall.

Role of interim stability data: in a reactive submission, if formal stability data are not yet complete, interim stability data may be submitted first, but a commitment must be made to submit the long-term stability results.

3.8 Impact of Contamination Control Strategy during Reprocessing

Original Text

As discussed above, it is critical to ensure microbial controls are considered and protected during reprocessing events. The reprocessed biological drug substances and sterile drug products should adhere to all CQAs associated with bioburden and endotoxins.

During the reprocessing of a sterile biological drug substance and drug product, European Union (EU) Annex 1 (20), provides general guidance that should be used in the design and control of facilities, equipment, systems, and procedures used for the manufacture of all sterile products. The principles of quality risk management (QRM) should be applied to ensure that microbial, particulate, and endotoxin/pyrogen contamination is prevented in the final drug substance and drug products. QRM is also applicable to low-bioburden biological intermediates and drug substances (20).

Critical aspects considered in QRM include, but are not limited to:

  • Process step where reprocessing occurs
  • Time of product exposure (open operations)
  • Air classification of the reprocessing work zone (where the product is exposed in absence of closed system)
  • Particulate counts and bioburden trends
  • Training records of personnel carrying out reprocessing
  • Sanitization and disinfection procedures
  • Material movement and area segregation controls (e.g. CCS impact, pre/post viral areas)
  • Hold times during reprocessing
  • Line clearance before and after reprocessing
  • Cleaning and/or change over limits, in cases of multiproduct facilities
  • Filter validation studies (critical on operating ranges while handling concentrated protein solutions)
  • Sampling techniques and number of samples taken for studies

The Quality Control Unit should establish a more comprehensive monitoring program for operators involved in reprocessing operations, which are especially labor-intensive and require complex manipulations and time criticality.

Processes, equipment, facilities, and manufacturing activities should be managed in accordance with QRM principles to provide a proactive means of identifying, scientifically evaluating, and controlling potential risks to quality. Where alternative approaches are used, these should be supported by appropriate rationale, risk assessment, and mitigation and should adhere to principles of CCS. A combination of QRM and a contamination control strategy (CCS) should be established for a robust assurance of contamination prevention during reprocessing. The CCS should be actively reviewed and, where appropriate, updated, and it should drive continual improvement of the manufacturing and control methods. (See PDA Technical Report No. 90: Contamination Control Strategy Development in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing for further details (21, 24), Contamination Control Strategies (CCS) in Low Bioburden Biologic Drug Substance Manufacturing (25), and Contamination Control Strategy Guidance (26).)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

CCS (Contamination Control Strategy) is a proactive management framework, not a passive checklist. The 2022 version of EU Annex 1 formally requires all sterile product manufacturers to establish a written CCS, which must be reviewed and updated regularly.

Reprocessing places additional challenges on the CCS: compared with routine batches, reprocessing typically involves:

  • longer product exposure time
  • more equipment openings and disassembly/assembly operations
  • unplanned operator interventions
  • complex manual operations under time pressure

Each of these factors increases the risk of microbial, endotoxin, and particulate contamination.

Importance of pre/post-viral area segregation: particularly critical for biologicals. Segregation of areas before and after the viral removal/inactivation step is a mandatory GMP requirement, and the reprocessing design must ensure that this segregation principle is not violated.

Analogy

CCS in reprocessing is like infection control during emergency surgery in a hospital. Routine surgery has a standard pre-operative preparation process, but emergency surgery (analogous to reactive reprocessing) is more prone to infection control lapses under time pressure, and therefore needs an even stricter monitoring program so that basic protection is not sacrificed to urgency.

Key Notes

Direct requirements of EU Annex 1 2022: although TR74 cites EU Annex 1, for CDMOs under TFDA/PIC/S jurisdiction the content of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) is almost identical. Section 4 of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) requires all sterile manufacturers to establish a CCS and to explicitly cover contamination risks related to reprocessing. (PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 4)

Strengthened QC monitoring program requirements: TR74 explicitly states that operators performing reprocessing need a "more comprehensive monitoring program." This includes: more frequent gowning qualification, glove integrity testing, increased environmental monitoring sampling, and re-confirmation of personnel qualification.

Additional considerations for multiproduct facilities: a CDMO is usually a multiproduct facility, so the standards for cleaning validation and line clearance after reprocessing must be stricter to prevent cross-contamination.

Decision hierarchy reminder: in CCS design, sterility assurance always takes priority over timeline and cost considerations. If reprocessing cannot be performed while maintaining sterility assurance, the batch should be rejected.

Practical Application — CDMO CCS Design

A fill-finish CDMO finds that the filter integrity test fails after the final sterilizing filtration step (post-use integrity failure). The filter must be replaced and the product refiltered in a grade B environment.

CCS reinforcement measures (reprocessing scenario):

  • Confirm that the filter replacement is carried out under grade A local protection
  • Additional environmental monitoring: one active air sampling before and one after the replacement
  • Operator qualification: confirm that the most recent gowning qualification has been completed
  • Pre-use integrity test (PUPSIT) of the new filter after replacement
  • Record all time points, operators, and equipment lot numbers
  • Increased sterility test samples for the final batch (enhanced sterility sampling)

  • Fundamental difference between proactive and reactive: with the proactive approach the reprocessing plan is pre-approved at the time of the marketing application and is executed directly when triggered; the reactive approach is a response after the fact, requiring a supplemental application and additional communication with the regulatory authority. 4.0 Introduction to the Regulatory Framework

Original Text

Guidelines published by ICH contain harmonized guidance specifically for to-be-marketed and marketed pharmaceuticals, including biologicals, specifically for submissions involving reprocessing of biologicals. Some of the common characteristics harmonized to enable efficient reprocessing submissions include:

  • To-Be-Marketed: Initial market application to contain details of any anticipated future reprocessing in the relevant sections of the Common Technical Document (CTD) referred to as a Post-Approval Change Management Protocol (PACMP). Allowing this inclusion of reprocessing protocols for relevant steps provides reprocessing flexibility and minimizes post-approval reporting.
  • Marketed: Post-market clear guidance and well-defined timeframes for review and approval of Post-Approval Changes (PAC), as well as PACMPs.
  • Acceptance of science- and risk-based approaches for developing data to support PACs and PACMPs.
  • Relatively fewer requests for additional documentation not included in CTD Module 3.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PACMP (Post-Approval Change Management Protocol): when first applying for marketing authorization (NDA/BLA/MAA), reprocessing steps that may be performed in the future, and their acceptance criteria, are declared proactively in the CTD. After the authority has reviewed them in advance, if reprocessing actually occurs later, only an Annual Report (AR) or CBE-level filing is needed, rather than a PAS. This is the core strategy of proactive planning to reduce future regulatory burden.

CTD (Common Technical Document): the globally harmonized submission format defined by ICH M4. Module 3 contains the CMC data and is the main place where the reprocessing protocol is held.

Analogy

A PACMP is like an architect obtaining a "pre-approval for future renovation" while the design drawings are being reviewed. After the house is built, renovations that follow the approved drawings need no new building permit application, only a filing. If the owner renovates without prior review, a full re-review is required (= PAS). For biologicals reprocessing, this time difference can mean months or even a year of delay.

Key Notes

The core benefit of the ICH harmonized framework: one PACMP can support both an FDA BLA and an EMA MAA, greatly reducing the documentation rework for a CDMO serving Sponsors in multiple markets. Taiwan's TFDA accepts PIC/S standards, and PIC/S mainly follows the EMA framework. Therefore a PACMP should be designed using EMA requirements as the baseline, and FDA compatibility then confirmed.

4.2 Regulatory Guidance (Summary of ICH Guidelines)

Original Text

See Table 4.2-1 below for a comprehensive overview of guidance on reprocessing/reworking across relevant ICH guidelines. Key ICH guidelines covered include ICH Q7 (GMP for APIs), ICH Q9(R1) (Quality Risk Management), and ICH Q13 (Continuous Manufacturing).

Under ICH Q7, the following principles apply:

  • Reprocessing of APIs: Repeating a crystallization step or other appropriate chemical or physical manipulation steps is generally considered acceptable. However, if such reprocessing is used for a majority of batches, it should be included as part of the standard manufacturing process.
  • Continuation after in-process control: Continuation of a process step after an in-process control test has shown that the step is incomplete is considered to be part of the normal process — NOT reprocessing.
  • Unreacted material: Introducing unreacted material back into a process and repeating a chemical reaction is considered reprocessing unless it is part of the established process. Such reprocessing should be preceded by careful evaluation to ensure product quality is not adversely impacted.

Under ICH Q7 Reworking:

  • Before a decision is taken to rework batches, an investigation into the reason for non-conformance should be performed.
  • Reworked batches should be subjected to appropriate evaluation, testing, stability testing if warranted, and documentation.
  • Concurrent validation is often the appropriate validation approach for rework procedures.
  • Procedures should provide for comparing the impurity profile of each reworked batch against batches manufactured by the established process.

Under ICH Q7 Q&A: It may be acceptable to reprocess or rework expired API where the API manufacturer has all related historical GMP documentation and additional stability data. There may be registration/filing considerations beyond the scope of ICH Q7.

Under ICH Q9(R1): Quality Risk Management applies to materials management — assessment and evaluation of suppliers and contract manufacturers, use of materials, and to determine appropriateness of reprocessing, reworking, use of returned goods.

Under ICH Q13 (Continuous Manufacturing): In the event of a filter failure, a clear strategy for material diversion and refiltration (reprocessing) should be defined. Filtration steps in CM may be subject to longer filtration periods — control strategy must allow for filter changes and pre/post-use integrity testing without interrupting the process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

An important distinction in ICH Q7: "continuing to perform a process step that has not yet been completed" ≠ reprocessing. For example, if filtration has not yet reached the target flow, continuing to filter is normal processing; but refiltration after a filtration failure is reprocessing. This distinction is critical for CDMOs performing sterile filling steps.

Concurrent validation: instead of waiting for three complete validation batches, validation is performed at the same time as the reworked batch is produced. It is suitable for one-off or very few rework batches, but the acceptance criteria must be defined in advance.

Analogy

The ICH Q7 distinction between reprocessing and normal processing is like a restaurant chef:
• The steak is not yet cooked, so keep grilling → normal processing (not reprocessing)
• The steak is burnt, so cut off the charred crust and re-season → reworking
• The steak is about to burn, so take it off, let it cool, and grill it again → reprocessing (acceptable if defined in an SOP beforehand)

Key Notes

Reprocessing of expired API (ICH Q7 Q&A): technically feasible, but the following must all be met: (1) a complete GMP history record, (2) additional stability data, and (3) confirmation of whether a supplementary filing is needed. If a Taiwan CDMO provides this kind of service for a Sponsor, responsibility must be clearly assigned in the QTA: the filing obligation remains with the MAH (marketing authorization holder), not with the CMO.

ICH Q13 and continuous manufacturing: as continuous manufacturing (CM) technology is introduced for biologicals, the refiltration strategy after a filtration failure must be predefined in the CPMP (Continuous Process Monitoring Plan) and cannot be remedied after the fact.

Practical Application

At a Taiwan CDMO, the integrity test fails after sterile filtration of a bulk compounded batch. In this situation:
(1) If a pre-approved refiltration protocol already exists in the BLA or PACMP → it is reprocessing (approved protocol), with a lower reporting category (AR or CBE)
(2) If there is no pre-approved protocol → it is reprocessing without an approved protocol → PAS level (FDA approval required before release)
Decision principle: sterility assurance comes first → when uncertain, confirm with the regulatory authority

4.3 Specific Guidance from the United States (FDA)

Original Text

In addition to implementation of the ICH Guidelines, the FDA provides general guidance for biologicals during the clinical investigation period and specific guidance for biologicals during the post-market approval period.

4.3.1 During Clinical Investigation Period

In 2008, FDA published Guidance for Industry: CGMP for Phase 1 Investigational Drugs that stressed the importance of documenting how the manufacturing process was carried out during the clinical investigational period:

  • A record of manufacturing data that details the materials, equipment, procedures used, and any problems encountered during manufacturing. Manufacturers should retain records sufficient to replicate the manufacturing process.
  • A record of changes in procedures and processes used for subsequent batches along with the rationale for any changes.

Since reprocessing and/or reworking are significant changes, they are required to be reported to the FDA under an information amendment to the Investigational New Drug (IND) Application on file to carry out the human clinical trials (21 CFR 312.31). Although there is no required time period of delay of implementation for these information amendments, manufacturers typically wait for a period of time to allow the FDA reviewers opportunity to review the amendment.

Note: "Consider the impact of changes to be discussed with regulatory authorities in order to determine the need for additional toxicology studies."

FDA in 2020 published Guidance for Industry: CMC Information for Human Gene Therapy Investigational New Drug Applications (INDs). While there is no mention of reworking, the following guidance for reprocessing applies:

  • Drug Substance: For early stage INDs, there may be differences between manufacturing of toxicology lots and clinical study material. For later stage INDs, process changes or reprocessing step(s) may exist. Describe how manufacturing differences are expected to impact product safety and activity; provide batch analysis in CTD Section 3.2.S.4.4.
  • Drug Product: Under certain conditions, repeating sterile filtration (if applicable) may be critical. Clearly describe the conditions and justifications for each reprocessing procedure and demonstrate product consistency between reprocessed lots and normal production lots.

4.3.2 Post-Market Approval Period

Under 21 CFR 601.12, each post-approval change in the production process established in the approved BLA must be reported using the submission type associated with one of the three tier-based reporting categories. This depends on the potential (i.e., risk) of the change to have an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product. The submission types are:

  • Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): Change that has a substantial potential to have an adverse effect on product quality (major changes). A PAS must be approved by FDA prior to distribution of the product manufactured using the change (21 CFR 601.12(b)).
  • Changes Being Effected in 30 Days / CBE (CBE-30/CBE): Changes that have a moderate potential to have an adverse effect on product quality. Report at least 30 days prior to distribution (21 CFR 601.12(c)).
  • Annual Report (AR): Changes that have a minimal potential to have an adverse effect. Documented by the applicant in an annual report (21 CFR 601.12(d)).

In 1997, FDA published Guidance for Industry: Changes to an Approved Application for Specified Biotechnology and Specified Synthetic Biological Products. Key provision: reprocessing without a previously approved reprocessing protocol is considered a change with substantial potential to adversely affect product quality, safety, or effectiveness — requiring a PAS.

In 2021, FDA published Guidance for Industry: Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Changes to an Approved Application: Certain Biological Products (scope includes vaccines, plasma-derived protein products, cellular and gene therapy products). Table 4.3.2-1 lists the guidance provided for both reprocessing and reworking. See Table 4.3.2-1 below.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

IND Information Amendment: during clinical trials, if reprocessing or reworking occurs in the manufacturing process, an information amendment under 21 CFR 312.31 must be submitted to FDA. Key point: there is no mandatory waiting period, but industry practice is to allow FDA an opportunity to review before continuing. This differs from the mandatory wait for a post-approval PAS.

The three-tier reporting structure of 21 CFR 601.12:

  • PAS (Prior Approval Supplement) = major change; FDA approval must be awaited (usually 6–12 months)
  • CBE-30 = moderate change; release is possible 30 days after submission
  • AR (Annual Report) = minor change; disclosed together in the annual report

Key Notes

Key ruling: no pre-approved protocol = PAS
Under the 1997 FDA guidance, reprocessing without a previously approved protocol is classified directly as a PAS. This is the most common practical trap: if a CDMO performs refiltration in an emergency and the BLA does not contain this protocol, a PAS is triggered, and the batch must be held until FDA approval.
Countermeasure: cover all anticipated reprocessing steps in advance through a PACMP.

Practical Application — Taiwan CDMO Serving a US Sponsor

Analogy

PAS / CBE-30 / AR are like building renovation permits:
• PAS = major structural change; a full re-review of the building permit is needed (wait for approval before starting work)
• CBE-30 = medium interior renovation; work may start after 30 days if there is no objection
• AR = minor repair; just file it with the year-end report
The CDMO's job is to include all the "renovations that may be needed" in the permit at the design stage (PACMP), so as to avoid facing the long PAS wait in an emergency.

For a CDMO whose manufacturing site is in Taiwan, TFDA regulations mainly follow the PIC/S GMP requirements (EMA framework). However, if the Sponsor is a US company applying for an FDA BLA, the batch reporting obligations are carried out under 21 CFR 601.12 and borne by the MAH (the BLA holder, usually the Sponsor). The CDMO must specify clearly in the QTA (Quality Technical Agreement) within how many working days the CDMO must notify the Sponsor when reprocessing occurs, so that the Sponsor can decide on the reporting category.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

IMPD (Investigational Medicinal Product Dossier): the EU equivalent of the FDA IND. During clinical trials, if reprocessing occurs in the manufacturing process, it must be assessed whether it constitutes a "substantial modification"; if so, it must be notified to the competent authorities of the Member States.

  • Type II (major change) ≈ FDA PAS
  • Type IB (minor change) ≈ FDA CBE-30
  • Type IA (minimal change) ≈ FDA AR (but with different timing: to be notified within 12 months)

Key Notes — EMA's Position on Reworking

The EMA guidance (the 2022 clinical-phase guideline and the Variation Regulation for the commercial phase) does not mention reworking at all. This is not an oversight but the EMA position: reworking of biologicals is regarded as unacceptable or an extreme exception, and the default is to require case-by-case consultation with EMA, with no standardized reporting pathway.

By comparison, the 2021 FDA guidance also lists N/A for reworking, but at least shows it explicitly in the table, implying that case-by-case evaluation is needed. EMA does not mention it at all, indicating a more conservative attitude.

Analogy — Cultural Differences between EMA and FDA

FDA's attitude toward reprocessing/reworking is more "engineering-oriented": as long as sufficient data support it, a reporting pathway can be found. EMA is more "precautionary-principle-oriented": reworking of a biological is almost equivalent to remanufacturing and is preferably avoided.

When a Taiwan CDMO serves both the FDA and EMA markets, it should use the stricter EMA position as the design baseline: if the EMA "exceptional circumstances" requirement can be met, FDA can usually accept it as well. The reverse is not true.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PIC/S (Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-Operation Scheme): currently has 57 member regulatory authorities, including TFDA (Taiwan), TGA (Australia), Health Canada, and others. The PIC/S GMP Guide mainly follows the structure of the EMA GMP guidelines and is regularly updated in step with the EMA Annexes. TFDA GMP requirements are therefore essentially highly consistent with those of the EMA.

Key Notes — The Golden Rule

The Note at the end of this section is the most important operating principle of the whole chapter:

"If reprocessing or reworking is not specifically mentioned in the submitted manufacturing process documentation, it should be correctly assumed that it has not been approved."

This means: silence ≠ permission. When in doubt, the authority must be contacted for confirmation, rather than proceeding and reporting afterwards. Before accepting a Sponsor's authorization, the CDMO should confirm whether the BLA/MAA/IND already contains explicit reprocessing provisions.

WHO / PIC/S / BioPhorum are like the ratings and rules of an international food delivery platform (Uber Eats):
• FDA = law of the US market (mandatory)

A Taiwan CDMO that serves customers in both the FDA and EMA "markets" must comply with both sets of laws, and use PIC/S standards to ensure its own GMP status.

5.0 Introduction to Reworking

Original Text

It is the primary responsibility of the Manufacturing Authorization Holder (MAH) of a biological drug substance and/or drug product to consistently meet all quality, safety, purity, and identity requirements. This authoring team has strict recommendations on how and when such a reworking action should be considered by the MAH. The ultimate approval of a reworked drug substance and/or drug product for human use is solely at the discretion of the approving regulatory agency.

ICH and EU define reworking as:

"Subjecting an intermediate or API that does not conform to standards or specifications to one or more processing steps that are different from the established manufacturing process to obtain acceptable quality intermediate or API." (ICH Q7; EU GMP)

During reworking, the nonconforming material is subjected to a different manufacturing process to get a product that meets all the predefined acceptance criteria and specifications for quality, safety, purity, and identity (QSIP).

The scope of reworking for biologicals includes a diverse array of therapeutic proteins, each with unique challenges and considerations for manufacturing and quality control including, among others:

  • Interferons
  • Cytokines
  • Growth factors
  • Interleukins
  • Coagulation factors
  • Blood factors
  • Monoclonal antibodies (IgM, IgD, IgG, IgE, IgA)
  • Bispecific antibodies (IgG-based bsAbs, fragment-based bsAbs)
  • Antibody drug conjugates targeting cancer therapy
  • Fusion proteins
  • Glycoprotein hormones (e.g., rhCG, rFSH)
  • Insulins
  • Vaccines

Each of these therapeutic categories requires specific attention to factors like protein stability, aggregation prevention, and purification processes to ensure product purity, quality, efficacy, and safety.

Due to the intricate sourcing, including the nature of their manufacturing processes and stability concerns, cell and gene therapies are excluded from the scope for reworking. The complexity of these treatments often involves different considerations that require specialized processes and controls distinct from those applicable to the protein-based biologicals listed above.

MAH (Marketing Authorization Holder): The organization with ultimate regulatory responsibility for the quality, safety, and efficacy of a medicinal product throughout its lifecycle. In a CDMO model, the MAH is usually the sponsor, not the CMO itself.

QSIP: Quality, Safety, Purity, and Identity: the four core axes for judging drug product quality.

Core definition of reworking: Subjecting a nonconforming intermediate or API to one or more steps that are different from the established manufacturing process. This "difference" is the dividing line between reworking and reprocessing; see Section 5.2.

Analogy

Imagine a batch of premium whisky casks whose spirit fails the quality standard because of a temperature control error during aging.

  • Reprocessing = putting the spirit back into the original type of cask and repeating the original aging procedure → the same steps, with a known outcome.
  • Reworking = deciding to use a cask finish (wine cask finish), a new step outside the original process plan, to correct it → a different step, an unknown risk → regulatory approval must be obtained in advance.

For a biological product, this "whisky" is a living protein molecule with far less tolerance for error than a spirit, and every additional step may affect immunogenicity.

Key Notes

Cell and gene therapy (CGT) is outside the scope of reworking. TR74 explicitly excludes CGT because of its process complexity, the particular nature of living cells and viral vectors, and the possibility that the reworking operation itself could irreversibly alter the product. If a CGT batch is OOS at a CDMO, reject it; do not attempt reworking.

Decision framework: sterility assurance > product CQAs > commercial considerations. Reworking must never be triggered by batch cost alone; there must be a clear patient supply need driving it.

Original Text

1. Gather Relevant Data and Stakeholders

  • Collect all information related to the reworking process, including technical assessments, quality data, regulatory considerations, and risk analyses.
  • Assemble a cross-functional team comprising manufacturing, quality assurance, regulatory affairs, and process development experts.

  • Clearly specify which aspects of the biological drug substance are being considered for rework (e.g., purification, formulation, in-process controls, final product adjustments).

3. Identify Rework Tasks or Issues

  • List all potential rework activities, corrections, or modifications needed to meet quality and regulatory standards.

  • Must have (M): Critical for human survival, compliance, safety, efficacy, or regulatory approval. These are nonnegotiable and should be completed before proceeding.

  • Consider Could-Have tasks, if time and resources allow, to enhance overall process robustness.
  • Leave Won't-Have tasks for future considerations or continuous improvement.

6. Document and Communicate

  • Record the prioritization outcomes clearly, including rationales for each category.
  • Communicate the priorities to all relevant teams and stakeholders to ensure aligned execution.

7. Implement and Monitor

  • Proceed with rework activities according to the established priorities.
  • Monitor progress and adjust priorities if new information or issues arise.

8. Additional Measures

  • Ensure compliance with GMP and regulatory requirements throughout the rework process.
  • Use risk assessments to support the categorization, especially for CQAs.

Key Concepts

The MoSCoW method originated in agile software development. TR74 introduces it into the GMP reworking decision framework, emphasizing a patient-centric order of priority rather than one driven by commercial pressure or cost recovery.

What the four levels mean in GMP terms:

  • Must-have: critical to patient survival, regulatory compliance, or product safety and efficacy → without it the batch cannot be released
  • Should-have: a critical supply need, but with some time flexibility → must be done, but can be scheduled after the Must-haves
  • Could-have: strengthens process robustness and supports specific patient populations (rare disease, cancer)
  • Won't-have: out of scope this time → move into the CAPA or CI (continual improvement) program

Imagine the emergency command center of a city after an earthquake, deciding how to assign rescue teams:

Key Concepts

This is the most central pair of definitions in TR74. Mastering these two tables means mastering 90% of the regulatory logic of GMP reworking.

Simplest way to remember:

  • Reprocessing = a repeated step within the existing process → may already be covered by advance regulatory authorization
  • Reworking = a new step outside the existing process → a regulatory filing must be submitted and approved before execution

Analogy

Think of the manufacturing process as a chef's standard recipe (the SOP):

  • Reprocessing: the soup is too bland, so simmer the whole pot again (the recipe already contains this step; you simply repeat it)
  • Reworking: the soup is too strong, so add an ingredient that is not in the recipe to neutralize it (new step → the recipe has changed → the head chef (the regulator) must sign off)

Key Notes: Special Features of Drug Product

Table 5.2-2 points out an important rule that is often overlooked:

DP (drug product) can usually not be "reworked," only "reprocessed."

For example, if aseptic filling is interrupted by an equipment failure, re-sterile filtration followed by refilling is allowed (reprocessing). But you cannot add a "new step outside the original process" onto product already filled; that would be changing the drug product itself.

For a sterile filling CDMO, the conclusion for a DP-level OOS is, in the great majority of cases, to reject the batch, not to rework it.

Quick Comparison Summary

Reprocessing → repeats an existing step → can be pre-authorized in the license
Reworking    → introduces a new step   → must be submitted and approved in advance as a PAS / Type II

Reprocessing → includes nonconformances caused by equipment failure
Reworking    → limited to quality nonconformances of batches not caused by equipment failure

Reprocessing → if most batches use it, it should be incorporated into the standard process
Reworking    → unique to each event; should not become routine

5.3 Background to Manufacturing Changes

Original Text

Reworking is an unexpected occurrence and is not preapproved as part of the marketing authorization. The MAH's Quality Unit should oversee investigations into a nonconforming (OOS) in-process drug, a bulk process intermediate (final biological bulk intermediate), a drug substance of a single batch or lot to an alternate manufacturing process during reworking decisions, prior to release to ensure that the details of rework in question has been submitted to the regulatory agency and formal approval has been obtained.

Changes that have a substantial potential to have an adverse effect on product quality (i.e., major variations) require the manufacturer (applicant) to report the change to the FDA in a supplement to the approved BLA. A PAS (Prior Approval Supplement) must be approved by the FDA prior to distribution of the product manufactured using the change to protect the public from products whose identity, strength, quality, purity, potency, safety, or effectiveness may be compromised (10, 33, 46).

If biological drug products are reworked, they must be reported in a supplement to FDA prior to distribution, unless the reworking was done according to a procedure previously approved by the Agency. The type of notification is based on the potential risk of the change having an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product as it may relate to the safety or effectiveness of the product (24).

The Variation Regulation is supplemented by the European Commission's Guidelines on the Details of the Various Categories of Variations, on the Operation of the Procedures Laid Down in Chapters II, IIa, III and IV of Commission Regulation No 1234/2008 of 24 November 2008. The EU guidelines define a major variation of Type II as one that is not an extension of the Marketing Authorization and that may have a significant impact on the quality, safety, or efficacy of a medicinal product (37).

Key Notes: CDMO Responsibility Boundaries

Who submits the PAS / Type II Variation?

The MAH (usually the sponsor), not the CDMO. The CDMO's role is to:

  1. Notify the sponsor QU immediately on detecting a nonconformance
  2. Provide technical data and assessment support
  3. Draft the reworking protocol (reviewed by the sponsor QU)
  4. Execute the reworking after regulatory approval has been obtained
  5. Never initiate or carry out reworking without sponsor authorization

If a CDMO initiates reworking on its own without sponsor or regulatory approval, the regulatory consequences fall on the MAH (sponsor), but liability for breach of the commercial contract points to the CDMO.

Key Concepts

Proactive Reworking Agreement (PRA): The overall process in which, before any reworking operation starts, the MAH proactively consults the regulatory authority, submits a detailed reworking protocol (PACMP), obtains prior approval, and only then carries out the reworking.

Figure Analysis: Figure 5.4-1

The Proactive Reworking Agreement (PRA) flowchart shows a linear decision path of nine steps; the key gates are:

  • Gate at Steps 1-2: QU investigates the root cause → decides to reject the batch or start the reworking assessment
  • Gate at Steps 3-4: QU detailed assessment → draft the reworking protocol (PACMP format)
  • Gate at Step 5: formal submission (PAS/Type II) → wait for regulatory approval (no reworking operation may be performed before this step is complete)
  • Gate at Step 6: regulatory review questions and answers → MAH responds → prior approval obtained
  • Gate at Steps 7-9: execution → evaluation → final submission for release

The core spirit of the whole PRA process: ask first, act later. In the GMP world, "act first, report afterward" is not acceptable for reworking.

Analogy

The PRA is like the pre-operative discussion and informed consent process before surgery:

  • The doctor (MAH/CMO) cannot skip the pre-operative assessment and operate straight away just because the patient's condition is urgent
  • The doctor must first explain to the patient's family (the regulatory authority) why surgery is needed, how it will be done, and what outcome is expected
  • Only after informed consent (approval) is obtained can the surgery (the reworking) be performed
  • After the surgery, a complete operative report (the final submission) must still be documented

Key Notes: Practical Pain Points for CDMOs

The step in the PRA process most prone to problems is between Steps 1 and 3:

During the "investigation waiting period" after an OOS is found, if the biological molecule is not held under optimal conditions (non-validated hold time, temperature excursions), the protein structure may already have changed irreversibly. TR74 states explicitly that if the QU judges that "protein integrity has been compromised during the investigation waiting period," the whole reworking request may be rejected outright by the QU (the Note in Step 3).

Practical conclusion: one focus of routine GMP management at a CDMO is to ensure that hold condition records during OOS investigations are complete and traceable; this is a precondition for reworking to be feasible.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: At 4 p.m. on a Friday, a Phase III clinical mAb batch is found after purification to have host cell protein (HCP) above the limit. QC confirms the test was valid, and the QU opens an investigation. What should be done under the PRA process?

  1. Quarantine the batch immediately and confirm that the hold conditions comply with the validated DS storage specification (the hold time must not be exceeded)
  2. Notify the sponsor QU (the MAH) and start a joint investigation in parallel
  3. Confirm the root cause: degraded chromatography resin performance? A buffer preparation error? Or an analytical method problem?
  4. If, once the root cause is confirmed, the assessment indicates that an additional purification step could reduce HCP → start PRA Step 3 (detailed assessment)
  5. Do not perform any reworking operation until sponsor authorization has been obtained and the PAS has been submitted and approved by FDA

Original Text

ICH Quality Guideline Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System was the first official global guideline known to firmly position QRM (Quality Risk Management) and knowledge management (KM) as important enablers of an effective pharmaceutical quality system (PQS). Specifically, QRM and KM jointly enable (i) achieving product realization, (ii) establishing and maintaining a state of control, (iii) facilitating continual improvement, and (iv) critical decision-making during the reworking strategy (57).

ICH Q9(R1) emphasizes the relationship between risk and knowledge, and it highlights the importance of KM in managing risks. It places knowledge and KM repeatedly and explicitly at the core of all QRM activities, further reinforcing the connection of scientific knowledge and risk in several other regulatory guidance documents. Specifically, the definition of risk-based decision-making reads:

"An approach to, or a process of, making decisions that considers knowledge about risks relevant to the decision and whether risks are at an acceptable level." (ICH Q9(R1))

The Risk Knowledge Infinity Cycle (RKI Cycle) establishes and promotes the following concepts:

  • Knowledge is both an input to and an output from QRM
  • Knowledge has an inverse relationship with risk
  • Knowledge should flow effortlessly and on demand to inform risk and risk-based decisions
  • The Cycle is continuous and perpetual

The RKI Cycle can be applied to support the goals of the PQS as defined in ICH Q10. A set of six principles corresponding with six "nodes" along the Cycle has been developed to better guide the tangible connection between QRM and KM practices as illustrated in Figure 5.5-1 (58).

Knowledge
Prior knowledge is a critical tool to identify and justify quality during the critical planning, decision-making, and execution of the OOS of the biological drug substance or a drug product to be reworked. Three forms of knowledge are critical:

  1.   1. The evaluation of the risk to quality should be based on scientific knowledge and ultimately linked to the protection of the patient.

Principle 1: Be based on scientific knowledge and ultimately protect the patient.
In reworking, this means that every decision to "allow reworking" must be supported by scientific data (ICH Q9(R1) §3.1): analytical data, stability data, and an immunogenicity assessment, not just "we feel it should be fine."

5.7 Established Conditions

Original Text

The implementation of ICH Quality Guideline Q12: Technical and Regulatory Considerations for Pharmaceutical Product Lifecycle Management by different regulatory agencies will also enable the recognition of Established Conditions (ECs) that require regulatory notification as well as those non-ECs that have been recognized as not necessary to ensure product quality and, therefore, can be managed prior to implementation notification. The amount of flexibility in a control strategy is based on the totality of product and process understanding (e.g., prior knowledge, development studies) in the context of QRM principles described in ICH Q9(R1).

A control strategy is designed to ensure that a product of required quality will be produced consistently. This planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, assures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components, capability of the verified mathematical models, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control. The control strategy is based on the development of the CQAs which, in turn, have been based on thorough scientific work.

Pharmaceutical development activities result in an appropriate control strategy of the ECs, which are critical elements identified and proposed by the MAH to assure product quality and, therefore, would require a regulatory submission if changes impacted any EC. ECs can include CPPs, critical material attributes (CMAs), and CQAs as defined in ICH Q8(R2).

In the event of reworking of a drug substance or drug product, CMC changes can occur. Thus, the ECs for the manufacturing process should be defined based on enhanced product and process understanding, considering all the relevant elements of the control strategy. To identify the ECs, an enhanced approach is recommended with an increased understanding of interaction between the changed key and critical input parameters along with the outputs, and the impact on quality attributes together with a corresponding control strategy. Once ECs are identified for the material to be reworked, a detailed initial risk assessment must be developed from the prior knowledge gathered to identify the potential risk to product quality associated with changing the ECs and considering the overall control strategy.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Established Conditions (EC): As defined in ICH Q12, ECs are "the list of CMC elements that the MAH has filed with the regulatory authority and that must remain unchanged after approval". Only the parameters or attributes written into the EC list require a formal regulatory filing when changed.

Non-EC vs EC:

  • EC (filing required): for example, the pore size of the terminal sterilizing filter, or the brand/model of a critical chromatography column
  • Non-EC (can be managed within the PQS): for example, the preparation order of a non-critical buffer, or a supportive cleaning step

The three components of an EC: CPP (critical process parameters), CMA (critical material attributes) and CQA (critical quality attributes), all derived from the QbD framework of ICH Q8(R2).

Analogy

ECs are like the "structural drawings" of a building: the positions of beams, columns and load-bearing walls that have been filed with the government authority are all ECs. To move any of these elements, you must reapply for a building permit (regulatory filing). Interior decoration and paint color, however, are Non-ECs: the owner decides, and only needs to keep a record.

A rework is like discovering a structural deviation in the building. You must confirm whether the repair plan touches the filed structural drawings (ECs), and if it does, you must report to the authority.

Key Notes

The revolutionary significance of ICH Q12: Before Q12, almost every significant process change required a PAS (Prior Approval Supplement), with a waiting time of 6-12 months. The EC framework of Q12 allows "changes that do not affect ECs" to go through CBE-30 or even be managed within the PQS, greatly shortening timelines.

Practical challenge for Taiwan CDMOs: Before 2024, the TFDA had not formally adopted ICH Q12. If the BLA was approved by the US FDA, the US Sponsor expects a PACMP/EC framework; but if product is also manufactured in Taiwan for the Taiwan market, the TFDA may still require the equivalent of the traditional Type II variation process. It is advisable to build a dual-track filing strategy with the Sponsor's RA department rather than simply copying the US application.

5.8 Post-Approval Change Management Protocols (PACMP)

Original Text

The PACMP tools described in ICH Q12 enable the planning and implementation of changes to ECs and/or CPPs, and thereby to regulatory filings, in an efficient structured manner. The PACMP (or comparability protocol) approved by the relevant health authority provides an agreement between the MAH and the regulatory agency and describes the changes and level of detail complying to the complexity, major variation, risk assessment, control strategy, and other pertinent elements. Once a PACMP is approved, when the proposed study design or acceptance criteria changes, an update of the protocol should be submitted for implementation as agreed upon in the original protocol. If changes are needed due to unanticipated or unforeseen changes in plans, prior approval of a new or revised PACMP by the regulatory agency may be required.

In the event of a reworking, the proposed comparability protocol (CP) for a modified manufacturing process should sufficiently cover the intended change and provide an increased control when compared to the existing process. A CP can be appropriately designed and/or drafted using a risk-based approach, with an in-depth or a high-level knowledge of process and product understanding. A risk assessment should be conducted on the potential drug substance and/or intermediate CQAs to be affected by parameter changes, including unspecified parameters.

Knowledge from previous developmental studies, small-scale studies, and/or pilot studies can be critical to identify potential risks to quality and provide support in the development of a suitable evaluation plan. The risk of adverse effects as a result of cumulative changes during a reworking should be carefully identified, justified, and included in the supporting information for the CP.

The reworked material should be assessed with suitable structural characterization, analytical and procedures. It should meet the criteria to demonstrate the lack of adverse effect, including immunogenicity on the reworked product quality considering the impact of structural changes in drug substance and/or drug product. Depending on the type and complexity of the drug substance, functional characterization and additional studies should also be included.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PACMP (Post-Approval Change Management Protocol): A "change framework agreement" pre-agreed between the MAH and the regulatory authority, equivalent to a "pre-approved change blueprint". Once a PACMP is approved, future changes executed according to this blueprint can benefit from a faster review pathway.

Comparability Protocol (CP): The specific technical version of a PACMP, describing how to demonstrate that product quality after the change is comparable to that of the original process. It must cover the analytical testing plan, acceptance criteria, stability strategy and immunogenicity assessment.

Cumulative Changes: A rework may involve simultaneous adjustments to multiple process steps. The risk of each step alone may be low, but cumulatively they may have unexpected effects on protein structure. The CP must explicitly discuss this risk.

Analogy

A PACMP is like a "scope-of-work permit" applied for in advance from the government: it first determines which types of repairs can be decided independently within a defined scope and which require an individual application each time. Once the permit is obtained, work within the permitted scope only needs to be filed; only work beyond the scope requires a new application. A rework PACMP has the same meaning: agreement on the framework is obtained in advance, speeding up the response when an event occurs.

Key Notes

Immunogenicity is the bottom line: One of the strictest requirements for protein rework is the immunogenicity assessment. Any step that may change protein structure (pH change, additional filtration, thermal stress) may affect the patient's antibody response. This is a patient safety consideration and takes priority over all commercial considerations.

Practical measures when the CDMO has no PACMP:

  1. Confirm with the Sponsor whether an approved PACMP or CP already exists in the current BLA. If so, the CDMO only needs to execute within that framework.
  2. If not, the CDMO needs to help the Sponsor draft a new CP, and should expect an agency review time of 4-8 weeks (CBE-30 pathway), or longer for the PAS pathway.
  3. This preparatory work should be included as an RA milestone in the Technology Transfer plan.

5.9 Instabilities and Quality Concerns

Original Text

Protein degradation, aggregation, poses significant challenges in the manufacturing and quality control of biologicals, particularly during reworking processes. Given that, generally, these proteins are only marginally stable in solution, they are susceptible to various forms of instability. Chemical instabilities like deamidation, oxidation, and hydrolysis, alongside such physical instabilities as denaturation and noncovalent aggregation, can compromise the quality and efficacy of the drug substances.

During an event of reworking, proteins will be exposed to stress conditions, such as elevated temperatures, low pH, or hydrophobic surfaces, which might trigger conformational instabilities. Formulation of drug substances at high concentrations can further encourage protein aggregation, impacting long-term stability.

To mitigate these risks, purification steps are essential for removing impurities and aggregates. It is crucial to implement appropriate process controls that manage pH, buffer components, and denaturants to prevent protein unfolding, which can lead to aggregation. These controls are vital to ensuring the drug substance meet specifications, maintains good appearance without visible particulates, retains stability during holding and storage, preserves efficacy, and minimizes immunogenicity concerns.

Protein manufacturing and formulation are complex processes with several factors that can impact the quality and stability of therapeutic proteins, such as:

  • Protein Concentration and Viscosity: During reworking, increased protein concentration can lead to higher viscosity, which is problematic for formulations intended for subcutaneous injections. Techniques such as rheology and dynamic light-scattering can be employed to evaluate viscosity impacts.
  • Thermal Stress: Elevated temperatures, particularly during viral inactivation steps, can lead to protein denaturation and aggregation.
  • High Ionic Strength: The use of high-ionic-strength eluents in ion-exchange and hydrophobic interaction chromatography may promote aggregation.
  • pH Considerations: Eluents with pH values near the protein's isoelectric point, or far from it, can lead to aggregation.
  • Filtration Processes: Membrane contact, presence of air bubbles, and shear stress during filtration can induce aggregation. Ultrafiltration at high protein concentrations requires careful management.
  • Formulation Challenges: Bulk storage, freeze-thaw cycles, and agitation during formulation processes can stress proteins and cause aggregation.
  • Air-Water Interfaces: Exposure to air-water interfaces during formulation can promote aggregate formation by lowering the energy barrier for protein unfolding.
  • Excipients in Formulation: Certain excipients can stress proteins and accelerate aggregation. For instance, magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) have been noted for their potential to increase aggregation rates in some formulations.
  • Visible Particulates: It is critical to effectively monitor visible particulates after any reworking.
  • Leachables and Extractables: Detected in various sterilizing-grade filters (PVDF, PES, mixed cellulose ester). These demonstrate complex patterns of destabilizing and stabilizing effects on the physical and chemical stability of mAbs, including formation of visible and subvisible particles.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The two major types of protein instability:

Chemical Instability:

  • Deamidation: hydrolysis of Asn/Gln side chains, affecting charge distribution
  • Oxidation: oxidation of Met/Trp/Cys, affecting binding activity
  • Hydrolysis: cleavage of peptide bonds, producing fragments

Physical Instability:

  • Denaturation: unfolding of the three-dimensional structure, with loss of activity
  • Noncovalent Aggregation: reversible or irreversible aggregates, with a risk of triggering an immune response
  • Fibrillation: ordered aggregate structures, highly immunogenic

Analogy

A protein molecule is like a delicate piece of origami: only the correctly folded three-dimensional structure is functional. The extra process steps in a rework are like reprocessing the origami: a pH shift is soaking the paper in water (the paper swells and deforms), high temperature is drying it (the structure shrinks), and agitation is folding it repeatedly (fatigue and breakage). Each step may permanently deform the origami so that it cannot be restored.

Key Notes

Distinguishing true instability from analytical method interference: If SEC-HPLC shows an increase in aggregates after rework, first confirm: (1) Did sample preparation introduce artificial aggregation? (2) Has the analytical method been validated as suitable for the matrix of this reworked sample? (3) Was a forced degradation control run in parallel? Only after analytical interference has been excluded can the result be interpreted as a true quality deviation.

Emerging warning on Leachables & Extractables: Leachables from sterilizing-grade filter membranes such as PVDF and PES may have a "surfactant effect" on mAbs and accelerate protein aggregation. If a new filter contact material is introduced during rework, an L&E assessment must be performed. This is an important item within "Analytical Testing (4-8 weeks)" in the §5.11 timeline.

Decision hierarchy warning: Immunogenicity risk = a direct threat to patient safety, so it always takes priority over production schedule or cost considerations (first place in the decision hierarchy).

Original Text

People drive reworking projects and ensure timely delivery of the biological drug product to the patient. 5.11 Submission & Approval Timelines

Original Text

Reworking a biological drug substance involves carefully revisiting and potentially modifying production processes to ensure product quality, safety, and efficacy. The timeline for reworking such a drug substance and/or drug product can vary significantly depending on several factors, including the complexity of the product, extent of modifications needed, regulatory requirements, and manufacturing facility's capabilities.

Typical phases and approximate timelines:

  1. Assessment and Planning (2–4 weeks): Evaluation of the current process and product; identifying reasons for rework; developing a detailed rework plan and risk assessment.
  2. Process Development and Optimization (4–12 weeks): Adjusting upstream or downstream processes; small-scale laboratory or pilot studies; analytical method development or validation if needed.
  3. Analytical and Stability Testing (4–8 weeks): Confirming product integrity, potency, and purity post-rework; stability testing to ensure product remains within specifications.
  4. Scale-up and Validation (8–16 weeks): Manufacturing at larger scale; process validation batches to confirm reproducibility.
  5. Regulatory Documentation and Submission (4–12 weeks): Preparing amendments or supplemental dossiers; addressing agency queries and obtaining prior approval.
  6. Manufacturing and Release (2–6 weeks, variable): Final production run; quality release-testing and documentation.
"Total Estimated Timeline: Typically, reworking a drug substance can take approximately 6 to 12 months, but in some cases, especially with complex modifications or regulatory hurdles, it may extend longer." — PDA TR74 §5.11

Factors Influencing Reworking Timeline:

  • Regulatory Pathways: The need for prior approval or additional filings can significantly affect timelines. Approval processes differ by region.
  • Extent of Process Changes: More extensive changes require deeper assessments and validation efforts.
  • Availability of Raw Materials and Manufacturing Capacity: Supply chain considerations and manufacturing capacity can cause delays.
  • Quality and Analytical Testing Turnaround Times: Time for quality and analytical testing can impact timelines, especially if retesting is required.
  • Single-use Systems: If new single-use systems with greater process contact-surface area or new polymers are introduced, a detailed leachable and extractable impact assessment will need to be performed before execution.
  • Regulatory Agency Review Times: Variability in how long agencies take to review submissions and respond can introduce uncertainties.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Comparison of Filing Pathways

Three US FDA filing pathways (biologics BLA):

  • Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): approval is required before implementation; the FDA review goal is 6 months (standard) or 2 months (expedited). Applies to high-risk process changes where CQAs may be affected.
  • Changes Being Effected in 30 Days (CBE-30): may be implemented 30 days after submission (if the FDA has no objection). Applies to moderate-risk changes.
  • Annual Report (AR): consolidated annual filing, with no waiting before implementation. Applies to low-risk, minor changes.

Main EU EMA pathways:

  • Type II Variation (major change): review time limit of 60 days (effective), corresponding to the FDA PAS, for high-risk process changes.
  • Type IB Variation (moderate change): may be implemented after a 30-day notification, corresponding to the FDA CBE-30.
  • Type IA (IAIN, immediate notification) / annual assessment: corresponds to the FDA Annual Report.

Analogy

A rework timeline is like the "airworthiness restoration plan" after an aircraft emergency landing: engineers assess the damage (phase 1), design a repair and test it at small scale (phase 2), run complete structural tests (phase 3), verify with a test flight (phase 4), apply to the aviation authority for permission to resume flying (phase 5), and formally return to service (phase 6).   3. Stability data gap: A reworked batch needs at least initial stability data (usually 3 months) to support the filing documents. The stability study must be started immediately after the final scale-up validation batch is completed.

  1. Risk Assessment and Planning: Conduct thorough risk assessment; prepare detailed rework plan with objectives, resources, timelines, and personnel responsibilities.

Key Concepts

The dual role of the Quality Unit:

5.13 Conclusions

Original Text

In urgent situations, such as a global pandemic (e.g., COVID-19), wars, or natural disasters (e.g., Category 5 hurricanes, catastrophic earthquakes, major floods, meteor catastrophe), certain regulatory flexibilities or deviations may be considered by the MAH to address critical supply issues, like immediate drug shortages. However, reworking an OOS drug substance generally involves significant quality and regulatory considerations, such as:

  • Regulatory Frameworks and Guidance: Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. FDA, EMA, and other agencies offer guidance on managing deviations, OOS results, and manufacturing challenges. In urgent situations, these agencies may provide tailored guidance or emergency procedures. However, deviations from approved specifications generally require proper justification and thorough documentation.
  • Reworking of OOS Drug Substance: Reworking involves processing material that initially failed to meet specifications to bring it into compliance. This process must be validated and scientifically justified, demonstrating that reworking does not compromise product quality, safety, stability and efficacy.
  • Regulatory Approval and Documentation: Any decision to rework should be thoroughly documented, including risk assessments and validation data. For licensed products, regulatory authorities often require that deviations or exceptions from established processes be reported. Depending on the jurisdiction and the specific nature and impact of the change, prior approval may be necessary.
  • Risk Management and Justification: In emergencies or shortages, regulators may offer some flexibility, but the company must ensure reworking does not compromise drug quality. A comprehensive risk assessment, incorporating knowledge management, should be conducted to evaluate its impact on safety and efficacy.
  • Good Manufacturing Practice Compliance: Reworking must adhere to GMP standards, and the process should be validated. Proper change control procedures should be followed.
"Reworking an OOS drug substance should never be treated as routine. However, in urgent situations such as a pandemic or critical shortage, biotechnology companies may consider reworking if it is properly justified, validated, and documented. They should proactively engage with regulatory authorities, possibly under emergency use or contingency provisions, to ensure compliance and maintain product integrity." — PDA TR74 §5.13

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Regulatory Flexibility in Emergencies: During COVID-19, the FDA and EMA both issued emergency guidance allowing exceptional deviations in the process under specific conditions. This was not an exemption from "no filing required" but a procedural adjustment of "expedited review + subsequent supplementary submission". The precondition for regulatory flexibility remains that patient safety is not compromised.

The core meaning of "never regard as routine": A rework is by nature an exception event. If a site is considering a rework for every batch, this is no longer a rework problem but a systemic problem of inadequate fundamental process capability. It must go through CAPA to improve from the root cause, rather than repeatedly applying for rework approval.

Analogy

A rework is like an "emergency treatment plan" in surgery: sometimes necessary, but it should never be the norm for unplanned surgery. If a hospital has to resort to "emergency treatment" for the same type of surgery every week, the problem is not the emergency procedure but the preoperative assessment and the surgical standards themselves. The philosophy of pharmaceutical rework is exactly the same: be prepared to respond, but aim to avoid it.

Key Notes: Proactive Mindset

The core message of TR74 §5.13: prevention is better than response:

  • Establish the PACMP in advance: Do not wait until an OOS occurs to draft it. That would be like applying for a fire permit only after a fire breaks out.
  • Periodic EC review: Each year, confirm with the Sponsor and RA that the current EC list reflects the actual state of the current process.
  • Proactively engage with regulatory authorities: TR74 explicitly recommends "proactively engage with regulatory authorities". Consulting before a problem grows is more effective than reacting passively. For a Taiwan CDMO, this means working with the Sponsor to proactively seek informal communication with the FDA/EMA (Type C meeting, scientific advice).
  • CAPA culture first: After every rework event, root cause analysis and a CAPA plan should be regarded as mandatory outputs, not optional ones. Preventing recurrence is the most effective long-term cost control.

Original Text

Chromatography is used to purify a product (e.g., mAb, target proteins, compounds) considering the properties of the target solute, feed stream, required purity level, productivity, costs, process sequence, process parameters, and implementation of the designed purification process. Biomolecules are purified using chromatography techniques that separate them according to the difference in their specific properties as summarized in Table 6.1-1. In ion exchange chromatography (IEX), biomolecules are separated according to the difference in their net surface charges.

See Table 6.1-1 (Chromatography Techniques) and Table 6.1-2 (Quality Attribute Risk Assessment Topics) below.

The overall goal of a chromatographic step is to remove impurities, such as host-cell-derived proteins (HCP), nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and other undesired compounds.

In general, a full-scale chromatography system will consist of the following components:

  • Buffer and Product Reservoirs: Containers (hard-piped or single use disposables) used to supply the buffers, product feed, and for elution collection required during the chromatography operation.
  • Pumps: Peristaltic pumps used to feed the buffers into chromatography columns. Depending on scale, other types of pumps may be utilized considering process requirements and equipment design.
  • Column: The chamber where the chromatographic media is loaded. The size (diameter and height) of the column will be defined based on the process requirements. The material of construction (e.g., glass, polycarbonate, steel) is selected based on process conditions (e.g., pressure and flow rates).
  • Resin: Chromatography media intended for the purification process. The packaging of the columns is commonly conducted by creating a slurry where the particles are suspended, that is, poured into the column.
  • Valves: Sanitary systems used to control the flow of materials during the operation of the chromatography system. These may be manual or pneumatic units.
  • Sensors: Applications intended to monitor process parameters required for the chromatography process. These may include pH, air, temperature, flow, pressure, and UV monitor.
  • Controller/Computer: Used to control and monitor the operation of the system.

The purification strategy involving a chromatography step is based on the characteristics (e.g., properties and stability) of the product under different conditions, final specifications, feed properties (e.g., concentration of product and impurities), nature of the process (e.g., continuous or discontinuous), and nature of the substances (e.g., hazardous).   • Size → SEC: Size Exclusion Chromatography, used for aggregate analysis
  • Hydrophobicity → HIC / RPC: Hydrophobic Interaction / Reversed Phase Chromatography
  • Biorecognition → Affinity: Protein A affinity chromatography is the workhorse first purification step for mAbs

Flow-Through Mode: AEX is usually operated in flow-through mode. At the target pH/conductivity the mAb is positively charged or neutral, does not bind to the anion resin and flows straight through, whereas HCP, DNA and endotoxin are negatively charged and bind to the resin, which removes these impurities. This is quite different from Protein A (bind-and-elute mode).

Key Notes

The reprocessing design must demonstrate no adverse effect on these CQAs, which is a core concern of regulators.

DS (Drug Substance) level: This case concerns the DS/upstream purification process, which differs from aseptic filling of the DP (Drug Product), but the same quality risk management logic applies. When a CDMO discusses with a Sponsor or DS CMO, this framework provides a common language.

Analogy

Think of the chromatography column as the cartridge in a water filter: different cartridge designs capture different kinds of impurities (by size, charge, hydrophobicity or specific recognition). The AEX flow-through mode is like using a negatively charged screen: negatively charged impurities (HCP, DNA) are held back, while the target protein (mAb), which is positively charged, passes straight through the screen, achieving purification.

Original Text

The elements in Section 6.2 should be considered as part of reprocessing a chromatography column. These elements are further discussed in PDA Technical Report No. 14 (Revised 2008): Validation of Column-Based Chromatography Processes for the Purification of Proteins.

6.2.1 Column Packing

The resin utilized for chromatography is loaded into the column in a controlled manner to ensure a uniform distribution while preventing the formation of channels that will affect the performance of the unit operation. Commonly, a slurry containing resin in a process buffer is poured into the column. Once the resin is settled, the integrity of the packaged column is confirmed. In the case of reprocessing, the integrity of the column bed should be verified and confirmed prior to its use. Any issue affecting the bed should be solved prior to reprocessing, considering resuspension and repacking of the resin if applicable.

6.2.2 Cleaning and Sanitization

Full cleaning and sanitization of the column must be conducted in preparation for reprocessing. Cleaning and sanitization will follow existing procedures and practices, including the regeneration of the resin. (These elements are discussed further in (68, 69).)

6.2.3 Performance

Performance of the chromatography step must be monitored according to the routine established procedures and must satisfy predefined acceptance criteria. The criteria should include, but not be limited to, the operational conditions (e.g., asymmetry, height equivalent to the theoretical plate, and/or transition analysis) and quality attributes (65).

6.2.4 Regeneration and Lifecycle

The resin must be regenerated consistent with established procedures and must satisfy the acceptance criteria defined and validated. The use of the resin for a reprocessing activity will be within the validated number of reuses if the reprocessing activity is not associated with a malfunction of the chromatography column and resin.

6.2.5 Validation and Filings

Validation of reprocessing will be performed under a prospective protocol. Detailed guidance for validation activities of chromatography processes for the purification of proteins is provided in PDA TR 14 (68). In the event that changes are required to an existing filing, guidance is provided in PDA Technical Report No. 38: Manufacturing Chromatography Systems Post-Approval Changes (ChromPAC).

In this example, a protocol for prospective validation of reprocessing at the manufacturing scale was written and approved; however, the potential of a concurrent validation could be considered if the frequency for reprocessing did not justify a prospective protocol. The protocol required a single GMP run at manufacturing scale, including all routine in-process and release testing as well as additional nonroutine testing, for example, analysis of HCP, acidic variants, aggregate levels, fragmentation, oxidation, and potency in reprocessed material. The protocol also required placing the resulting drug substance in the stability program. The protocol acceptance criteria for nonroutine testing were based on the comparability criteria established in Section 6.4.3 and demonstrated at small-scale. (See Section 6.5.6.)

Yet, a full prospective validation approach may not be feasible or practical for reprocessing in all cases. Instead, a concurrent validation strategy may be more appropriate, allowing the manufacture and testing of products and only reprocessing when and if necessary. In the case of concurrent validation, the acceptance criteria shall be aligned with the approved specifications for the process.

Key Concepts

Five points to consider (6.2.1–6.2.5) determine the technical feasibility of reprocessing:

  • 6.2.1 Column Packing: Column bed integrity must be reconfirmed before reprocessing. Common indicators are HETP (Height Equivalent to a Theoretical Plate), asymmetry and transition analysis. If the bed shows cracks or channeling, reprocessing is pointless.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis): A prospective, structured risk assessment tool and one of the methods recommended in ICH Q9(R1). It has three scoring dimensions:

  • S — Severity: Assesses the potential impact of the failure on CQAs (three levels: 1/3/5)
  • O — Occurrence: Assesses the probability that the failure mode occurs (three levels: 1/3/5)
  • D — Detection: Assesses whether the failure can be detected in time before the step is completed (three levels: 1/3/5)

RPN = S × O × D, range 1–125 (in this case all three dimensions have the highest score of 5).

IPC (In-Process Control): In this case the IPC limit for AEX eluate HCP is set at ≤80 ng/mg (80% of the action limit of ≤100 ng/mg), serving as the alert value for the reprocessing trigger point.

Analogy

FMEA scoring is like the risk checklist for regular car maintenance: the technician evaluates each part for "how serious is it if it fails (S)" × "how easily does it fail (O)" × "if it fails, can it be detected in time (D)" = overall risk score. A failed brake pad is very serious (S=5), wears out easily if not replaced for a long time (O=5), but a warning light can detect it (D=1), so RPN = 25. By contrast, an HCP problem at the AEX step is serious (S=5) × occurs occasionally (O=5) × cannot be detected immediately before the step is completed (D=5) = RPN 125, the highest of all steps, so it was chosen as the reprocessing candidate step.

Formula

FMEA scoring logic (this case):

HCP problem at the AEX step:
  S = 5 (HCP out of limit → directly affects CQA)
  O = 5 (occurs once in every <100 batches)
  D = 5 (HCP in the AEX eluate cannot be detected before the step is completed)
  RPN = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125  ← highest score, confirmed as the candidate step

Comparison: Protein A step:
  S = 3, O = 1, D = 1 → RPN = 3 (low risk)

Comparison: CEX step:
  S = 5, O = 3, D = 1 → RPN = 15 (medium risk)

IPC trigger point design:
  Action limit: HCP <100 ng/mg
  IPC alert limit: ≤80 ng/mg (= action limit × 80%)
  → 80 ng/mg exceeded → triggers a reprocessing investigation

Key Notes

D = 5 (cannot be detected) is the key reason AEX was chosen as the reprocessing step: HCP requires dedicated analytical methods (ELISA or LC-MS) and cannot be monitored in real time during the process; samples can only be taken and analyzed after the step is completed. This is why D = 5, and why the reprocessing plan must be designed in advance rather than improvised after a problem occurs.

Three-option decision framework: Quality (CQA compliance) is the prerequisite for all options. When all three options can ensure product quality, Option 3 (reprocessing) becomes the first choice because it has the lowest cost and does not require process redevelopment. Decision hierarchy: CQA → Business.

6.5.2 Reprocessing Triggers

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reprocessing Trigger: Reprocessing is not carried out automatically; it must be initiated by an objective, quantitative indicator. In this case the trigger is an AEX eluate HCP >100 ng/mg. This threshold is supported by the scaled-down model in 6.5.8 and was also identified as a high-risk failure mode in the FMEA analysis in 6.3.

6.5.3 Reprocessing Design

Original Text

The design of the AEX reprocessing step took into consideration the issues described in Sections 6.3.1, 6.3.2, 6.5.1, and 6.5.2. Since the step operated in flow-through mode, the AEX eluate was considered to be suitable as a feed-stream for AEX reprocessing without any adjustments.

Prior development studies reviewed in Section 6.5.1 showed that all ranges for feed-stream conditions expected for the reprocessing operation were evaluated and shown to produce drug substance with acceptable quality. Studies for the reprocessing step demonstrated that the AEX column—after being stripped, cleaned, and re-equilibrated—would readily remove HCP to well below acceptable levels.

All other aspects of the design of the reprocessing step, including operating conditions, buffer solution, equipment sizes, and others were similar to the design of the original AEX step.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Advantage of Flow-Through Mode: AEX operates in flow-through mode (the antibody does not bind to the resin; impurities do), so the eluate itself is a suitable re-feed stream, with no need to adjust the buffer or protein concentration. This greatly simplifies the reprocessing design.

By contrast, in bind-and-elute mode the re-feed would need its pH and conductivity readjusted, and the design would be far more complex.

Key Notes

The key to a successful reprocessing design is: do not change the operating conditions of the original AEX step. This means the existing validation data (CPP ranges, viral clearance LRF) remain valid, there is no need to revalidate all parameters from scratch, and the regulatory filing burden is greatly reduced.

Between the two runs the column must go through the full sequence: Stripping → CIP → Re-equilibration. This is the core requirement of resin management, ensuring that the HCP binding capacity is fully restored by the time of the second load.

A summary of the design impact assessment is included in Table 6.4.1-1 in the "Other" category. The two most significant areas of concern identified were Buffer/Solution Supply and Equipment Cleaning.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Design Impact Assessment: Once the reprocessing design is complete, its knock-on effects on the manufacturing site must be assessed. This case reveals two major practical challenges:

  • Buffer/Solution Supply: Reprocessing requires additional buffer, so the buffer preparation capacity and spare stock at the production site must be confirmed.
  • Equipment Cleaning: The equipment cleaning turnaround time affects the hold time, so it must also be confirmed that the existing cleaning procedures can be completed in time after reprocessing is triggered.

Analogy

A design impact assessment is like a restaurant adding a new dish to its existing menu and having to assess: does the kitchen have enough prepared ingredients (Buffer Supply)? Does the time needed to clean the equipment allow it (Equipment Cleaning)? Otherwise, when the new dish goes live, the rhythm of the whole kitchen may be disrupted.

Although the protein concentration is reduced by 30% (due to dilution) and is lower than the original feed, the AEX Load Concentration is a noncritical parameter in this case and does not affect HCP removal performance. This is an important judgement: not every parameter change requires a supplementary study; the key is to first confirm the classification (CPP or not).

The hold time is extended from ≤72 hours to ≤120 hours; the additional 48 hours accommodate the HCP test (8 hours) plus equipment/buffer preparation time.

6.5.6 Scaled-Down Models

Original Text

The qualified scaled-down (laboratory-scale) models used in original AEX development studies were appropriate for reprocessing development. Data demonstrating the validity of the laboratory-scale models were available, and the model was shown to be predictive of manufacturing-scale behavior.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Scaled-Down Model: In biopharmaceutical development, representative studies are performed at laboratory scale (for example, a 1 mL column in place of a 50 L plant column), provided that the scaled-down model has been shown to be predictive of manufacturing scale.

In this case the original AEX development already established a qualified SDM, which can be reused directly for reprocessing development without requalification; this is a key saving of time and resources.

Analogy

A scaled-down model is like an architect testing a new design with a 1:100 model. As long as the model has been verified to behave consistently with the real building, various design changes can be evaluated at small scale, without building a real building each time. The same applies to the AEX SDM: first confirm at laboratory scale that reprocessing is feasible, then run the confirmation batch at the plant.

Key Notes

When is an SDM sufficient? When is full scale needed?

  • The SDM is suitable for: assessment of product quality attributes (Aggregate, HCP, viral clearance), process parameter exploration, and worst-case design (Double Reprocessing)
  • A full-scale confirmation batch (1 GMP run) is still needed: for the confirmatory execution of the validation protocol, and as required for the CBE-30/Type IB submission report

The final confirmation required by regulation must be performed at manufacturing scale; the SDM provides development confidence but cannot replace a manufacturing-scale GMP batch. (PDA TR74, Section 6.2.5)

6.5.7 Representative Materials

Original Text

In addition, it was possible to run the pilot-scale process under conditions that could intentionally deliver a higher-than-normal level of HCP as source material for the laboratory-scale models. All buffers and reagents were equivalent to those used in GMP manufacturing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Representative Materials: The reprocessing trigger scenario is HCP above the normal value, but material from failed plant batches cannot be obtained in advance. The solution is to use Pilot batch material + an HCP Spike to simulate the feed composition in a real trigger scenario.

Key Notes

The logic of the HCP Spike: if the HCP level of the test material is already close to normal, the HCP clearance capability of the reprocessing step cannot be evaluated. Only by deliberately raising the HCP concentration of the feed can the worst case (the real situation when reprocessing is triggered) be simulated.

All buffers and reagents must be equivalent to those used in GMP manufacturing; if reagents of a different grade are used, the SDM data lose their predictive value for full scale.

6.6 Conclusions

Original Text

Reprocessing activities involving AEX is a feasible option during routine manufacturing. However, proper documentation aligned with GMPs is required to demonstrate the controls in place and effectiveness of the step.

Among the considerations for reprocessing are:

  • Prospective or concurrent validation protocol
  • Clear rationale for reprocessing (e.g., reason for reprocessing)
  • Proper management of the column and resin
  • Proper management of the intermediate material requiring reprocessing
  • Proper documentation (e.g., procedures and batch records in place)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Core message of the conclusions: AEX reprocessing is technically feasible, but this "feasible" comes with preconditions; the following must be in place:

  1. Solid development work (the full set of studies described in 6.5.1-6.5.9)
  2. A complete GMP documentation system (SOPs, Batch Record, training records)
  3. A clear regulatory filing strategy (prospective or concurrent validation, CTD content, CBE-30/Type IB)

If any one element is missing, "feasible" becomes "non-compliant".

Analogy

AEX reprocessing is like a system that allows a "make-up exam": the system acknowledges that an exam may occasionally go wrong because of external factors, so it has designed a make-up mechanism. But the make-up exam is not the default; it must meet the application conditions (investigation, HCP IPC trigger), and the make-up exam questions (reprocessing design), the grading criteria (Comparability Criteria) and the exam record (Batch Record) must all be prepared in advance. A make-up exam improvised without preparation is not recognized.

Key Notes

Column & Resin Management: This point is specifically mentioned in the conclusions because AEX resin has a validated upper limit on the number of uses. Reprocessing adds one use, so it must be confirmed that the validated maximum number of reuses (Lifecycle Validation) is not exceeded, and the use must be correctly recorded in the batch record. (PDA TR74, §6.2.4)

CDMO application scenario: For a multi-product CDMO, if a client's process includes an AEX step and HCP clearance is occasionally challenging, proactively designing the reprocessing framework at the CMC development stage (rather than improvising after a failure) can greatly reduce the risk of batch rejection after commercialization.

7.0 Appendix II: Case Study — Filtration (Introduction to Filtration Operations)

Original Text

Filtration is among the more likely unit operations to be subjected to reprocessing; therefore, it is discussed separately in this case study. The complexity and quality impact of filtration reprocessing (referred to hereon as refiltration) tend to be low compared to other unit operations, such as the chromatography example discussed in Section 6.0 (Appendix I).

The most common refiltration scenarios would include bioburden-reduction filters and virus-reduction filters, as further elaborated in Section 7.2 and Section 7.3. Reprocessing of tangential flow filtration is more complex and would tend to follow a framework more similar to chromatography (as described in Section 6.0). Refiltration for nonproduct-containing solutions, such as media and buffer, is generally acceptable and could be desirable (e.g., due to supply constraints on certain ingredients), in which case, potential impact (such as adsorption) should also be evaluated. For most refiltration steps, additional nonroutine product and stability testing of the drug substance and drug product may not be required.

  • For refiltration of an in-process bioburden-reduction filter that is used throughout the process, routine release and hold-time stability data may be adequate to support validation of reprocessing.
  • Refiltration may also occur during bulk drug substance filling, where the final container was found compromised (e.g., bioprocess container (Single Use systems) found to be leaking). Reprocessing at that point may require additional validation, such as pooling of filtered and unfiltered bulk drug substance and additional bioburden-testing.
  • Validation of reprocessing using a viral filter could be performed using routine product-testing, assuming the hold-time data is adequate and viral segregation is maintained.
"Scenarios where product quality is compromised, such as if the drug substance or drug product exceeds a bioburden specification, if unexpected precipitation is observed in the drug substance or drug product, or if viral segregation is compromised, then reprocessing is unlikely to be an option."

The following case studies are based upon the experience of the PDA Technical Report Team members.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Refiltration: passing a batch of material that has already been filtered, or is waiting to be filtered, through a filter again because of a specific failure mode (such as an integrity test failure, bioburden exceeding the action limit, or an SU bag leak). TR74 positions it explicitly as a low-complexity reprocessing unit operation.

Bioburden-reduction filter: a filter that removes microorganisms, commonly used in the middle of the DS process; the risk of refiltering with it is generally lower than with a virus-removal filter.

Virus-reduction filter: for example the Planova and Viresolve series, used to ensure the viral clearance step. The consequences of an integrity test failure are more serious because the continuity of the viral clearance validation is at stake.

Viral segregation: strict separation of the material streams before and after virus filtration, so that the pre-filtrate cannot recontaminate the post-filtrate. Once viral segregation has been compromised, refiltration cannot solve the problem.

Analogy

Refiltration is like a chef who finds that a restaurant stock is not clear enough and decides to pass it through a fine mesh once more. If there are only a few impurities (bioburden above the action limit), a second pass is entirely workable. But if the stock has already spoiled (bioburden above specification, or sediment has precipitated out), filtering again cannot save it, and it can only be discarded.

This judgment of "can be refiltered" versus "must be discarded" is the core logic of the whole framework in TR74 Section 7.0.

Key Notes

Reinforcing the decision hierarchy:

  1. Sterility assurance ← always the first priority
  2. CQA (critical quality attributes) ← patient safety
  3. Business (commercial flexibility) ← recovering capacity

Note: TR74 states explicitly that when bioburden exceeds the specification, when unexpected precipitation occurs, or when viral segregation has been compromised, reprocessing is not an option. This is the hierarchy of Sterility > CQA > Business in action: commercial pressure must never lead you to try refiltering a batch that is already out of specification.

"Exceeds the action limit but not the specification" versus "exceeds the specification" is the key regulatory dividing line: the former can be refiltered under a validated protocol; the latter must be discarded. (PDA TR74, Section 7.0)

Practice Questions

  1. TR74 indicates that the reprocessing framework for tangential flow filtration (TFF) is closer to that of chromatography than to ordinary filtration. Why? What does this mean for how your company designs a TFF reprocessing SOP?
  2. For refiltration after an SU bag leak, TR74 recommends evaluating the pooling of filtered and unfiltered bulk DS. What risk consideration is this design based on?
  3. If filtration of a solution that does not contain drug (such as a buffer) fails, TR74 considers refiltration generally acceptable, but adsorption must be evaluated. Give an example of which buffer component is most likely to cause an adsorption problem.
7.1 Case Study 1: Proactive Drug Substance Refiltration
7.1.1 Issue

Original Text

The manufacturer validated the process for filling a liquid biologic drug substance into the drug substance containers (e.g., carboys, single-use bags). Prior to validating the filling process, a failure modes analysis was performed to identify potential failures that could occur during the filling process. Three potential failures were identified:

  • Mechanical failures during bulk fill (e.g., structural failure such as leakage in the feed vessel, pinholes in the feed tubing, and/or single-use bags, post-use filter-test failure, failure in the filling apparatus)
  • Out-of-range results of protein concentration (e.g., higher than specification, which requires further dilution with formulation buffer and refiltration) and/or spiked excipients (e.g., lower than specification polysorbate, which requires additional spiking and refiltration)
  • Bioburden limits exceeded the action limit but were less than the specification

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Failure modes analysis: before process validation, systematically identify the failures that could occur at each filling step. This corresponds to the QRM (quality risk management) tool FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) in ICH Q9(R1).

Post-use filter-test failure: after filling is complete, an integrity test (such as bubble point or forward flow) is run on the sterilizing-grade filter (0.22 µm). A failing result means the integrity of the filter throughout filling cannot be confirmed. This is one of the most serious failure scenarios in aseptic filling.

Refiltration logic for the three types of failure mode:

  1. Mechanical failure (SU bag leak, damaged tubing) → evaluate whether bioburden or contamination has affected the batch
  2. Protein concentration or excipient out of specification → the batch can be diluted or spiked and then refiltered; this is formulation adjustment + refiltration
  3. Bioburden above the action limit but not above the specification → a pre-validated refiltration protocol can be triggered

Analogy

A manufacturer that performs a failure modes analysis before validating the filling process is like a pilot who practices simulated emergency procedures before takeoff: not because the plane is sure to have a problem, but so that when a failure does occur there is a clear decision path rather than improvisation on the spot.

The essence of Case Study 1 is that the failure modes are identified before batch production, and the corresponding refiltration protocols are validated in advance and included in the license in advance. That is what "proactive" means.

Key Notes

CDMO practical view: for a CDMO, the failure modes analysis for DS filling should normally be completed before PPQ (process performance qualification). If these three types of failure mode have not been identified in advance, then when a post-use integrity test failure or a protein concentration OOS occurs, the CDMO faces the problem of having no pre-approved refiltration protocol. It can only run an emergency deviation investigation, which delays release.

Define failure modes in advance → validate the refiltration protocol in advance → include it in the MBR and regulatory filing. This is a design step a CDMO should proactively require to be completed when it takes on a new client's DS process. (PDA TR74, Section 7.1.1)

Practice Questions

  1. Suppose that after DS filling a batch is found to have a damaged SU bag and also fails its post-use integrity test. Two failure modes occur at the same time. How do you decide whether this batch can be refiltered? Which failure is more decisive?
  2. When the polysorbate 80 (PS80) concentration is below specification, TR74 considers that it can be spiked and then refiltered. Why is refiltration necessary, rather than simply adding PS80?
7.1.2 Considerations

Original Text

Up to three full-scale refiltration validation runs would be performed against a preapproved protocol capturing the failure mode, of which at least one run evaluated the failure mode of interest. Each individual refiltration protocol would be performed on a separate lot of drug substance. Samples from the validation runs would then be tested according to the specification to confirm meeting release criteria, and if necessary, placed on GMP stability for the shelf life of the drug substance. The resultant data package would provide additional confirmation that refiltration had no impact on product quality at release as well as on stability.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Full-scale refiltration validation: unlike a lab-scale model, validation here is explicitly required to be done at full scale (manufacturing scale), with a separate lot of DS used for each refiltration protocol to avoid cross-interference between lots.

Preapproved protocol: the validation protocol must be approved by the Quality Assurance Unit before execution, ensuring that every failure mode has a corresponding protocol. In the MBR this corresponds to refiltration instructions in the form of an "appendix" or a "conditional step".

GMP stability: if samples from after refiltration are to be placed on the formal stability program, the impact of refiltration on shelf life must be evaluated, that is, confirm that refiltration does not accelerate degradation.

Key Notes

The design logic of "up to three lots": TR74 uses "up to three", meaning the number of validation lots is decided by the number and complexity of the failure modes. One full-scale lot corresponds to the evaluation of one failure mode. If there is only one failure mode (such as simple bioburden above the action limit), one complete validation lot may be enough.

In a CDMO setting, if the client's DS lot size is limited (for example Phase I/II clinical trial material), three full-scale validation lots may be impractical. In that case, the reason for the insufficient number of lots should be recorded in the QRM (quality risk management) documentation, together with stricter release testing as a compensating measure. (PDA TR74, Section 7.1.2; ICH Q9(R1))

Practice Questions

  1. TR74 requires each refiltration protocol to use a "separate lot of drug substance". Why can't validation be completed by repeating the refiltration three times on the same lot?
  2. If a GMP stability sample taken after refiltration shows degradation in the accelerated study at month 6, but real-time stability is normal, how do you interpret this result? Does it affect approval of the refiltration protocol?
7.1.3 Resolution and Regulatory Filing

Original Text

The results generated during the validation would then be filed in the BLA. The application would indicate that refiltration could only be performed once and that it must occur within a specified time period supported by the validations (e.g., validated hold stability). The application should specify that the Quality Assurance Unit must be notified and provide approval of refiltration. The refiltration procedure could be deemed as acceptable for approval as part of the license.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

BLA (Biologics License Application): the US FDA marketing application for biologics. The Taiwan equivalent at TFDA is the registration and review of biological products. The core logic is the same: the refiltration protocol must be filed in advance as part of the approved license before it can be legitimately carried out on clinical or commercial batches.

Hold stability: time-dependent stability data for DS or DP under specific storage conditions, used to define the maximum allowed time window for refiltration. For example, validation may show that refiltration must be completed within 72 hours of DS preparation.

The "only once" limit: refiltration may be performed only once. This is an explicit TR74 limit, intended to prevent the accumulation of quality risk from repeated refiltration. Any situation that would require a second refiltration should be treated as a trigger for rejecting the batch.

Key CDMO Practice: Pre-Authorized MBR Design

The regulatory pathway in TR74 Section 7.1.3 maps directly onto the design of the CDMO batch record (MBR):

  1. Design a "conditional step" in the MBR: record, in IF/THEN format, the conditions that trigger refiltration (such as bioburden above the action limit, integrity test failure, or protein concentration OOS) and the corresponding operating steps.
  2. QA Unit authorization field: the conditional refiltration step in the MBR must include a "QA Approval Signature" field, ensuring that QA has given written approval before each execution, consistent with the electronic record requirements of 21 CFR Part 11 and PIC/S GMP Part II.
  3. Time window record: the MBR should state the "latest completion time for refiltration" (based on hold stability validation data), and the operator enters the actual execution timestamp in the batch record.
  4. Regulatory filing link: the MBR should carry a cross-reference to the filed refiltration validation report or the relevant section of CTD Module 3.

If the MBR has not been designed with refiltration conditions in advance, then when a failure occurs the only route is a deviation report, which is time-consuming, high-risk, and may not save the batch. (PDA TR74, Section 7.1.3)

Practical Application

Taiwan CDMO scenario (TFDA / PIC/S framework):

A CDMO is performing DS filling of a Phase III clinical batch for a client. The MBR has no pre-approved refiltration conditions. During filling an SU bag develops a micro-leak, and the bioburden result exceeds the action limit (but not the specification). Can refiltration be started as an emergency measure?

Answer: in the spirit of PIC/S Annex 1 2022, this is not recommended. PIC/S Annex 1 2022 stresses that all reprocessing steps should be predefined, validated and authorized (§9.37). Starting a refiltration that has not been pre-approved is unvalidated reprocessing; it must go through the full change control + validation + TFDA supplement process before it can be legitimately used for later batches. For the current failed batch, a full risk assessment is needed, after which QA decides whether it can be released.

Preventive approach: when accepting each new client's DS process, the CDMO should proactively ask the client to carry out, or jointly carry out, the failure modes analysis, include conditional refiltration steps at the MBR design stage, and then file them in advance through the TFDA Minor Amendment pathway.

7.2 Case Study 2: Proactive Virus-Removal Refiltration
7.2.1 Issue

Original Text

Post-use integrity test failures of a normal-flow virus-retentive filter are a known failure mode affecting lot release. Refiltration of the virus-filtered pool through a new integral virus filter can be performed to ensure the validated clearance of virus; however, this reprocessing procedure has the potential to impact product quality due to extended processing times, for which the product load and/or pool stability should be considered.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Normal-flow virus-retentive filter: for example the Planova series from Asahi Kasei (20N, BioEX) or Viresolve Pro from Merck Millipore. These filters operate in normal-flow (dead-end) mode and retain parvovirus (~18-20 nm) by pore size.

Post-use integrity test failure: the virus filter undergoes an integrity test after filtration (such as a diffusion test or pressure hold test). If the result does not meet the manufacturer's specification, it cannot be confirmed that the filter remained intact throughout filtration, so the continuity of the viral clearance validation cannot be assured.

Pool stability: virus filters have low flow rates and long processing times (several hours to over ten hours), and refiltration extends the processing time further. The risk of protein aggregation and degradation under the load conditions must therefore be evaluated.

Analogy

A virus filter post-use integrity test failure is like a pilot finding, after landing, that a seal on the wing fails its test: even if everything looked normal during the flight, it cannot be confirmed that the seal was intact for the whole flight.

For a virus filter, this means that even if the virus test on the final product is negative, the regulatory justification for viral clearance has a gap, because the integrity of the filter cannot be confirmed. Refiltration (with a new, intact filter) is a remedy, but only if pool stability allows the extended processing time.

Key Notes

Impact of single-use vs reusable virus filters:

  • Single-use systems (such as the Planova BioEX capsule): discarded after filtration. If the post-use integrity test fails, a new filter simply replaces it for refiltration, with no cleaning validation issue.
  • Reusable systems (such as the traditional Planova 30N, used in some facilities): to refilter after a failure, you must first confirm whether cleaning affects the retention performance on the next use, and evaluate whether it affects the continuity of the viral clearance validation.

CDMOs now mainly choose single-use virus filters, which make refiltration simpler and lower the risk to viral segregation. However, when the vendor or filter model is changed, the viral clearance validation must be reassessed. (PDA TR74, Section 7.2.1)

Answers to Key Questions for Taiwan CDMOs

Q: If the 0.22 µm sterilizing-grade filter fails its post-use integrity test during DP filling, can the material be refiltered? What is the regulatory pathway under the PIC/S framework in Taiwan?

A (based on PIC/S Annex 1 2022 and the TR74 framework):

Technically, "can it be done": an integrity test failure at the final DP filtration step (sterilizing filtration) goes to the heart of sterility assurance, and refiltration there is more stringent than for a bioburden-reduction filter at the DS stage. The key decision points:

  1. If the batch has already been partly filled into vials, the filled portion cannot be refiltered, and the affected vials must be discarded.
  2. If the material is still in the bulk container and filling has not started, and a pre-validated refiltration protocol exists, refiltration can in theory be performed; but it must be confirmed that DP pool stability allows the extended operating time.
  3. PIC/S Annex 1 2022 §8.86 requires that the integrity of the sterilizing filter be tested both before filtration (PUPSIT) and after filtration. A post-use failure must trigger a deviation investigation and an assessment of whether any filled lots are affected.

TFDA regulatory pathway in Taiwan:

  1. Immediate action: raise a deviation report, quarantine the affected batch, and notify QA.
  2. Root cause investigation: determine why the integrity test failed (filter damage, test procedure error, pressure equipment problem, etc.).
  3. If a pre-approved refiltration protocol exists (already included in CTD Module 3 and approved by TFDA): refiltration can be carried out under the MBR conditional step, with written QA authorization recorded.
  4. If there is no pre-approved protocol: the batch should be rejected or held and must not be released until the full change control + validation + TFDA supplement is completed.
  5. TFDA notification requirement: for a commercial batch where there is concern that product has reached the market, proactive notification may be required under Article 68 of the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act and TFDA drug GMP regulations.

Bottom line: a DP integrity test failure should be planned for at the MBR design stage; prevention is better than remediation. (PIC/S Annex 1 2022, §8.86; PDA TR74, Section 7.2.1)

Practice Questions

  1. What is the essential difference in regulatory consequences between a post-use integrity test failure of a virus filter and that of a sterilizing-grade filter (0.22 µm)? Which is harder to remediate, and why?
  2. If the pre-use integrity test of a virus filter (applying the PUPSIT concept) passes but the post-use test fails, how do you assess whether viral clearance during filling is still valid?
7.2.2 Considerations

Original Text

Small-scale validation studies can be performed using a representative scaled-down model to assess the potential impact to product quality resulting from refiltration of virus-filtered pools. For these studies, samples of virus-filtration load can be obtained from different lots at manufacturing scale. Each of these virus-filtration loads should be refiltered at laboratory-scale through two sets of new, integral virus filters to represent worst-case. The validation studies can help to confirm that refiltration had no impact on product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Scaled-down model: use a small filter that is geometrically similar to the commercial-scale filter (typically an area ratio of about 1:50 to 1:200), simulating the commercial-scale flux, operating pressure and processing time to predict large-scale process behavior.

Two sets of filters to simulate the worst case: refiltration requires passing through two new, intact virus filters, in order to simulate the worst case. If the filter used in the first filtration was damaged during use (causing the integrity test failure), refiltration must ensure two complete virus retention steps to make up for any retention lost in the first pass. This is a "worst-case bracketing" design from a regulatory standpoint.

Filtration load samples from different lots: obtain virus-filtration load samples from different commercial lots, so that the validation results cover natural lot-to-lot variation (such as aggregate content and glycosylation differences) rather than the good performance of one lot by chance.

Key Notes

Choosing between small-scale and full-scale validation strategies:

Case Study 1 (DS bioburden refiltration) uses full-scale validation; Case Study 2 (virus filter refiltration) uses small-scale validation. The reasons for the difference:

  • A scaled-down model of a virus filter is already mature industry practice, because virus clearance validation itself (per ICH Q5A) is done through small-scale spiking studies, so a small-scale validation of refiltration is consistent with the existing framework.
  • For DS bioburden refiltration, it is harder to use a small-scale model to fully simulate mechanical failure scenarios of the filling equipment (SU bag, tubing geometry), so full scale was chosen.

When designing a refiltration validation plan, a CDMO should first confirm the regulatory precedent: if the authority (TFDA, FDA) has already accepted small-scale for a given filtration step, unnecessary full-scale validation only adds cost and material consumption. (PDA TR74, Section 7.2.2; ICH Q5A(R2))

Practice Questions

  1. Why does small-scale validation of virus filter refiltration need "two sets" of new, intact filters rather than one? If only one set were used, what assumption would that represent?
  2. When designing a small-scale model, how do you confirm that your model is "representative" of commercial scale? Which parameters must be controlled to be scaled equivalently?
7.2.3 Resolution and Regulatory Filing

Original Text

Results from the small-scale study of refiltration through a virus-retentive filter were reported in a regulatory submission. Validation of the refiltration unit operation would be completed for one full-scale batch and would include data on product yield, quality, and stability. Upon completion of the validation study and submission of the data, the refiltration procedure would become an amendment to the license.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two-phase submission strategy:

  1. Phase 1: small-scale study report: provide the quality data from the small-scale validation in the regulatory submission as the basis for "scientific justification".
  2. Phase 2: full-scale validation (one lot): confirm that at process scale refiltration has no adverse effect on yield, quality (purity, potency, aggregates) or stability; once complete, it becomes supporting data for the license amendment.

Amendment to the license: under the FDA BLA system this is usually a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) or CBE-30 (Changes Being Effected in 30 days), depending on the significance of the change. At TFDA the equivalent is an application to change the drug registration license.

Key Notes

Data requirements for yield, quality and stability:

  • Yield: the virus filter itself already causes protein adsorption loss (typically 5-15%), and refiltration through a second set of filters adds further loss. Full-scale validation must quantify the cumulative loss and confirm it remains commercially acceptable.
  • Quality: aggregates (HMW species), degradation fragments (LMW species) and potency are the CQAs most in need of monitoring after refiltration.
  • Stability: refiltration extends the DS handling time (hold time), so it must be confirmed that it does not affect subsequent release stability and shelf life.

When a CDMO accepts a client's DS and finds that the client has not yet completed the regulatory filing for virus filter refiltration, this should be raised at the tech transfer stage, to avoid being unable to remediate legitimately if an integrity test failure occurs in the future. (PDA TR74, Section 7.2.3)

Practical Application

New items for the CDMO Tech Transfer Checklist:

Based on the lessons of TR74 Case Study 1 and Case Study 2, when a CDMO accepts the tech transfer of a new DS process it should proactively confirm:

  1. Has the client completed a failure modes analysis for DS filling and identified all scenarios that could trigger refiltration?
  2. Has the MBR been designed to include conditional refiltration steps, and is the QA authorization process defined?
  3. Has the refiltration protocol for virus filter post-use integrity test failure completed small-scale validation and been included in the regulatory submission?
  4. Has the hold stability (time window) for refiltration been defined by validation data and recorded in the MBR?
  5. Does the TFDA license already include the refiltration-related amendment? If not, the tech transfer plan should include the filing timeline.

The purpose of this checklist is to turn refiltration from an "ad hoc firefighting measure" into a "pre-designed quality protection mechanism", truly realizing the "proactive" spirit that TR74 advocates.

  1. Case Study 2 uses a two-phase strategy of "small-scale study + one full-scale confirmation lot", whereas Case Study 1 uses "up to three full-scale validation runs". If you were a reviewer, how would you assess the equivalence of these two strategies in scientific rigor?
  2. For a license amendment for virus filter refiltration, if TFDA requires Prior Approval, how should your company handle a batch if an integrity test failure actually occurs during the approval period? What compliance options exist?

Section Summary

  • Refiltration is a relatively low-complexity reprocessing operation, but the boundary must be drawn strictly between situations where it is allowed (bioburden above the action limit) and where it is not (out of specification, unexpected precipitation, compromised viral segregation).
  • The core lesson of Case Study 1 is that proactive failure mode analysis should be completed before batch production; the refiltration protocol must be validated in advance (full scale, up to 3 lots) and included in the BLA/TFDA approval as part of the license, and written QA approval is mandatory.
  • Pre-authorized MBR design is a key competitive strength for a CDMO: designing conditional refiltration steps in the MBR (with trigger conditions, time window and QA authorization field) turns refiltration from an "ad hoc deviation" into a "pre-approved quality protection mechanism", markedly improving batch recovery rates and client trust.
  • The core lesson of Case Study 2: a virus filter post-use integrity test failure is a known failure mode that can be validated with a small-scale model (two new filter sets, worst case), then confirmed with one full-scale lot, and finally legitimately authorized through a license amendment.
  • Post-use integrity test failure of the 0.22 µm filter in DP filling: under PIC/S Annex 1 2022 §8.86, a deviation investigation is required whether or not a pre-approved protocol exists, and vials already filled cannot be refiltered; only material still in bulk, and only where a pre-approved protocol exists, may be considered for refiltration. Prevention is better than remediation.
  • The decision hierarchy always applies: Sterility > CQA > Business. Any decision to refilter an out-of-specification batch under commercial pressure violates this hierarchy and must be vetoed by QA.
