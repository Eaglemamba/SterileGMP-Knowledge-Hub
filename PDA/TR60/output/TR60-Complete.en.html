1.0 Introduction

Original Text

The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Association (EMA) consider process validation (PV) an essential element in the assurance of drug quality and include both general and specific requirements in their current good manufacturing practice (GMP) guidelines (1-3).

The PV lifecycle concept links product and process development, qualification of commercial manufacturing processes, and maintenance of the commercial production process in a coordinated effort (1). When based on sound process understanding and used with quality risk management (QRM) principles and change control, the lifecycle approach allows manufacturers to use continuous process verification (enhanced approach, e.g., real-time release) in addition to, or instead of, traditional PV (1, 2, 4, 5). The lifecycle approach may not be linear or sequential. As the product/process knowledge becomes available, the changes could be initiated at any part of the process. These changes may include all areas of validation, such as PV, the physical equipment requalification, control hardware, and software changes and qualification, as all of these systems are subject to GMP controls and the lifecycle approach.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Process Validation, PV: Providing scientific evidence that a manufacturing process consistently produces product that meets its predetermined quality standards. Both FDA and EMA regard it as an indispensable element of drug quality assurance.

PV Lifecycle Concept: Treating a product, from development through commercial manufacturing to post-marketing maintenance, as one complete, coordinated process rather than a one-time testing event.

Quality Risk Management, QRM: Systematically identifying, assessing, and controlling risk factors that may affect product quality.

Analogy

Imagine you run a restaurant chain. Process validation is like making sure every branch consistently turns out dishes of the same quality. It is not enough to cook a few trial dishes before opening (the traditional approach). Instead, it runs from developing the menu (Stage 1), to the acceptance test before formal operation (Stage 2), to ongoing quality monitoring once the restaurant is open (Stage 3), forming one complete quality assurance cycle.

Key Notes

TR60 stresses from the outset that the PV lifecycle approach "may not be linear or sequential". This is a very important point: when new product/process knowledge emerges, changes can be initiated in any part of the process. This is very different from the traditional linear thinking of "finish development first, then validate, then maintain".

1.1 Purpose and Scope

Original Text

Depending on the context, process, and product history, PV activities can differ. The information in this technical report applies to the manufacturing processes for drug substances (DSs) and drug products (DPs), including:

  • Pharmaceuticals, sterile and nonsterile
  • Biological/biotechnological products, including vaccines, blood-derivative products, advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMP), and regenerative medicine advanced therapy (RMAT) products
  • Active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs)
  • Radiopharmaceuticals
  • Veterinary medicines
  • Drug constituents of combination products (e.g., a combination of drug plus a medical device)

This technical report was prepared for global use and applies to both new and existing commercial manufacturing processes; however, its scope does not include manufacturing processes for:

  • Medical devices, except when part of a combination product
  • Dietary supplements
  • Medicated feed
  • Human tissues, except human-tissue engineering when part of an ATMP

Although these product categories are outside the scope of this technical report, regulatory guidance documents require the manufacturer to develop their validation programs. As such, the Parenteral Drug Association's (PDA) Technical Report No. 60 (Revised): Process Validation: A Lifecycle Approach would be a useful reference in the development of PV lifecycle approaches for other product categories. While the validation of ancillary supporting operations used in pharmaceutical manufacturing processes is not discussed in TR 60, separate PDA technical reports are available that provide specific guidance for such procedures as cleaning, aseptic process simulation (APS), moist-heat sterilization, and dry-heat sterilization (6-9).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Drug Substance, DS: The active ingredient used in drug manufacturing, which is later formulated with excipients into the final drug product.

Drug Product, DP: The dosage form in its final packaging, i.e., the finished product supplied to the market.

ATMP (Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products): Includes gene therapy, cell therapy, and tissue-engineered products, a frontier area of drug development in recent years.

RMAT (Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy): FDA's special expedited review pathway for regenerative medicine products.

Key Notes

The scope of TR60 is very broad and covers almost all drug types. Pay particular attention to the following points:

  • Applies to processes for new products and existing commercial products
  • Applies to global use scenarios
  • Medical devices "on their own" are out of scope, but the drug constituent of combination products is included
  • Supporting operations such as cleaning validation, aseptic process simulation (APS), and sterilization validation have their own dedicated PDA technical reports

Analogy

TR60 is like a general "cooking handbook" that applies to Chinese, Western, and Japanese cuisine, desserts, and other types of cooking. Specialized operations such as "how to wash knives properly" (cleaning validation) or "how to sanitize a chopping board" (sterilization validation) are covered by their own dedicated manuals. Likewise, dietary supplements (health foods) and animal feed have their own regulatory systems and fall outside the scope of this general handbook.

1.2 Background

Original Text

The lifecycle concept includes all phases in the life of a product from initial development and registration through commercial production, including post-approval changes and product discontinuation. The use of a lifecycle approach to pharmaceutical product quality is widely thought to facilitate innovation and continual improvement as well as strengthen the link between pharmaceutical development and manufacturing. The lifecycle philosophy is fundamental to the quality guidelines developed by the International Council of Harmonisation (ICH) that cover pharmaceutical development, QRM, pharmaceutical quality systems (PQS), development and manufacture of DSs, and technical and regulatory considerations for pharmaceutical product lifecycle management. Additional ICH guidelines have been issued for continuous manufacturing and method validation analytics (10-12).

These ICH principles provide the product lifecycle framework and quality-system enablers that have been used in pharmaceutical PV guidance documents. These documents advise that PV is not a one-time event, but an activity that spans the product lifecycle, linking process development to the commercial manufacturing process and its maintenance during routine commercial production.

ICH Quality Guideline Q8(R2): Pharmaceutical Development defines procedures for linking product- and process-development planning to the final commercial-process control strategy and quality system. It describes an enhanced scientific- and risk-based approach to product and process development that emphasizes statistical analysis and design of experiments (DoE), as well as the incorporation of knowledge gained from similar products and processes. Manufacturing capabilities and the quality system must be integrated into the process development plan to ensure effective and compliant commercial operations. The functionality and limitations of commercial manufacturing equipment are primary considerations in the process design.

ICH Quality Guideline Q9(R1): Quality Risk Management describes the use of a risk-based approach to pharmaceutical development and manufacturing quality. These approaches identify and prioritize those process parameters and product quality attributes with the greatest potential to affect product quality. Specific guidance on the application of ICH Q9(R1) concepts can be found in PDA Technical Report No. 54: Implementation of Quality Risk Management for Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology Manufacturing Operations, PDA Technical Report No. 54-5: Quality Risk Management for the Design, Qualification, and Operation of Manufacturing Systems, and PDA Technical Report No. 59: Utilization of Statistical Methods for Production and Business Processes. The FDA Guidance for Industry: Process Validation: General Principles and Practices stresses using a risk-based approach to develop criteria and process performance indicators and improve the design and execution of other validation-related activities (1).

The FDA and EMA PV guidances follow the principles of ICH Quality Guideline Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System, which conveys the essential need to integrate process design into the quality system. Throughout the development effort, the input and alignment of product and process development from a firm's Quality Unit are required to ensure compatibility with the quality system. Key considerations in product and process design include knowledge management (KM), development of a commercial control strategy, and use of modern QRM practices. Quality and regulatory organizational components should be part of the cross-functional team from the beginning of the PV study design to ensure that it is compatible with the firm's quality system and that it will meet regulatory agency expectations.

The Quality Unit provides the appropriate oversight of the PV studies required under GMP guidelines. Although not all lifecycle activities are required to be performed under GMPs (e.g., some Stage 1–Process Design studies), including representatives from the Quality and Regulatory Units on the cross-functional team is a wise decision (5). Documentation is an important element at all stages of lifecycle validation and, though the degree and type required varies throughout, documentation requirements are greatest during the process qualification and verification stages. Studies during these stages should conform to GMPs and should be approved by the Quality Unit.

The process validation master plan (PVMP) should describe the overall rationale, validation strategy, and list of specific studies and should reside within the firm's quality documentation system. A successful validation program is one that is initiated early in the product lifecycle and is kept current through change control throughout the lifecycle. A comprehensive corporate policy that defines the expectations and commitment to PV lifecycle principles is the foundation of a successful validation program. This policy should define the quality management philosophy, components of validation, change control, documentation requirements (including a PVMP), validation protocols and reports, and responsibilities of key stakeholders within the organization (13).

This document follows the principles and general recommendations presented in the current FDA validation guidance document and uses the traditional and nontraditional (enhanced) PV terminology employed by EMA. In this context, nontraditional or enhanced PV may use continuous process verification as an alternative approach to traditional PV. With the enhanced approach, manufacturing process performance is continuously monitored and evaluated. The enhanced approach is a science- and risk-based, real-time approach to verify and demonstrate that a process operates within specified parameters and consistently produces material that meets quality and process performance requirements (14).

The three-stage PV lifecycle nomenclature used by FDA—Stage 1–Process Design, Stage 2–Process Qualification, and Stage 3–Continued Process Verification (CPV)—is also used in this technical report (1). Section 3.0, Section 4.0, and Section 5.0 discuss implementation of these stages in detail. Figure 1.2-1🔍 shows the relationship between the relevant ICH guidance documents and the FDA stage approach to PV across the product lifecycle.

"Continuous process verification and continued process verification are distinct terms and have different meanings. Continuous process verification refers to validating manufacturing processes that use advanced manufacturing and analytical technologies (e.g., process analytical technology (PAT) systems). FDA uses the term continued process verification generally to mean those activities that maintain the process in a state of control, and it encompasses all manufacturing scenarios."

A variety of regulatory guidances combined with the experience and knowledge of a cross-section of industry professionals covering products within the scope of this document (e.g., large and small molecules, sterile and nonsterile products) provide the basis for this revision. It presents approaches to best practices that are scientifically sound, represent good business, and are designed to meet current regulatory requirements and expectations.

2.0 Glossary

Original Text

Definitions have been provided to help clarify the concepts discussed in this document. While some definitions used vary among companies, geographic location, etc., the definitions described below are consistently used within this Technical Report document. Where a definition is based on another published source, the source is cited.

Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API)

Any substance or mixture of substances intended to be used in the manufacture of a drug (medicinal) product and that, when used in the production of a drug, becomes an active ingredient of the drug product. Such substances are intended to furnish pharmacological activity or other direct effect in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease or to affect the structure and function of the body (15).

Note: The significant structural component of APIs is called "API Starting Material".

Note: Equivalent to drug substance (DS).

Attribute

A physical, chemical or microbiological property or characteristic of an input or output material (16).

Critical Quality Attribute (CQA)

A physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality (17).

Quality Attribute

A molecular or product characteristic that is selected for its ability to help indicate the quality of the product. Collectively, the quality attributes define identity, purity, potency and stability of the product, and safety with respect to adventitious agents. Specifications measure a selected subset of the quality attributes (18).

Component

Component means any ingredient intended for use in the manufacture of a drug product, including those that may not appear in such drug product (19).

Continued Process Verification (CPV)

Assuring that during routine production the process remains in a state of control (1).

Continuous Process Verification

An alternative approach to process validation in which manufacturing process performance is continuously monitored and evaluated (17).

Control Strategy

A planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control (5).

Data Integrity

The assurance that the data remains complete, consistent, accurate, trustworthy, and reliable throughout its life cycle. Maintaining data integrity is crucial for ensuring the validity of AI-driven insights, supporting regulatory compliance, and enabling reliable decision-making in pharmaceutical manufacturing (20).

Design of Experiments (DoE)

A structured, organized method for determining the relationship between factors affecting a process and the output of that process (17).

Note: This is typically referred to as "Formal Experimental Design".

Design Space

The multidimensional combination and interaction of input variables (e.g., material attributes) and process parameters have been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality. Working within the design space is not considered as a change. Movement out of the design space is a change and would normally initiate a regulatory post approval change process. Design space is proposed by the applicant and is subject to regulatory assessment and approval (17).

Drug Product (DP)

The dosage form in the final immediate packaging intended for marketing (15).

Drug Substance (DS)

The material which is subsequently formulated with excipients to produce the drug product. It can be composed of the desired product, product-related substances, and product- and process-related impurities. It may also contain excipients including other components such as buffers (21).

Good Engineering Practice

Established engineering methods and standards applied throughout the lifecycle to deliver appropriate and cost-effective solutions (22).

Intermediate

A material produced during the steps of the processing of an API that undergoes further molecular change or purification before it becomes an API. Intermediates may or may not be isolated (15).

Knowledge Management (KM)

Knowledge management is a systematic approach to acquiring, analyzing, storing and disseminating information related to products, manufacturing processes and components. Sources of knowledge include, but are not limited to, prior knowledge (public domain or internally documented); pharmaceutical development studies; technology transfer activities; process validation studies over the product lifecycle; manufacturing experience; innovation; continual improvement; and change management activities.

Lifecycle

All phases in the life of a product, from the initial development through marketing until the product's discontinuation (17).

Normal Operating Range (NOR)

A defined range, within the Proven Acceptable Range, specified in the manufacturing instructions as the target and range at which a process parameter is controlled, while producing unit operation material or final product meeting release criteria and Critical Quality Attributes (23).

Materials

A general term used to denote raw materials (starting materials, reagents, solvents), process aids, intermediates, APIs, and packaging and labeling materials.

In-Process Material

In-process material means any material fabricated, compounded, blended, or derived by chemical reaction that is produced for, and used in, the preparation of the drug product (19).

Raw Material

A general term used to denote starting materials, reagents, and solvents intended for use in the production of intermediates or APIs (12).

Operating Characteristic Curve

Depicts the discriminatory power of an acceptance sampling plan. The operating characteristic curve plots the probabilities of accepting a lot versus the fraction defective.

Critical Process Parameter (CPP)

A process parameter whose variability has an impact on a critical quality attribute and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces the desired quality (17).

Noncritical Process Parameter

A noncritical parameter has no impact on quality at the process step in question. Note: It may have an impact on the performance of the next process step and so may be monitored for process control purposes.

Process Parameter

An input variable or condition of the manufacturing process that can be directly controlled in the process. Typically, these parameters are physical or chemical (e.g., temperature, process time, column flow rate, column wash volume, reagent concentration, or buffer pH) (24).

Note: This is typically referred to as "Operational Parameter".

Performance Qualification (PQ)

The documented verification that systems and equipment can perform effectively and reproducibly based on the approved process method and product specifications.

Platform Manufacturing

The approach of developing a production strategy for a new drug starting from manufacturing processes similar to those used by the same manufacturer to manufacture other drugs of the same type (25).

Process Analytical Technology (PAT)

A system for designing, analyzing, and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements (i.e., during processing) of critical quality and performance attributes of raw and in-process materials and processes with the goal of ensuring final product quality (17).

Process Capability Index (CpK)

A statistical tool, to measure the ability of a process to produce output within a customer's specification limits. In simple words, it measures the producer's capability to produce a product within the customer's tolerance range. Cpk is used to estimate how close a given target is and how consistent it is with the average performance. Cpk provides the best-case scenario for the existing process. It can also estimate future process performance, assuming performance is consistent over time.

Process Performance Index (PpK)

A statistical tool to verify if the sample that has been generated from the process is capable of meeting the customer's requirements. It measures the performance of the process related to both dispersion and centeredness. In simple words, Ppk is an index of process performance that tells how well a system is meeting specifications and how well the process is centered within the specification limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Hierarchy of Terms

The TR60 glossary is more than a dictionary; it reflects the core logical structure of process validation. Let us understand the terms according to their logical relationships:

Hierarchy of quality attributes:

  • Attribute: A physical, chemical, or microbiological property of an input or output material -- the broadest concept
  • Quality Attribute: A molecular or product characteristic selected to indicate product quality -- all attributes related to quality
  • Critical Quality Attribute, CQA: An attribute that must be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality -- the most critical subset

Key Concepts - Classification of Process Parameters

Hierarchy of process parameters:

  • Process Parameter: An input variable or condition of the manufacturing process that can be directly controlled (e.g., temperature, time, flow rate)
  • Critical Process Parameter, CPP: A process parameter whose variability affects a CQA and therefore must be monitored or controlled
  • Noncritical Process Parameter: A parameter with no impact on quality at that process step, but which may affect the performance of the next step

Hierarchy of operating ranges:

  • Design Space: The multidimensional combination of input variables and process parameters demonstrated to assure quality -- the outermost layer
  • Proven Acceptable Range, PAR: The parameter range that has been demonstrated to be acceptable -- the middle layer
  • Normal Operating Range, NOR: The target and range specified in the manufacturing instructions -- the innermost layer, the actual target for daily operation

Analogy - Design Space vs NOR vs PAR

Imagine you are driving on a highway:

  • Design Space = the guardrails at the edges of the whole road; go beyond them and you "crash" (quality risk)
  • PAR (Proven Acceptable Range) = the lane markings; driving within the markings has been proven safe
  • NOR (Normal Operating Range) = the center of the lane your steering wheel actually aims for, the target of daily operation

The important point: operating within the Design Space is not a "change", but moving outside the Design Space is a change and requires initiating the regulatory post-approval change procedure.

Key Notes - CpK vs PpK

These two statistical indices are often confused, but there are important differences between them:

  • CpK (Process Capability Index): Measures the inherent capability of the process in a "stable state", calculated using within-subgroup variation. It represents the process's "best-case" performance.
  • PpK (Process Performance Index): Measures the "actual performance" of the process, calculated using total variation (both within-subgroup and between-subgroup variation). It reflects the process's true performance.

Practical judgment: if CpK >> PpK, the process has significant batch-to-batch variation (special-cause variation) that needs to be investigated.

Key Concepts - Other Important Terms

Control Strategy: A planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, that includes parameter controls, material attributes, facility and equipment conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and so on. It is one of the core outputs of the entire PV lifecycle.

Data Integrity: Ensuring that data remain complete, consistent, accurate, trustworthy, and reliable throughout the data lifecycle. TR60 (2026 revision) specifically mentions "the validity of AI-driven insights", reflecting the trend of digital transformation.

Knowledge Management, KM: A systematic approach to acquiring, analyzing, storing, and disseminating information related to products, manufacturing processes, and components. Sources of knowledge include prior knowledge, development studies, technology transfer, validation studies, manufacturing experience, and so on.

Platform Manufacturing: Developing a manufacturing strategy for a new drug by starting from similar processes the same manufacturer has already used to make similar drugs. This is especially important for CDMOs, as it can greatly shorten development time.

Analogy - Platform Manufacturing

Platform manufacturing is like a carmaker's "shared chassis platform" strategy. Toyota's TNGA platform can yield different models such as the Camry, RAV4, and Lexus ES. Similarly, a biopharmaceutical CDMO's monoclonal antibody production platform can be quickly adapted to different clients' mAb products by adjusting only a few specific parameters, greatly shortening development and validation time.

Key Notes - PAT and the Enhanced Approach

Process Analytical Technology, PAT: A system for designing, analyzing, and controlling manufacturing through real-time measurements (during processing). PAT is the technical foundation for realizing the enhanced approach and continuous process verification.

Practical examples of PAT:

  • Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIR) for real-time monitoring of blend uniformity
  • Raman spectroscopy for real-time monitoring of the crystallization process
  • Multivariate data analysis (MVDA) for real-time prediction of CQAs

  • FDA three-stage framework: Stage 1 (Process Design) → Stage 2 (Process Qualification) → Stage 3 (Continued Process Verification), forming the backbone of the PV lifecycle

3.1 Establish Quality Target Product Profile

Original Text

The aim of Stage 1 – Process Design is to design a quality product with a manufacturing process that consistently delivers the intended performance of the DP. Pharmaceutical development begins with the establishment of predefined objectives, ensuring the desired quality while considering product safety and efficacy. These objectives are described in the quality target product profile (QTPP). ICH Q8(R2) defines QTPP as a prospective summary of the quality characteristics of a DP that ideally will be achieved to ensure the desired quality of the DP, considering safety and efficacy. The QTPP is determined at the initiation of Stage 1 and is referenced throughout the product lifecycle (17).

The QTPP captures the high-level quality characteristics relevant for the DP, in addition to such characteristics as route of administration, dosage form, and strength, that should be derived from risk-based tools and should follow the principles outlined in ICH Q9(R1). The severity of harm, that is, the potential impact of the attributes on safety and efficacy, should be considered to identify and document the critical quality attributes (CQA). Various risk assessment tools referred to in ICH Q9(R1) can be used to evaluate the criticality of individual quality attributes. PDA Technical Report No. 81: Cell-Based Therapy Control Strategy provides examples of suitable risk assessment tools with low variability.

The initial manufacturing process used in GMP production for early clinical stages should be based on the knowledge obtained from early process-development activities and should be designed to ensure consistent product quality focusing on quality attributes related to patient safety. When defining the manufacturing process during development (and prior to the definition of a mature, comprehensive control strategy), a process description can be used to assist in implementing risk assessments and developing the control strategy. At this early stage, the manufacturing process could be heavily influenced by the need to quickly generate clinical-trial material where leveraging existing equipment and container–closure systems would be advantageous (as described further in bulleted listing below). The initial manufacturing process may be changed in consideration of the patient's needs, clinical trial outcomes, manufacturing constraints, and learnings from process development. Changes could come in equipment, product strength, product composition, or final pharmaceutical or biochemical form.

The manufacturing process is described as a series of constituent operations, which may be visualized in many formats including, but not limited to, a block or process-flow diagram that describes each unit operation. The following information should be included in the description of each item:

  • Process requirements, including raw materials, scale, and order of operations
  • Equipment train (make and model)
  • Set points and ranges (proven acceptable range (PAR), normal operating range (NOR), residence time distribution) for the process parameters
  • Identification and quantity of all material flows (additions or inputs, wastes, product streams)
  • Testing, sampling, and in-process controls
  • Hold times and hold conditions for product or process intermediates and additional solutions
  • Estimated step yields and processing durations
  • Sizing for equipment, including such items as chromatography columns and filtration units
  • Specific identification (e.g., manufacturer, part number) for manufacturing (e.g., filters) and product components (e.g., vials, stoppers)
  • Other information necessary to successfully reproduce the process

Process descriptions and scale-up effects, if any, are documented in reports and may be incorporated into the technology transfer (TT) package for the product. The process may change during Stage 1 for many reasons, for example, increases in material demand (i.e., process and analytical development, clinical needs), improved product understanding that leads to changes to CQAs, improved process understanding that results in the addition, elimination, or adjustment of unit operations and the corresponding process controls and in-process analytical tests. Documentation should capture these changes, and the supporting justifications for this information should be archived in the KM system.

The Process Description is a lifecycle document that is initiated in Stage 1 to support early clinical manufacturing, maintained, and appropriately updated to support Stages 2 and 3. Increased knowledge gained during process characterization studies may require additional changes to the process description. All changes to the process should be approved through change control procedures as defined by the organization's quality system.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile) is a core concept defined in ICH Q8(R2). It is a prospective document that sets, at the very start of product development, the quality goals for what the final product should look like.

The QTPP typically includes the following elements:

  • Route of administration: e.g., intravenous, subcutaneous
  • Dosage form: e.g., lyophilized powder, solution for injection
  • Strength: e.g., 100 mg/vial
  • Quality attributes: purity, sterility, endotoxin, particulates, etc.
  • Stability: storage conditions and shelf life
  • Container closure system: vial, prefilled syringe, etc.

Analogy

The QTPP is like the architectural blueprint and specification drawn up before building a house. Before construction (process development) begins, you must define clearly how many floors the house will have, what structure it will use, and how much wind and earthquake load it must withstand. Likewise, the QTPP defines the quality goals the product must meet at the start of development. Without these goals, every later design decision lacks a basis.

Key Notes

The Process Description is a lifecycle document, and this point is very important. The source text states clearly that it starts in Stage 1 and is updated continuously to support Stage 2 and Stage 3.

The ten items of required information listed in the source text (raw materials, equipment, process parameter ranges, material flow, sampling plan, etc.) are in essence a complete summary of process knowledge. For a CDMO, this document is the core of the Technology Transfer package.

PAR vs NOR

The source text mentions two key range concepts:

  • PAR (Proven Acceptable Range): the range of a parameter within which, as demonstrated by experiments, operation does not affect product quality
  • NOR (Normal Operating Range): the range actually used in routine production, usually narrower than the PAR

The PAR provides a safety margin, while the NOR is the "best-practice window" for routine operation. When designing the control strategy, the NOR sits within the PAR, while the regulatory-approved specification limits may be wider than or equal to the PAR.

Practical Application

For a CDMO receiving a technology transfer from a client, the completeness of the Process Description directly affects the success of the project. A good process description should include:

  • A complete list of unit operations and their sequence
  • PAR and NOR data for each step
  • Detailed specifications of critical raw materials and components (supplier, part number)
  • Equipment size specifications (e.g., chromatography column, filter area)

If a client provides only a vague process description, the CDMO should request supplementary information; otherwise it will face major challenges at scale-up.

3.2 Identify Critical Quality Attributes

Original Text

The QTPP provides an understanding of what will ensure the quality, safety, and efficacy of a specific product for the patient. Once the QTPP has been determined, its content can be used to establish the quality attributes. A quality attribute is a characteristic of a product that relates to quality; and is considered critical when those attributes are likely or certain to impact the safety and efficacy of a product.

As defined in ICH Quality Guideline Q8(R2): Pharmaceutical Development, a CQA is a "physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality" (17).

"A CQA is a physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality." — ICH Q8(R2)

Identifying CQAs is an integral part of manufacturing process development. At an early stage of process development, the information available on product attributes may be limited. For this reason, the first set of CQAs may come from prior knowledge obtained during early development or from similar products rather than from extensive product characterization. The degree of criticality assigned to quality attributes should be derived from risk-based tools following the principles outlined in ICH Q9(R1). The severity of harm, that is, the potential impact of the attributes on safety and efficacy, should be considered to identify and document the CQAs (28). Various risk assessment tools referred to in ICH Q9(R1) can be used to evaluate the criticality of individual quality attributes.

Following comprehensive assessments of scientific evidence and risk, quality attributes are ranked according to the degree of criticality. This may be a continuum that more accurately reflects the complexity of structure–function relationships and varying levels of uncertainty around attribute classification.

The identification of potential CQAs is an ongoing activity initiated early in product development. It makes use of general knowledge about the product and its application, as well as available clinical and nonclinical data. CQAs are subject to change in the early stages of product development and, thus, require a QRM approach that evolves as knowledge about the product and process is generated (refer to Section 6.1). CQAs for commercial products should be defined prior to the initiation of Stage 2 activities via a subsequent risk assessment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The CQA (Critical Quality Attribute) is a core concept in the QbD (Quality by Design) framework. ICH Q8(R2) defines a CQA as:

"A physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality."

The key phrase is "should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution": not every quality attribute is a CQA. Only those attributes with a possible or definite impact on safety and efficacy are classified as CQAs.

Common CQA examples:

  • Biological products: glycosylation pattern, aggregate content, charge variant distribution, potency
  • Small-molecule products: dissolution, content uniformity, impurity content, crystal form
  • Sterile products: sterility, endotoxin content, particulate content, container closure integrity

Analogy

Think of CQAs as the pre-flight safety checklist for an aircraft. An aircraft has thousands of parts and parameters, but not every one directly affects flight safety. Engine thrust, fuel quantity and the flight control system are "critical" (CQAs). The color of the seat covers and the placement of the in-flight magazines are also "quality attributes", but they are not "critical" quality attributes. The purpose of risk assessment is to distinguish which parameters truly affect "flight safety" (patient safety) and which merely affect the "passenger experience".

Key Notes

The source text makes two very important points:

  1. The criticality of a CQA is a continuum, not black and white. This reflects the modern view in ICH Q8-Q12: the criticality of quality attributes should be graded progressively rather than split into a simple "critical / non-critical" dichotomy.
  2. CQAs are dynamic and change as knowledge grows. Early on, the initial CQA list may be set based on prior knowledge or experience with similar products, but as development progresses and more data accumulate, the CQA list and rankings should be updated continually.

In practice, this means your risk assessment is not "done once and finished"; it must be revisited at each development milestone.

Regulatory Emphasis

The source text states explicitly: CQAs for a commercial product should be defined through subsequent risk assessment before Stage 2 activities begin.

This is a key timing requirement. Before entering PPQ, you must have completed:

  • A complete quality attribute risk assessment
  • Final confirmation and documentation of the CQAs
  • Design of the control strategy based on the CQAs

If this work is not completed before Stage 2, the design of the PPQ protocol will lack a scientific basis, which may lead to validation failure or regulatory deficiencies.

Practical Application

In a CDMO setting, the CQA identification process usually runs as follows:

  1. Initial assessment: build an initial CQA list based on product type, route of administration and experience with similar products
  2. Risk ranking: use FMEA (Failure Mode Effects Analysis) or other tools to score by Severity, Occurrence and Detectability
  3. Experimental verification: use Process Characterization studies to confirm which quality attributes are truly affected by process parameters
  4. Periodic review: keep updating the CQA list as clinical data and manufacturing experience accumulate

  2. If you are responsible for identifying the CQAs of a biosimilar, what sources can you draw on for "prior knowledge"?

3.4 Analytical Methods

Original Text

Analyses of raw materials, in-process samples, DS, and DP are important aspects of the control strategy and process characterization studies (refer to Section 3.8). Analytical methods used for such studies should be appropriate for their intended use, scientifically sound, reliable, and reproducible. Early-stage analytical method development may be optimized further down the lifecycle as appropriate, both on the improvement of the analytical technique as well as integrating different sample preparation processes to ensure meaningful test results (30). Strategies for qualification or validation of the analytical methods used during development have been published and provide approaches for evaluating tests used at this stage of the lifecycle (31).

The analytical method qualification should establish the expected performance of the method throughout the range of variation of the attribute experienced in quality control activities (e.g., range experienced at release, in-process controls, during stability studies). Guidance on expectations for the analytical methods is also outlined in the FDA's Guidance for Industry: Process Validation: General Principles and Practices. The process characterization plan and the associated study reports should document information on the analytical methods used during process characterization studies and the qualification of the methods. Methods should be targeted not only to assess the product-specific characteristics but also to ensure the correct monitoring of additional aspects with potential impact (e.g., bioburden, endotoxins in contamination controls).

The equipment utilized for process characterization studies (typically performed in development laboratories) must be adequately calibrated and maintained, and also satisfy the basic requirements for data integrity (DI).

The analytical test methods used to support PV programs must be developed in accordance with the analytical quality-by-design (AQbD) concepts, qualified, and subsequently monitored for continuous improvement adhering to a lifecycle approach. Test methods can be a significant contributing factor to the variability in reported results. ICH Quality Guideline Q14: Analytical Procedure Development incorporates the application of quality by design (QbD) principles into analytical method development (32). An analytical target profile (ATP), similar to a QTPP, provides a predefined objective that specifies the performance requirements for the analytical methods. ATPs include quality data attributes of the reportable result, such as accuracy and measurement uncertainty. The heightened understanding of the critical test method attributes and the development of an analytical control strategy can ensure reliable test results throughout the lifecycle. The assessment of the risk level of each method variable and determination of its impact on the reportable results is identified based on sound science, prior knowledge, and the design of analytical experiments. Method development activities design strategies to minimize input variables such that their impact on the test results is minimal. The development strategy considers aspects including preparation, measurement, instrumentation, and sampling strategy. Analytical lifecycle stages can be summarized into three stages as depicted in Figure 3.4-1🔍 and in Figure 3.0-1🔍 (33, 34).

With the adoption of the lifecycle concepts outlined in ICH Q14, the initial identification of CQAs is followed by a quality risk assessment (QRA) in Stage 1. This is a cause-and-effect analysis (e.g., fishbone or Ishikawa diagram) to identify process input parameters where variability is likely to have the greatest impact on product quality or process performance. This assessment is based primarily on prior knowledge or early development work, and the outcome provides the foundation for the process characterization studies that will follow (refer to Section 3.7).

Understanding the impact of process parameter variability and applying the appropriate controls is a fundamental element in the definition and development of the commercial control strategy. The analytical method development should take into consideration the inherent variability of the process and known impact of critical process parameters (CPP) onto CQAs. The overall process variability may include variance due to the process, as well as variance posed by the analytical method. ICH Q8(R2) defines a CPP as one "whose variability has an impact on a CQA and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces the desired quality" (35).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Analytical Quality by Design (AQbD): Applying QbD principles to analytical method development. Just as a process has a QTPP, an analytical method has an Analytical Target Profile (ATP), which predefines the performance requirements of the method (such as accuracy and measurement uncertainty).

ICH Q14 (Analytical Procedure Development guideline): A new guideline formally issued in 2023 that brings analytical method development into a lifecycle management framework. It emphasizes that a method must not only be "validated and passed" but also be continuously monitored and improved throughout the product lifecycle.

Data Integrity (DI): Ensuring that data remain accurate, complete, consistent and reliable throughout their lifecycle. Even laboratory equipment used during development must meet the basic requirements of DI.

Analogy

An analytical method is like a ruler:

  • If the ruler itself has an error of ±2 cm, then even if you measure 100 times, the data you obtain cannot truly reflect the actual dimension
  • AQbD is about first confirming "is this ruler accurate?" and then keeping it calibrated over its whole service life
  • The ATP is like declaring up front: "I need a ruler accurate to ±0.5 mm." This is your target specification

If you do not consider analytical method variability, you may mistake "measurement noise" for "process variability", leading to wrong parameter classification decisions.

Key Notes

Total variability = process variability + analytical method variability

This is a very important but often overlooked concept. When you observe variability in a CQA during a process characterization study, you must ask yourself: "How much of this variability comes from the process itself, and how much comes from imprecision of the test method?"

If analytical method variability accounts for more than 30% of the total observed variability, your assessment of the influence of process parameters may be seriously distorted. This is why TR60 emphasizes that analytical methods should also follow lifecycle management.

Formula

Total Observed Variance = Process Variance + Method Variance

Var(total) = Var(process) + Var(method)

Target: Var(method) / Var(total) < 0.3 (rule of thumb)

If the coefficient of variation (CV) of the analytical method is > 1/3 of the process CV,
improve the analytical method first, then proceed with the process characterization study.

Key Concepts

Critical Material Attribute (CMA): An attribute of a raw material, medium, solution, starting material or process intermediate whose variation affects a CQA. The CMA concept extends the scope of quality control from "process parameters" to "input materials".

Parameter Criticality Decision Tree: A structured decision tool used to systematically classify process variables as Critical, Key or Operational. The first step of the tree is to decide whether the variable is a "controllable input" or a "monitored output", which is the most basic classification logic.

Iterative Risk Assessment: Risk assessment is not a one-time activity. Before process characterization studies, make an initial assessment based on prior knowledge; after the studies are complete, update the assessment with the experimental data. Parameter designations may change in light of new data.

Analogy

A parameter designation decision tree is like emergency room triage:

  • When a patient arrives, the nurse first uses a standardized triage protocol (such as the ESI scale) for a quick assessment: life-threatening? Needs urgent treatment? Or can wait?
  • The decision tree works the same way: first ask "is this an input or an output?" → then "does it affect a CQA?" → then "does it affect process performance?"
  • Just as ER triage may be reassessed when a patient's condition changes, parameter designations are updated iteratively as new data become available

Key Notes

The challenge of non-standardization: TR60 states explicitly that, apart from the CPP defined in ICH Q8(R2), the methods for designating parameters are "not standardized and may differ between companies." This means:

  • Your company must establish unified internal definitions and ensure that all team members understand them consistently
  • These definitions must be kept consistent throughout the product lifecycle and must not vary by person or over time
  • This is especially important in a CDMO environment, because you may need to align with the parameter classification systems of different clients

3.6 Process Characterization

Original Text

Process characterization is a set of documented studies in which process parameters are purposely varied to determine their effect on product quality attributes and process performance. The approach uses the knowledge and information from process risk assessments to determine a set of process characterization studies to examine the main effect and the interaction effect of process parameters on proposed ranges of variation on CQAs. The resulting information is used to determine the parameter criticality and to define the PAR for the process parameters. If using an enhanced approach (i.e., incorporating advanced analytical and/or manufacturing control technologies), it can also be used to set the final parameter ranges and develop a design space to process development. Experiments can be designed to examine proposed ranges and explore ranges wider than those that would normally be used in operation (refer to Section 4.3).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Process Characterization: The core experimental activity of Stage 1. Researchers deliberately vary process parameters to systematically explore how these changes affect product quality and process performance. This is not "trial and error" but planned experimentation based on risk assessment.

Main Effect vs. Interaction Effect:

  • Main effect: The effect of a change in a single parameter on a quality attribute (for example, raising the temperature from 20°C to 25°C reduces yield by 3%)
  • Interaction effect: The combined effect produced when two or more parameters change at the same time (for example, higher temperature plus lower pH reduces yield by 15%, which is more than the sum of their individual effects)

Design Space: A concept defined in ICH Q8(R2): a multidimensional combination of parameters within which operation assures product quality. If the "Enhanced Approach" is adopted, process characterization data can be used to establish the design space, which represents the highest level of process understanding.

Analogy

Process characterization is like a mapping expedition:

  • You know roughly where you are going (the product quality goals), but you do not know the terrain of the land
  • You send out expedition teams (DOE experiments) along different routes and record which routes are safe (within the PAR) and which are dangerous (outside specification)
  • In the end you draw a "safe-zone map" (PAR or Design Space) that tells future travelers: "As long as you travel within this area, you will reach your destination safely"

The Enhanced Approach not only draws the safe routes but also uses mathematical models to predict the terrain of the whole region, giving you greater operating flexibility.

Key Notes

The studied range can be wider than the operating range: TR60 states explicitly that process characterization experiments may "explore a wider range than normal operation." This is not about making nonconforming product; it is about truly understanding where the boundaries of the process lie.

Only when you know "where it will fail" can you say with confidence "within this range it will succeed." This is the scientific basis for establishing a PAR.

Two approach pathways:

  • Traditional Approach: Establish a PAR and define a fixed operating range
  • Enhanced Approach: Establish a Design Space that allows flexible operation within a multidimensional space. It requires more data and deeper understanding but provides greater process flexibility.

Practice Questions

  1. Why should process characterization experiments be designed based on risk assessment results rather than studying all parameters?
  2. Give a concrete example of how an interaction effect can cause a more serious quality impact than a main effect.
  3. What kinds of products or processes suit the Traditional Approach (establishing a PAR) and the Enhanced Approach (establishing a Design Space), respectively?

4.0 Performance Qualification (Stage 2)

Original Text

Performance qualification (PQ) during Stage 2 demonstrates that the process, as designed, works as intended and yields reproducible commercial product. It should be successfully completed before the release of commercial product lots and consists of two elements:

  • Design and qualification of the facility, equipment, and utilities, which should be completed prior to PQ
  • PPQ, which confirms the process design and demonstrates the ability of the process to manufacture product that meets predetermined quality attributes

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Performance Qualification, PQ: The core goal of Stage 2 is to demonstrate that "the designed process actually performs as intended at commercial scale." This is not just a matter of running a few successful batches; it means systematically demonstrating the reproducibility of the process.

The two pillars of Stage 2:

  • System Qualification: Facilities, equipment, and utilities must first be qualified as fit for purpose. This is the foundation.
  • PPQ (Process Performance Qualification): Manufacturing with the commercial process on the qualified systems. This is building the house.

The sequence is strict: system qualification must be completed before PPQ.

Analogy

Imagine opening a new restaurant. Stage 2 is like the soft-opening phase:

  • System qualification = confirming that the kitchen equipment (ovens, refrigerators, exhaust hoods) is correctly installed and working properly
  • PPQ = actually using that equipment to produce complete meals from the menu and confirming that quality is consistent

You would not start baking cakes to sell to customers before the oven has passed acceptance. In the same way, PPQ should not start before the equipment is qualified.

Key Notes

PQ must be successfully completed before commercial product is released. This is the regulators' bottom line. Both the FDA 2011 Process Validation Guidance and EU GMP Annex 15 state this explicitly. Any batch released before PQ is complete may face regulatory risk.

4.1 Strategies for System Design and Qualification

Original Text

Facilities, equipment, utilities, and instruments (collectively referred to as systems) used in the manufacturing process should be suitable and capable for their intended process use, and their performance during the operation should be robust and reliable. Systems that affect product quality should be qualified to reduce the equipment performance as a process variable. The review and qualification of these systems should be performed according to a predefined project plan. System qualification should precede Stage 2 PPQ activities. Qualification studies should be completed, reviewed, and approved, with all deviations addressed, prior to the start of PPQ studies.

Section 4.0 provides considerations for the preparation and performance of system qualification. More information on approaches to planning and performing system qualification may be found in several sources (27, 47-49). Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Systems: TR60 refers to facilities, equipment, utilities, and instruments collectively as "systems." This common term emphasizes that this hardware is not a set of isolated items but one integrated manufacturing platform.

Why qualify systems? The purpose of qualification is to "eliminate equipment performance as a process variable." In other words, when equipment performance is stable and reliable, the variation observed in the process can be attributed to real process parameters rather than equipment instability.

Key Notes

Key timing requirements:

  • System qualification must be executed according to a predefined project plan
  • Qualification studies must be completed, reviewed, and approved, and all deviations resolved, all before PPQ begins
  • This is a gate criterion: PPQ cannot start until qualification is complete

Analogy

This follows the same logic as building a house: you must complete the foundation inspection (system qualification) before starting the walls and finishing (PPQ). If the foundation has cracks (unresolved deviations), you would not rush to build on top of it, because that only makes the problem harder and more expensive to fix.

4.1.1 Engineering and Design

Original Text

Facility, equipment, and utilities should be designed to meet process requirements. All qualification and validation activities should be planned, taking the lifecycle of facilities, equipment, utilities, process and product into consideration. The design of the facility and commissioning of the equipment and utilities should ensure the capability of operating as required for routine manufacturing. These activities and all commissioning-related tasks should be conducted according to good engineering practices and recorded according to good documentation practices, with oversight by the Quality Unit. A risk-based approach should be used, as appropriate, to evaluate the risks to product quality and patient safety related to the manufacturing system and the corresponding design solution to assure adequate controls and verification.

Specification and design activities should include a focus on those aspects that have been identified as being critical to product quality and patient safety. System design should be based on process parameters, control strategies, and performance requirements developed or identified during Stage 1. This information is transferred to those designing the engineering requirements for facility and manufacturing systems. Design qualification involves a review of the system design to assure that it is aligned with process control strategy and performance requirements. Specifications for equipment, facilities, utilities, or systems should be defined in User Requirements Specifications (URS) and/or a functional specification. The essential elements of quality need to be built in at this stage, and GMP risks should be mitigated to an acceptable level. The URS should be a point of reference throughout the validation lifecycle.

In situations where the process is being transferred to an operational facility with qualified equipment, a risk assessment should be performed to identify any equipment control gaps (36). These can be addressed through equipment modifications (which may require requalification) or through operational controls. Applying a life-cycle model to these situations is best practice.

A risk-based and science-based approach to the specification, design, verification, and qualification ensure that the manufacturing systems and equipment are fit for intended use (27).

4.1.1.1 Risk Assessment

Risk assessments determine which systems and system components have an impact on the establishment and maintenance of process parameters and conditions that affect product quality and patient safety. This information helps develop system qualification plans, protocols, test functions, and acceptance criteria. The process steps and systems that affect the product quality, mode of effects, and correlation between system performance and the control of process variables should be studied and understood. For more information on risk assessments, refer to Section 6.1.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Design Qualification, DQ: DQ is the first step of the qualification process: reviewing whether the system design is consistent with the process control strategy and performance requirements. DQ is not done after the equipment arrives; it must be completed at the design stage.

User Requirements Specifications, URS: The URS is the reference baseline for the entire qualification lifecycle. It defines the functional requirements that the equipment, facility, or utility must meet, and is derived from the process knowledge gained in Stage 1.

Good Engineering Practices: Commissioning activities should follow good engineering practices and be documented under good documentation practices, with the Quality Unit in an oversight role.

Analogy

The URS is like the "requirements list" you give a designer when renovating a new home: "the kitchen needs a double-bowl sink, a range hood with an exhaust rate of at least X CFM, and a four-burner gas stove." This list is the basis for later acceptance (qualification). If you find only two burners at handover, that is a "deviation" that must be addressed.

Key Notes

Special considerations for technology transfer: When a process is transferred to an operating facility that already has qualified equipment, this does not mean qualification can be skipped. TR60 explicitly requires a risk assessment to identify "equipment control gaps." Gaps can be closed through equipment modifications (which may require requalification) or operational controls.

This is especially important for CDMOs, because they frequently transfer different clients' processes onto existing lines.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A CDMO takes on a new client's protein drug filling project that must use an existing isolator filling line. Although the line is already qualified, the new product's viscosity is significantly higher than that of previous products. The CDMO should:

  • Conduct a risk assessment of the impact of high viscosity on filling pump performance and fill accuracy
  • Confirm whether the existing URS covers the new process requirements
  • If equipment modifications are needed (for example, replacing the pump head), perform partial requalification
  • Document all assessment results in the technology transfer documentation

Practice Questions

  1. What role does DQ play in the qualification sequence? Why must it be completed before IQ/OQ/PQ?
  2. If it is found during commissioning that the URS needs to be revised, how should this be handled? Which later steps would be affected?

4.1.2 Installation

Original Text

Upon completion of a setup, system testing and inspection should be used to verify that the systems have been fabricated, constructed, and installed to engineering and process specifications. The information from this verification should be accurate, reliable, and useful and may be leveraged or used to support qualification testing.

The start-up and commissioning of systems should confirm that they are in good working order and operate as designed. Engineering studies can provide confidence that the systems will perform under process conditions and will keep performing regardless of seasonal variations. Adjustments to the systems to achieve the specified level of performance and operation may be needed. Information on modifications or adjustments should be documented and transferred to the team preparing the qualification plans and protocols.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Commissioning: Confirms that the system is in good working order and operates as designed. Commissioning data can be "leveraged" to support formal qualification testing, which is an important opportunity to improve efficiency.

Installation: Verifies that the system has been correctly fabricated, built, and installed according to engineering and process specifications. The key is that the information must be accurate, reliable, and useful.

Key Notes

Seasonal variation: TR60 specifically notes that engineering studies should confirm that the system performs properly under different seasonal conditions. For example, high summer temperature and humidity may affect the dehumidification capacity of the HVAC system, and low winter temperatures may affect temperature maintenance in the Water for Injection (WFI) loop. These must be evaluated at the commissioning stage.

Information transfer: Any modification or adjustment made during commissioning must be documented and communicated to the team preparing the qualification plan and protocols. This ensures that qualification testing reflects the true state of the system.

Analogy

Commissioning is like a test drive before a new car is delivered: the salesperson takes you around the block to confirm that the car starts, steers, and brakes properly. If the steering wheel is found to be slightly off (and needs adjustment), that information must be recorded in the delivery report, not just communicated verbally with "it has been fixed."

4.1.3 Qualification Plan

Original Text

The qualification plan may be developed once the process requirements and correlation to process systems are understood. Early development of the qualification plans may provide valuable guidance to the design, installation, and commissioning efforts. To capture changes that result from start-up and commissioning, however, it may be prudent to complete the qualification plans and protocols after all the information from the commissioning has been transferred. This approach also enables a better understanding of the type and amount of information that can be leveraged from prequalification activities. This approach means that Stage 2 activities may be underway during and prior to completion of all Stage 1 activities.

4.1.3.1 Test Functions and Acceptance Criteria

System qualification tests or studies should be based on knowledge gained from previous activities, including Stage 1 and engineering studies. Test functions should be based on good scientific and engineering principles designed to demonstrate and assure that anticipated operating parameters will be met throughout the manufacturing process in a consistent and predictable manner. Acceptance criteria should be based on sound scientific rationale; the criteria should be useful, attainable, and where appropriate, quantifiable.

If sufficient process understanding is not available, or the scale-up effect is unknown, existing knowledge may be used during design and commissioning to define user requirements.

Formal system operating and maintenance procedures or instructions should be in place prior to the execution of test functions. Operators and those conducting studies should be trained in the operation of the systems and conduct of the tests. The training should be conducted under GMP conditions and documented according to good documentation practices (GDPs). All measuring and test instruments should be calibrated and traceable to appropriate standards.

When developing systems PQ studies, an effort should be made to design them as close to being representative of the future process as possible. Formulated placebos that possess major or critical qualities of the product being planned to run on the equipment could be used for this purpose. PQ studies conducted with such materials may help in future process understanding without using actual product. Deviations in the execution of qualification testing should be documented, investigated, and addressed. Conclusions should be based on the suitability and capability of the system to meet the process requirements. When necessary, systems may be modified, and studies may be repeated.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Qualification Plan: The qualification plan is the bridge document linking "process requirements" to "system capability." TR60 takes a pragmatic view: although drafting the qualification plan early provides guidance for design and installation, the final version is best finalized after commissioning is complete, because adjustments and changes made during commissioning need to be reflected in the plan.

Acceptance Criteria: These must be based on sound scientific rationale and should be:

  • Useful: Truly measure the suitability of the system
  • Attainable: Not unrealistic ideal values
  • Quantifiable: Expressed numerically wherever possible

Key Notes

Overlap of Stage 1 and Stage 2: TR60 states explicitly that Stage 2 activities may begin before Stage 1 is complete. This is not "skipping steps" but reasonable parallel work. The key is to ensure that the necessary Stage 1 information (such as process parameters and control strategy) is in place.

Use of placebos in PQ: Using formulated placebos for system PQ studies makes it possible to gain process understanding without using actual product. The placebo should have the main or critical characteristics of the product (such as viscosity and density).

Analogy

The qualification plan is like an exam syllabus: you first need to know what will be tested (process requirements) before you can design the questions (test items) and the grading standard (acceptance criteria). If the syllabus is issued too early and the teaching material is later revised (commissioning adjustments), the questions may no longer match the actual content. It is therefore best to finalize the syllabus after the teaching material is final.

  1. Why does TR60 recommend finalizing the qualification plan after commissioning is complete? What does this mean for project timeline management?
  2. What are the advantages and limitations of using placebos when designing PQ tests? In what situations must actual product be used?

4.1.4 Maintaining Systems in a State of Control

Original Text

Qualification studies ensure that the manufacturing systems, as designed and operated, are in a state of control throughout the lifecycle of products being manufactured. For the process to remain valid and controlled, the systems must be maintained to operate within the parameters established during qualification.

Periodic assessment and evaluation of the system to determine its control status is important. The assessment should include a review of information that indicates or supports assurance of control. This information may include, but is not limited to, such items as:

  • Calibration records
  • Preventive and corrective maintenance records
  • Equipment logs
  • Critical process alarms
  • Equipment (filters, compressed gasses, water) testing data
  • Training records
  • Standard operating procedures
  • Utilities standards
  • URS repositories
  • Change requests
  • Work orders
  • Monitoring results and trends
  • Nonconformance reports and deviations and their trending
  • Failure investigations and their trending
  • Periodic requalification assessments and studies

Periodic assessment of systems may lead to additional qualification-related activities or testing. In addition to periodic assessments, event-driven assessments and requalifications may arise from process-related changes, out-of-specification (OOS) results and trends, and investigations. The system assessments and the events that trigger event-driven assessments should be recorded in a formal procedure that also addresses the mechanism for deciding when requalification is warranted, the criteria for doing so, and those responsible for it. It is recommended that subject matter experts and the Quality Unit should also be involved in these decisions. Maintenance trending should be benchmarked and periodically reviewed at a quality council forum to assess a state of qualification at the facility that helps in determining PPQ readiness from an equipment, utilities, and facilities perspective. In case of an emergency, such as sudden novel disease outbreaks arising, submitting the proof of the manufacturing equipment qualification, as well as a list of critical equipment for a products' manufacturing, is especially important (27).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

State of Control: Qualification is not a one-time activity. Systems must be kept in a State of Control throughout the product lifecycle. This requires two mechanisms working together:

  • Periodic Assessment: Regularly review records and data to confirm that the system continues to operate within its qualified range
  • Event-Driven Assessment: Trigger a requalification assessment when changes, OOS results, or investigations occur

Key Notes

Quality review of maintenance trends: TR60 specifically requires that maintenance trends be reviewed regularly in a quality council forum. This is not solely an engineering matter; Quality must participate. This cross-functional review helps judge PPQ readiness from the equipment, utility, and facility perspective.

Emergency situations: In emergencies such as a sudden novel disease outbreak, submitting evidence of manufacturing equipment qualification together with a list of critical equipment is especially important. This reflects practical experience during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Analogy

Maintaining a State of Control is like maintaining a car: you cannot inspect it only when you buy it (initial qualification) and then ignore it afterwards. You need:

  • Scheduled servicing (periodic assessment): change the oil and check the brakes every 10,000 km
  • Event-driven repair (event-driven requalification): when the engine light comes on or an unusual noise appears, have it inspected immediately

More importantly, these maintenance records are not just for the technician. The manufacturer's quality department also reviews the trends regularly (similar to a quality council review).

Practical Application

CDMO practice: Build a digital dashboard of equipment qualification status that integrates the following information:

  • Calibration due dates and completion rate
  • Preventive maintenance completion rate and overdue items
  • Trends in deviations and OOS events
  • Impact assessment status of change requests

This dashboard can serve as a real-time "equipment readiness" reporting tool at quality council meetings, speeding up PPQ readiness decisions.

Practice Questions

  1. What is the difference between periodic assessment and event-driven assessment? Give one specific scenario for each that would trigger requalification.
  2. Why do maintenance trends need to be reviewed at the quality council level rather than managed solely within the engineering department?

4.2 Performance Qualification

Original Text

Performance qualification (PQ) must be performed at the end of the operational qualification (OQ) or in conjunction with the OQ or the PPQ. The following statements from EU GMP Annex 15 contains an accurate definition that PQ should include, but is not limited to, the following (50):

  • Testing using production materials, qualified substitutes or simulated product proven to have equivalent behavior under normal operating conditions with worst-case batch sizes. The frequency of sampling used to confirm process control should be justified.
  • Testing should cover the operating range of the intended process, unless documented evidence from the development phases confirming the operational ranges is available.

For parenteral products, the type of PQs to be performed can vary substantially and are designed to verify the ability to:

  • Clean components and equipment parts
  • Sterilize loads using steam or EtO (ethylene oxide), for example, and decontaminate via vapor phase hydrogen peroxide (VPHP) or other sterilants
  • Depyrogenate containers
  • Fill a placebo and fill media-filled units (APS)
  • Inspect placebo units
  • Ensure an environment meets microbiological and total-particle requirements
  • Perform clean-air projector use and unidirectional flows to protect from a possible contamination (air-flow pattern tests)

Usually, during the PQ, possible risks can be identified more precisely before going into the PPQ, because it is the first time that equipment, facility, components, and process come together. The fact that PQ is a partial simulation cannot rule out the possibility that some unexpected surprise could come up during the PPQ.

There is another potential activity, called Line PQ, that should be considered. Depending on the complexity of the system, there might be instances where the overall PQ of the system needs to be assessed following the successful PQ of specific components of the system. Therefore, the Line PQ is targeted to prove that a combination of individually qualified systems can interact successfully, yielding satisfactory performance. The Line PQ is focused on verifying the functionality of the system in its entirety with regard to real working conditions, including the use of manufacturing instructions. This can also test the full functionality and integration between the instructions, the process automation, and the equipment. If a product trial or APS is performed, the final report from these studies can fulfill the requirement of the Line PQ.

For this reason, it is also prudent to manufacture demonstration lots or engineering lots. These manufacture demonstration lots or engineering lots reflect the proposed process to be qualified, and may be manufactured with rejected DS or with non-commercial DS, such as material from engineering runs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PQ vs PPQ: the key difference:

  • PQ (Performance Qualification): Verifies the performance of an individual system or system component, and may use placebos, surrogates, or simulated product. The focus is "can the equipment/system do it?"
  • PPQ (Process Performance Qualification): Verifies the consistency of the entire process at commercial scale, using the actual commercial process and materials. The focus is "can the whole process consistently produce acceptable product?"

Line PQ: An important but often overlooked concept. After the individual systems have each passed PQ, you still need to confirm that these systems work smoothly "when combined." It is like a concert where every instrument has been tuned, but you only know whether they are in harmony once the whole orchestra plays together.

Key Notes

The many facets of PQ for injectables: TR60 lists the broad areas covered by PQ for injectables, from cleaning, sterilization, and depyrogenation to filling and environmental monitoring. This highlights the complexity of sterile manufacturing: it is not only the "filling" step, but the entire aseptic manufacturing system that must be qualified.

Value of Demo/Engineering Lots: Manufacturing demonstration or engineering lots before formal PPQ makes it possible to:

  • Find problems without compromising the integrity of PPQ data
  • Train operators
  • Verify that the batch record can be executed in practice
  • Use rejected or non-commercial DS to reduce cost

Analogy

The restaurant analogy for PQ vs PPQ:

  • PQ = testing each piece of kitchen equipment separately: can the oven reach the specified temperature, can the refrigerator hold cold-storage temperature, can the dishwasher get dishes clean
  • Line PQ = having the whole kitchen team use this equipment to run a complete simulated meal service
  • PPQ = the formal soft opening, with real ingredients, the real menu, and real customers
  • Demo lots = practicing the whole process with inexpensive ingredients before the soft opening

4.3 Process Performance Qualification

Original Text

PPQ marks the transition from development and clinical manufacturing to routine commercial production. The PPQ combines the actual facility, utilities, equipment (each now qualified), and trained personnel with the commercial manufacturing process, control procedures, and components to produce commercial batches. PPQ confirms the process design and the success of the process control strategy to produce batches at the commercial manufacturing scale, as expected. PPQ provides confidence that the systems of monitoring, control, and procedures in routine manufacturing are capable of detecting and compensating for potential sources of process variability over the product lifecycle.

The number of successful batches executed during the PPQ study should not be viewed as the primary objective of a PPQ campaign. While successful runs of commercial-scale batches can indicate overall operational proficiency and sound process design, these batches should also be viewed as a means to collect the data needed to demonstrate that the process control strategy is effective. The type and amount of data should be based on the understanding of the process, the impact of process variables on product quality, and the process control strategy developed during Stage 1. Prior knowledge should be used as well. For instance, in the cases of emergencies when the accelerated development and validation of products are desired, the data to support the formulation may differ depending on the platform but, at a minimum, should include the critical aspects of product characterization, a potency assay, and the data on stability generated, as well as the qualification state of the manufacturing equipment being used (27). The number of batches needed to acquire this information and data may be mainly based on a statistically sound sampling plan that supports the desired confidence level. It may also be influenced by the approach selected to demonstrate that the within-batch and batch-to-batch variabilities of CQAs are acceptable.

The entirety of Section 4.3 discusses design strategy for the PPQ, recommended content for the protocol and report, and transition to Stage 3 of the PV lifecycle.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PPQ (Process Performance Qualification): PPQ is the turning point from development/clinical manufacturing to routine commercial production. In essence, it brings all of the qualified elements together:

  • Qualified facilities, utilities, and equipment
  • Trained personnel
  • The commercial process, control procedures, and components

The aim is to demonstrate that the process control strategy is effective at commercial scale and can detect and compensate for process variability.

Key Notes

PPQ is not just "running a few successful batches": TR60 states explicitly that the primary goal of PPQ is not the number of successfully manufactured batches. Successful batches are the "means" of collecting data; the real goal is to demonstrate the effectiveness of the process control strategy. This shift in thinking is very important. In the past, industry often treated "three successful batches" as the ultimate goal of PPQ, but the modern lifecycle approach emphasizes data-driven confidence.

Flexibility in emergencies: TR60 also recognizes that when development and validation are accelerated in emergencies (such as a pandemic), data requirements may differ, but should at least include:

  • Key aspects of product characterization
  • Potency assay
  • Stability data
  • Qualification status of manufacturing equipment

Analogy

PPQ is like the formal driving road test:

  • The vehicle has passed inspection (equipment qualification)
  • You have been trained at driving school (personnel training)
  • The test route has been planned (process and control procedures)
  • The purpose of the road test is not just to "reach the destination" (successful batches), but to show that throughout the drive you follow traffic rules and can handle unexpected situations (effectiveness of the process control strategy)

PPQ Data Requirement Decision Logic

Factors that determine the number of PPQ batches:
  ├─ Level of process understanding (Stage 1 knowledge)
  ├─ Impact of process variables on quality
  ├─ Completeness of the process control strategy
  ├─ Availability of prior knowledge
  └─ Statistical sampling plan (supporting the required confidence level)

Dimensions of variability assessment:
  ├─ Within-batch variability
  └─ Batch-to-batch variability

4.3.1 Process Performance Qualification Readiness

Original Text

The transition from Stage 1 to Stage 2 of the PV lifecycle may not be strictly sequential. Completion of some Stage 1 activities may overlap with those from Stage 2 and may include parallel studies, such as mixing and/or hold studies. Likewise, some preparative Stage 2 activities will be initiated in parallel with those from later Stage 1 activities. Components of Stage 2 PPQ readiness activities (refer to Section 3.1) include, among others:

  • Drafting PV master plan
  • Drafting project plan
  • Qualifying facilities, utilities, and equipment
  • Completing successfully the required PQ studies, including APS
  • Training personnel
  • Drafting the initial CPV plan

Although the initiation of PPQ activities is not contingent upon completion of all Stage 1 activities, a readiness assessment should be conducted to determine the timing of sufficient information and completion of activities to support moving forward with PPQ batch manufacture. The readiness assessment should include deliverables from Stage 1, as outlined starting from Section 3.1, and other elements as required:

  • Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP): Initiated at the start of Stage 1, it should be updated to reflect the knowledge obtained from Stage 1 prior to initiating PPQ (17).
  • Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) with Criticality Assessment: CQAs are identified early in Stage 1. They are confirmed to account for additional analytical characterization, clinical and/or nonclinical data, and information gathered during Stage 1. CPPs that impact CQAs are reviewed and updated based on detectability and occurrence (17, 27).
  • Material Attributes with Criticality Assessment: Material attributes are identified early in Stage 1. They are confirmed to account for additional analytical characterization, clinical and/or nonclinical data, and information gathered during Stage 1. CPPs that impact material quality attributes are reviewed and updated based on detectability and occurrence (17, 27).
  • Commercial Manufacturing Process Description: This is started in Stage 1 and updated to reflect the finalized commercial process supported by Stage 1 studies and data. These include elements outlined in Section 3.4 and any changes resulting from the qualification of the facilities, utilities, and equipment as outlined in Section 4.1.
  • Analytical Methods: Appropriately validated or suitably qualified methods should be identified. Methods for product release and stability should be fully validated according to ICH requirements prior to initiating PPQ batch testing. Any additional tests beyond normal release testing used to support PPQ should be identified and suitably qualified/validated prior to being used to test PPQ batches (33, 34, 51). The justification of the status for use in the PPQ studies (qualified and/or validated) should be fully documented for each analytical method.
  • Approved Commercial Batch Production and Control Records: Changes may be made to batch records during Stage 1 to enhance, clarify, or optimize manufacturing instructions and/or reflect knowledge gained during process characterization. Master batch records reflecting the final commercial process to be studied in PPQ should be approved prior to PPQ batch execution.
  • Process Design and Development Report: This report (refer to Section 3.7) is documentation for the process design and development justifications, which includes risk-ranking of parameters and ranges for the process that will undergo PPQ study. This information should be finalized prior to the PPQ study design since it provides the scientific support to justify the PPQ acceptance criteria.
  • PPQ Risk Assessment Report Prior to Stage 2: This report documents risk assessment exercise(s) based on the knowledge summarized in the Process Design and Development Report. This activity summarizes decisions regarding the type and extent of process controls of the process being qualified. These risk management events should be conducted by an interdisciplinary team that includes experts from a variety of disciplines (e.g., process engineering, industrial pharmacy, analytical chemistry, microbiology, statistics, manufacturing, and quality assurance).
  • Process Validation Master Plan (PVMP): Drafting of the PVMP should begin in Stage 1 and should be finalized prior to initiation of the PPQ study. Elements of the PVMP are outlined in Section 3.8.
  • Quality System and Training: Qualified and trained personnel are essential to PPQ studies. Detailed, documented training specific to the PPQ is required for functional groups directly involved in the execution of each study. To minimize the risk of human error, personnel should understand their role in protocol execution. Quality Unit approval of PPQ activities should be completed prior to initiation of every PPQ study, and all PPQ studies should be conducted within the quality system.
  • Approved Protocols for PPQ Studies: Protocols for each study should be approved, including Quality Unit approval, and finalized prior to initiation of PPQ studies. Refer to Section 4.4 for the design and content of PPQ protocols.

4.4 Design Strategy for Process Performance Qualification

Original Text

Design Strategy for PPQ refers to a systematic approach used in the pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical industries to ensure that a manufacturing process is capable of consistently producing products that meet predefined specifications and quality attributes. The strategy focuses on validating the manufacturing process through testing and gathering data under normal operating conditions. This strategy should be internally evaluated by a multi-department team, including quality assurance and regulatory affairs personnel.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PPQ Design Strategy: This is not the traditional "just run three batches" mindset. A PPQ design strategy is a systematic approach that starts from process understanding and uses scientifically designed studies to demonstrate that the commercial process can consistently produce acceptable product.

The key phrase is "predefined specifications": all acceptance criteria must be defined before PPQ is executed, not adjusted after the fact. This reflects the Quality by Design (QbD) philosophy emphasized in ICH Q8/Q9/Q10.

Analogy

A PPQ design strategy is like planning a major final exam: you cannot wait until exam day to decide what is covered. The teacher (the process development team) must decide, based on everything taught during the term (Stage 1 data), what the exam questions are (PPQ tests), how it is graded (acceptance criteria), and how many questions are needed to assess the students' ability fully (number of batches and sampling strategy).

Key Notes

TR60 specifically stresses that the PPQ design strategy needs to be evaluated by a multi-department team, including QA and RA personnel. PPQ is therefore not only a manufacturing matter: early involvement of Quality Assurance and Regulatory Affairs is essential to ensure the strategy is compliant.

4.4.1 Use of Prior Knowledge and Stage 1 Data to Support PPQ

Original Text

In a lifecycle approach to PV, sources of data and information outside of the PPQ batches may be used to support a high degree of confidence in an ongoing state of process control. Prior knowledge is that which has been gained from similar products and processes. It may come from experience with a portfolio of similar molecules, where platform manufacturing strategies have been developed using existing facilities and equipment (e.g., platform manufacturing processes for monoclonal antibodies) or from similar process and unit operations. Leveraging the data from similar products and processes may provide an additional level of confidence in the process control of a product and process that uses a similar control strategy and unit operations. However, the situation could change in the case of molecule-specific sensitivity (e.g., sensitivity to gamma-irradiated critical consumables or VPHP) resulting in the need to apply changes to the platform manufacturing strategies (e.g., switching from gamma-sterilized to steam-sterilized consumables) and to consider additional aspects of the control strategy.

By contrast, likely first-in-class molecules and/or products manufactured in new facilities or with new equipment will probably require increased emphasis on data-gathering in Stage 1 to support PPQ readiness. To gather sufficient data to demonstrate an acceptable level of confidence in the commercial manufacturing process when little prior knowledge or Stage 1 data are available, the scope and extent of PPQ may need to be increased. This increased scope can both be translated in the need to run demo or engineering lots prior to official PPQ lots and/or by increasing the number of PPQ lots. Demo lots or engineering lots may be manufactured to gain knowledge into the process while also exploring operational ranges for CPPs. Successful demo or engineering lots can be used as a gateway to proceed into PPQ. In addition, successful completion of demo or engineering lots could be used to justify the number of lots needed for PPQ.

The rationale and scientific justification for the use of existing data (prior knowledge) to support Stage 2 should be documented in the PV master plan. All prior knowledge and Stage 1 data used to support PPQ must be retrievable, traceable, verified, and generated using good scientific practices.

Where there is greater prior knowledge or process design for a new product or process, that knowledge and data is used in a scientific, statistical, and risk-based manner to determine the appropriate size of PPQ studies. Limited prior knowledge will require proportionally more Stage 1 and/or PPQ knowledge.

Some examples of cases where prior knowledge may be useful for PPQ include:

  • Setting acceptance criteria in PPQ studies, for example, bioburden and endotoxin in-process acceptance criteria, in cases where facility history and limits for other processes can be applied to similar processes that employ the same facility and equipment. (Assumes the limits for the previous product are appropriate for the quality of the new product.)
  • Using data from other product PPQ supportive studies, for example, in platform purification operations where the same or similar buffer formulations will be used in the same vessels, buffer hold-studies already performed for a different product can be used to support the PPQ for buffers used for the new product (52).
Note: For more information regarding solid and semi-solid dosage forms, refer to TR 60-2 (53).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Prior Knowledge: Knowledge and data gained from similar products and processes. In biopharmaceuticals this typically comes from platform manufacturing strategies, for example a platform process based on monoclonal antibodies (mAbs).

TR60 clearly indicates an inverse relationship between prior knowledge and the scope of PPQ:

  • Sufficient prior knowledge → the PPQ scope can be moderately streamlined
  • Limited prior knowledge → more Stage 1 studies and/or more PPQ batches are needed

Demo / Engineering Lots: Non-commercial batches manufactured before the formal PPQ batches, used to explore CPP operating ranges, build process knowledge, and serve as a readiness assessment before entering PPQ.

Analogy

Prior knowledge is like the cooking experience you build up in a new kitchen. If you have already baked hundreds of cakes in the same brand of oven (a platform process), you roughly know how to set the temperature and time. But with a brand-new oven you have never used (first-in-class), you need a few trial bakes (demo lots) to get familiar with the new equipment before you can confidently deliver to customers.

And "molecule-specific sensitivity" is like discovering that certain ingredients react unexpectedly with a particular type of baking tray (for example, acidic fruit touching aluminum foil): even if you know the oven well, you still need to adjust your strategy for special materials.

Key Notes

The four requirements for using prior knowledge: All prior knowledge used to support PPQ must be:

  • Retrievable: the original data can be found
  • Traceable: the source and the generation process of the data can be traced
  • Verified: the accuracy of the data has been confirmed
  • Generated using good scientific practices: studies are designed and executed in line with scientific principles

These requirements ensure that prior knowledge is not just a "gut-feeling" opinion but reliable data with a scientific basis.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Suppose your CDMO has three mAb products that have completed PPQ, all using the same Protein A chromatography column and the same CIP procedure. When a fourth mAb product comes in, you can cite the column cleaning validation data of the first three products as prior knowledge to reduce the PPQ testing scope for this unit operation in the fourth product. The scientific justification must, however, be documented in detail in the PVMP.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Conditions for using Stage 1 data to support PPQ:

  • Scale Representativeness: Stage 1 data must be representative of the commercial-scale process, or come from a qualified small-scale model. This is the prerequisite for using small-scale data to support large-scale PPQ.
  • Sufficient Scientific Evidence: Data cannot be cited simply because it "exists"; there must be a scientific rationale explaining why the data are applicable at commercial scale.

Qualified Small-Scale Model: This is an important tool in process development. A small-scale model being "qualified" means it has been scientifically shown to faithfully reflect the behavior of the large-scale process, including CQA trends and variability.

Analogy

Using Stage 1 data to support PPQ is like using architectural model testing to support the safety assessment of an actual building. Architects can use data from a small wind-tunnel model to predict the wind load of the real building, but only if the model has been through rigorous "scale verification" (that is, a qualified small-scale model), confirming that the behavior of the scaled-down version can be reliably extrapolated to full size.

Key Notes

Confirmation Strategy: Even when Stage 1 data can be used to support PPQ, TR60 still recommends stricter testing at commercial scale to confirm the small-scale results. For example:

  • Small-scale chromatography column lifetime study → at commercial scale, enhanced impurity monitoring until the maximum reuse limit is reached
  • This approach takes advantage of the efficiency of small-scale data while providing a safeguard at large scale

This reflects the process validation philosophy of "Trust but verify".

4.4.2.1 Risk-Based Process Performance Qualification Sample-Size Justification

Original Text

QRM or risk-based approaches are used to assist in demonstrating control and capability throughout the PV lifecycle activities. Risk-based rationales are used to justify the number of batches manufactured and the number of samples taken during PPQ. It should be noted that there is no set number of expected batches prescribed in regulatory expectations or industry standard practice because the number of batches will be unique to a given product and process based upon the variability. The rationales assure sufficient data is available for the evaluation to demonstrate, with a high level of assurance, a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product, allowing the normal range of variation and trends to be established and providing sufficient data for evaluation. The evaluation of data should include objective measures (e.g., statistical metrics), wherever feasible and meaningful, to achieve adequate assurance.

Additionally, FDA's GMP regulations regarding sampling set forth a number of requirements for validation:

  • Samples must represent the batch under analysis (54)
  • Sampling plan must result in statistical confidence (55)
  • Batch(es) must meet its predetermined specifications (55)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Risk-Based Sample-Size Justification: This is the most challenging part of PPQ design and the part most often scrutinized by regulators. The core principles include:

  • No fixed number of batches: Neither regulations nor industry standards prescribe a single "correct" number of PPQ batches. The traditional "three-batch validation" concept has been abandoned.
  • Based on variability: The number of batches depends on the variability characteristics of the specific product and process. The greater the variability, the more batches are generally needed to build confidence.
  • Objective measurement: Data evaluation should use objective methods such as statistical metrics wherever possible, rather than relying solely on subjective judgment.

Analogy

Deciding how many batches PPQ needs is like deciding how many dishes you must taste to judge the quality of a restaurant. For a chain restaurant (platform process), tasting a few signature dishes may be enough to form a judgment; for a brand-new creative-cuisine restaurant (first-in-class), you may need several visits and more dishes before you can be confident the quality is stable.

And sample representativeness is like not tasting only the surface of the soup: you have to stir it and sample from different depths for the sample to represent the flavor of the whole pot.

Key Notes

The three FDA GMP sampling requirements:

  1. Representativeness: Samples must be representative of the batch being analyzed, which means the choice of sampling points (location, time) must have a scientific basis
  2. Statistical Confidence: The sampling plan must be capable of producing statistically meaningful conclusions, which requires that the number of samples be statistically justified
  3. Meet Predetermined Specifications: Batches must meet specifications set in advance; acceptance criteria cannot be modified after the results are seen

Together these three requirements form the regulatory foundation of the PPQ sampling strategy, and every PPQ Protocol must address them clearly.

Statistical Considerations

Although TR60 does not give a specific formula in this section, sample-size justification typically involves the following statistical concepts:

Confidence Level = usually 95% or 99%
Coverage = usually 95% or 99%
Tolerance Interval = used to evaluate within-batch and between-batch variability

Example: 95% confidence level / 95% coverage
= at least 95% confidence that at least 95% of the product meets specifications

The number of samples n depends on:
- Known process variability
- Required confidence level
- Required coverage
- Acceptable risk level

4.4.2.1 Enhanced Sampling Plans & Statistical Tools for PPQ

Original Text

Finally, the number of samples should:

  • Be adequate to provide sufficient statistical confidence of quality both within a batch and between batches
  • Have risk-based statistical testing levels tied to the relative criticality of the particular attribute under examination
  • Allow the normal range of variation and trends to be established
  • Provide sufficient data for evaluation

Sampling during this stage should be more extensive than is typical during routine production; this can be called an enhanced sampling plan. The plan has risk-based components, uses general prior knowledge, and allows for statistically based confidence statements regarding the firm's understanding of within- and between-batch variability.

There are three basic components or tools commonly used to demonstrate statistical confidence and understanding of variation for enhanced sampling plans both within a batch and between batches for aseptically filled products. The basic components included in the PPQ data analysis of each attribute are (refer to Section 6.2):

  • Individual Value Plots: General or basic analysis and graphing, preferably using individual value plots; visually describes inter- and intra-batch variation.
  • Tolerance Intervals: Tolerance interval is one of the most commonly used tools applied during PV; it provides a method to measure the control and capability of product attributes. The tolerance interval level being tested ties back to the relative criticality of the product attributes and compares the variation seen for each product attribute (by batch) to the product specification. This comparison is an objective measure that allows for a capability statement to be provided as part of PV for each attribute tested.
  • Variance Components: In the case of an aseptically filled DP, variance components describe the percentage of variance that can be attributed to within-batch or batch-to-batch variability meeting guidance requirements.

Enhanced sampling plans for aseptically filled products (e.g., vaccines, biologics, small-molecule parenterals) take samples that align with three time periods: beginning, middle, and end positions (B/M/E). 4.4.2.1.1 Sample-Size Selection Considerations

Original Text

Generally, enhanced sample-size selection is based on a combination of prior knowledge and the relative criticality of the attributes. Prior knowledge may come from legacy PPQ batches or pre-PPQ batches (e.g., engineering, clinical), while the relative criticality of the attribute is derived during Stage 1 CQA determination. The approach uses the same analysis tool that will be applied during the PPQ analysis, the tolerance interval. Normal tolerance intervals are constructed and compared to the release-acceptance criteria for each evaluated attribute to provide confidence that all current and future measurements will fall within a percentage of the required coverage (based on criticality).

The enhanced plan has risk-based components, uses general prior knowledge, and allows for statistically based confidence statements regarding the firm's understanding of within- and between-batch variability.

Occasionally, due to a number of considerations, such as in a pandemic response, prior knowledge is not available to construct these intervals. As such, each attribute selected for enhanced sampling can be sampled at the minimum, that is, n = 6 (2 each for B/M/E), at a calculated, uniform 95/95 confidence level and coverage. The minimum n = 6 sample size is appropriate as it will allow statements of confidence and of inter- and intra-batch variability using variance components for each attribute within the enhanced sampling plan.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Logic for Deciding the Sample Size

The sample size is not decided by guesswork; it is based on two inputs:

  • Prior Knowledge: development data from Stage 1, including historical data from clinical batches, characterization studies or engineering batches
  • CQA Criticality: the criticality level of the attribute, already determined in the Stage 1 risk assessment

The core method is to use Stage 1 data to estimate the required sample size, using the same tool as the PPQ analysis, the Tolerance Interval, to work backwards to the number of samples needed to reach the target confidence level.

Key Notes: Minimum Sampling Strategy in Emergency Situations

TR60 specifically mentions special situations such as pandemic response. When there is not enough prior knowledge, a uniform minimum sampling strategy can be adopted:

  • n=6 for every attribute (2 each at B/M/E)
  • A uniform 95/95 confidence/coverage level
  • Although the sample size is the minimum, it is still sufficient for variance components analysis and confidence statements

This is a "safety floor" design: it ensures that meaningful statistical conclusions can still be obtained even in the most urgent circumstances.

Analogy

It is like a medical check-up plan. If you have a full medical history (prior knowledge), the doctor can design customized tests around your risk factors (risk-tiered sampling). But if it is your first visit and you have no records (no prior knowledge), the doctor will arrange a standard basic check-up package (uniform 95/95 minimum sampling) to make sure the main problems are at least detected.

4.4.2.1.3 Drug Product PPQ Analysis of Enhanced Samples

Original Text

If possible, the drug-product enhanced sample analysis should be structured in the PPQ in the following manner:

  • Summary table for all attributes provides summary statistics as well the tolerance intervals and variance components, as appropriate, per batch.
  • Summary analysis for each attribute, including:
    • Graphical analysis using individual value plots per attribute. All batches should be plotted on the graph for maximum effect. Reference lines denoting specification limits should be used to show the data in context.
    • Tolerance interval charts per attribute per batch.
    • Variance component analysis table per batch.
    • Summary statement per attribute regarding achieved-control and capability-based upon the analysis presented.

There are cases when analysis cannot be executed, for instance when:

  • Values are below a limit of detection, which means there are no values to report, in which case, neither graphical nor statistical analysis can be performed.
  • There are not enough unique values to perform the analysis either due to the rounding of the data, precision of the method, or very little variation in the process. This can be minimized by requesting raw analytical data (full recorded values) rather than reported data (which may be truncated). In the case of not-enough unique values, graphical analysis can be performed, but the tolerance interval and variance component cannot. Graphical analysis in this situation can still provide powerful visual support for control and capability within and between batches.

The samples must be tested and analyzed using statistical tools and graphing, as appropriate, to provide sufficient statistical confidence of quality within and between batches as well as evidence the product and process are ready for commercial manufacturing. Minimum acceptance requires that the test results are within release specification based on the release criteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Structured Analysis Framework for the PPQ Report

TR60 recommends that the PPQ report contain a clearly layered analysis structure:

  1. Overview summary table: summary statistics, tolerance intervals and variance components for all attributes, presented by batch
  2. Detailed analysis for each attribute:
    • Individual value plot (all batches on the same plot)
    • Tolerance interval chart
    • Variance components analysis table
    • Control and capability summary statement for each attribute

This structured approach lets reviewers quickly grasp the overall picture while still being able to examine individual attributes in depth.

In practice there are two common situations in which statistical analysis cannot be performed:

  • Below the limit of detection (LOD): all values are "<LOD" and there is no reportable value, so neither graphical nor statistical analysis can be carried out
  • Not enough unique values: because of data rounding, limits of method precision or very small process variation, most results are identical; tolerance intervals and variance components cannot then be calculated, but graphical analysis can still provide strong visual evidence

Practical recommendation: ask the analytical laboratory for the untruncated raw analytical data rather than the rounded reported data; this can greatly reduce the "not enough unique values" problem.

  • Control statements = the summary investment recommendation for each stock

Reviewers (like an investment committee) need to see both the big picture and the individual details to make a decision.

4.4.2.2 Risk-Based Justification for Number of PPQ Batches

Original Text

An effective PPQ should evaluate a sound process design, an effective control strategy, and operational proficiency at commercial scale (56). The number of batches in the PPQ study(ies) will be influenced by many factors such as the:

  • Study(ies) performance and acceptance criteria
  • Analyses to be performed and the type and amount of data necessary to perform those analyses
  • Level of process knowledge and understanding gained from Stage 1, as well as from the engineering runs in the commercial facility
  • Type and complexity of manufacturing technology employed in the various unit operations
  • Knowledge from previous experience with similar well-controlled processes
  • Inherent or known variability of the process resulting from raw materials, age of the equipment, or operator experience

Using risk-based approaches allows a balance between the number of batches studied and the risk of the process. They can also be used in conjunction with objective approaches to determine the number of batches to include.

Where practical, statistical methods are recommended to guide the determination of the number of PPQ batches needed to achieve a desired level of statistical confidence (refer to Section 6.1.1 and Section 8.0). However, this approach alone may not always be feasible or meaningful. One such example is PPQ studies of a protein DS process with a limited number of clinical batches. This limited output could be due to such factors as manufacturing scale or product indications (e.g., orphan drug) where infrequent future manufacturing campaigns are to be performed. In addition to limitations on manufacturing batch production, the nature of protein drug-substance manufacturing makes increased number of samples of the process streams of limited usefulness to achieve a statistically significant sample size.

When it is not feasible or meaningful to use conventional statistical approaches, a practical, scientifically based, holistic approach may be more appropriate. In this case, the following factors may be used to support the rationale for the number of PPQ batches selected based on knowledge accumulation:

  • Prior knowledge (e.g., development/clinical batches) and platform manufacturing information or data
  • Risk analysis of the process to factor the level of risk into the batch number selection
  • Increased reliance on Stage 1 data to support that the process is under control and to add to the dataset
  • Continuation of heightened sampling or testing plans during CPV until a sufficient dataset to achieve statistical confidence has been accumulated
  • For very expensive products, understanding of the probability of product approval for release-to-market versus the demonstrated shelf life (i.e., a late approval could mean wasting PPQ lots)

When a combination of approaches and data are used, the rationale and justification should be clearly documented in the PVMP. Also, references to all supporting source data should be included.

The risk-based assessment method for the selection of an acceptable number of PPQ batches is based upon process, product, business needs and site knowledge. As knowledge increases, the potential starting point for the number of batches decreases.

The approach described here makes use of a decision-tree or checklist approach. These approaches employ Yes or No questions to guide the decision-making process, which reduces the approach/assessment subjectivity bias. A risk-evaluation table is used to guide the determination (refer to Table 4.4.2.2-1🔍).

There are three basic steps to the selection process:

  • Step 1: Determine the baseline number of batches using product and process knowledge.
    • The level of product and process knowledge are used to determine the minimum number of batches (or baseline) that need to be produced during PPQ.
    • Answers to the questions provide guidance on one of three risk-based evaluation starting points for N batches. As product and process knowledge increases, the potential starting point for the selection of the number of batches decreases.
  • Step 2: Determine if any additional controls, activities, or technical rationales are required.
    • This step helps to confirm the selection of the number of batches from Step 1 and determines specific site-level knowledge.
    • Risk control activities determine if additional studies or batches need to be performed/executed.
  • Step 3: Determine if there are any non-GMP-related risks, such as business, technical, or strategic justifications, for the addition or reduction of batches.
    • This is a non-quality risk-based step.
    • After Step 3, the number of batches, N, is provisionally set.

Core Concept: The Three-Step Method for Deciding the Number of PPQ Batches

How many PPQ batches are needed? This is the question asked most often in every validation project. TR60 provides a systematic three-step decision framework:

  1. Step 1 - Determine the baseline number of batches: based on the level of product and process knowledge, determine the minimum number of batches (baseline N). The more knowledge, the fewer batches to start with.

4.4.2.2.1 Justification of Baseline

Original Text

Risk-based approaches and application of QRM to the PV lifecycle are noted in health authority guidance(s), including those of the FDA and EU.

The determination of the number of batches should be based upon risk-based principles as well as product and process knowledge. The more knowledge one possesses (with proof), the relative risk to move into commercial production is reduced, and the number of batches required within a PPQ campaign may be lessened.

While all the guidances state that a risk-based approach must be used, EU GMP Annex 15 provides additional support for the use of N = 3 as a starting point for the evaluation table used in this approach.

  • The number of batches manufactured and the number of samples taken should be based on QRM principles, should allow the normal range of variation and trends to be established, and should provide sufficient data for evaluation within the PPQ report.
  • It is generally considered acceptable that a minimum of three consecutive batches manufactured under routine conditions could constitute a validation of the process.
  • An alternative number of batches may be justified, taking into account whether standard methods of manufacture are used and whether similar products or processes are already used at the site (50).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Regulatory Basis for N=3

The N=3 baseline is the starting point for the number of PPQ batches most commonly used in industry. TR60 cites EU GMP Annex 15 to provide the scientific and regulatory basis:

  • "It is generally considered acceptable that a minimum of three consecutive batches manufactured under routine conditions could constitute a validation of the process"
  • But N=3 is not a fixed answer — it can be adjusted based on the use of standard methods and on experience with similar products/processes at the site.

Key message: N=3 is a starting point, not the answer. You must justify through risk assessment and accumulated knowledge that your choice is reasonable.

Key Notes: Regulatory Expectations

Both FDA and EU regulatory guidance require a risk-based approach. Note, however:

  • The FDA 2011 Process Validation Guidance does not specify a number of batches; it emphasizes "sufficient statistical confidence"
  • EU GMP Annex 15 explicitly names N=3 as a starting point but also allows alternatives
  • Whatever number of batches is chosen, the quality of the justification matters more than the number itself

Inspectors will not ask "why did you do three batches?" but "is your justification complete, scientific and traceable?"

Analogy

N=3 is like a "passing grade of 60" at school — a widely accepted minimum standard, but:

  • For a high-risk subject (a high-criticality product), the teacher may require 80 to pass (N > 3)
  • A student who already holds relevant exemption credit (rich platform knowledge) can apply for a partial exemption from the exam (N < 3)
  • Either way, you need to provide the transcript and learning records (justification documents)

4.4.2.3 PPQ at Normal Operating Conditions

Original Text

PPQ studies are typically conducted in a manner that demonstrates a state of control under normal operating conditions to assess the process variability expected during routine production. Process characterization (robustness) studies conducted during Stage 1 serve as the foundation for establishing NOR, PAR, and design space, if appropriate. Effects-of-scale should also be considered if scaled-down models are suitably qualified, well-planned, and executed. Study data on robustness should support conducting commercial-scale PPQ under routine manufacturing conditions. Supplemental engineering studies at scale may be appropriate to evaluate extremes of the NOR (e.g., line speed or compression speed). In most cases, available Stage 1 data makes it unnecessary to execute PPQ over the entire operating range during the commercial manufacturing process. The PVMP should provide the justification for the approach used and reference all source data.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: PPQ Is Run Under Normal Operating Conditions

PPQ is normally run under Normal Operating Conditions, not under extreme conditions. This is because:

  • NOR (Normal Operating Range): the parameter range actually used in day-to-day production
  • PAR (Proven Acceptable Range): the wider range already demonstrated as acceptable in Stage 1
  • Design Space: the multidimensional combination of parameters defined in ICH Q8

Process characterization/robustness studies in Stage 1 have already explored the extremes of the parameters, so PPQ does not need to repeat that work.

Key Notes: When Supplemental Engineering Studies Are Needed

Although PPQ is run under normal conditions, in some cases supplemental engineering studies may be needed to evaluate the extremes of the NOR:

  • The fastest and slowest filling line speed settings
  • Extreme tablet press speeds
  • Evaluation of scale effects (if the scale-down model has been appropriately confirmed)

These engineering studies can be performed before or in parallel with PPQ, but they need not be part of the formal PPQ.

Analogy

PPQ is like the certification test flight of a new aircraft: the test pilot tests the aircraft's performance under normal flight conditions, not in a typhoon. Tests of extreme conditions (stalls, extreme altitudes and so on) have already been completed in earlier ground simulations and engineering tests (Stage 1). But if some ground simulations cannot fully represent real conditions, some boundary-condition flight tests (supplemental engineering studies) may be needed before the formal test flight.

4.4.2.4 PPQ Using Individual Operation Studies

Original Text

The PPQ of a manufacturing process can be achieved by performing PPQ studies on each individual unit operation (or related groups of operations). This approach calls for the writing of individual protocols that outline the studies to be conducted on each unit operation to achieve PPQ for the entire process. Protocols should define the testing performed and acceptance criteria for the output of the unit operation (intermediate) as prescribed by GMP. They may also require that the final DS or DP meets all specifications and predefined acceptance criteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Unit-Operation-by-Unit-Operation PPQ

Individual Unit Operation PPQ splits the whole manufacturing process into individual unit operations, with a separate PPQ protocol written and executed for each.

Advantages of this approach:

  • Each unit operation has its own tests and acceptance criteria
  • GMP quality standards can be set for the intermediate
  • When a deviation occurs, it is easier to locate the source of the problem
  • It is particularly suitable for long or complex biologic DS processes

Key Notes

When using unit-operation-by-unit-operation PPQ, do not neglect validation of the overall process. Passing PPQ for the individual unit operations does not mean the whole process necessarily meets requirements. The final DS or DP must still comply with all specifications and predefined acceptance criteria.

This approach is common for protein drug substance (protein DS) processes, because they have many process steps, each with its own distinct critical parameters.

Analogy

Unit-operation-by-unit-operation PPQ is like assembling a car: you can test the performance of the engine, gearbox and braking system separately (unit operation PPQ), but you still have to do a complete road test in the end (confirming the final DS/DP specifications) to make sure the overall performance meets the standard once all the parts are assembled.

4.4.2.5 PPQ Using Bracketing, Matrix, and Family Approaches

Original Text

Many operations involve similar or identical process operations or equipment. In these cases, designs where grouping is used may be considered (57). Some process variables that might be amenable to approaches using bracketing, matrix, or family grouping PPQ include:

  • Batch sizes
  • DP dosage strength
  • Identical equipment
  • Different size vessels, tanks, or similar configurations of the same design and operating principle or in-kind equipment
  • Various vial sizes and/or fill volumes of the same DP (e.g., smallest and largest vial size)
  • Filling line speeds (e.g., fastest and slowest line speed)
  • Product packaging (e.g., bottle heights or dosage counts)
  • Transport validation for biological products

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Grouping Validation Strategies

When multiple products or configurations share similar processes and equipment, grouping strategies can be used to reduce the total PPQ workload:

  • Bracketing: validate only the extremes (such as the smallest and largest batch sizes), assuming that intermediate values will also perform acceptably. Suitable for continuous variables (batch size, line speed, fill volume).
  • Matrix: select a representative subset from all combinations of multiple factors for validation. Suitable where there are several independent variables.
  • Family Grouping: group products that use the same process/equipment into a family and select representative members for validation. Suitable for multiple products on the same platform.

Analogy

Grouped validation is like trying on shoes:

  • Bracketing = you try on only the largest and smallest sizes, and once both fit you trust that the sizes in between will be fine
  • Matrix = you have three colors and three sizes, nine pairs in all, but try on only a few representative combinations
  • Family = different styles from the same brand and series; you know they share the same last, so trying on one style predicts how the others will fit

Key Notes

When using a grouping strategy, note the following:

  • There must be a scientific justification for why these products/conditions can be placed in the same group
  • If the result for one condition in the group is not as expected, the scope of validation may need to be expanded
  • Grouping strategies are particularly suitable for CDMOs, because a CDMO usually manufactures products for multiple clients on the same equipment
  • Transport validation can also use grouping strategies, especially for biological products with multiple transport routes

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: your filling line can handle 2R to 50R vials. Rather than running a full PPQ for every vial type, you can:

  1. Use Bracketing: run a full PPQ only on the smallest (2R) and largest (50R) vial types
  2. Use Matrix: combine vial type and fill volume and select representative combinations
  3. Justify that the behavior of intermediate vial types can be inferred from the results at the extremes
  4. Document the scientific basis for the grouping strategy in the PVMP

This can reduce the PPQ workload from a dozen or so batches to a few batches while maintaining scientific rigor.

  2. If you use Family Grouping to validate a product in three dose strengths and the result for the lowest strength is not as expected, how would you handle it?

  • Enhanced Sampling Plan: sampling in the PPQ stage must be broader than in routine production, using three statistical tools — Individual Value Plots (visualization), Tolerance Intervals (capability measurement) and Variance Components (analysis of variation sources) — to provide statistical confidence in within-batch and between-batch quality.
  • Risk-tiered sampling: the number of samples is tiered by CQA criticality (high/medium/low), corresponding to different confidence/coverage requirements (95/99, 95/95, 95/90). Aseptically filled products are sampled at B/M/E time points, with a minimum of n=6 (2 each) and a maximum of n=15 (5 each).
  • Logic for deciding the sample size: based on the combination of prior knowledge (Stage 1 data) and CQA criticality, using the tolerance interval method to work backwards to the required sample size. Where there is no prior knowledge (for example pandemic response), a uniform 95/95 minimum sampling standard can be adopted.
  • Three-step method for the number of PPQ batches: Step 1 determines the baseline number of batches (the more knowledge, the fewer batches to start with); Step 2 assesses whether additional controls or studies are needed; Step 3 considers non-GMP commercial factors. N=3, based on EU GMP Annex 15, is the commonly used starting point, but it must be supported by a complete risk-assessment justification.
  • PPQ report structure: should include summary statistics tables for all attributes, graphical analysis for each attribute (individual value plots), tolerance interval charts, variance components tables, and a control and capability summary statement for each attribute. Where statistical analysis cannot be performed (for example below LOD or insufficient unique values), the reason should be explained and alternative methods used.
  • Flexibility in PPQ execution strategy: PPQ is normally run under normal operating conditions (extremes were explored in Stage 1); PPQ can be run separately for individual unit operations; for multiple products/configurations, Bracketing, Matrix or Family Grouping strategies can be used to improve efficiency.
  • All decisions and justifications must be documented in the PVMP, including the sampling strategy, the rationale for the number of batches, the scientific basis for grouping strategies, and references to all sources of supporting data.

5.0 Continued Process Verification (Stage 3)

Original Text

Continued process verification (CPV) is a regulatory and quality assurance approach used in the pharmaceutical and manufacturing industries. It involves ongoing monitoring, assessment, and validation of processes throughout the lifecycle of a product. CPV aims to ensure that the processes remain in a state of control, consistently producing products that meet predefined quality standards and regulatory requirements.

Unlike traditional batch validation, which focuses on verifying processes at specific intervals, CPV involves continuous monitoring, data collection, and analysis over time. This allows firms to detect deviations, make adjustments, and ensure that any changes in the process do not negatively impact product quality or safety.

The primary goals of CPV are:

  • To ensure consistent product quality:
    • By verifying that the manufacturing processes remain stable and within specified limits over time.
  • To identify potential risks early:
    • Through continuous data analysis, potential issues can be detected before they become major problems.
  • To improve operational efficiency:
    • Continuous monitoring can help optimize processes and reduce waste, leading to cost savings.
  • To comply with regulatory expectations:
    • Regulatory agencies, like the FDA, often require CPV as part of GMPs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Continued Process Verification (CPV): This is the third stage of the process validation lifecycle (Stage 3), an ongoing activity that begins once PPQ (Stage 2) has been completed successfully. Unlike the traditional "three-batch validation", CPV stresses that "a process is not finished once it has been validated": it must be monitored continuously, with data collected and analyzed throughout the product lifecycle, to ensure the process remains in a state of control.

State of Control: The process runs stably within predefined parameter ranges and consistently yields product that meets quality standards. This is not a static conclusion but a dynamic state that must be demonstrated continuously.

Batch Validation vs. CPV: Traditional batch validation is a "snapshot" confirmation of specific batches at a specific point in time; CPV is "video-style" continuous observation that can capture trends and shifts over time.

Analogy

Imagine you buy a new car (PPQ completed). You would not stop servicing it forever just because it passed the factory inspection. CPV is like the car's regular servicing and on-board computer monitoring: engine temperature, fuel consumption, tire pressure and other parameters are monitored continuously. If a reading starts to rise abnormally (a trend shift), you spot the problem and act before the car actually breaks down. Traditional batch validation is more like the annual vehicle inspection: it shows only the condition on one day and cannot capture gradual deterioration.

Key Notes

The logic behind the four CPV objectives:

  • Quality consistency: This is the most basic requirement: ensure that every batch of product meets specification
  • Early risk identification: This is CPV's greatest added value: detect trends before a problem becomes an OOS
  • Improved operational efficiency: Data-driven decisions can reduce waste and lower the deviation rate, directly affecting a CDMO's profitability
  • Regulatory compliance: The FDA PV Guidance (2011) and EU GMP Annex 15 both explicitly require CPV

For a CDMO, a mature CPV program is not only a regulatory requirement but also an important tool for demonstrating technical capability and winning customer trust.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: Your company has just completed PPQ for a biologic product (3 PPQ batches successful). The customer asks to see your CPV plan. You will need to:

  • State clearly which CQAs and CPPs the CPV monitors
  • Define the data collection frequency and the statistical analysis methods
  • Establish alert limits and action limits for trend shifts
  • Explain who is responsible for analyzing the data and how often it is reviewed

A well-structured CPV plan can be a highlight of a commercial proposal, demonstrating the maturity of your quality management.

  2. If a CPV plan monitors only the release test results of the final product, do you think that is sufficient? Why?
  3. How is the CPV objective of "early risk identification" achieved in practice? Give an example of process parameter drift.

5.1 Establishing a Monitoring Program

Original Text

Establishing a monitoring program for CPV is a critical step in ensuring that manufacturing processes remain in control and consistently produce products that meet quality standards. A CPV monitoring program involves systematically collecting, analyzing, and interpreting data throughout the lifecycle of a product to verify that the process is consistently operating within its defined parameters (4, 59, 60).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Monitoring Program: The backbone of CPV. It is not simply "collecting data" but a systematic program covering three core activities:

  • Collecting: Determine which parameters to monitor, the sampling frequency and the data sources
  • Analyzing: Use statistical methods (such as SPC) to process the data and identify trends
  • Interpreting: Turn the statistical results into actionable decisions (for example, whether an investigation or a process change is needed)

These three activities form a continuous PDCA cycle that runs from product launch until product discontinuation.

Analogy

Think of the CPV monitoring program as a smart health watch. It does not just take your temperature when you are ill (passive reaction); it tracks your heart rate, blood oxygen and sleep quality around the clock (proactive monitoring). When it detects that your resting heart rate has been abnormally high for three days in a row, it alerts you that you "may need rest or a doctor": this is early warning before the problem becomes serious. What CPV does for the process is what the health watch does for your body.

Key Notes

Note that the source text cites three references (4, 59, 60), which typically correspond to the FDA PV Guidance, ISPE guidance and PDA's own technical reports. The design of the monitoring program must be based on:

  • Process knowledge established in Stage 1 (Process Design)
  • Critical parameters and attributes confirmed in Stage 2 (PPQ)
  • The results of risk assessment (which parameters most need continued monitoring)

This means CPV is not designed from scratch: it is a natural extension of the work done in Stage 1 and Stage 2.

5.1.1 Purpose and Strategy

Original Text

The FDA PV guidance defines CPV as the assurance that the process remains in a state of control during routine production (1). EU GMP Annex 15 defines "Ongoing Process Verification," also known as CPV, as documented evidence that the process remains controlled during commercial manufacture (50).

Maintenance of a validated state requires having a robust quality system in place encompassing aspects such as change control, training, auditing, and periodic review (4, 60). A CPV program (Stage 3a and 3b) provides a means to ensure that processes remain in a state of control following successful PPQ (Stage 2). The information and data collected during Stages 1 and 2 set the stage for an effective control strategy in routine manufacturing and a meaningful CPV program. Understanding functional relationships between process inputs and corresponding outputs established in earlier stages are fundamental to the success of the CPV program. The program minimizes process failures through early detection of adverse trends and a quick reaction by applying data analysis and scientific methods for better decision-making. Figure 5.1.1-1🔍 is a conceptual chart depicting a high level of sampling at Stage 3a, the strategies applied when a lifecycle change is proposed, and the achieving of process stability.

"Continued monitoring of process variables enables adjustments to inputs covered in the scope of the CPV plan and detects the potential for process variability, ensuring that outputs remain consistent."

Since all sources of potential variability may not be anticipated and defined in Stages 1 and 2, unanticipated events or trends identified from CPV may indicate process control issues and/or highlight opportunities for process improvement. Science- and risk-based tools help achieve high levels of process understanding during the development phase, and subsequent knowledge management (KM) across the product life stages facilitates implementing CPV (refer to Section 3.0 and Section 4.0).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The key distinction between Stage 3a and Stage 3b:

  • Stage 3a (enhanced monitoring period): The initial phase immediately after PPQ is completed. Sampling frequency is higher (usually close to the PPQ level), with the aim of building a sufficient data baseline over the early commercial batches. It is the bridge from "validation mode" to "routine monitoring".
  • Stage 3b (routine monitoring period): Once Stage 3a has accumulated enough data to demonstrate that the process is stable, sampling can be reduced to the routine level. This is the long-term, ongoing monitoring activity.

Knowledge Management (KM): TR60 places particular emphasis on the transfer of knowledge from Stage 1 to Stage 3. The quality of a CPV plan depends directly on the process knowledge accumulated in the first two stages: if you do not know which inputs affect which outputs, you cannot design a meaningful monitoring program.

Analogy

The transition between Stage 3a and 3b is like a new driver who has just received a license. Stage 3a is the "learner period": you drive with extra care, perhaps install a dashcam and have an instructor ride along a few times (enhanced monitoring). After a few months of safe driving, you enter Stage 3b, the "regular driving period": you no longer need the instructor, but you still service the car regularly and watch the dashboard (routine monitoring). If an accident or a modification to the car occurs along the way (a process change), you may need to go back temporarily to enhanced monitoring.

Criteria for moving from Stage 3a to Stage 3b:

In practice, how do you decide when it is acceptable to step down from enhanced to routine monitoring? Typically the following are needed:

  • At least 20-30 batches of commercial production data accumulated
  • SPC charts for all CQAs and CPPs show the process to be in a state of statistical control
  • Process capability indices (Cpk/Ppk) meet the predefined target (usually ≥ 1.33)
  • No significant deviations or trend warnings
  • Review and approval by the cross-functional team

5.1.3 Documentation and Knowledge Management

Original Text

The CPV program requires comprehensive documentation to ensure traceability, consistency, and regulatory compliance. This documentation supports the knowledge management (KM) framework that enables effective transfer of process understanding across the product lifecycle.

Key documentation elements include:

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The relationship between APR / PQR and CPV:

Annual Product Review (APR) is the annual evaluation required by the FDA (21 CFR 211.180(e)), and Product Quality Review (PQR) is the corresponding EU GMP requirement. The relationship between the CPV report and the APR/PQR is as follows:

  • CPV provides timely data monitoring (possibly monthly or quarterly)
  • The APR/PQR is a consolidated annual evaluation
  • CPV data is the primary source for the process trend analysis in the APR/PQR

The "golden thread" of knowledge management:

5.1.4 Legacy Products

Original Text

Legacy products are those that were validated using older approaches (e.g., traditional three-batch validation) prior to the introduction of the lifecycle-based process validation framework. These products present unique challenges for CPV implementation because the depth of process understanding from Stage 1 characterization may not exist.

For legacy products, the CPV program should:

  • Leverage available historical batch data to establish baseline process performance
  • Use risk assessment to identify which process parameters and quality attributes warrant ongoing monitoring
  • Consider the product's compliance history, including deviations, OOS results, and CAPA history
  • Build process knowledge retrospectively using accumulated manufacturing data
  • Prioritize products based on risk, complexity, and regulatory visibility

The goal is not to repeat Stage 1 and Stage 2 activities retroactively, but rather to develop a monitoring strategy that reflects the current state of process understanding and progressively builds process knowledge over time.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Legacy Products: These are products validated with the traditional "three-batch validation" approach before (or shortly after) the 2011 FDA PV Guidance was issued. The core challenges they face are:

  • Lack of Stage 1 data: Systematic process characterization studies (DoE, etc.) may not have been done at the time
  • Unclear CPP/CQA relationships: Functional relationships may not have been established scientifically
  • A large amount of historical data: The good news is that these products have been manufactured for years and have accumulated rich batch data

TR60's pragmatic approach is that there is no need to "go back and redo" Stage 1 and Stage 2; instead, historical data is used to retrospectively build process knowledge.

Analogy

CPV for a legacy product is like moving into an old house with no building blueprints. You cannot go back to the time of construction to redo the structural analysis (Stage 1), but you can:

  • Review past maintenance records (historical batch data)
  • Have a structural engineer assess the house on site (risk assessment)
  • Install sensors to monitor structural health (CPV monitoring program)
  • Address the highest-risk areas first (risk-based prioritization)

You do not need to demolish and rebuild (redo Stage 1-2), but you do need to establish a pragmatic monitoring system.

Key Notes

Prioritization strategy for legacy CPV:

A CDMO often has dozens or even hundreds of products that need a CPV. The pragmatic approach is to prioritize by risk:

  • High priority: High-risk products (sterile injectables, biologics), products with a history of compliance problems, products that are a regulatory focus
  • Medium priority: Products of moderate complexity, and products with a stable compliance history but no CPV
  • Low priority: Low-risk products (oral solid dosage forms, products with good historical performance)

A phased rollout plan is more practical and effective than trying to establish CPV for all products at once.

Practical Application

Practical steps for retrospective data analysis:

  1. Data collection: Extract the past 3-5 years of batch records from the ERP/MES systems
  2. Data cleaning: Identify and handle missing values, outliers and inconsistent data formats
  3. Preliminary analysis: Build descriptive statistics for each parameter (mean, standard deviation, distribution)
  4. SPC analysis: Build control charts and retrospectively assess whether the process is in a state of statistical control
  5. Process capability assessment: Calculate Cpk/Ppk to confirm that process capability meets requirements
  6. Baseline establishment: Use the analysis results to define the CPV monitoring limits

5.2 Data Science and Advanced Tools for CPV

Original Text

The evolving landscape of pharmaceutical manufacturing increasingly leverages advanced analytical tools and data science methodologies to enhance CPV programs. The integration of modern technologies with traditional process monitoring represents a significant advancement in ensuring product quality and process control.

Key technological enablers for modern CPV include:

  • Statistical Process Control (SPC) for systematic monitoring and trend detection
  • Multivariate Data Analysis (MVDA) for understanding complex process relationships
  • Digital twins for process simulation and predictive analysis
  • Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) for pattern recognition and predictive modeling
  • Process Analytical Technology (PAT) for real-time process monitoring

These tools complement rather than replace traditional CPV approaches, providing deeper insights into process behavior and enabling more proactive quality management.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The technical evolution spectrum of CPV:

From traditional to advanced, CPV analytical tools can be arranged along a maturity spectrum:

  • Level 1 - Descriptive analytics: Control charts, process capability indices (What did we see?)
  • Level 2 - Diagnostic analytics: Multivariate analysis, root cause analysis (Why did it happen?)
  • Level 3 - Predictive analytics: Trend forecasting, machine learning models (What will happen next?)
  • Level 4 - Prescriptive analytics: AI-driven process optimization (What should we do?)

Most companies are currently at Level 1-2, and TR60 (2026) encourages the industry to move toward Level 3-4.

Key Notes

The importance of "complement, not replace":

TR60 states clearly that advanced tools supplement traditional CPV methods and do not replace them. This means:

  • You still need basic SPC control charts and process capability analysis
  • The outputs of AI/ML models need human review and verification
  • The predictions of a Digital Twin need to be checked against actual production data
  • Regulators are currently still cautious about purely AI-driven quality decisions

This is a tiered approach: lay the foundation first (SPC), then introduce advanced tools step by step.

Analogy

These four analytical levels are like the evolution of weather forecasting:

  • Level 1: Read the thermometer and rain gauge: "20 mm of rain fell yesterday" (descriptive)
  • Level 2: Analyze changes in air pressure and humidity: "It rained because a low-pressure system moved in" (diagnostic)
  • Level 3: Weather model forecasts: "There is an 80% chance of rain tomorrow" (predictive)
  • Level 4: AI-driven extreme weather warning system: "Activate flood prevention measures 48 hours in advance" (prescriptive)

5.2.1 Statistical Process Control (SPC)

Original Text

Statistical Process Control (SPC) is the foundational analytical method for CPV monitoring programs. SPC uses control charts and statistical techniques to monitor process behavior over time, distinguishing between common cause variation (inherent to the process) and special cause variation (assignable to specific events).

Key SPC elements in CPV include:

  • Control charts (e.g., X-bar/R, X-bar/S, Individual/Moving Range) for monitoring process central tendency and variability
  • Process capability indices (Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk) for quantifying process performance relative to specification limits
  • Western Electric rules and Nelson rules for detecting non-random patterns in control chart data
  • Trend analysis using CUSUM (Cumulative Sum) and EWMA (Exponentially Weighted Moving Average) charts for detecting small, sustained shifts

The selection of appropriate SPC tools should be based on the type of data (variable vs. attribute), the process characteristic being monitored, and the sensitivity required for detecting changes. Establishing meaningful control limits is critical and should be derived from process data rather than specification limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The core principle of SPC: two types of variation:

Common Cause Variation: Variation that is inherent to the process, random and expected. Examples include small differences between raw material batches and natural fluctuations in ambient temperature and humidity. This type of variation is "normal noise".

Special Cause Variation: Non-random variation caused by specific, identifiable causes. Examples include equipment failure, operator error and abnormal raw material quality. This type of variation is a "signal that needs investigation".

The core value of SPC is that it helps you distinguish between the two types of variation and avoid either overreacting (taking unnecessary action on common cause variation) or underreacting (ignoring a genuine special cause).

Formula & Key Indices

The meaning of the process capability indices:

Cp  = (USL - LSL) / (6 x sigma)
    → Measures the "potential capability" of the process (ignoring shift)

Cpk = min[(USL - mean) / (3 x sigma),
          (mean - LSL) / (3 x sigma)]
    → Measures the "actual capability" of the process (accounting for shift)

Pp / Ppk → Use the overall standard deviation; reflect long-term performance
Cp / Cpk → Use the within-subgroup standard deviation; reflect short-term capability

Practical acceptance criteria:
  Cpk ≥ 1.33  → Process capability is good (general requirement)
  Cpk ≥ 1.67  → Process capability is excellent
  Cpk < 1.00  → Process capability is inadequate and needs improvement

Key Notes

Control limits vs. specification limits: the most common mistake:

TR60 states clearly that Control Limits should be derived from process data, not from Specification Limits. This is one of the most common errors in CPV:

  • Specification Limits: Determined by product requirements: "the product must fall within this range to be acceptable"
  • Control Limits: Calculated from process data: "when the process runs normally, it should fall within this range"

If you use specification limits as control limits, the control chart loses its ability to detect trend shifts, because the range is too wide. For a process with Cpk = 2.0, the control limits will be much narrower than the specification limits, which is what lets you spot a problem before the product goes out of specification.

Analogy

The value of CUSUM and EWMA charts:

A traditional X-bar control chart is like a sluggish thermometer: it triggers an alarm only when the temperature changes substantially. A CUSUM chart is like a highly sensitive temperature recorder: it accumulates every small shift and triggers an alarm when the accumulated amount exceeds a threshold. An EWMA chart is like a thermometer with memory: it gives more weight to the most recent data and can track a trend smoothly.

Imagine you take your temperature every day: 36.5, 36.6, 36.7, 36.6, 36.8, 36.7, 36.9... The change each day is small, and an X-bar chart may not trigger an alarm. But CUSUM will notice that "body temperature is continuing to rise" and warn you before you actually develop a fever.

5.2.2 Digital Twins

Original Text

A digital twin is a virtual representation of a physical manufacturing process that mirrors real-time operations using data integration, simulation models, and machine learning algorithms. In the context of CPV, digital twins provide a powerful tool for:

  • Process prediction: Simulating the impact of process parameter changes before implementation
  • Root cause analysis: Isolating the most likely causes of observed deviations by running virtual experiments
  • Optimization: Identifying optimal operating conditions within the validated design space
  • What-if analysis: Evaluating the potential impact of proposed changes without disrupting production

The digital twin model is continuously updated with real-time process data, creating a living model that evolves with the process. This enables predictive capabilities that go beyond traditional statistical analysis, supporting more proactive and informed decision-making in CPV.

Implementation of digital twins requires:

  • Robust data infrastructure and integration with manufacturing execution systems (MES)
  • Validated mechanistic or hybrid models of the process
  • Clear governance for model maintenance, updating, and version control
  • Qualified personnel with expertise in process modeling and data science

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Digital Twin: Simply put, a "virtual replica" of a manufacturing process built on a computer. This virtual model continuously receives real-time data from actual production, which allows it to:

  • Mirror the current state of the actual process
  • Simulate and predict what would happen if a parameter were changed
  • Look back to analyze what might have caused past deviations

Living Model: This is the key characteristic of a Digital Twin: it is not a model that stays fixed once built, but a dynamic model that is continuously updated and recalibrated with new data.

Design Space: A concept defined in ICH Q8, meaning the multidimensional combination of parameters that has been shown to produce product of acceptable quality. A Digital Twin can help find the optimal operating point within this space.

Analogy

A Digital Twin is like a flight simulator. Airlines do not let pilots practice every kind of emergency on a real aircraft (too costly, too risky). They use a flight simulator: a virtual system that accurately replicates the behavior of the real aircraft. In the simulator, pilots can:

  • Practice responding to an engine failure (= simulating the impact of process deviations)
  • Test different flight routes (= what-if analysis)
  • Learn how the aircraft behaves in extreme weather (= exploring the boundaries of the design space)

And the simulator is constantly updated with the aircraft's latest maintenance records and performance data (= Living Model). A pharmaceutical Digital Twin does exactly the same thing, except that the subject changes from an aircraft to your manufacturing process.

Key Notes

Barriers to implementing a Digital Twin:

Although the concept is attractive, implementing a Digital Twin faces considerable challenges:

  • Data infrastructure: Requires real-time data interfaces to MES/SCADA systems, which many older facilities may lack
  • Model validation: The Digital Twin model itself needs to be validated: how do you prove that your virtual model truly reflects the real process?
  • Model maintenance: Processes change over time (equipment aging, raw material supplier changes, etc.), so the model needs regular updating and recalibration
  • Talent requirements: Needs cross-disciplinary people who understand both pharmaceutical processes and data science

Recommended strategy: start with a pilot on one critical unit operation, and expand step by step once the value has been demonstrated.

Practical Application

Application scenario of a Digital Twin in biologics CPV:

Suppose your CDMO site produces monoclonal antibodies, where cell culture is the most critical unit operation. You can build a Digital Twin of the cell culture:

  • Receive real-time bioreactor data such as temperature, pH, DO, agitation speed and feed rate
  • The model predicts cell density and viability trends over the next 24-48 hours
  • If the predicted trend deviates from expectations, an alert is issued in advance
  • When the feeding strategy needs adjusting, simulate the effect of different options in the Digital Twin first

This not only improves the predictive capability of CPV but also provides a safe "virtual laboratory" for process optimization.

5.2.3 AI and Machine Learning Applications

Original Text

The application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) in CPV represents an emerging frontier in pharmaceutical manufacturing. These technologies offer capabilities that extend beyond traditional statistical methods:

  • Pattern recognition: ML algorithms can identify complex, multivariate patterns in process data that may not be apparent through conventional analysis
  • Predictive modeling: AI models can forecast process performance and product quality based on input parameters and historical data
  • Anomaly detection: Unsupervised learning methods can identify unusual process behavior without predefined rules
  • Process optimization: Reinforcement learning and optimization algorithms can suggest optimal process settings within the validated design space

However, the use of AI/ML in CPV requires careful consideration of:

  • Model interpretability: The ability to explain model predictions and decisions is critical for regulatory acceptance and quality decision-making
  • Data quality: AI/ML models are only as reliable as the data used to train them
  • Validation and qualification: AI/ML models used in GxP-regulated contexts require appropriate validation, including performance monitoring and revalidation triggers
  • Change management: Model updates and retraining must be managed through formal change control processes
  • Regulatory landscape: FDA and other agencies are developing frameworks for AI/ML in pharmaceutical manufacturing, and alignment with evolving guidance is essential

Organizations should adopt a phased approach, starting with AI/ML as supplementary analytical tools to augment human decision-making, rather than as autonomous decision systems.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four main application areas of AI/ML in CPV:

  • Pattern Recognition: Using ML to discover complex relationships in high-dimensional data that humans can hardly perceive. For example, analyzing 50 process parameters simultaneously to find hidden combinations that affect final product quality.
  • Predictive Modeling: Training models on historical data to predict the quality performance of future batches. This lets CPV evolve from "retrospective analysis" to "forward-looking prediction".
  • Anomaly Detection: Unsupervised learning methods do not need "what is abnormal" to be defined in advance; they learn the "normal" pattern automatically and then flag any data that deviates from it. This is more flexible than traditional fixed rules.
  • Process Optimization: Within the validated design space, AI can find the optimal operating point that balances quality, efficiency and cost.

Key Notes

Model Interpretability: the biggest challenge for AI in a GxP environment:

In the pharmaceutical industry, "why" matters more than "what". If an AI model says "this batch may go out of specification", the quality head's first question will certainly be "why?" If the model is a "black box" (such as a deep neural network) and cannot explain the reasons behind its prediction, this is unacceptable in a GxP environment.

For this reason, TR60 stresses that "interpretability" is a prerequisite for regulatory acceptance of AI/ML in CPV. Interpretable ML models should be preferred:

  • Recommended: Decision trees, random forests, LASSO regression, PLS (partial least squares)
  • Use with caution: Deep learning and complex neural networks (unless paired with interpretability tools such as SHAP or LIME)

Analogy

The "phased adoption" strategy for AI:

Imagine you are teaching a new employee to operate a production line independently:

  • Phase 1 - Observational learning: The AI only "watches" the data and assists human analysis without making any decisions (= supplementary analytical tool)
  • Phase 2 - Advisory mode: The AI provides recommendations, but all decisions are still made by humans, who record their rationale (= decision support)
  • Phase 3 - Supervised autonomy: The AI can execute certain actions automatically within predefined limits, while humans continue to supervise (= limited autonomy)
  • Phase 4 - Full autonomy: The AI operates fully autonomously in validated scenarios (= a future goal; the current regulatory framework is not yet mature)

TR60 recommends that the industry should currently stay at Phase 1-2 and advance gradually toward Phase 3.

Key Notes - Regulatory Landscape

The FDA's stance on AI/ML in pharmaceutical manufacturing:

  • 2023: The FDA issued the "AI/ML in Drug Manufacturing" discussion paper, establishing the basic principles of transparency, interpretability and human oversight
  • 2024-2025: FDA's CDER and CBER began asking about the use of AI/ML during Pre-Approval Inspections (PAI)
  • 2026 outlook: The FDA is expected to issue more specific AI/ML guidance covering requirements for model validation, change management and continued monitoring

The EU EMA is also developing a similar framework. For a CDMO, starting now to build an AI/ML governance framework is a wise investment.

5.3 Reacting to Continued Process Verification Data Signals

Original Text

The practical implementation of CPV is expected to reduce the cost of quality, provide opportunities to improve product quality, increase product and process knowledge, and reduce regulatory compliance risks. A system or systems for continually and efficiently detecting unplanned departures from the process as designed is essential to accomplish the CPV goal. Collection and evaluation of process performance data should allow detection of process drift if adequately monitored through application appropriate methodologies. These efforts can identify variability in the process, signal potential process improvements, and provide opportunities to mitigate and control those variabilities if properly carried out.

"The FDA PV guidance specifically states that trending should be performed in such a way that it guards against overreaction to individual events."

An upfront approach for addressing the relevant variables and signals is therefore important in avoiding under-detection or overreaction. The CPV signals are "yellow flags" that represent the process variability and drifts and the severity of the signal is then determined.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

CPV Data Signals: A CPV signal is not an OOS (out of specification) or OOT (out of trend) deviation event. It is a "yellow flag" on a statistical process control chart, indicating that the process is varying or drifting. The significance of a signal must be evaluated further; it does not call for immediate corrective action.

Process Drift: The slow movement of a process away from its original design center over time. If it is not detected in time, drift can eventually lead to OOS results or quality failures.

Analogy

Imagine driving on a highway. A CPV signal is like a lane departure warning system: when the car drifts slightly toward the edge of the lane, the system beeps to alert you (a yellow flag). This does not mean you have already had an accident (OOS); it is a reminder to watch the steering wheel. If you yank the wheel every time you hear a beep (overreaction), you create a hazard; but if you ignore the warning entirely (under-detection), you may eventually leave the lane. The essence of CPV is "proportionate response."

Key Notes

FDA expectation: Trending methods must prevent overreaction to individual events. This means companies need to establish a systematic decision-making process rather than letting individual judgment determine the course of action.

Four benefits of CPV:

  • Reduced cost of quality
  • Opportunities for product quality improvement
  • Increased product and process knowledge
  • Reduced regulatory compliance risk

5.3.1 Continued Process Verification Decision-Making

Original Text

Routine CPV trending within established alert limits, signal detection and action enables organizations to maintain an enhanced product control strategy. A CPV signal can be for a quality attribute, material attribute, process parameter defined in the control strategy, and/or performance indicator with varying degrees of severity. A risk-based categorization of signals received (e.g., CPP, CQA) is also required, as all signals are not equally significant, which leads to requiring different action plans to address the signals on a case-by-case basis. When a significant amount of data is available, the suggested action in Figure 5.3.1-1🔍 may be applied. Paths are determined based on practical relevance and the statistical strength of the signal. For instance, taking Path 1 is proposed when the observed drift results from an assignable event extrinsic to the process and characterized by an isolated event which may or may not have been identified as a quality management system (QMS) deviation or incident.

Figure 5.3.1-1🔍 Decision-Tree Guideline (Stage 3b)

The CPV signal, once determined not to be an OOS/OOT/deviation, is further assessed for statistical significance. A change review step provides insights into any potential impact of the sources of variability, that is, personnel, raw material, process, test method, equipment, utilities, facility, and environmental conditions. Understanding the causes of variability allows for control at the source.

The preliminary assessment allows for determining the signal as either assignable, common, or special-cause variation. The proposed path of either continuing monitoring, assessment of control strategy, or a change requirement is then documented. Defined systemic decision-making steps guard against overreaction to individual events as well as against failure to act on the observed process variability.

The systematic steps justify the robustness of routine CPV operational decision-making process, reduces person-to-person variability, and minimizes operational delays, holds, or product loss. A CPV program with both Stage 3a and Stage 3b would need different approaches for each stage because the data set available during Stage 3a is less compared to Stage 3b. When limited data is available, a science- and statistics-driven deep dive into the available data is warranted to understand what actions can be taken until additional data are generated.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Signal Classification

Assignable Cause Variation: Variation arising from a specific, identifiable cause, such as an abnormal raw material lot, an equipment calibration deviation or a newly assigned operator. The root cause of this type of variation can be traced and eliminated.

Common Cause Variation: Random fluctuation inherent to the process, which is normal within statistical control limits. Examples include natural fluctuations in environmental temperature and humidity, or small differences between raw material lots.

Special-Cause Variation: Non-random variation outside the normal statistical range, which usually points to a systemic problem. It requires immediate investigation and may require modification of the control strategy.

Decision Tree Logic

The core logic of the decision tree comprises three paths:

  • Path 1 (continue monitoring): The signal comes from an assignable, external, isolated event (such as a one-time equipment anomaly). No change to the control strategy is needed, and routine CPV monitoring continues
  • Path 2 (evaluate the control strategy): The signal is statistically significant, and a deeper analysis is needed to determine whether the control strategy is still appropriate
  • Path 3 (request a change): The signal is strong and persistent, indicating that the current control strategy is no longer adequate and a change must be made

Key steps: CPV signal → rule out OOS/OOT → statistical significance assessment → change review → determine the type of variation → select the path

Analogy

The CPV decision tree works like a hospital triage system. An emergency room does not treat every patient in the same way:

  • Green (minor): A mild cold = Path 1; observe and follow up
  • Yellow (needs attention): A persistent fever = Path 2; further examination and diagnosis are needed
  • Red (urgent): Cardiac arrest = Path 3; intervene immediately

Likewise, not all CPV signals are equally important; signals related to CQAs generally carry a higher risk level than signals related to KPIs.

Stage 3a vs. Stage 3b Difference

The document specifically points out that Stage 3a and Stage 3b require different CPV approaches:

  • Stage 3a: Data are limited (the first few batches after launch) and statistical confidence is low, so a more conservative and cautious scientific analysis is needed
  • Stage 3b: Sufficient data are available to establish a robust statistical baseline and control charts, so the full decision tree process applies

It is like weather forecasting: predicting from only 3 days of data (Stage 3a) and modeling from 30 years of historical data (Stage 3b) require completely different methodologies.

5.4 Applying Continued Process Verification for Lifecycle Change Management

Original Text

Applying CPV to lifecycle change management refers to the integration of CPV principles and practices to monitor and ensure consistent product quality and process stability throughout the entire lifecycle of a product, especially during and after any changes in the process or product.

In regulated industries like pharmaceuticals, medical devices, or manufacturing, lifecycle changes—such as adjustments in raw materials, equipment upgrades, process optimization, or even regulatory changes—are inevitable. Applying CPV in these contexts helps ensure that these changes do not negatively impact the product's quality, safety, or compliance with regulatory requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept

Lifecycle Change Management: Throughout a product’s commercial lifecycle, changes in raw materials, equipment, processes and regulations are unavoidable. The role of CPV is to ensure that these changes do not adversely affect product quality, safety or regulatory compliance.

CPV is not a static, "verified and done" activity. It is a dynamic, ongoing monitoring framework that is closely integrated with the company’s change control system.

Analogy

Think of CPV as a car’s engine control unit (ECU) monitoring system. When you fit a new brand of tires (raw material change), upgrade engine parts (equipment upgrade) or adjust the driving mode (process optimization), the ECU keeps monitoring performance indicators (fuel consumption, emissions, engine temperature) to ensure that these changes do not affect the safety and performance of the whole vehicle. CPV is the pharmaceutical industry’s "ECU."

The tools are based on product and process understanding and are enabled by procedures that monitor, measure, analyze, and control the process performance. Communication of CPV signals to the manufacturing, quality, and regulatory stakeholders (for improvement and/or compliance reasons) is an iterative and essential part of the lifecycle.

Feedback mechanisms, where required, can vary between immediate (intra-batch or real-time), after each batch, after a series of batches, or after a defined period. Tutorial Commentary

Feedback Mechanisms Hierarchy

TR60 describes four levels of feedback mechanisms, ranging from real-time to long-term:

  • Intra-batch / Real-time: For example, real-time PAT (process analytical technology) monitoring allows parameters to be adjusted during manufacturing
  • After each batch: CPV data are reviewed after each batch is completed to decide whether adjustments are needed for the next batch

  • Manufacturing: Responsible for carrying out the actual process adjustments

Practical Application: CDMO Scenario

  • Real-time: Notify the manufacturing team to intensify equipment checks on that line
  • After each batch: Compare the filling pump calibration data after every batch
  • After a series of batches: Perform an equipment wear analysis and assess whether a preventive maintenance schedule is needed
  • Periodic: Include the results of this trend analysis in the next APQR

5.4.2 Regulatory Commitments and Established Conditions

Original Text

Although the common technical document (CTD) format has been defined for regulatory submissions, harmonized approaches to defining what changes would require a submission have yet to be determined. Established conditions (ECs) are legally binding information (or approved matters) considered necessary to assure product quality. They are contained in a regulatory submission, proposed by the applicant, and approved by the regulatory authority. Any change to ECs necessitates a submission to the regulatory authority that is consistent with regional regulations or guidances or has been agreed upon during the review and approval of the marketing application.

ICH Q12 provides a framework to facilitate the management of post-approval CMC changes more predictably and efficiently across the product lifecycle. It allows regulators to better understand the firm's PQS for the management of post-approval CMC changes. Incremental changes over time should also be considered, and the need for any additional actions (e.g., enhanced sampling) should be assessed.

The lack of harmonized requirements for lifecycle management is a disincentive for manufacturers to make improvements to increase process robustness. One post-approval change can take three to five years to implement across all regions, resulting in additional costs and potential supply disruption due to the need for multiple inventories since opportunities for "operational flexibility" offered by the science- and risk-based approaches in ICH guidelines Q8–Q11 have not been fully realized.

Additional information and approaches to manage process changes can be found in PDA Technical Report No. 91: Post Approval Change Management and Implementation for Biologics and Pharmaceutical Drug Products — A User's Guide.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Established Conditions (ECs): Legally binding information in regulatory approval documents that is considered necessary to assure product quality. Any change to an EC must be submitted to the regulatory authority. The EC concept is central to ICH Q12, whose purpose is to define clearly which information is regulatory-binding.

ICH Q12: Its full title is "Technical and Regulatory Considerations for Pharmaceutical Product Lifecycle Management." It provides a framework for managing post-approval CMC changes, making the change process more predictable and more efficient.

Regulatory Pain Point

TR60 points directly to an industry pain point: a single post-approval change can take 3-5 years to implement across all regions worldwide. This is because:

  • Regulations in different countries are inconsistent in how changes are classified and filed
  • Multiple versions of inventory (pre-change and post-change product) must be maintained at the same time
  • The "operational flexibility" offered by ICH Q8-Q11 has not yet been fully used

This problem is especially serious for CDMOs, because a client’s product may be marketed in dozens of countries, each with a different regulatory timeline.

Analogy

Established Conditions are like the information on a property deed. If you want to change the structure of a house (for example, removing a load-bearing wall), you must apply to the building authority for a permit. But if you are only repainting the walls or changing the furniture, you usually do not need to file anything. The purpose of ICH Q12 is to help industry and regulators clearly separate the changes that are "removing a load-bearing wall" (filing required) from those that are "changing the furniture" (no filing required).

Stage 3a Overview

Stage 3a (initial continued process verification) is the first phase of enhanced monitoring after a new product is launched. Its core characteristics are:

  • Protocol-driven: There is a defined sampling plan, statistical analysis method and completion criteria

5.5.2 Stage 3b Reporting

Original Text

SPC tools and predefined rules are primarily used in CPV to alert to potential drifts. Triggering a signal indicates with reasonable statistical confidence that something may have changed within the process that may have an impact on the product's quality, safety, process robustness, and control. While these signals should trigger a response reaction, the reaction strategy should depend on the risk level.

The continual Stage 3b sampling and testing requirements may be included in the master batch record and monitored batch-to-batch electronically through other controls. Systems should be in place to ensure that Stage 3b signals, if any, are addressed prior to the start of processing the next batch of the product or similar products.

Qualified monitoring systems with real- or near-real-time data trending integrate product information, parameters, and quality attributes, thus allowing for swift decision making on an observed signal. Section 5.3.1 discusses a decision-making process that can be followed. The assessment outcome is documented electronically or according to standard operating procedure (SOP) requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Stage 3b Reporting Framework

Stage 3b reporting differs fundamentally from Stage 3a:

  • Stage 3a: A one-time summary report written after the enhanced sampling is complete
  • Stage 3b: An ongoing monitoring report built into routine batch production operations

The sampling and testing requirements for Stage 3b can be incorporated directly into the Master Batch Record, which means that CPV monitoring is not a separate, additional activity but part of the routine production process.

Critical Timing Requirement

TR60 sets out an important timeliness requirement: Stage 3b signals must be addressed before the next batch of product is started. This means:

  • CPV data review cannot wait until the end of the month or quarter
  • A real-time or near-real-time data trending system is needed
  • The decision-making process must be streamlined enough not to cause production delays

For high-frequency products (for example, several batches per day), this is a major challenge for both data systems and staff efficiency.

Analogy

Stage 3b monitoring is like an airline’s flight safety inspection system. After every flight, the crew reports any abnormality (post-batch data review). If any signal is found (such as abnormal engine vibration), it must be resolved before the next takeoff; you cannot let an aircraft with a potential problem keep carrying passengers. Likewise, CPV signals must be addressed before the next batch of product is started.

6.1 Application of Risk Management

Original Text

A detailed explanation of a QRM program used to support the PV effort can be found in TR 54 in the "How to Use Quality Risk Management as an Enabler" section. Comprehensive lists of risk management tools can also be found in TR 54, including a comparison of their respective benefits, as well as in ICH Q9(R1) (37, 78).

QRM is an enabler or an enabling process. When correctly applied, it adds supportive elements to the product lifecycle and other systems (e.g., the PQS). The application of risk management principles and approaches is instrumental in effective decision-making in the PV lifecycle.

Management of variability is one example of applying risk management in the validation lifecycle. The level of control required to manage variability is directly related to the level of risk that variability imparts to the process and product. The use of risk management to address variability requires an understanding of the origin of the variability, the potential range of the variability, and the impact of the variability on the process, product and, ultimately, the patient.

Risk assessments should occur early in the lifecycle. Risks should be controlled appropriately to an acceptable level and effectively communicated. Risk management increases product and process knowledge, which translates into greater control of product and process variability and a lower residual risk to patients.

The PV lifecycle provides continued assurance that processes will manufacture product in a predictable and consistent manner. Where decisions related to product quality or process performance are made, risk should be assessed at several points throughout the PV lifecycle.

QRM should be applied throughout the PV lifecycle including in the following areas:

Stage 1 – Process Design

  • Identification of product attributes that may affect quality and patient safety.
  • Criticality analysis of product CQAs.
  • Cause-and-Effect Analysis or Risk-Ranking and Filtering, which link the process steps and parameters to product quality attributes and process performance for the categorization of process parameters (e.g., CPP identification). These can be used to screen potential variables for future process characterization (e.g., DoE) and testing.
  • Preliminary Hazards Analysis or early Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA).

Stages 1-2 – Transition from Process Design to Process Qualification

  • Determining process control strategies that address the risk of failure for each process step.
  • Evaluation of residual risk remaining or created as a result of risk mitigation, process improvement, and process knowledge.
  • Risk-based gap assessments and risk assessments for TT.

Stage 2 – Process Qualification

  • Determination of process steps and parameters to test in PPQ, including sampling plans and the confidence and coverage they provide.
  • Facility and equipment/system impact assessments to prioritize qualification efforts.
  • Determination of effective acceptance criteria for each test function.
  • Analytical test results and deviations.

Stage 3 – Continued Process Verification (CPV)

  • Determination of parameters that should be monitored as well as how they should be sampled and analyzed (e.g., sampling plans, confidence required, and length of enhanced sampling).
  • Evaluation of commercial manufacturing data to determine the best course for process improvement.

6.1.1 Risk Management in Stage 1 – Process Design

Original Text

Conducting risk assessments during Stage 1 lays the groundwork for variables to be controlled and monitored. It also determines the extent to which continued monitoring will ensure a state of control during routine manufacturing. This begins with a criticality analysis: An initial definition of product quality attributes and an assessment of their relative importance.

Inputs for the criticality analysis are:

  • QTPP
  • All relevant prior knowledge for the product being evaluated

Outputs from the criticality analysis are:

  • Initial CQA list
  • Initial relative severity listing of the CQAs

Criticality of product attributes is assessed on a quantitative scale (i.e., not only "yes" or "no"). This is accomplished by performing a risk assessment analysis that uses Severity and Likelihood, rather than the usual Severity and Occurrence. The process, which is iterative, is based on building product and process knowledge. The level of severity assigned is based on the potential patient impact, while likelihood is based on how much information (product knowledge and clinical experience) is available to determine the potential severity level for the specific attribute. Part of the output of this assessment will be further scientific studies to reduce the amount of uncertainty for higher-risk attributes (refer to Table 6.1.1-1🔍) (79).

Key Concepts

Criticality Analysis: This is the core risk management work in Stage 1. Note that it uses a Severity x Likelihood matrix, not the Severity x Occurrence x Detectability of traditional FMEA.

Why the difference? Because in Stage 1 you do not yet have manufacturing data to assess Occurrence, nor have you established detection methods to assess Detectability. You have only two dimensions available:

Analogy

Imagine you are a novice surgeon planning an operation. Severity is like "what happens if this step goes wrong": cutting an artery versus a superficial skin wound; the severity stays fixed. Likelihood is like "how well do you know this step": having done it 100 times versus having only read about it in a textbook. When severity is high and experience is low, you need more preparation and practice (scientific studies). This is the logic behind High Severity + High Likelihood = Critical in the table.

Formula

Relationship between confidence level and sample size:

When using attribute sampling,
the minimum sample size needed to reach a given confidence level with zero defects:

n = ln(1 - C) / ln(1 - p)

where:
C = confidence level
p = acceptable defect rate

For example:
99% confidence, 1% defect rate → n = ln(0.01)/ln(0.99) ≈ 459
95% confidence, 1% defect rate → n = ln(0.05)/ln(0.99) ≈ 299
90% confidence, 1% defect rate → n = ln(0.10)/ln(0.99) ≈ 229

The higher the severity, the larger the sample size required!

6.1.3 Risk Management in Stage 3 – Continued Process Verification

Original Text

The CPV stage is the longest segment of the PV lifecycle. It starts with an assessment of process capabilities and continues through a review of the output from process characterization, PPQ, and historical data. The level of enhanced sampling that may be implemented, as necessary, before commercial manufacturing starts, can be determined by a statistical review of the PPQ data. The capabilities of the processes help determine the level of enhanced sampling for an attribute and the length of time that sampling should continue at that level (refer to Section 6.2). The statistical capability of the process is usually related to the level of occurrence rating in the risk assessments (higher occurrence leading to lower capability). The more robust a process, the lower the occurrence rate for a potential failure and the lower the overall risk to the process. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Enhanced Sampling: The start of CPV usually involves more intensive sampling than routine monitoring; this is enhanced sampling. Its intensity and duration depend on the process capability shown by the PPQ data.

Inverse relationship between process capability and risk: The source states clearly that high process capability (that is, a robust process) → low Occurrence → low risk → a shorter enhanced sampling period is acceptable. Conversely, low process capability calls for a longer enhanced sampling period to confirm process stability.

Analogy

Enhanced sampling is like the supervised-driving period for a new driver. Just after getting your license (passing PPQ), you may still need an instructor in the passenger seat (enhanced sampling). CPV is the longest stage of the validation lifecycle. This is often overlooked: many organizations focus their effort on PPQ, and CPV becomes a passive routine. TR60 reminds us that CPV is what ensures the process remains in a state of control "continuously", and that its sampling strategy should be driven jointly by PPQ data and risk assessment.

The Review Period is also influenced by risk level: high-risk attributes may need monthly review, while low-risk attributes may only need quarterly or annual review.

DoE only in Stage 1: In principle DoE is performed in Stage 1 because it involves experiments that deliberately change process parameters, which is not permitted in a commercial manufacturing environment that has already entered the validation stage.

6.2.1 Design of Experiments (DoE)

Original Text

The statistical DoE is a powerful tool often used during Stage 1 Process Design to:

  • Determine which process input parameters have a significant effect on the output quality attributes
  • Help determine the "design space" levels of the input parameters that will produce acceptable output quality attribute results
  • Optimize the output of quality attributes, such as yield and acceptable levels of impurities
  • Determine the levels of input parameters that will result in a robust process that reduces its sensitivity to parameter variability

DoE differs from the classical approach to experimentation, where only one parameter is varied while all others are held constant. That "one factor at a time" type of experimentation cannot determine process parameter interactions, where the effect of one parameter on a quality attribute differs depending on the level of the other parameters. The basic steps for the DoE approach are summarized below:

1. Determine the input parameters and output quality attribute to study

  • This is best done as part of a team approach to identify potential CPPs and quality attribute; in many cases, the process may be well-understood and the parameters and attributes for experimentation readily determined.
  • If there are many input parameters, an initial screening design, such as a fractional factorial or Plackett-Burman design, may be used (82). The purpose of a screening experiment is to identify the critical parameters that have the most important statistical effect on the quality attributes. Since screening designs do not always clearly identify interactions, the reduced number of parameters identified by the screening experiment will be included in further experiments.
  • If the change is to an existing process, it is often valuable to construct a multi-variable chart or SPC chart from current process data (83). A multi-variable chart can be used to identify if the biggest sources of variation are within-batch variation, between-batch variation, or positional variation (e.g., between fill heads on a multi-head filler). Variance components can also be calculated from the data to determine the largest component of variance. Process parameters that could be causing the largest sources of variation are then identified and included in subsequent experiments.
  • Data may also be used to create SPC charts to determine if the process is in statistical control. Since a lack of statistical control will contribute to experimental error variation, it will be more difficult to understand the results of an experiment if the process is not in statistical control. Lack of statistical control may also mean that there are "lurking variables" not on the list of process parameters that are contributing to process variation.

2. Conduct experiment(s) to determine which parameters have a significant main or interaction effect on the quality attributes

  • This will usually be a full factorial design for two to four parameters. A full two-level factorial design has a low (-) and high (+) level selected for each factor (parameter). At least one experiment is run at each combination of the factor levels. For two factors, 22 = 4 combinations exist; for three factors, 23 = 8 combinations exist; for four factors, 24 = 16 combinations exist. Full factorial designs are seldom used for more than four factors since so many experiments are required. Fractional factorial experiments, where only one-half or one-quarter of the combinations are used, are often done for four to six parameters.
  • If possible, control runs at the nominal midpoints (0) between the low (-) and high (+) levels of the factors should be included in the experimental design. Using control runs at the beginning and the end of the factorial experiment and, ideally, also during the factorial experiment, will allow detection of any process drift during the experiments. Control runs at the beginning and end of experiments that do not give similar results may indicate the presence of another uncontrolled variable. Replicate control runs at the nominal values also provide a true estimate of inherent process variation (called experimental error).
  • If possible, the parameter effects on both the mean and variation of the quality attributes should be determined. Some parameters may affect the mean only, variation only, or both. This information can be used to minimize the variation while optimizing the mean, which results in a robust process. Standard DoE approaches, as well as the Taguchi method, may be used for this (84).

3. Optimize the response surface experiments and determine design space

  • Occasionally, the science behind a process will be understood well enough to skip screening and two-level factorial experiments and start with response surface experiments. If enough information is learned from two-level factorial studies, no additional experiments will be required, and this step can be skipped. However, it is often necessary to conduct more extensive experiments at three to five levels for the parameters identified as the most important from earlier factorial experiments. The goal of response surface experiments is to develop an equation that accurately models the relationship between the input parameters and the output quality attributes. This equation is then used to determine the design space region of the input parameters where the output quality attributes will meet specifications.
  • The most common response surface experimental designs are Box-Behnken, central composite, three-level full factorial, and computer-generated optimal designs (D-, G-, I- or, more generally, OMARS (Orthogonal Minimally Aliased Response Surface)) (82). All of these have experiments where at least three levels of the parameters are included in order to estimate curvature (quadratic) effects. The results are analyzed to determine regression equations to model the process with such computer programs as Minitab, JMP, and SAS (83).
  • Another aspect of optimization is to develop a robust process. The regression equations already developed can be used to locate input parameter settings that are "forgiving," that is, when the process is run at these settings, variation in the input parameters will not result in unacceptable variation in the quality attributes. The idea is to stay away from boundaries or areas in the parameter design space where variation in the parameter will result in rapid quality deterioration. This is accomplished by using the quadratic and interaction effects to minimize variation. The Taguchi method of experimental design uses a slightly different approach to also develop robust processes (85, 86).
  • The results may also be used to calculate the percent of total variation attributable to each parameter. This is called a variance components analysis. The input parameters contributing the most to the output quality attribute variation can be controlled the most tightly, made robust by running the process at a particular level of the other parameters, or improved by a process design change to reduce the impact of the parameter.

4. Confirm DoE results

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Design of Experiments (DoE): DoE is the most powerful statistical tool in Stage 1 process design. It is fundamentally different from the traditional "one factor at a time" (OFAT) approach: DoE can change several parameters simultaneously and thereby reveal interactions between parameters.

The four functions of DoE:

  • Screen: identify which parameters really matter
  • Model: build the mathematical relationship between inputs and outputs
  • Optimize: find the best operating conditions
  • Robustness: make sure the process is insensitive to variation

Analogy

OFAT vs DoE: Imagine you want to brew the perfect cup of coffee. With OFAT you first adjust only the water temperature (everything else fixed), and once you find the best temperature you adjust only the grind size, then the water-to-coffee ratio... But the problem is that the best temperature may depend on the grind size! The two interact. With DoE you test combinations of temperature x grind x ratio at the same time, and a single experiment reveals all the main effects and interactions. It actually needs fewer runs and gives more reliable conclusions.

Key Notes

The four-step DoE approach:

  1. Step 1 — Decide the parameters and quality attributes to study: Start with screening (Screening Design). If there are too many parameters (>4), use a Fractional Factorial or Plackett-Burman design first to shorten the list.
  2. Step 2 — Factorial experiments (Factorial Design): Use a Full Factorial design for 2-4 parameters and a fractional factorial design for >4. Be sure to include center points as control runs.
  3. Step 3 — Response Surface design: For the important parameters, run experiments at 3-5 levels, build a mathematical model, and define the Design Space.
  4. Step 4 — Confirm the results: Confirmation experiments verify that the model predictions agree with the actual results.

Formula

Number of runs in a full factorial design:

Number of runs in a full factorial design = L^k

where:
L = number of levels
k = number of factors

Two-level full factorial designs:
2 factors: 2² = 4 runs
3 factors: 2³ = 8 runs
4 factors: 2⁴ = 16 runs
5 factors: 2⁵ = 32 runs (a half fraction = 16 is usually used instead)

Three-level response surface design (central composite design):
3 factors: about 15-20 runs
4 factors: about 24-30 runs

Common design types:
- Box-Behnken: three or more factors, no corner runs
- Central Composite (CCD): the most commonly used, includes star points
- D-Optimal: computer-generated optimal design
- OMARS: orthogonal minimally aliased response surface

Advanced Concepts

The importance of Control Runs:

  • Placed at the start and end of the experiment, they can detect process drift
  • If the control runs at the start and end give inconsistent results, "lurking variables" are present
  • Repeated control runs provide an estimate of the true inherent process variability (experimental error)

The core idea of Robust Design: find a "forgiving" operating point, one where the output quality attributes do not deviate significantly even when input parameters fluctuate. This is achieved by staying away from the edges of the design space and using quadratic and interaction terms to locate the region of minimum variability.

Key Notes

Three-level variability decomposition in the Multi-Vari Chart: The source specifically notes that a Multi-Vari Chart can distinguish three sources of variability:

  • Within-batch: differences within the same batch
  • Between-batch: differences between different batches
  • Positional: for example, differences between the filling heads of a multi-head filling machine

This is critical when deciding "what to control": if the main source of variability is positional, improving uniformity between filling heads is more effective than adjusting process parameters.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: application of DoE in the filling process:

Suppose you are developing a filling process for a viscous biologic. The parameters suspected of affecting filling accuracy include filling speed, tubing temperature, pump pressure, and product viscosity.

Strategy:

  1. First screen the 4 parameters with a 24-1 half-fraction screening design (8 runs + 3 center points = 11 runs)
  2. The results may show a significant interaction between filling speed and product viscosity
  3. Then study these 2 key parameters in depth with a CCD response surface design (about 13 runs)
  4. Build a regression equation: filling accuracy = f(speed, viscosity)
  5. Define the design space and select a robust operating point

6.3 Process Analytical Technology (PAT)

Original Text

PAT is a method of process control, where the product or in-process material quality attributes are monitored and measured during processing, and the process parameters and conditions are adjusted to compensate for the variability of the quality attributes with the goal of ensuring a consistent final product quality.

PAT can provide high levels of product quality assurance through the analysis of material attributes and the process adjustments. In that quality attributes do not vary outside of the prescribed ranges, product and material quality is maintained (98).

PAT can support PV whether it is a parallel activity (concurrent with PV), reductive activity (reduces execution of existing tests), or replacement activity (alternative to traditional testing).

Effective use of PAT to provide process control relies on the selection of the correct quality attributes, process performance ranges, and methods for monitoring and reporting. It also relies on the proper design, use, and validation of the PAT monitoring, measurement, and control loop systems.

The validation of the PAT system is based in part on the following principles:

  • Measurement of the correct product and in-process quality attributes
  • Accuracy and understanding of the correlation between these quality attributes and the process parameters that will be adjusted
  • Reliability, suitability, capability, and accuracy of the monitoring, measurement, and process control loop or adjustment systems
  • Acceptable performance of the PAT system throughout commercial manufacturing, including the ability to identify opportunities for process improvement

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PAT (Process Analytical Technology): PAT is more than a "testing technology"; it is a process control philosophy. The core idea: rather than testing whether the product conforms after the process has finished, monitor quality attributes in real time while the process is running and adjust process parameters dynamically, so that consistent product quality is built in at the root.

Three modes of supporting PV:

  • Parallel activity: PAT runs alongside conventional testing to build confidence
  • Reductive activity: once sufficient PAT data exist, the frequency of conventional testing is reduced
  • Replacement activity: PAT fully replaces the conventional test method

Analogy

Imagine cooking a pot of soup. The traditional quality approach is to taste it only after it is done (finished product testing). The PAT approach is to taste as you cook and adjust the salt and seasoning along the way (real-time monitoring and adjustment). The benefit: you are very unlikely to end up with an inedible soup, because any problem is caught and corrected the moment it occurs.

The PAT "control loop" works like a smart thermostat: a sensor measures the temperature, the controller judges whether it deviates from the set point, and heating/cooling is adjusted automatically, forming closed-loop control.

Key Notes

The four principles of PAT validation are interlocking: (1) measure the right thing, (2) understand the correlation, (3) ensure the instrument is reliable, (4) keep it effective over time. If any one link fails, the quality assurance capability of the whole PAT system is weakened. For a CDMO, the return on PAT investment lies in shorter batch release times, fewer OOS events, and better process consistency, all of which translate directly into customer trust and commercial competitiveness.

6.3.1 Selection of Process Analytical Technology

Original Text

PAT is an enabler to product and process understanding and an element of the control strategy. Prior to the selection of the PAT system, the product and manufacturing process must be developed and well-understood. Selecting the right PAT system should be based on fitness for purpose, system ruggedness, and vendor customer service. Selection criteria should include, but are not limited to, specificity, sensitivity and accuracy, electronic integration requirements of information technology compatibility, data management, and communication.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Fitness for Purpose: When selecting a PAT system, the most important thing is not to pick the most advanced technology but the one best suited to the specific process need. It is like choosing a measuring tool: you do not need a precision analytical balance to weigh a person.

Selection criteria cover multiple dimensions:

  • Analytical performance: specificity, sensitivity, accuracy
  • System integration: IT compatibility, data management, communication interfaces
  • Operational considerations: ruggedness, vendor service and support

Key Notes

TR60 specifically stresses that "the product and manufacturing process must already be developed and well understood before a PAT system is selected." This means PAT should not be the starting point of process development; it is a tool for strengthening the control strategy on the basis of sufficient process knowledge. For a CDMO, it also means that when accepting a technology transfer from a client, the process must first be thoroughly understood before the introduction of PAT can be properly evaluated.

6.3.2 Process Validation Considerations during the PAT System Design

Original Text

During PAT system design, information is developed to confirm that the correct product and in-process quality attributes are being measured, and that the correlation between these quality attributes and the process parameters that will be adjusted is understood and accurate. PAT system design begins in Stage 1 and is qualified in Stage 2 and continually verified in Stage 3. During PAT system design, an understanding of how process-parameter changes affect product attributes is established. Process monitoring and control systems are designed and linked to specific product attributes. Ranges of acceptable process-parameter variation are determined. PAT design efforts should include risk assessment, system feasibility and selection, in-process application development, and consideration of regulatory requirements.

6.3.2.1 Risk Assessment

The risk assessment should identify product and in-process quality attributes that influence final product quality. The risk assessment should identify process steps and conditions that affect these attributes and can be measured and adjusted to ensure product quality. Quality attributes and corresponding process steps and conditions that are not monitored by the PAT system may require other means to ensure or validate performance. Having PAT systems is expected to lower the risk to product quality by having additional controls, timely responses, increased detectability, increased understanding, and information (e.g., identification, measurement, control of CQAs). These features enable a more informed risk-assessment decision. Tools for the assessment and evaluation of PAT processes and systems are discussed in Section 6.1 of this technical report, as well as in ICH Q9(R1), TR 54, and the FDA PAT Framework guidance, among other publications (37, 78, 99).

6.3.2.2 In-Process Application and Method Development

The PAT methods for in-process product measurement and process adjustment should be selected and validated for specificity, linearity, range, accuracy, precision, repeatability, robustness, detection limit, and quantitation limit to ensure that the method is fit for purpose (37).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Lifecycle mapping of PAT system design:

  • Stage 1 (Process Design): design the PAT system and establish the relationship between process parameters and quality attributes
  • Stage 2 (Process Qualification): confirm that the performance of the PAT system meets requirements
  • Stage 3 (Continued Process Verification): continue to verify the performance of the PAT system in commercial production

Validation of the PAT system itself also follows the lifecycle approach and aligns neatly with the three stages of process validation.

Analogy

PAT risk assessment is like a hospital building a monitoring plan for a patient: first assess which vital signs matter most (heart rate, blood pressure, blood oxygen?), then decide the monitoring frequency and intervention thresholds. Not every indicator needs continuous monitoring (some can be measured periodically), but missing a critical indicator can have serious consequences. Likewise, quality attributes not monitored by PAT "may require other means to assure or verify performance."

Key Notes

Completeness of PAT method validation: TR60 explicitly cites the nine elements of analytical method validation (specificity, linearity, range, accuracy, precision, repeatability, ruggedness, detection limit, quantitation limit), consistent with ICH Q2. This is a reminder that a PAT method does not get lower validation standards just because it is "online" or "real-time."

Two-way value of risk assessment: Introducing PAT both lowers process quality risk (better control and detection capability) and gives risk assessment a fuller information base, creating a virtuous cycle.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, introducing PAT for different clients' products calls for a platform strategy: can a common PAT platform (for example, NIR monitoring of content uniformity) be established that applies to multiple client products? This spreads equipment investment and validation cost and improves the return on investment of the PAT system. However, each product still requires its own method validation and correlation confirmation.

6.3.3 Process Qualification Considerations for Process Analytical Technology

Original Text

The process qualification stage is where information is developed to confirm that the monitoring, measurement, and process control or adjustment systems are suitable, capable, accurate, and reliable. One key to effective PAT process control is the reliable operation of instruments and equipment. For implementation, an implementation and validation team should be assembled to categorize the validation requirements and propose acceptance criteria for each unit of operation, based on the application or intended use of the PAT system and method. These requirements and criteria will ultimately be included in a validation protocol and described in the validation report. The acceptance criteria should be aligned with the expected specification, protocol requirements, development experience, and manufacturing practice.

The function and operation of the equipment and instrumentation used in the PAT system should be qualified to ensure that it will monitor and control the process parameters accurately and reliably. Equipment and instruments used during the process should be qualified to verify that they are suitable for in-process use, including compatibility with process materials and conditions, accuracy, sensitivity, security, and reliability.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four elements of PAT qualification (SCAR):

  • Suitable: fit for the specific process application
  • Capable: able to achieve the required measurement precision
  • Accurate: measurement results agree with the true value
  • Reliable: performs stably over the long term

These four aspects are the key bridge for taking a PAT system from "workable in the laboratory" to "reliable in commercial manufacturing."

Key Notes

TR60 stresses the need to assemble an "implementation and validation team" to drive PAT qualification. This implies that introducing PAT is not the job of a single department; it requires a cross-functional team: process development, analytical methods, equipment engineering, IT systems, quality assurance, and so on. In a CDMO in particular, technical representatives from the client may also need to participate.

Special considerations for equipment suitability qualification: PAT equipment often contacts process materials directly or operates under harsh environmental conditions, so additional confirmation is needed of compatibility with process materials and conditions (for example, corrosion resistance, reliability under sterile conditions, cleaning validation).

6.3.4 Continued Process Verification Considerations for Process Analytical Technology

Original Text

The CPV stage is where information is obtained to confirm that the PAT system performs at an acceptable level throughout commercial manufacturing. It also determines where product and in-process quality attributes, or process parameters, fall out of expected ranges; those that do are identified, investigated for cause, and addressed.

PAT provides continuous process and product attribute verification. Stage 3 activities should therefore focus on accuracy and reliability of control methods, possible process-control improvements, and process variables missed during process development and qualification. Evaluation of PAT and or in-process derived data should be part of the quality system and review processes (17). Where data trending shows excursions in anticipated monitoring results, an analysis of the cause of the excursion should be conducted to determine if changes to the control system are needed or what opportunities for process improvement can be identified.

When variables are found that are not being monitored adequately, changes to the monitoring methods may be needed. All changes should be evaluated for the impact on the process and product attributes. Changes should be evaluated, and actions should be implemented to assure that residual risks do not adversely affect process performance or product quality. These actions may include steps to qualify the changed process and equipment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The continuing value of PAT in Stage 3: PAT is inherently suited to "continued verification" because it collects data throughout the process. The focus of Stage 3 is no longer to prove that the PAT system works, but to:

  • Confirm that the accuracy and reliability of the PAT control method continue to be maintained
  • Identify process improvement opportunities (the vast amount of PAT data is a treasure trove for improvement)
  • Identify process variables missed during the development and qualification stages

Analogy

The role of PAT in Stage 3 is like a vehicle's dashcam and OBD (on-board diagnostics). The dashcam continuously records driving conditions, and OBD monitors engine health in real time. When an abnormality appears, the system alerts the driver (drift detection), and the accumulated historical data help identify long-term trends (such as brake wear or changes in fuel consumption) so that maintenance and improvement can be carried out in advance.

Key Notes

Embedded change management: TR60 specifically reminds us that when a need to adjust the monitoring method or the process is found, every change must be evaluated for its impact on the process and product attributes. This ties PAT continual improvement activities closely to the change control procedure in the quality system, ensuring that improvements do not introduce new risks.

Trend analysis of PAT data should be incorporated into the quality system and review processes (such as the annual product quality review / APR); this is an explicit regulatory expectation.

Practical Application

The commercial value for a CDMO of using PAT data in Stage 3: (1) providing clients with real-time reports on process capability, which strengthens trust; (2) using trend analysis to proactively propose process optimization, demonstrating added technical value; (3) detecting drift early to avoid batch losses and reduce client costs. These are all tools for CDMO differentiation.

Original Text

Original Text

KM for CPV refers to the systematic approach of capturing, storing, sharing, and utilizing knowledge gained from the CPV process to enhance the efficiency, consistency, and quality of manufacturing processes. It involves managing the insights, data, and experiences accumulated throughout the lifecycle of a product or process, especially in regulated industries like pharmaceuticals, where maintaining product quality and regulatory compliance is crucial.

In the context of CPV, KM ensures that relevant data, process performance information, lessons learned, and best practices are accessible to all stakeholders.   • Utilizing: turn knowledge into improvement actions and a basis for decisions

Tacit vs. Explicit Knowledge：

  • Explicit Knowledge: documented knowledge, such as SOPs, batch records, and validation reports
  • Tacit Knowledge: undocumented knowledge held in people's experience, such as "this instrument needs a 20-minute warm-up after start-up before measurements become stable"

Analogy

Knowledge management is like a library management system. Raw data are like loose pages scattered everywhere (raw data); once organized they become books (information); specialists then write guides and indexes (knowledge); and finally readers can quickly find the knowledge they need and apply it (application).

Tacit knowledge is like the experience in an old librarian's head: "That obscure reference book has actually been misclassified; you have to go to a certain corner to find it." If that librarian retires, this knowledge is lost for good unless someone writes it down. This is why KM must pay special attention to capturing and converting tacit knowledge.

Key Notes

ICH Q10 requirements for KM: ICH Q10 explicitly states that the management of product and process knowledge should continue from the development stage through to product discontinuation. This means knowledge management is not a one-time activity but a continuing process throughout the entire product lifecycle.

Special KM challenges for CDMOs:

  • Knowledge for multi-client products must be managed in isolation (confidentiality obligations), while platform knowledge needs to be shared
  • With higher staff turnover, the risk of losing tacit knowledge is greater
  • Technology transfers are frequent, and each one tests the organization's knowledge management capability

Practical Application

Recommended KM practices for CDMOs:

  • Build a process knowledge database: systematically record each product's CPP/CQA relationships, the history of process parameter optimization, deviation handling experience, and so on
  • Mechanisms for converting tacit knowledge: hold regular technical sharing sessions and process review meetings to turn the experience of senior operators into documented knowledge
  • Cross-product learning: where confidentiality allows, establish generic process platform knowledge (such as the methodology for freeze-drying cycle development) as a shared organizational asset
  • Personnel handover procedure: establish a structured knowledge handover checklist to reduce knowledge loss when staff change roles

Section Summary

  • PAT (Process Analytical Technology): PAT is a control approach that monitors quality attributes in real time during the process and adjusts process parameters dynamically; it can serve as a parallel, reductive, or replacement activity for PV. Its validation must cover four principles: measurement correctness, understanding of correlations, system reliability, and continued effectiveness, and it aligns neatly with the three PV stages (design, qualification, continued verification).
  • PAT selection and design: PAT system selection should be based on fitness for purpose, taking into account analytical performance, system integration, and operational needs. The design stage should include risk assessment, feasibility assessment, and method validation, and the validation standards for PAT methods are the same as for conventional analytical methods (the nine ICH Q2 elements).
  • PAT qualification and continued verification: the process qualification stage must confirm the suitability, capability, accuracy, and reliability (SCAR) of the PAT system, and requires cross-functional teamwork. In Stage 3, PAT provides ongoing verification of process and product attributes, and trend analysis of the data should be incorporated into quality system reviews.
  • Technology Transfer: the goal of TT is to transfer process knowledge effectively between development and manufacturing and between different manufacturing sites. The information must be useful, accurate, and complete. TT activities are distributed across the three PV stages, and the depth of knowledge accumulates as the lifecycle advances. The success criterion is that the receiving unit can pass process validation and achieve reliable manufacturing.
  • TT activity matrix: Stage 1 focuses on development knowledge (DoE, CQA/CPP definitions); Stage 2 carries out the most TT activities (PPQ, site equivalence, method transfer); Stage 3 draws on the richest historical data for post-approval change transfers, which must follow SUPAC and ICH Q12 requirements.
  • Knowledge Management: KM is an enabler of the PV lifecycle, covering the capture, storage, sharing, and utilization of knowledge. ICH Q10 requires KM to continue from development through product discontinuation. Particular attention is needed to the conversion of tacit knowledge, because it is often the key to process success yet the asset most easily lost.
  • Integration of the three enabling systems: PAT generates data, KM turns data into knowledge, and TT passes knowledge effectively to the receiving unit. The three support one another and together form the knowledge infrastructure of the process validation lifecycle. For a CDMO, the maturity of these three systems directly determines technology transfer efficiency, PPQ success rate, and continual improvement capability.

9.0 Appendix I: Statistical Methods for Determining the Number of Lots

Original Text

Listed below are statistical approaches used to determine the number of lots that may be required at the process performance qualification (PPQ) stage. Other approaches may also be suitable. Statistical justification of the number of lots may be particularly important when the level of risk on quality at the time of PPQ-readiness assessment remains high. Involvement of a statistician or a subject matter expert skilled in this domain is recommended, when possible. As there is no standard industry approach to statistically determine the number of lots, multiple options are offered, and Section 9.0 (Appendix I) will provide applied statistical methods for determining the number of lots. It will also stimulate further discussion on this issue.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Statistical Determination of PPQ Lot Number: The industry has long lacked a uniform standard for how many PPQ lots should be run. TR60 Appendix I provides several statistical methods so that a company can choose the method best suited to its risk level and product characteristics and "quantify" the number of lots.

Key message: when the PPQ-Readiness Assessment shows that quality risk is still high, a statistical method is especially needed to define the number of lots, rather than relying only on the "industry convention of three lots".

Analogy

Imagine you are a driving instructor deciding how many road tests a student must take before you are comfortable issuing a license. If the student performs steadily in practice (low risk), 2-3 tests may be enough; if the student often makes mistakes in practice (high risk), you may need to observe many more tests before you can trust their driving. The logic for deciding the number of PPQ lots is exactly the same: the higher the risk, the more statistical evidence is needed.

Key Notes

TR60 explicitly recommends involving a statistician or a subject matter expert (SME) with relevant expertise wherever possible. This is not just a "recommendation"; it is the key to giving a convincing answer when a regulatory inspector asks, "Why only three lots?"

The methods in this appendix are not mutually exclusive. Companies can combine them, for example using ARL for initial screening and then Ppk for detailed confirmation.

9.1 Average Run Length to Detect a p x 100% Lot Failure Rate

Original Text

The average number of lots, or average run length (ARL), until the first lot failure is ARL = 1/p, where p is the lot failure rate that is important to detect.

"Example: A lot failure rate of 20% is deemed unacceptable for a given process. A lot failure rate of 20% would be detected on average in 1/0.2 = once every 5 lots."

Common choices for p would be 25%, 20%, 10%, and 5%, depending on factors such as prior knowledge, risk, and production rate. Five percent (5%) would generally be the tightest value to consider since a process running right at the acceptance quality limit (AQL) is still expected to have a 5% lot rejection rate. If applicable, this approach may be particularly useful when there are many quality attributes to assess. Rather than determining the number of lots required separately for each attribute, the PPQ stage is complete when all CQAs pass their acceptance criteria for the required number of lots.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Average Run Length (ARL): This is the most intuitive method. If the lot failure rate is p, then on average 1/p lots must be observed before a failure is "encountered". Conversely, if 1/p consecutive lots all pass, the failure rate is likely lower than p.

  • p = 25% → ARL = 4 lots
  • p = 20% → ARL = 5 lots
  • p = 10% → ARL = 10 lots
  • p = 5% → ARL = 20 lots

Formula

ARL = 1 / p

Where:
  p = lot failure rate to be detected
  ARL = expected average number of lots before the first failure

Example:
  Unacceptable failure rate = 20%
  ARL = 1 / 0.20 = 5 lots
  Meaning: on average, 1 failure occurs every 5 lots

Analogy

If a die has a 20% chance of rolling a "1", a "1" will appear on average once every 5 rolls. If you roll 5 times in a row and never see a "1", you start to suspect that the chance of rolling a "1" is actually lower than 20%. Likewise, 5 consecutive passing PPQ lots provide indirect evidence that the failure rate is below 20%.

Key Notes

Consideration of the AQL lower limit: Even if the process runs exactly at the AQL threshold, a 5% lot rejection rate is still expected. Therefore p = 5% (requiring 20 lots) is usually the most stringent consideration for this method.

Advantage for multiple attributes: The ARL method is particularly practical when a product has multiple CQAs to evaluate. There is no need to calculate the number of lots separately for each attribute; it is enough to require that all CQAs pass within the specified number of lots.

9.2 Range of Expected Between-Lot Variation

Original Text

As shown in Table 9.2-1🔍, the range of between-lot variation to be covered by number of lots (nL) is calculated as: 100 x (nL - 1) / (nL + 1). This follows from the expected percentile of an order statistic. The order statistic's rank is divided by n + 1 (1). This approach does not require between-lot normality. The method may be modified to provide confidence levels of coverage instead of expected coverage. The approach may be used to determine "step-down sampling" during CPV. For example, a higher level of sampling may be used in PPQ for the first three lots until 50% coverage is reached. At that time, the PPQ is considered complete. Reduced sampling for critical characteristics may continue into Stage 3 CPV for four more lots until 75% coverage is reached, at which point, routine sampling begins (2).

Table 9.2-1🔍 Expected Between-Lot Variation in Number of Lots

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Between-Lot Variation Coverage: This method answers the question, "How many lots do I need to run to observe most of the range of between-lot variation?" The formula is based on the expected percentile of an order statistic. It is mathematically elegant and does not require the between-lot data to be normally distributed.

Step-Down Sampling: This is a staged strategy. Sampling starts at a higher intensity during PPQ; once a certain coverage is reached, PPQ is considered complete. Sampling then continues at a lower intensity in the CPV stage until the target coverage is reached, after which sampling returns to routine levels.

Formula

Expected Coverage (%) = 100 x (nL - 1) / (nL + 1)

Example calculations:
  nL = 3 lots → 100 x (3-1)/(3+1) = 100 x 2/4 = 50%
  nL = 5 lots → 100 x (5-1)/(5+1) = 100 x 4/6 = 67%
  nL = 7 lots → 100 x (7-1)/(7+1) = 100 x 6/8 = 75%

Inverse calculation: number of lots needed to cover X%:
  nL = (1 + X/100) / (1 - X/100)   [round to an integer]

Analogy

Imagine you want to understand a city's temperature range. If you look at only 2 days, you cover only about 33% of the variation; a full week (7 days) covers about 75%. To cover 95% of the temperature variation, you might need to observe 39 days! The same applies to between-lot variation: the more lots you observe, the more complete your understanding of the range of variation.

Key Notes

Practical significance of reading the table:

  • A 3-lot PPQ covers only 50% of the between-lot variation, which means the other 50% has not yet been observed
  • 5 lots cover 67%, but 1/3 of the variation range is still unknown
  • Reaching 90% coverage requires 19 lots, which is usually impractical in the PPQ stage
  • A staged strategy (PPQ + CPV) is therefore the pragmatic approach

No normality assumption needed: A major advantage of this method is that it does not require the between-lot data to be normally distributed, because it is based on order statistics from non-parametric statistics.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: A CDMO is asked to review a client's PPQ plan, which calls for only 3 PPQ lots. Using this table, the CDMO can explain to the client that 3 lots cover only 50% of the between-lot variation, and recommend continuing to monitor at least 4 more lots in the CPV stage after PPQ, reaching 75% coverage (7 lots in total) before moving to routine monitoring. This staged strategy meets regulatory expectations without creating an excessive PPQ lot requirement.

9.3 Within-Lot and Between-Lot Tolerance Intervals

Original Text

Statistical tolerance intervals are commonly used in validation. Tables of normal tolerance interval factors for variable data are widely available and implemented in statistical software. Specialized software is available to optimally calculate the desired confidence statement. Normal tolerance intervals for the total process variation over time are more complicated; they include both within- and between-lot variation. Standard normal tolerance interval factors assume that there is only one population in the data. However, most PPQs contain multiple populations, since each lot is a separate population.

If, given quantitative attributes for which several units are tested per lot, there are no significant differences between the lots, the simplest way to deal with multiple lots is to combine the data. An Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test may be used to compare lot means; within-lot variation may be compared with the Levene, Browne-Forsythe, Bartlett, Cochran, or Fmax statistical tests (2-4). An omnibus test may also be used. The sample size per lot and the number of lots should be statistically determined to have adequate power to detect any between-lot variation.

"Example: The specification for cap-removal torque for a small-volume parenteral product is 8.0–12.0 inch-pounds. Limited data from Stage 1 showed a standard deviation of about 0.5. The production AQL for removal torque is 1.0%. The acceptance criterion for the PPQ is to show with 90% confidence that at least 99% (1 minus the AQL) of the cap torques are within specifications."

Three lots are included in the PPQ to evaluate the within-lot and between-lot variations. A sample size of 30 units per PPQ lot was tested to detect between-lot variation as large as the within-lot variation with 90% confidence (5). Samples from throughout each of the three lots were tested, and the acceptance criteria for each lot was met. An individual/moving range (I/MR) statistical process control (SPC) chart indicated that the process was in control during each lot. Normality tests for each lot did not indicate significant non-normality. Since ANOVA and Levene's tests showed no significant difference among the three lots, the data were combined.

The 90 test results had a mean of 9.59 and standard deviation of 0.51. This interval is within the specification limits of 8.0-12.0. Thus, the PPQ has shown with 90% confidence that at least 99% of torque results are expected to meet specifications. Those calculations may be performed using Commercial-Off-The-Shelf statistical software (e.g., JMP©, Minitab©, SAS©). If there are statistically significant differences between lots, the tolerance interval should be constructed with more advanced methods that take the between-lot variance component into account (6,7).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Statistical Tolerance Interval: Unlike specification limits, a tolerance interval is calculated from data and is used to state that "with X% confidence, at least Y% of the product falls within this range". In validation, it is an important tool for demonstrating process capability.

ANOVA (analysis of variance): Used to compare whether the means of multiple lots differ significantly. If ANOVA shows no significant difference, the data can be pooled to calculate the tolerance interval.

Levene's Test (test for homogeneity of variances): Used to check whether the within-lot variation is consistent across lots. Unlike ANOVA, which compares means, Levene's test compares the degree of dispersion.

Analogy

Suppose you have math exam scores from three classes. If the three classes have similar average scores and similar spread (ANOVA and Levene's test show no significant difference), you can pool the scores and analyze them together to get a more precise estimate of the "school-wide score distribution". But if one class is clearly higher or lower, pooling the data will mask that difference and lead to a misleading conclusion. The logic for pooling PPQ lot data is exactly the same.

Key Notes

Key points of the example:

  • Specification: 8.0-12.0 inch-pounds (cap removal torque)
  • Stage 1 estimated standard deviation: approximately 0.5
  • Acceptance criterion: 90% confidence, at least 99% conforming
  • 30 units per lot, 3 lots = 90 units; the pooled data are used to calculate the tolerance interval
  • Result: mean 9.59, standard deviation 0.51; the tolerance interval falls within the 8.0-12.0 specification

Important warning: If there is significant between-lot variation, the data cannot simply be pooled! An advanced method that accounts for the between-lot variance component must be used to construct the tolerance interval.

Formula

Normal Tolerance Interval:
  X-bar +/- k * s

Where:
  X-bar = sample mean (9.59)
  s     = sample standard deviation (0.51)
  k     = tolerance interval factor (from tables; depends on n, confidence level, and proportion covered)

Prerequisites for pooling:
  1. ANOVA p-value > alpha → no significant difference in lot means
  2. Levene's test p-value > alpha → no significant difference in within-lot variation
  3. Normality test p-value > alpha → data approximately normal
  4. SPC chart shows the process is in control

9.4 Statistical Process Control Charts

Original Text

Most SPC references suggest obtaining data from 20-30 time periods before calculating control limits to assess whether the process is in control. Samples could be taken at 30 time periods across three or more lots. For three lots, 10 sets of "rational subgroup" samples could be selected from each lot. The SPC chart limits are then calculated, and the process is assessed for statistical control. The number of lots to use can be based on the power of the SPC chart to detect undesirable between-lot variation.

A potential problem with the use of SPC charts, such as Xbar/S charts plotted across lots, is that they define a process as being in statistical control if there is no underlying lot-to-lot variation (8). This is often not the case, and some lot-to-lot variation is typical and expected, especially for lot means. In these cases, an I/MR chart for the mean and/or standard deviation or three-way between/within chart can be used to detect out-of-statistical-control between-lot variation (9).

If there is only one test result per lot, such as a lot assay or pH of a tank of solution, the 20-30 time periods become 20-30 lots. This is seldom feasible for PPQ. An alternative is to select a smaller number of lots, perhaps 5-10, and construct a preliminary I/MR control chart. If it shows an in-control process, the PPQ would be complete, and the control chart would extend into Stage 3 to verify longer-term statistical control during CPV.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Rational Subgroup: In SPC, a rational subgroup is a set of samples produced within a short time under similar conditions. Variation within a subgroup reflects "common cause" variation, while variation between subgroups may reflect "special causes". In PPQ, 10 rational subgroups are drawn from each lot, giving 30 across 3 lots, which meets the 20-30 time periods needed to establish SPC control limits.

I/MR Chart (Individuals/Moving Range chart): Used when each lot yields only one result (such as lot assay or pH). Unlike Xbar/S charts, I/MR charts do not require a subgroup structure.

Analogy

An Xbar/S control chart is like monitoring the "class quiz average" in every class: if the average changes from class to class, the chart raises an alarm. The problem is that fluctuation in results between different "semesters" is normal (between-lot variation), and an Xbar/S chart may misread this normal fluctuation as abnormal. An I/MR chart is more like tracking the trend in the "final exam average of each semester", and can distinguish normal semester-to-semester fluctuation from a genuine anomaly.

Key Notes

The Xbar/S chart trap: When an Xbar/S chart is plotted across lots, the control limits are calculated from within-lot variation. If normal between-lot variation exists (almost every process has it), the chart will produce many "false alarms", with shifts in lot means wrongly flagged as out of control. This is a common statistical misuse in validation.

Pragmatic alternative: Establish a preliminary I/MR control chart with 5-10 lots; once PPQ confirms the process is in control, extend the chart into Stage 3 CPV for continued monitoring, gradually accumulating more data points.

9.5 Process Performance Index (Ppk) & Process Capability Index (Cpk)

Original Text

Ppk is the most common statistic used to assess long-term process capability (refer to Figure 6.2.2.3-1🔍). Acceptable values of Ppk depend on the criticality of the characteristic, but 1.0 and 1.33 are commonly used (10). Smaller or larger values may be used depending on the risk factors involved. The Ppk acceptance criterion may be based on a point estimate or a one-sided lower-confidence interval. If there is significant between-lot variation, caution should be exercised in using confidence intervals for Ppk calculated by statistical software. Most statistical software programs do not take between-lot variation into account and may provide optimistic confidence intervals that are too narrow (11).

"Example: Fill-volume specification limits for a small-volume parenteral product are 98-102. PPQ acceptance criteria are that each lot is Ppk ≥ 1.0; also, that the overall process Ppk is ≥ 1.0 with 95% confidence. To detect a between-lot standard deviation that is half of the within-lot standard deviation with 90% confidence, 33 units will need to be tested from across each of five PPQ lots."

The data from the five lots were analyzed by control charts, histograms, normality tests, Levene's test, and ANOVA. These analyses indicated that the data from the five lots could be combined. The Ppks of each of the five lots were > 1.0. The calculated Ppk from the combined data was 1.14, with a lower 95% confidence interval of 1.03. Since each lot met its Ppk requirement and the lower confidence interval for the overall process, Ppk, was above the acceptance limit of 1.0. Thus, the PPQ acceptance criteria were met.

An alternative to calculating a parametric confidence interval for Ppk is to require each of four or five consecutive lots in a row to meet the Ppk acceptance criteria. For example, four PPQ lots, each with Ppk ≥ 1.0, provides over 90% confidence that the process median Ppk is ≥ 1.0; five lots provide over 95% confidence.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Ppk vs Cpk：

  • Cpk (Process Capability Index): Uses the within-lot standard deviation (short-term variation) and measures the "potential" capability of the process in a stable state
  • Ppk (Process Performance Index): Uses the total standard deviation (including between-lot + within-lot variation) and measures the "actual" performance of the process
  • Ppk is always ≤ Cpk, because Ppk includes more sources of variation
  • The ideal case of Ppk = Cpk means there is no between-lot variation

Commonly used acceptance criteria:

  • Ppk ≥ 1.0: the process mean is at least 3 standard deviations from the nearest specification limit
  • Ppk ≥ 1.33: at least 4 standard deviations away (more stringent; often used for critical characteristics)

Formula

Ppk = min( (USL - X-bar) / (3 * sigma_total),
           (X-bar - LSL) / (3 * sigma_total) )

Cpk = min( (USL - X-bar) / (3 * sigma_within),
           (X-bar - LSL) / (3 * sigma_within) )

Example calculation (fill volume):
  Specification = 98 - 102
  Pooled data: X-bar = some value, sigma_total = some value
  Calculated Ppk = 1.14
  95% lower confidence bound = 1.03 > 1.0 (acceptance criterion)
  → PPQ acceptance criterion met

Alternative method (lot-by-lot Ppk):
  Ppk ≥ 1.0 for all 4 lots → > 90% confidence that median Ppk ≥ 1.0
  Ppk ≥ 1.0 for all 5 lots → > 95% confidence that median Ppk ≥ 1.0

Analogy

Think of the lanes on a highway. The specification limits are like the lane boundary lines, and Ppk measures the distance from your car (the process mean) to the nearest lane line divided by the width of your car (the process variation). Ppk = 1.0 means the car just fits entirely within the lane; Ppk = 1.33 means there is extra safety space on both sides. If your car sometimes drifts left and right (between-lot variation), you need more safety margin, which is why Ppk (accounting for all the drift) is more conservative than Cpk (steady state only).

Key Notes

Software trap warning: Most statistical software (JMP, Minitab, SAS) does not account for the between-lot variance component when calculating Ppk confidence intervals. When significant between-lot variation exists, the software gives an overly optimistic (too narrow) confidence interval, which may lead to wrongly passing the PPQ acceptance criterion.

Non-parametric alternative: Requiring Ppk ≥ 1.0 for 4-5 consecutive lots is a simple alternative that does not rely on confidence interval calculations. It uses a property of the binomial distribution: if the median Ppk is < 1.0, the probability of 4 consecutive lots all having Ppk ≥ 1.0 is less than 6.25% (0.5^4 = 6.25%).

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: The client requires Ppk ≥ 1.33 for the fill volume PPQ. Your 5 lots, pooled, give Ppk = 1.28, but ANOVA shows significant differences between lots. In this situation:

  1. The data cannot simply be pooled to calculate the Ppk confidence interval
  2. An advanced method that accounts for the between-lot variance component should be used
  3. Or switch to the lot-by-lot Ppk method: if the individual Ppk of all 5 lots is ≥ 1.33, there is > 95% confidence
  4. Discuss with the client whether a Ppk ≥ 1.0 acceptance criterion is within acceptable risk

  1. Why is Ppk better suited than Cpk for evaluating long-term process capability in PPQ?
  2. In the example, why are 33 units per lot (rather than fewer) needed to detect a case where the between-lot standard deviation is half the within-lot standard deviation?
  3. If the calculated Ppk point estimate is 1.14 but the lower confidence bound is 0.97, how should you proceed?

9.6 Assure the Lot Conformance Rate is Above an Acceptable Rate with Specified Confidence

Original Text

An approach commonly referred to as the "confidence for reliability" method, demonstrates that the percentage of lot conformance is acceptable. It identifies unacceptable variation due to either common or special causes. Table 9.6-1🔍 shows the required number of conforming lots. This method is sometimes called "confidence for reliability."

Table 9.6-1🔍 Number of Lots to Demonstrate Confidence for Lot Conformance Rate

"Example: To demonstrate the process is acceptable, the PPQ acceptance criterion will be to show with 90% confidence that the process-lot conformance rate (the lot pass rate) is at least 90%. A total of 22 passing lots in a row will demonstrate this."

Requiring many lots during PPQ to reach 90% or 95% confidence is difficult. An alternative is to use 50% confidence in PPQ and monitor the process further during CPV to reach the final desired confidence. Crossing the 50% confidence threshold is the point at which the selected lot conformance rate is more likely to be met. For the example above, once seven (7) passing lots are reached, it is more likely that the conformance rate is greater than 90% rather than less than 90%, and the PPQ could be considered complete. An additional 15 lots during early CPV would reach the required 22 lots. This approach may be particularly useful when there are many quality attributes to assess. Rather than determining the number of lots required separately for each attribute, the PPQ stage is complete when all attributes pass for the required number of lots.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Confidence for Reliability: The logic of this method is that if n consecutive lots all pass (zero failures), we can claim with C% confidence that the "true conformance rate is ≥ R". The mathematical basis is the one-sided confidence interval of the binomial distribution.

Derivation: we require P(0 failures in n trials | true failure rate = 1-R) ≤ 1-C

Simplified: Rn ≥ 1 - C, that is, n ≥ ln(1-C) / ln(R)

Formula

n = ln(1 - C) / ln(R)     [round up]

Where:
  C = confidence level
  R = conformance rate
  n = number of consecutive passing lots required

Verification example:
  C = 90%, R = 90%
  n = ln(1-0.90) / ln(0.90) = ln(0.10) / ln(0.90)
    = -2.3026 / -0.10536 = 21.85 → rounded up = 22 lots

50% confidence + 90% conformance rate:
  n = ln(0.50) / ln(0.90) = -0.6931 / -0.10536 = 6.58 → 7 lots

Analogy

Imagine you are interviewing a new chef. If he cooks 7 dishes in a row well (50% confidence), you feel he is "probably good" and can be hired. But to be truly "confident" (90%) that he can sustain a dish acceptance rate above 90% over the long term, you may need to watch him cook 22 dishes.

TR60's pragmatic suggestion is to first observe 7 dishes (the PPQ period) and hire him formally if all are acceptable, then keep observing the remaining 15 dishes during the probation period (CPV), finally reaching the high confidence level of 22 dishes.

Key Notes

How to use the table:

  • 50% confidence = crossing the break-even point: Beyond this point, the probability that "conformance rate ≥ target" is greater than the probability that it is "< target"
  • Key to the staged strategy: PPQ reaches 50% confidence → CPV continues accumulating to 90% confidence
  • A 99% conformance rate is extremely hard to verify: Even 50% confidence requires 69 lots! This shows that verifying a 99% conformance rate in the PPQ stage is almost impossible

Multi-attribute strategy: Like the ARL method, this method can be applied uniformly across all quality attributes; it is enough that all attributes pass within the specified number of lots.

Practical Application

Regulatory interview scenario: An FDA reviewer asks, "How do you demonstrate that your process has 90% confidence of achieving a 90% lot conformance rate?"

Suggested response: "We use a staged approach. In PPQ, 7 consecutive passing lots give 50% confidence (that is, the probability that the conformance rate is ≥ 90% is more than half). We continue monitoring in early CPV, and reach 90% confidence when a cumulative 22 consecutive lots have passed. This method is based on the binomial distribution, with reference to TR60 Appendix I Section 9.6."

10.1 Control Charts for Variables Data

Original Text

Commonly used control charts for variables data are (8,9):

  • x̄/R or x̄/S "Shewhart" chart using ±3-sigma limits
  • Individuals/Moving Range (I/MR) chart
  • Levey-Jennings chart
  • Moving average chart
  • Exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) chart
  • Cumulative sum (CUSUM) chart

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Variables Data: Data that can be measured on a continuous scale, such as fill weight (mg), pH, assay (%), and dissolution (%). This type of data carries more information than attributes data and can detect process shifts more sensitively.

Key Notes

How to choose between Shewhart charts and EWMA/CUSUM:

  • Shewhart charts detect large shifts (≥2σ) quickly but are not sensitive to small shifts
  • EWMA and CUSUM have excellent ability to detect small shifts (0.5σ to 1.5σ)
  • In the Continued Process Verification (CPV) stage the process is usually stable, so the ability to detect small shifts matters more; EWMA/CUSUM are then the better choice

Analogy

Imagine you are monitoring the water level of a water tower:

  • A Shewhart chart is like glancing at the level gauge at intervals: you will notice a sudden large drop, but a slow leak is easy to miss
  • An EWMA chart is like a smart sensor that gives weight to the recent trend in water level, so it can warn you even when only a little is leaking each time
  • A CUSUM chart is like a ledger that keeps accumulating deviations: even if each deviation is small, the running total will eventually trigger an alarm

10.2 Control Charts for Attributes Data

Original Text

The most-used control charts for attributes data are (8,9):

  • p-chart for fraction or percent nonconforming (binomial data)
  • np-chart for number of units nonconforming (binomial data)
  • c-chart for number of defects (Poisson data)
  • u-chart for average number of defects (Poisson data)

In addition, the I/MR chart can be used for attributes data if most of the plotted values are not zero (0).

For very large sample sizes, for instance, greater than 500 or 1,000 (e.g., percent of tablets rejected across lots on a sample of 1000 or more tablets inspected per lot with an automatic metal detector) and long time periods, the assumptions of a binomial or Poisson distribution that attributes charts use is often violated. In that case, alternative approaches to the common charts for attributes data could be used:

  • Laney's p-chart and u-chart deal appropriately with under- or over-dispersion in the data, which lead respectively to an increased or a decreased number of points outside the control limits with the corresponding classical charts for attributes (17)
  • I/MR or Levey-Jennings's control chart

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Attributes Data: Data that can only be expressed as discrete values (conforming/nonconforming, number of defects). Examples: the number of rejects in visual inspection, particle counts, and pass/fail results of container closure integrity testing.

Binomial vs Poisson distribution:

  • Binomial: Each unit has only two outcomes, "conforming" or "nonconforming". The p-chart and np-chart are based on this distribution
  • Poisson: Counts the number of defects per unit area, volume or time; each unit can have zero or more defects. The c-chart and u-chart are based on this distribution

Key Notes

The large-sample trap: over-dispersion:

This is a very practical problem that is easily overlooked. When the sample size is very large (for example n > 500 or 1,000), the control limits of the traditional p-chart and u-chart become extremely narrow (because the standard deviation is inversely proportional to √n). This leads to:

  • Many data points falling outside the control limits, producing excessive false alarms
  • These out-of-limit points do not represent real process shifts; the actual data variation is larger than the Binomial/Poisson model assumes (that is, over-dispersion)
  • Laney's p'/u' chart corrects the width of the control limits by introducing a sigma(Z) factor, which solves this problem

Analogy

Imagine you are counting traffic violations at a busy intersection in a big city. If you observe for only 10 minutes (a small sample), a few extra violations now and then are normal fluctuation. But if you observe for a whole month, recording every second (a very large sample), tiny fluctuations in the "daily violation rate" will all show up as "abnormal" on a traditional control chart. Laney's method takes into account, when calculating the control limits, the extra variation that naturally comes with such large-scale observation, bringing the limits back to a reasonable range.

Practical Application

Case: monitoring the tablet rejection rate of an automatic metal detector

Suppose your plant checks 5,000 tablets per batch and records the number rejected by the metal detector. If a traditional p-chart is used:

  • The control limits are extremely narrow (because n=5,000 is large); the UCL may be only 0.3%
  • Normal batch-to-batch variation (for example 0.1% to 0.5%) causes many batches to be flagged as OOC (Out of Control)
  • The plant's investigation team will be worn out investigating these "false alarms", wasting a great deal of resources

By switching to a Laney's p'-chart or an I/MR chart (using the rejection rate of each batch as an individual value), the control limits become more reasonable and real process shifts will be correctly identified.

Performance of Control Charts: Average Run Length (ARL)

Original Text

The ability of a control chart to detect a shift in the process is measured by the average number of sample periods before the shift is detected. This is called the Average Run Length (ARL). When the process has not changed (a shift of 0), it is desirable to have a large ARL to keep the false-alarm rate low. However, if the process has shifted, it is desirable to have a small ARL to detect the change quickly. ARL is most important in CPV, when it is desirable to detect a significant process change within a short period of time. The ARL can be used to help select the sample size and frequency of sampling. Tables of ARLs comparing different control chart types are available and can also be calculated by software.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Average Run Length, ARL: The average number of sampling points that must be observed after a process shift occurs before the control chart detects it (that is, before the first point falls outside the control limits).

  • ARL0 (process in control): The larger this value, the better, as it means false alarms are not frequent. The ARL0 of a Shewhart chart is 370 (on average, one false alarm every 370 sampling points)
  • ARL1 (process has shifted): The smaller this value, the better, as it means problems are detected quickly. For example, for a 1σ shift the ARL1 of a Shewhart chart is ≈ 44, while the ARL1 of an EWMA chart is ≈ 10

Formula

Concept of ARL calculation for a Shewhart control chart:

ARL = 1 / P(point falls outside the control limits)

Process in control (±3σ limits):
  P(out of limits) = 0.0027 (the normal distribution area beyond ±3σ)
  ARL_0 = 1 / 0.0027 ≈ 370

Process shifted by 1σ:
  P(out of limits) ≈ 0.0228
  ARL_1 = 1 / 0.0228 ≈ 44

Process shifted by 2σ:
  P(out of limits) ≈ 0.1587
  ARL_1 = 1 / 0.1587 ≈ 6.3

Key Notes

The link between ARL and sampling strategy:

  • ARL directly affects your decision on sampling frequency. If ARL1 = 44, you have to wait an average of 44 sampling points to detect a 1σ shift. With one sample per month, it could take nearly 4 years to find it!
  • This is also why TR 60 points out that ARL is most important in the CPV stage: the goal of CPV is to detect process changes in time, not to deal with them only after large quantities of nonconforming product have been produced
  • In practice, an ARL comparison table can be used to select the most suitable control chart type and sampling scheme

Analogy

ARL is like the sensitivity setting of a smoke detector:

  • You do not want it to sound when there is no fire (the larger ARL0, the better = not so sensitive that it gives frequent false alarms)
  • But you also want it to sound quickly when there is a real fire (the smaller ARL1, the better = real problems are detected quickly)
  • Different control charts are like different brands of detector: some react quickly to a big fire but are insensitive to smoldering (Shewhart), while others can detect even a small flame (EWMA/CUSUM)

  2. How can the choice of control chart type (for example, switching to EWMA) reduce ARL1 without lowering ARL0 so much that too many false alarms occur?
