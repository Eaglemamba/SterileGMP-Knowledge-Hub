1.0 Introduction

The design and implementation of an environmental monitoring (EM) program allows the confirmation that controls to minimize the risk associated with processes, people, equipment, utilities, and facilities are designed correctly and continue to be effective in the manufacture of sterile pharmaceutical products. As such, it forms an important part of the contamination control strategy (CCS).

PDA first published Technical Report No. 13 in 1990 and revised it two previous times, 2001 and 2014. This third revision intends to align the content of the document with current industry trends and regulatory expectations. Specifically, it will provide additional guidance and focus on the:

  • Use of quality risk management (QRM) principles to establish a robust EM program by suggesting practical step-by-step approaches
  • Increased expectations concerning data management and data integrity
  • Prerequisites to an effective EM program
  • Qualification of controlled environments and how to maintain them in a qualified state
  • Rapid microbiological methods (RMM) or automated monitoring systems, since becoming more and more convenient, performant and accepted, or even recommended, by regulatory bodies

Members of the task force who developed this technical report represent or provide expertise to global companies to ensure that the methods, terminology, and practices reflect not only U.S., but also international procedures. Technical peer reviews were completed by prominent EM scientists.

Although this TR has been updated to ensure its alignment with existing current regulations and guidelines, current and/or newly updated regulations or guidelines should be consulted when implementing or changing an environmental monitoring program. This TR should be viewed as technical guidance; it is not intended to replace existing or future regulations, nor should it be considered an all-inclusive document.

1.1 Purpose

This document was created to aid in the establishment of an environmental monitoring program that is robust, meaningful, and practical whilst embracing innovation and the principles of quality risk management. This revision updates microbiological and total airborne particulate control concepts and principles as they relate to facilities involved in the manufacture of sterile pharmaceutical products and other designated cleanroom environments.

1.2 Scope

TR-13 serves as a resource applicable for controlled environments in general; however, its primary focus is environmental monitoring for sterile product manufacturing.

This document discusses international standards and regulatory guidances, elements of an EM program, and EM application. Current (at the time of publication) guidelines for typical EM levels and frequencies are presented in Section 3.0 and levels for pharmaceutical water are covered in Section 6.8.

A comprehensive environmental monitoring program should demonstrate the effectiveness of a solid CCS focusing on:

  • Sound facility design, including barrier systems (e.g., isolators and RABS), operation and maintenance
  • Established documentation systems
  • Qualified sanitization, disinfection, and decontamination procedures
  • Reliable process controls
  • Good housekeeping practices
  • Effective area access controls
  • Consistent sample collection and analysis
  • Effective training, certification or qualification, and personnel evaluation programs
  • Quality assurance and control of materials, facilities, and equipment
  • Continuous improvement and quality risk management programs

Bulk and intermediate product bioburden monitoring (except for pharmaceutical water) is not considered part of all EM programs and is therefore outside of the scope of this technical report. For more specifics on this topic, refer to PDA Technical Report No. 69.

Interpretation and Application

The Core Positioning of TR13

TR13 is PDA's most established and most widely cited guidance on environmental monitoring. Since its first edition in 1990 it has been revised three times (2001, 2014, 2022), and it is a key reference for the global sterile manufacturing industry when establishing an EM program.

The 2022 revision places particular emphasis on alignment with EU GMP Annex 1 (2022). Annex 1 requires companies, for the first time, to establish a written CCS (contamination control strategy), and TR13 states clearly that EM is an important component of the CCS.

The Five Key Points of the 2022 Revision

Compared with the 2014 edition, this revision adds or strengthens the following:

  • Practical QRM: no longer just the concept of "applying risk management", but a step-by-step method
  • Data integrity: responds to the strong attention FDA and EMA have given to data integrity in recent years
  • EM prerequisites: facilities, equipment and personnel must first be confirmed as qualified before EM can start
  • Maintaining environmental qualification: not only the initial qualification, but also maintaining the qualified state
  • RMM (rapid methods): acknowledges that rapid microbiological methods are accepted by regulators and encourages their adoption

Analogy: EM is like a "health check-up" for the plant

Environmental monitoring is like a regular "health check-up" for the manufacturing environment: measuring blood pressure (particle counts), running blood tests (microbial sampling), taking X-rays (trend analysis). The check-up itself does not cure anything, but it detects abnormalities early and helps prevent "disease" (product contamination).

The CCS is the whole "preventive medicine program", and EM is the "periodic examination" part of it.

The Ten CCS-Focused Elements of the Scope

TR13 Section 1.2 lists ten aspects in which an EM program must demonstrate effectiveness; these elements form the backbone of the whole report:

  • Facility design → Section 4 (fundamentals of environmental control)
  • Disinfection program → read together with TR70
  • Sampling and analysis → Sections 5-6 (sampling methods)
  • Trend analysis → Section 8 (data management)
  • Personnel training → Section 4 (personnel behavior requirements)

Scope Exclusion: Bioburden Monitoring

TR13 explicitly excludes bioburden monitoring of API intermediates (except for pharmaceutical water). That topic is covered specifically by PDA TR69 (Bioburden and Biofilm Management). When establishing an EM program, the boundary needs to be clearly defined.

Key Terms Explained

Action Limit vs. Alert Level: The Difference Asked About Most Often

This question comes up in almost every audit, and it is the cornerstone of EM data management:

  • Alert Level: An early warning. When exceeded, the result needs "attention and follow-up" but does not necessarily require corrective action. Think of it as the yellow light on a dashboard.
  • Action Limit: Exceeding it means the process has departed from its normal range, and an investigation and corrective action must be initiated. Think of it as the red light.

Both are set from historical data using statistical methods, not by experience alone. EU GMP Annex 1 Sections 9.27-9.30 set out clear requirements on this.

Analogy: "Alert" and "Action" Values for Blood Pressure

Think of the Alert Level as a blood pressure reading on a health check report flagged as "slightly high, follow up" (e.g., 130/85 mmHg): the doctor makes a note but does not necessarily prescribe medication. The Action Limit is like a confirmed diagnosis of hypertension (e.g., 140/90 mmHg): medication and follow-up visits are required.

EM follows the same logic: an Alert triggers attention, and an Action triggers action.

CCS (Contamination Control Strategy): The Most Important New Concept in the 2022 Version

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) lists the CCS as a mandatory requirement for the first time (Sections 2.4-2.7). TR13 positions EM as a key component of the CCS:

  • The CCS is a comprehensive contamination control planning document
  • EM is the tool in the CCS for "verifying that control measures remain effective"
  • The CCS covers facility design, personnel controls, disinfection, process controls and more
  • EM data provide objective evidence of CCS effectiveness

Further reading: PDA TR90 (CCS Development in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing) provides more complete guidance on establishing a CCS.

Isolator vs. RABS: Choosing a Barrier Technology

TR13 defines two main barrier systems, both of which are Grade A/ISO 5 environments:

  • Isolator: Fully enclosed isolation in which personnel have no direct access to the interior. It provides the highest level of sterility assurance. It is further divided into Closed (fully sealed) and Open (with engineered openings).
  • RABS: A restricted access barrier with physical separation, but not as completely enclosed as an Isolator. It is divided into Open RABS (with vents connecting to the background environment) and Closed RABS (air recirculates within the cabinet).

The choice of technology directly affects the EM monitoring strategy, sampling point configuration and limit setting.

The Three Levels of Microbial Identification

TR13 distinguishes three levels of depth in microbial analysis:

  • Characterization: Morphology + Gram stain + key diagnostic tests. Example: "Gram-positive, coagulase-negative cocci"
  • Classification: Placing organisms into taxonomic groups based on similarity
  • Identification: Determining the genus and species (e.g., Staphylococcus epidermidis)

The required depth of identification differs by environmental grade: Grade A/B typically requires full Identification, while Characterization may be sufficient for Grade C/D.

At Rest vs. In Operation: Why Two States?

"At Rest" is the baseline capability of the environment itself: equipment installed but no personnel and no production, like the cleanliness of a restaurant kitchen before opening. "In Operation" is performance during actual operation: people moving about and materials flowing, like the kitchen during the lunch rush.

EU GMP Annex 1 Tables 3-4 set different particle limits for each state. The At Rest limits are stricter and are used to verify the capability of the HVAC system; the In Operation limits reflect actual manufacturing conditions.

2.1 Abbreviations

Abbreviations Quick Reference and Additional Notes

Media Abbreviations: Essential Knowledge for the Microbiology Laboratory

  • TSA / SCDA: Tryptic soy agar (soybean-casein digest agar), used for total aerobic count (bacteria), typically incubated at 30-35°C
  • SDA: Sabouraud dextrose agar, used specifically for counting fungi (molds and yeasts), typically incubated at 20-25°C

Environmental monitoring normally uses both TSA and SDA, to count bacteria and fungi respectively.

VBNC: An Overlooked Microbial Risk

Viable But Not Culturable is a hot topic in recent microbiology. Under stress conditions (such as disinfectants or low nutrients), some microorganisms enter a VBNC state: they are still alive but cannot grow on conventional culture media.

This means conventional CFU counts may underestimate the actual microbial population. TR13 mentions VBNC, suggesting that RMM (rapid microbiological methods) may become a more reliable alternative in the future.

Risk Management Tools: FMEA vs. HACCP

  • FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis): Systematically assesses the possible failure modes, causes and effects of each process step and ranks them by RPN (risk priority number). Commonly used for EM sampling point selection.
  • HACCP (hazard analysis and critical control points): Originating in the food industry, it identifies the critical control points in a process. In EM it is used to decide which locations and time points are the highest-risk monitoring points.

Both are tools recommended for quality risk management in ICH Q9, and TR13 encourages their use in designing EM programs.

CRR (Contamination Recovery Rate): Why Does It Matter?

CRR expresses the proportion of microorganisms that a sampling method can actually recover. If a sampling method has a CRR of only 50%, then when a surface carries 100 CFU you will detect only 50 CFU.

Understanding the limits of CRR is necessary to interpret EM data correctly: a low CFU reading does not necessarily mean the environment is clean; it may reflect low recovery efficiency.

3.0 Environmental Classifications: Regulatory Expectations

Overview — Regulatory Bodies Covered

An EM program should be designed and implemented in conformance with the requirements of the government agencies regulating the manufacturing site as well as requirements of international standard-setting organizations. If the intent is to serve multinational markets, the most stringent requirements should be evaluated as the basis of an EM program.

This section compares regulations and guidance instituted for EM by these authorities:

  • International Organization for Standardization (ISO)
  • U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)
  • U.S. Pharmacopeia (USP)
  • European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur.)
  • European Medicines Agency (EMA)
  • Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan (MHLW)
  • Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP)
  • Chinese Pharmacopeia (ChP)
  • Pharmaceutical Inspection Convention (PIC) and Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S)
  • World Health Organization (WHO)

Although the regulations and guidelines of these organizations are similar in many respects, there are important differences among them in terms of the information each provides, particularly with respect to cleanroom classifications.

Commentary: Regulatory Overview

This section is the core regulatory comparison chapter of TR13. Sterile manufacturers in Taiwan or elsewhere in Asia usually have to meet the requirements of several regulatory systems at once (for example, EU Annex 1 for export to Europe, the FDA Guidance for export to the US, and MHLW guidelines for export to Japan).

Key principle: If a product is sold in multiple markets, the EM program should be designed to the "most stringent regulatory requirement". This means the regulations of every target market must be cross-checked when building the monitoring program.

TR13 condenses the requirements of ten major regulatory bodies and standards into two comparison tables, which are a practical tool for EM personnel at pharmaceutical companies worldwide.

Practical Reminder

China (ChP) also requires an ISO 8 cleanroom (at rest) for non-sterile products, which is stricter than most other regulations. EU GMP requires ISO 8 for non-sterile manufacturing only for inhalants. Pay particular attention to differences like this when designing an EM program.

ISO 14644-1 Classifications

The most commonly accepted international cleanroom standard related to total airborne particulates is ISO 14644-1:2015 Cleanrooms and Associated Controlled Environments — Part 1: Classification of Air Cleanliness by Particle Concentration.

ISO class designations are based on the number of particles greater than a specified size (0.1–5 μm) per cubic meter of air sampled. ISO 14644-1 defines classes from 1 to 9, with ISO 1 being the cleanest. ISO classes 5 through 8 are used in the pharmaceutical industry for sterile-product manufacture.

ISO does not specify:

  • The operating state (as built, at rest, or in operation)
  • The particle-size thresholds to be employed
  • Distinction between viable and nonviable particles

The following activities are generally expected in each ISO class:

  • ISO 5: Aseptic processing zone; sterile product and/or packaging component exposed; unidirectional airflow required
  • ISO 7: Area immediately surrounding the aseptic processing zone
  • ISO 8: Non-sterile formulation, materials, and component preparation; filling area for terminally sterilized product

Commentary: ISO Classification and Practical Application

ISO 14644-1 is the "common language" of cleanroom classification worldwide, but it is only a framework. It tells you "how to measure" and "what the numerical upper limits are", but it does not tell you:

  • In which state to measure (at rest / in operation)
  • Which particle size to measure (0.5 μm or 5 μm)
  • Whether the particles are viable (viable vs. nonviable)

These details are supplemented by each country's regulations. This is why, even for the same ISO 5, the specific requirements of the FDA and the EU can differ.

ISO vs. EU Grade Correspondence

EU/PIC/S/WHO use the letter-based Grades A-D, and each Grade corresponds to two ISO classes, one for at rest and one for in operation:

  • Grade A = ISO 5 at rest & in operation (ISO 4.8 when counted at ≥5 μm)
  • Grade B = ISO 5 at rest, ISO 7 in operation
  • Grade C = ISO 7 at rest, ISO 8 in operation
  • Grade D = ISO 8 at rest, in operation not defined

This means a Grade B cleanroom must meet ISO 5 when no operators are present (as clean as Grade A!), but only needs to maintain ISO 7 when people are working in it.

EU, PIC/S, WHO & Japan Classifications

The EU, PIC/S members, and WHO use an alphabetic classification of Grades A–D. For each grade, a ≥0.5 μm and ≥5 μm particle-count limit is specified. These limits are set for both at-rest and in-operation states.

Japan has adopted similar classifications in its Guidance on the Manufacture of Sterile Pharmaceutical Products by Aseptic Processing. One minor difference: Japan refers to the Grade A classification as ISO 5 rather than ISO 4.8, although the limit for particles ≥5 μm technically corresponds to ISO 4.8.

For the United States, the FDA’s 2004 Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing and USP <1116> both discuss the application and environmental requirements for cleanrooms. These include ISO classes 5–8 and their corresponding Federal Standard 209E classes (e.g., ISO 5/Class 100). Limits are set only for particles ≥0.5 μm. Both are silent on the state of the area to be tested but clearly imply areas to be "in operation."

Commentary: Differences Between Regulatory Systems

Is Grade A ISO 5 or ISO 4.8?

EU Annex 1 sets the ≥5 μm particle limit for Grade A at 20 particles/m³ (the table shows 29 particles/m³ at rest for Grade B). Strictly speaking, this limit corresponds to ISO 4.8 rather than ISO 5. Japan MHLW, however, simply calls it ISO 5. This difference does not affect equipment design in practice, but be careful with the terminology when drafting regulatory documents.

Special features of US FDA/USP:

  • Only particle limits at ≥0.5 μm are specified; measurement at ≥5 μm is not required
  • The test state (at rest / in operation) is not clearly defined, but in operation is implied
  • The older Federal Standard 209E equivalents are still retained (e.g., Class 100 = ISO 5)

Special features of EU/PIC/S:

  • Both ≥0.5 μm and ≥5 μm particle sizes are specified
  • At rest and in operation limits are defined separately for each Grade
  • The in operation limit for Grade D is not defined (it depends on the operation)

Analogy: Cleanroom Classification Is Like Hotel Star Ratings

ISO 14644-1 is like an "international hotel star standard". It defines a general framework for how clean a five-star hotel should be. But each country's tourism bureau can add its own requirements on top of this framework:

  • US FDA: Checks only the dust in guest rooms (≥0.5 μm) and does not check large particles
  • EU: Checks both dust and debris (≥0.5 μm + ≥5.0 μm), and inspects twice: "before guests check in" and "while guests are using the room"
  • Japan: The standard is almost the same as the EU, but the wording in documents differs slightly

This is why the same cleanroom can have a different compliance status under different regulations.

Commentary: Interpreting Table 3.0-2

The Unique Approach of USP <1116>: Contamination Recovery Rate (CRR)

All other regulations use a maximum CFU per sample as the action limit, but USP <1116> takes a statistical approach:

  • ISO 5: fewer than 1% of samples show microbial contamination
  • ISO 7: fewer than 5% of samples show microbial contamination
  • ISO 8: fewer than 10% of samples show microbial contamination

The advantage of this approach is that it reflects the overall trend rather than a single event. However, because other regulations still use CFU limits, most companies apply both evaluation methods in practice.

Regulatory differences in monitoring frequency:

  • Grade A/ISO 5: All regulations consistently require monitoring each shift
  • Grades C, D: The EU takes a risk-based approach, while Japan MHLW gives explicit frequencies (twice/week or once/week); JP XVI has the same requirement as MHLW
  • Isolators: USP <1116> gives separate frequency recommendations for isolators (critical zone once daily, surrounding area once monthly)

Gaps in surface and personnel monitoring:

  • The FDA does not specify surface limits, which is an obvious gap
  • No regulation clearly specifies numerical limits for gowns
  • Glove limits apply only to Grades A and B; Grades C/D are not required

Airflow & Pressure Differentials

Common factors among the various guidelines include the requirement that ISO 5 areas, where aseptic conditions must be maintained, typically require unidirectional airflow.

ISO 14644-1 describes unidirectional flow as 0.45 meters per second (90 ft/min) ±20% measured 150–300 mm from the supply filter face. EU Annex 1, though, recommends this measurement be taken as close to the work surface as practical.

All regulatory authorities recommend an air pressurization scheme to ensure airflow from the cleaner zone to the less-clean zone. The broadly acceptable guidance value is a differential pressure (ΔP) of 10–15 Pa (0.04–0.06 in H2O) between zones of differing classes.

Several guidance documents describe the use of air locks to maintain differential pressures while doors are used between different Grades. Where an open ISO 5 unidirectional zone is placed within an ISO 7 background, this pressure differential is not required.

Commentary: Airflow and Pressure Differential Requirements

Unidirectional Airflow:

  • ISO 5 areas must use unidirectional airflow
  • Standard air velocity: 0.45 m/s ±20% (i.e., 0.36-0.54 m/s)
  • ISO 14644-1 requires measurement 150-300 mm from the HEPA filter face
  • EU Annex 1 recommends measuring as close to the working level as possible, which is a practical difference

Pressure differential (ΔP):

  • A pressure differential of 10-15 Pa must be maintained between cleanrooms of different classifications
  • Airflow direction: from the cleaner area to the less clean area
  • Use airlocks to maintain the pressure differential when doors are opened and closed
  • Exception: No pressure differential is needed when an open ISO 5 laminar air flow (LAF) hood is installed in an ISO 7 background environment

Shop-Floor Scenario

Imagine a sterile filling plant:

  • LAF above the filling line (ISO 5 / Grade A): Unidirectional airflow at 0.45 m/s works like an "air curtain", blowing clean air directly onto the product exposure zone and keeping outside particles out.
  • Filling room (ISO 7 / Grade B): This is the "background environment" of the LAF, and no pressure differential is needed between it and the LAF (because the LAF airflow itself is the isolation mechanism).
  • Corridor (ISO 8 / Grade C): A pressure differential of 10-15 Pa must be maintained between the filling room and the corridor, so that air does not flow back from the corridor into the filling room when the door is opened.
  • Personnel gowning room (airlock): Designed as an "airlock" so that personnel entering and exiting do not disrupt the pressure differential of the adjacent cleanrooms.

Section 3.0 Summary

Key Regulatory Differences

  • ISO 14644-1 provides the classification framework; each regulator adds its own requirements for operating state, particle sizes, and viable limits.
  • EU/PIC/S/WHO use Grade A–D with both at-rest and in-operation limits; FDA/USP use ISO classes with implied in-operation testing only.
  • FDA/USP specify only ≥0.5 μm particles; EU/Japan also specify ≥5.0 μm.
  • USP <1116> uniquely uses Contamination Recovery Rate instead of CFU limits.

Key Practical Points

  • For sales in multiple markets, base the EM design on the most stringent regulation.
  • Grade A = ISO 5（at rest & in operation），Grade B = ISO 5 at rest / ISO 7 in operation。
  • ISO 5 areas: unidirectional airflow 0.45 m/s ±20%, with a pressure differential of 10-15 Pa between classifications.
  • The FDA does not specify numerical surface or personnel limits, but the EU/Japan have explicit values.
  • China ChP also requires ISO 8 (at rest) for non-sterile products, which needs particular attention.

4.0 Environmental Monitoring Qualification of Cleanrooms

Cleanroom Classification & Initial Qualification

Clean areas for the manufacture of sterile products are classified according to the required characteristics of the environment. Each manufacturing operation requires an appropriate environmental cleanliness level in the operational state to minimize the risks of particulate or microbial contamination of the product or materials being handled.

For new areas, the initial qualification should be performed in a prospective mode. For existing facilities that require a repeat of the initial qualification, the requalification can be performed in concurrence with routine production activities if historical data of existing rooms are shown to be in a good state of control. Release of product should await final release of the cleanroom qualification exercise.

To meet the in-operation conditions, these areas should be designed to reach certain specified air-cleanliness levels in the "at rest" occupancy state. Section 9.0 (Appendix 1) provides additional guidance to ensure and verify that critical system components meet user requirements and are properly identified, installed, and calibrated (typically covered within the installation qualification (IQ) and operational qualification (OQ) phases of cleanroom qualification).

To prepare the cleanroom for classification, air filters should be evaluated for cleanliness and replaced, if necessary, especially in new or modified facilities where excess construction debris or dust may cause clogging. Cleaning and disinfection protocols should be defined and performed prior to conducting qualification.

TR13 Section 4.0 / Overview

This section covers the key qualification process that takes a cleanroom from "built" to "ready for use". Imagine you have built a new factory: it cannot start producing the moment construction ends. It must pass the "acceptance trilogy" (IQ / OQ / PQ) to confirm that the environment is genuinely clean enough.

Factory Acceptance Analogy

Think of cleanroom qualification as the "handover inspection of a new house":

  • IQ (Installation Qualification) = checking that the plumbing, wiring, doors and windows are installed correctly: confirming that the HEPA filters, HVAC system and instrument calibration are all in place.
  • OQ (Operational Qualification) = switching on all the equipment and testing its functions: confirming that the HVAC can achieve the specified cleanliness grade.
  • PQ (Performance Qualification) = simulating daily living to see whether the house is usable: testing continuously under real production conditions to confirm the environment consistently meets requirements.

TR13 stresses that a new facility must be qualified prospectively; you cannot start production first and qualify afterwards. For an existing facility with good historical data, requalification may run concurrently with routine production, but product release must wait until qualification is complete.

IQ / OQ Phases & At-Rest vs In-Operation

At-Rest & In-Operation Testing

Performance of the total airborne particulate cleanroom classification described in ISO 14644-1 would confirm the air-cleanliness levels in the at-rest occupancy state. This initial exercise should be supplemented by an at-rest qualification using viable tests.

Viable at-rest locations might focus on more specific aspects related to the actual design of the cleanroom and can be documented as part of the EM risk assessment (see Section 5.2). Although human activity and material transfers (the primary sources of microbial contamination) are not evaluated in the at-rest state, the associated sampling locations will provide valuable information about the intrinsic microbial state-of-control of the cleanroom prior to starting the qualification under in-operation conditions.

Specifications applied for viable qualification at rest might be more stringent than those applicable for in-operation conditions. All necessary measures should be taken to minimize the impact of personnel during at-rest monitoring (e.g., surface monitoring at the start of the run, only EM technician present, delay timers on automated samplers).

Subsequently, it should be confirmed that the cleanroom can maintain operational conditions when executing the activities foreseen within the area. The method detailed in ISO 14644-1 can be used to confirm the total airborne particulate cleanroom classification for the in-operation status. This initial qualification is normally performed by simulating manufacturing activities, ensuring that at a certain stage the maximum number of personnel and the worst-case level of activities are covered.

The runs described in this section may form part of the OQ of the cleanroom. Data obtained during this stage should be used as part of the EM risk assessment to define routine sampling locations. For both total airborne particulate runs (in the at-rest and in-operational conditions), one run conducted according to ISO 14644-1 is considered sufficient.

At-Rest vs In-Operation / Analysis

It is important to understand these two states. At-rest tests the cleanroom's "hardware baseline": can the HVAC, HEPA filters and airflow design keep the room clean on their own? In-operation adds the human factor. People are the biggest source of microbial contamination, so you must confirm that the environment still meets requirements even in the busiest production state.

An important reminder from TR13: during at-rest testing, keep personnel interference to a minimum, for example by using a delayed-start sampler so that technicians can leave the room before sampling begins.

Performance Qualification (PQ) & Extended PQ

PQ Execution & Requirements

A performance qualification (PQ) should follow the successful completion of the OQ. However, it may commence before the full OQ results are available, based on preliminary results (business risk approach). If this approach is chosen, the PQ protocol should list the results which should be available before the PQ start.

PQ typically consists of three consecutive runs during routine (in-operation) conditions. In batch-related production rooms, a run would typically comprise a batch covering all applicable shifts and activities. In supporting areas, a run should cover all relevant activities to be performed within that area. Simulating activities during performance qualification might reduce the total runtime (i.e., by executing them in a condensed period of time).

The sampling plan should be based on the viable and total airborne particulate sample locations defined as part of the routine monitoring (EM risk assessment) augmented with additional EM locations to cover remote parts of the room in order to confirm and document that the complete cleanroom environment is in control. This PQ can be performed during APS or activities can be simulated.

There is a business risk, however, performing PQ during APS since the facility is not operating in a fully qualified environment and deficiencies could be detected that first require corrective measures. Consequently, at a certain stage, the maximum number of personnel and the worst-case level of activities need to be covered during one or more PQ runs. The maximum limits for microbial and total airborne particulate contamination during qualification should correspond to the action limits applied during routine monitoring.

Extended PQ

Following the initial PQ, an extended PQ may be conducted for a defined time period based on the routine sampling program or continuation of extended sampling. The intent of this process would be to account for conditions which may not have been covered in the initial PQ (e.g., seasonal variation of microbes, establishment of appropriate alert levels, selection of microorganisms for disinfectant efficacy tests, additional evaluation of the periodic cleaning program, etc.).

Production or room configuration changes with a possible impact on EM (e.g., gowning procedures, cleaning set-up, layout, material or personnel flow, or other areas) should include a new assessment on the qualified status of the cleanroom and may require requalification as part of the change.

PQ Three Consecutive Runs / In-Depth Interpretation

Core PQ Requirements

  • Three Consecutive Runs: demonstrating that the environment meets requirements not "occasionally" but "consistently".
  • Batch-related rooms: each run = one complete batch, covering all shifts and activities.
  • Support areas: each run must cover all relevant activities in that area.
  • Sample sites = routine monitoring sites + additional sites (covering remote corners of the room).
  • PQ limits = the Action Limits used in routine monitoring (not looser criteria).

Analogy: A Restaurant Soft Opening

PQ is like a restaurant's "soft opening": it is not an empty-room equipment test (that is OQ), but a real trial with chefs at work, guests seated and the whole workflow running for three days. Only if the hygiene inspections pass on all three consecutive days does the restaurant open officially. Extended PQ is like continuing to observe for several months after the soft opening, to see whether seasonal changes, such as more insects in summer or dry indoor heating in winter, affect the hygiene status.

When Is Requalification Needed?

The following changes may trigger requalification:

  • Changes to gowning procedures
  • Adjustments to cleaning and disinfection procedures
  • Changes to room layout or equipment reconfiguration
  • Changes to personnel flow or material flow paths

Key principle: any change that could affect EM results requires a re-evaluation of the cleanroom's qualification status.

5.0 Environmental Monitoring Program

EM Program Fundamentals

An EM program (including EMPQ) should be in conformance with current GMP, which includes appropriate training and authority of personnel involved in implementing the EM program. Equipment used should be qualified and calibrated, and microbiological growth media should be properly qualified, prepared, and tested. In addition, all operational procedures should be written, approved, and followed with appropriate controls to support their use. The methods selected should be justified and qualified for use as appropriate.

Cleaning and disinfection of the manufacturing area, and material handling and gowning processes, as well as appropriate aseptic behavior of the personnel, are key components of an effective contamination control strategy.

Selection of sampling methods (see Section 6.0), sampling sites, and sampling frequencies should be based on the principles of risk management according to ICH Q9: Quality Risk Management to ensure that the respective production area is under control and meets regulatory requirements.

Establishment of alert levels and action limits may be based on individual sample sites or groups of related sample sites with a similar design or activities. Data obtained are subject to continual review, and alert and action decisions are made by qualified personnel. A documentation system should be in place for identifying and investigating excursions and adverse trends and the effectiveness of the corrective actions taken. All data should be documented and periodically trended.

EM Program Overview / Commentary

Section 5.0 defines the "big picture" of the environmental monitoring program. TR13 stresses that EM is not simply "periodic sampling" but a complete system that includes:

  • Personnel: appropriately trained and authorized
  • Equipment: qualified and calibrated
  • Media: qualified, prepared and tested
  • Procedures: documented, approved and executed under control
  • Methods: justified and validated

The Three Pillars of an EM Program

  1. Cleaning and disinfection (Section 5.1): reduces the microbial baseline
  2. Risk-based sample site selection (Section 5.2): monitoring in the right places
  3. Data trending: continuously reviewing alert/action data to drive improvement

Note that TR13 cites ICH Q9 as the foundation of its risk management framework, which is fully consistent with the contamination control strategy (CCS) emphasized in EU GMP Annex 1 (2022).

5.1 Cleaning, Sanitization or Disinfection and Decontamination

Cleaning & Disinfection in the EM Context

EM data demonstrates the effectiveness of the cleaning and disinfection program. The qualification of cleaning and disinfection procedures should demonstrate microbial reduction and maintenance of a microbiological state of control and should provide confidence in the effectiveness of the procedure.

This typically includes laboratory surface challenge or carrier studies for contact time and bioburden reduction, supplemented by in-situ studies.

Both USP <1072> and PDA Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities provide sufficient details of the requirements and in-vitro surface challenge methodology for qualification of the efficacy of the agent used.

Cleaning and Disinfection in Relation to EM / Commentary

The core message of Section 5.1 is simple but profound: EM data is the most direct evidence of the effectiveness of cleaning and disinfection.

Two-Part Verification of Cleaning and Disinfection Qualification

  • Laboratory Challenge: inoculating surfaces with known organisms in the laboratory to test the disinfectant's antimicrobial efficacy and contact time.
  • In-situ Studies: sampling in the actual manufacturing environment to verify how well the cleaning and disinfection procedures work under real conditions.

You need both: laboratory data shows the process is "effective in theory", while in-situ data shows it is "effective in practice".

TR13 specifically cites PDA TR70 and USP <1072> as methodological references for qualifying cleaning and disinfection. If you are responsible for an EM program, make sure you are familiar with both documents.

5.2 EM Sample Site Selection Using Risk Management Principles

Overview of Risk-Based Site Selection

Sample site selection, selection of the appropriate sampling methods, sampling volumes, and monitoring frequencies are critical components of contamination control within a facility. However, EM risk assessments are only intended to define the EM program as a part of the overall contamination control strategy.

This section addresses how to create and how to maintain an EM program applying a lifecycle approach (Section 5.2.1) and the use of quality risk management principles to identify and select sites for EM of classified environments including ISO 5-8/Grades A-D as well as personnel monitoring (Section 5.2.2). Sections 6.8 and 6.9 discuss utilities (WFI/Compressed Air, Nitrogen, etc.).

Risk-Based Sampling / Core Concept

Section 5.2 is one of the most methodologically in-depth sections of TR13. It answers a fundamental question: "Where do we sample?" The answer comes not from experience or habit but from a systematic risk assessment.

TR13 divides this process into two frameworks:

  • 5.2.1 Lifecycle Approach: "lifecycle management" of the EM program
  • 5.2.2 QRM Process: carrying out the risk assessment in practice with FMEA / HACCP / BioPhorum tools

5.2.1 Lifecycle Approach to an Environmental Monitoring Program

Lifecycle Model for EM Programs

The creation and maintenance of EM programs are best achieved using a lifecycle approach. This lifecycle approach encompasses the evaluation and definition of suitable sample sites, sample types, sampling volumes, and sample frequency for microbiological monitoring that are required before starting up a new facility, area, or room.

These sample sites are used to perform an EM performance qualification (PQ). Based on the PQ results, adaptation (mainly reduction or addition) of sample sites may be necessary before setting up the sample plan for routine and, if applicable, batch-related EM.

The impact of any major change (e.g., reconstruction, process change, or equipment relocation) needs to be evaluated and the affected area may need to be requalified.

A final EM evaluation may be necessary for decommissioning a room, isolator, or facility to demonstrate environmental control through to the end.

The model presented in Figure 5.2.1-1 outlines the general flow of the lifecycle approach of an EM program. Each step defined is designed to ensure that once an EM program is developed, it is carefully reviewed during the lifecycle of the area, product, or process to affirm that a state of control is maintained. A periodic reevaluation of the EM program (e.g., in response to an adverse trend report) may be necessary.

Lifecycle Approach / In-Depth Interpretation

The EM Program Lifecycle: Five Stages

  1. Design stage: define sample sites, sample types, sample volumes and sampling frequency
  2. EMPQ stage: perform the environmental monitoring performance qualification
  3. Routine monitoring stage: adjust (add or remove) sample sites based on PQ results and establish the routine monitoring program
  4. Change management: major changes (reconstruction, process changes, equipment relocation) trigger re-evaluation
  5. Decommissioning stage: a final EM assessment before the facility is taken out of service, demonstrating that control was maintained to the end

Analogy: Regular Vehicle Inspections

The lifecycle approach is like a car's "lifetime maintenance plan":

  • New-car delivery inspection = EMPQ (confirming the baseline)
  • Regular servicing = routine EM (continuous monitoring)
  • Re-inspection after modifications = requalification after changes
  • Final inspection before scrapping = decommissioning EM assessment

The point is that qualifying once does not make you safe forever; you must keep evaluating and keep adjusting.

Pay particular attention to the "periodic reassessment" mentioned in TR13: even without major changes, the EM program should be reviewed periodically (for example in response to adverse trend reports) to ensure the monitoring strategy remains appropriate.

5.2.2 Initiation of Quality Risk Management Process

Setting Up the Risk Assessment

Prior to executing a risk assessment, several elements must be defined: scope and objective, risk question or problem statement, risk management tool to be applied, and risk-ranking criteria.

To conduct an EM risk assessment, a risk facilitator should be assigned and a multidisciplinary team should be assembled representing the areas of microbiology, contamination control and hygiene, quality engineering, facilities and utilities, quality control, quality assurance, and manufacturing.

The scope and objective of the risk assessment is defined as the area of the facility or process under assessment. The scope can involve just one room, an isolator, or a larger manufacturing area. Independent of the scope, however, the manufacturing process associated with the area in scope is key to performing the risk assessment.

Thorough process understanding and process mapping is necessary to evaluate the associated risk of contamination at a specific position within a cleanroom. Prior to initiating the risk assessment, process mapping should be conducted on the shop/manufacturing floor using room layouts and indicating equipment locations, people flow, material flow, and waste flow in the facility.

The assessment team will select the most appropriate risk management tool among the different methods applicable, e.g., Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA), Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point (HACCP), among others. One risk assessment tool, developed by a collaborative of pharmaceutical industry representatives through BioPhorum, addresses the wide range of practices within the industry, allowing harmonization as well as compliance with regulatory guidelines. This toolkit provides an easy to use, step-by-step guidance for establishing EM programs.

QRM Process Initiation / Commentary

Four Essential Elements Before a Risk Assessment

  1. Scope & Objective: which area/process is being assessed
  2. Risk Question: what question needs to be answered
  3. Risk management tool: FMEA, HACCP or the BioPhorum toolkit
  4. Risk-ranking criteria: how risk levels are quantified

A cross-functional team is a requirement TR13 emphasizes repeatedly. An EM risk assessment is not the microbiology laboratory's job alone; it needs to involve microbiology, contamination control, quality engineering, facility maintenance, QC, QA and manufacturing.

The Importance of Process Mapping

Before starting the risk assessment, the team must first conduct a Gemba Walk on site and mark the following on a room layout drawing:

  • Equipment locations
  • People flow
  • Material flow
  • Waste flow

The purpose of this step is for the team to "see with their own eyes and walk it with their own feet", rather than assessing from memory in a meeting room.

The BioPhorum EM Risk Assessment Toolkit is a standardized tool developed collaboratively by the pharmaceutical industry. It provides step-by-step guidance for building an EM program and is being adopted by a growing number of companies.

5.2.2.1 General Points to Consider & Grid Profiling

Risk Factors & Grid Profiling Method

For process mapping, grid profiling can be useful to describe single process steps and consider activities specific to that grid. After gridding the room layout, the process steps in each grid are evaluated and the risk ranking is determined based on six risk factors:

  1. Cleaning and sanitization access
  2. Personnel presence and flow
  3. Material flow
  4. Proximity to open product or product contact material
  5. Interventions/operations by personnel and their complexity
  6. Frequency of interventions

Based on the risk scoring, sampling locations are determined for performing the EMPQ and, afterwards, for conducting the routine monitoring. A recommendation of the grid size for the respective grades is made (e.g., 0.5 m² for Grade A). In addition, grouping grids into functional sections (e.g., filling area) enables meaningful sample points to be defined.

Additional Points to Consider

Sample locations should be chosen based on:

  • Criticality of the process being performed in the room
  • Product exposure to the environment and susceptibility to microbial and particulate contamination
  • Locations of potential ingress from supporting areas or adjacent non-classified areas (gowning areas, airlocks, corridors)
  • Sites shown to be appropriately placed to detect a problem in the area

The following factors should be considered during the facility walk-through:

  1. Adherence to industry and regulatory guidelines (CFR, ISO 14644, USP, FDA, EU)
  2. Sites where contamination would most likely have an adverse effect on product quality:
    • Proximity to open product or product contact surfaces and critical sites
    • Activities linked with interventions
    • Areas most inaccessible or difficult to clean and disinfect
    • Locations with high frequency and/or complexity of activities
    • Areas with large number of personnel and high personnel flow
    • Areas with high material flow
  3. Uniform geometric pattern or grid-profiling within the cleanrooms
  4. Assessment and justification for locations not part of the EM program
  5. Historical data and/or data obtained during qualification
  6. Anaerobic organisms (periodic/investigational monitoring for Cutibacterium acnes)
  7. Airflow visualization studies (smoke studies)

Six-Factor Risk Model and Grid Profiling / In-Depth Interpretation

Analogy: Crime Hotspot Analysis in a City

Grid profiling is like the police dividing a city into blocks with a grid, then scoring each block on several factors (population density, night-time lighting, historical crime rate, and so on). The higher a block's score, the more patrols it gets.

In a cleanroom, the "crime" is microbial contamination, and the six risk factors are the indicators of how "likely to go wrong" each grid cell is.

Grid Size Recommendations

  • Grade A: 0.5 m² per grid (the finest, because it is the most critical area)
  • Grade B–D: adjust according to area size and process complexity (TR13 Appendix 2 gives detailed examples)

Combining grid cells into "functional sections" (such as the filling area or capping area) makes the definition of sample sites more meaningful, rather than mechanically sampling every cell.

TR13 Section 5.2.2.1 also lists seven categories of considerations, of which the following are particularly worth noting:

  • Anaerobe monitoring: strict anaerobes are unlikely to survive in an aerobic cleanroom, but microaerophilic organisms such as Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes) may be missed by standard aerobic culture, so periodic/investigational monitoring should be performed.
  • Smoke Studies: airflow visualization can reveal turbulence and eddy zones, which may need to be addressed through redesign of the airflow, process or facility.

Note: Factor 4, "proximity to product", carries a particularly high weight (Medium=4, High=8), because it is the most direct threat to product quality.

Risk classification: the scores of the six factors are multiplied together

  • Low Risk: 1–16
  • Medium Risk: 32–265
  • High Risk: 512–8,192

5.2.2.2–5.2.2.4 Risk Initiation, Assessment, Control & Review

5.2.2.2 Risk Initiation: Development of EM Risk Assessments

Typical approaches to EM risk assessments include the following activities:

  • Map the layout of the room(s), overlay with grids, and combine them into functional sections
  • Walk the process with the risk assessment team, detailing process activities grid-by-grid
  • Assess each grid against the risk factors and score them to determine the relative probability of contamination
  • Evaluate the results by functional sections to select sampling locations and methods
  • Create an EMPQ plan
  • Evaluate the EMPQ results
  • Determine routine EM sampling locations, methods, and frequencies
  • Review the risk assessment on a defined, periodic basis

For each grid, risk is scored based on the process used, and then ranked based on the six factors. Strategies regarding risk scoring and risk ranking can be found in the BioPhorum EM Risk Assessment Toolkit. Each risk factor is ranked as low (1), medium (2) or high risk (4), with exception of "proximity to product," which is ranked as low (1), medium (4) or high risk (8). The calculated product for each grid results in the risk scoring as low risk (1–16), medium risk (32–265) or high risk (512–8192).

5.2.2.3 Risk Assessment Phase

Once the elements of the EM risk assessment are defined, the team will begin the risk assessment phase. Hazards identified during the facility walk-through will be documented, and the hazards will be evaluated for risk factors given the current controls in place. During this phase, consider:

  • Timing of sampling and sample volume measured against the company standard
  • Frequency of sampling measured against the company standard
  • Additional criteria (e.g., height of sampling location)
  • Risk of sampling procedure for product contamination
  • Locations and areas where there is no monitoring
  • Historical data
  • Air flow studies

The outcome of the risk assessment includes a documented plan for monitoring and the rationale for the EM program (sampling locations, sampling volume, sampling frequency, methods).

If there is an existing EM program without a risk-based rationale, the risk team must be careful to ensure they are not performing an assessment to justify the current sampling program. The team must evaluate the facility/process (e.g., via a Gemba walk), taking into consideration historical data to prevent removal of sampling locations which have historically demonstrated adverse recovery trends.

5.2.2.4 Risk Control & Risk Review

Where an existing EM program was created using the principles of risk management, the team will evaluate the existing assessment to ensure that all conditions assumed in the original assessment are still in place. If there are discrepancies between the initial and current conditions, the assessment is modified, and the hazards are re-ranked to determine if the risks are below the required threshold. Any major deviations to the relevant criteria should be considered, and mitigation actions may be defined to control the risks.

Risk Assessment Execution Process / Full Interpretation

The Eight-Step EM Risk Assessment Process

  1. Draw the room layout, overlay the grid, and combine cells into functional sections
  2. Team Gemba Walk, recording process activities cell by cell
  3. Score cell by cell, calculating the risk score against the six factors
  4. Summarize by functional section, and select sampling locations and methods
  5. Establish the EMPQ plan
  6. Evaluate the EMPQ results
  7. Determine routine EM sampling locations, methods and frequencies
  8. Review the risk assessment periodically

Common Pitfall Warning

TR13 specifically warns: if an existing EM program has no risk assessment basis, the team performing a retrospective risk assessment must not work backwards to justify the existing sampling plan. They must start from scratch, walking the site (Gemba Walk), performing process mapping, and reassessing objectively.

Also note that you must not remove a sample site that has historically shown adverse recovery trends just because the risk assessment concludes "low risk"; historical data is an important reference.

Risk Control and Review (Section 5.2.2.4)

A risk assessment is not a "do it once and you are done" activity. TR13 requires:

  • Periodically check whether the assumptions of the original assessment still hold
  • If there are differences, re-rank the hazard levels
  • If risk exceeds the acceptable level, define mitigation measures
  • Significant deviations must be considered and acted upon

Analogy: Continuous Updating of Insurance Actuarial Models

A risk assessment is like an insurance company's actuarial model: you cannot set today's premium with ten-year-old data. The environment changes (new equipment, new personnel, new processes), so the risk model must be updated accordingly. The "periodic review" emphasized in TR13 ensures that your risk model always reflects reality.

For a new EM program (with no historical data): the team needs to rely on scientific experience and subject matter expert knowledge to minimize uncertainty in the assessment. As routine monitoring data accumulates, the actual risk level will gradually become apparent.

5.3 Alert Levels and Action Limits

5.3 Alert Levels and Action Limits

EM programs require action limits to be established based on the applicable regulatory guidelines or requirements. These guidelines frequently recommend alert levels also be established. Some companies also choose to set levels for individual cleanrooms or sample sites. The alert level will be established based on statistical analysis of the historic EM data.

Alert levels and action limits based on CFU count excursions have been eliminated from USP <1116>, which indicates a paradigm shift to contamination recovery rate (CRR). CRR is the percentage of environmental samples found to have microbial contamination (e.g., a CRR of 1% would mean that only 1% of the samples taken showed contamination, regardless of colony numbers). The CRR approach may lend itself to wider applicability considering emerging EM technologies, such as those not reliant on CFU measurement. However, current official regulatory guidances from both the EU and the US still retain GMP requirements for alert levels and action limits. Consequently, companies may want to voluntarily monitor CRR as an EM trending tool in addition to the required alert levels and action limits.

The response to alert levels or action limits should be written and employed consistently. To create consistency, investigatory and corrective action steps should be specified in advance. Records should show that the excursion was recognized, appropriate follow-up occurred, and appropriate corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) were taken. Established alert levels should be reviewed periodically as part of routine trend analysis. They may be revised to reflect improvements, advances in technology, changes in use patterns, or other changes.

When no regulatory or industry guidelines are provided, alert levels and action limits may be derived statistically from historical data. Statistically derived alert levels, based on performance of the area, might be low; however, one should be using those values to detect atypical recurrences. Other considerations in adjusting levels include improving process capability, consistency of alert levels and action limits for similar room classifications, level of gowning, and product contamination risk. Infrequent excursions from these levels are expected at frequencies characteristic for the specific mathematical model used in their derivation. The critical requirement is recognizing an adverse trend that may represent a loss of environmental control.

The alert levels and action limits do not define product attributes, such as sterility, and should not be considered as product specifications. Rather, alert levels are intended to indicate changes or drifts in environmental control so that corrective action may be taken before product quality is adversely affected. However, where an action limit is exceeded where critical aseptic process steps are conducted, the investigation should be completed prior to release of the batches impacted by the excursion, which should include an evaluation of the product impact, based on the most probable root cause identified.

Since there is no one best method for setting alert levels, the approaches described in Sections 5.3.1–5.3.3 may be taken within the pharmaceutical industry. Where compendial requirements exist, as with action limits, they supersede these examples.

Commentary: Alert Levels and Action Limits

Real-Life Analogy: Driving Speed Warnings

Picture the speedometer on a car dashboard:

  • Alert Level = the yellow warning light as you approach the speed limit (for example, with a limit of 100, the light comes on at 90). It means "be careful, you are approaching the boundary": it needs attention, but you do not have to stop immediately.
  • Action Limit = the red speeding alarm (the light turns red above 100). It means "you have exceeded the limit": corrective action must be taken immediately, and the cause must be documented and investigated.

In environmental monitoring, an Alert is an "early warning" and an Action is "mandatory response".

The USP <1116> paradigm shift: Traditionally, environmental monitoring focused on whether the CFU count of each sample exceeded a specific value. USP <1116> now promotes the use of the Contamination Recovery Rate (CRR), that is, "the percentage of all samples taken in which microorganisms are detected". This change matters because:

  • CRR does not depend on CFU counts, so it is applicable to emerging rapid microbial detection technologies that are not CFU-based
  • CRR gives a macro view of the overall "frequency" of contamination in the environment, rather than the "severity" of a single event
  • EU GMP Annex 1 and the FDA still require Alert/Action limits to be set, so CRR can be used alongside them as an additional trending tool

Key Point: EM Limits vs Product Specifications

TR13 states explicitly that Alert/Action limits are not product specifications and do not define sterility. They are the "thermometer" of environmental control, used to detect environmental drift so that preventive action can be taken before product quality is affected.

However, when an action limit is exceeded in an area of critical aseptic operations, the investigation must be completed before batch release, and the potential impact on the product must be assessed.

Warning: EM Data Is NOT Normally Distributed

Using standard statistical tools for setting alert levels and action limits can be misleading because environmental monitoring data is not normally distributed. No pharmaceutical industry or regulatory consensus exists on the best statistical model to use for setting these levels. Therefore, it may be appropriate to determine the model that best fits the data and use that model to set the levels. When data does not fall within a normal distribution and exhibits high levels of skewness towards lower counts and/or zero counts, a non-parametric approach to setting alert levels and action limits is suitable when large numbers of historical observations exist.

Practical implication: Environmental monitoring data is heavily concentrated at 0 or low CFU, producing a highly right-skewed distribution. The traditional Mean ± 2SD / 3SD approach assumes a normal distribution, and applying it to EM data yields misleading limits. TR13 therefore recommends the following three nonparametric methods.

5.3.1 Nonparametric Approach

5.3.1 Nonparametric Approach

A nonparametric evaluation is based on 99.9th and 99.99th percentiles and can be chosen as the alert and action levels. These levels correspond to about mean plus 3 and 4 sigma ranges, respectively. These levels are commonly selected as process alert and action levels. When the requirement of proper sample size is met (N≈1000), the alert levels analysis establishes confidence that during actual process monitoring 99.9% of the population will lie below the stated value. When these limits are exceeded, it will trigger investigation that will prevent contamination or an adverse trend condition.

Action levels can be selected at 99.99th percentile. The action level analysis establishes confidence that during actual process monitoring 99.99% of the population will lie below the stated value. Therefore, the derived action levels serve to identify an excursion at the selected percentile level. By definition, it is very unlikely that a validated system, operating in a state of control, will deliver results that will deviate significantly from the norm especially when multiple alert/action excursions in the same room on the same day are detected. This condition is likely indicative of a significant drift in the process control(s) or a systemic failure of the validated system has occurred.

Commentary: Nonparametric Percentile Method

Nonparametric Percentile Method

Alert Level  = 99.9th percentile  (approx. Mean + 3 sigma)
Action Limit = 99.99th percentile (approx. Mean + 4 sigma)

Required sample size: N ≈ 1,000

Interpretation:
  - 99.9% of all future results should fall BELOW the Alert Level
  - 99.99% of all future results should fall BELOW the Action Limit
  - Exceedance of these limits is statistically RARE in a controlled system

How the method works: All historical data are ranked from smallest to largest, and the values at the 99.9th and 99.99th percentiles are taken directly as the Alert and Action limits. Because no particular distribution is assumed, it is called "nonparametric".

Practical example: Suppose 1,200 active air sampling results have been collected in an ISO 7 room. Ranked from smallest to largest:

  • 99.9th percentile = the 1,199th value = 8 CFU → Alert Level = 8 CFU
  • 99.99th percentile = near the maximum value = 12 CFU → Action Limit = 12 CFU

Key caution: This method requires a large amount of data (N≈1000). If the data set is too small, extreme values (possibly outliers from out-of-control events) will be selected as the limits, inappropriately inflating them.

5.3.2 Nonparametric Tolerance Limit Approach

5.3.2 Nonparametric Tolerance Limit Approach

As environmental monitoring data, especially in cleanrooms, is typically not normally distributed (i.e., exhibits skew heavily toward lower counts or zero counts), a nonparametric tolerance limit approach to setting alert levels and action limits is recommended. These limits allow the user to assert with at least 95% (K=0.95) confidence that 100(P) or 99% of a population lies below the value, as determined by the stated action limits, for the respective data. For distribution-free tolerance limits, minimum sample sizes are N=60 for 95/95 (alert level) and N=300 for 95/99 (action limits).

Commentary: Nonparametric Tolerance Limit Method

Tolerance Limit Notation

Format: Confidence / Coverage  (K / P)

Alert Level:  95/95
  → 95% confidence that 95% of population is below this value
  → Minimum sample size: N = 60

Action Limit: 95/99
  → 95% confidence that 99% of population is below this value
  → Minimum sample size: N = 300

Difference from the percentile method: The percentile method simply gives the value at a certain position in the data, whereas the tolerance limit method adds a "confidence level". A 95/95 limit means "I am 95% confident that 95% of future data will fall below this value".

Advantages:

  • Requires far less data than the percentile method (60 vs 1000)
  • Makes no assumption about the distribution (distribution-free)
  • Provides an explicit statistical confidence guarantee

Suitable situations: For a newly built cleanroom or a newly introduced EM program where a large body of historical data has not yet accumulated, this method is more practical than the percentile method.

5.3.3 Cutoff Value Approach

5.3.3 Cutoff Value Approach

All the test data for a particular site, or group of similar sites, are arranged in a histogram, and the alert levels and action limits are set at values whose monitoring results are respectively 5% and 1% higher than the level selected (i.e., 95th and 99th percentile, respectively). Other percentiles may be used in establishing levels. Alert levels are typically determined per test type (active air, passive air, and surface monitoring).

The alert definition should be based on a timeframe allowing the accumulation of enough data to be representative of a population. A general text on statistics recommends a statistically significant value of at least 20 data points. Alert levels are typically set at about 50–60% of action until enough data is generated. An example is to take the last 100 monitoring results or data collected during one full year and use the 95th and 99th percentile values as the alert levels and action limits.

Areas that have the same activities may be grouped to provide more data points; for example, material airlocks of the same Grade, or Grade D hallways of same function can be grouped.

The calculated alert level should be challenged for its appropriateness, however, avoid modifying or increasing the alert as such it loses any correlation with your actual cleanroom performance. In addition, performance-based alert levels that seem to be well below the applied action limits should be considered as a confirmation of a solid microbial control of the environment and does not per definition to be considered as inappropriate. One should be aware that above alerts will occur and that this is inherent with the use of a cut-off approach. One should be more focused on the level of occurrence.

If the outcome of the periodic reassessment of the alert assessment results in a level increase, this should be interpreted as a warning that the facility may be slowly drifting out of control or might be a result of modifications of the activities within the area. It is recommended not to increase alert levels without a good understanding of the reasons behind it.

The frequency-based excursion rates and trends can be used to determine the state of the EM control and gowning process. The cumulative conforming count chart (CCC) is easy to use and more sensitive to short-term drifts in process parameter, enabling its user to judge the state of control. The idea behind the CCC is the fact that the number of conforming items between two consecutive nonconforming items changes when the fraction nonconforming shifts is observed. CCC plots the number of inspectional monitoring data sets between excursions. The method is suitable when large number of data exists.

At a specified excursion rate, a likely number of excursions per given number of samples can be estimated at a 95% confidence level. The CCC can be used to identify the average and the minimum number of continuous successful (within alert levels) facility- or gown-monitoring examinations before the data shifts. Such approaches can be used to evaluate limits, define trends, and assess training effectiveness, for example, gowning process and operator or analyst disqualification and requalification.

Other models based on negative binomial, Poisson, Weibull, or exponential distributions are possible. It may be appropriate to determine the model that best fits the data and use that model to set the levels. As noted, contamination in strictly controlled environments does not typically fall within a normal distribution. Environmental monitoring data may be evaluated to determine the suitability of the approaches to level setting.

The monitoring group should review the data for trends at an appropriate frequency. The quality unit should review quarterly and yearly monitoring reports.

Commentary: Cutoff Value Method and CCC Charts

Cutoff Value Method

Alert Level  = 95th percentile (top 5% are above)
Action Limit = 99th percentile (top 1% are above)

Minimum data: 20 data points (minimum)
Recommended:  100 results or 1 year of data

Initial (insufficient data):
  Alert = 50-60% of Action Limit

Grouping allowed:
  Same-grade rooms with same activities can be pooled

Summary comparison of the three methods:

CCC chart (Cumulative Conforming Count): The CCC chart is a special type of control chart used to monitor the interval between excursions in terms of consecutive "conforming" samples. The principle is as follows:

  • X axis: the sequence number of each excursion event
  • Y axis: the number of conforming samples between two excursion events
  • If environmental control is stable, the number of conforming samples between excursions should remain relatively steady
  • If the conforming interval suddenly shortens, the environment is deteriorating

Practical value of CCC:

  • More sensitive to short-term drift than traditional SPC charts
  • Can be used to evaluate the effectiveness of gowning training (the interval between passing and failing gowning assessments by operators)
  • Can be used to evaluate the maintenance of operator qualification and requalification

The Pitfall of Adjusting Alert Levels

TR13 specifically warns that if periodic reassessment shows that an Alert Level needs to be raised, this should be treated as a warning sign that environmental control may be deteriorating, not as a "normal adjustment". Do not raise an Alert Level arbitrarily without understanding the root cause, as that amounts to deceiving yourself by masking the problem.

An Alert Level that sits below the Action Limit should be viewed as a positive confirmation of good microbial control, not as an inappropriate setting.

5.3.4 Quantitative and Qualitative Trending Rules

5.3.4 Quantitative and Qualitative Trending Rules

Trend rules should supplement alert levels and action limits and focus on two important axes:

  • To rapidly detect gradual bioburden changes before it leads to real excursions with potential impact for production and/or the cleanroom environment (immediate term trending)
  • It should be able to periodically evaluate the general state of control of the cleanroom environment (short- and long-term trending)

A useful trending rule is to look at alert level excursions over time within the same room identifying correlation between/among locations, or frequent sustained recoveries above alert. When trend rules are exceeded, an investigation is recommended.

Immediate — short term trending rules typically look at an increase in the proportion of above alerts over a specified time period. Examples:

  • A specified number of above alerts at a single location/type of test
  • A specified number of alerts within one room (same and different types of tests)

Some statistical rules can be used as well in order to identify when the proportion of above alerts is moving to an atypical level.

Short Term — monthly or quarterly: The monitoring group should perform a comprehensive review of the data at an appropriate frequency as a function of the amount and criticality of the data generated. The quality unit should review and approve the periodic monitoring reports. Typically, a thorough analysis with a statement if data indicate that the facility is operating in a state of control or if data indicate some issues, how it was discovered, where and what corrective action was taken and what impact the issue had on the facility. The integration of graphs is highly recommended to visualize the state of control. One can assess:

  • Same sample type from same location exceeding the action limit multiple times
  • Increases of the number of alert level excursions (e.g., same monitoring type: active, passive or surface monitoring)
  • Recovery rate increases for the same location, or the same room or area

Long Term — annual: Annual trending should report the changes in a year's worth of data. Since it incorporates more data, it should be able to give a better holistic picture of the cleanroom environment. It might include:

  • Number of EM Deviations per grade with indication of concerned rooms and test type and corrective actions
  • Occurrence of atypical microorganism per grade and explanation of potential root cause identified
  • Microbial flora analysis and confirmation of the disinfection program efficiency and validated state
  • Global analysis of the site performance covered by the monthly/quarterly reports and comparison with the previous period
  • Key findings and highlights on any change in SOP or modification during the year
  • Year graphical representation and global analysis of the data from your cleanrooms and conclusion with a statement such as data indicate that the facility is operating in a state of control or if data indicate some issues and any related CAPAs
  • Analysis of potential correlations between observed product contamination (e.g., bioburden) and the micro-organisms recovered within the cleanroom environment

Commentary: The Three-Tier Structure of Trend Analysis

Trend analysis is the "preventive monitoring mechanism" of an EM program. TR13 divides it into three tiers:

Immediate trending rules (practical examples):

  • 2 Alert excursions at the same location within one week → trigger an investigation
  • Alerts at 3 or more different test points in the same room on the same day → possible systemic problem

Elements a short-term report should include:

  • Whether the same location has repeatedly exceeded the Action Limit
  • Whether the frequency of Alert excursions is increasing
  • Whether the recovery rate in the same area is rising
  • A clear concluding statement: whether the facility is in a state of control
  • The use of charts (trend charts, control charts) for visual presentation is strongly recommended

The annual report should cover:

  • The number of deviations at each grade, and the rooms and test types involved
  • Analysis of the occurrence of atypical microorganisms and their root causes
  • Microbial flora analysis → verification of the effectiveness of the disinfection program
  • Comparative analysis against data from previous periods
  • Analysis of the correlation between product contamination and environmental microorganisms

5.4 Data Management

5.4 Data Management

Routine review and analysis of EM data for trends at an appropriate frequency is essential to aid in the interpretation of process stability and assess overall environmental control performance. Management must be kept abreast of trends and the subsequent state of operations within facilities with review of quarterly and yearly monitoring reports.

Based on the large number of samples tested by a given facility, a computer-based data-tracking system is essential. Before implementation, all database applications used should be validated or qualified for specific software applications.

Commentary: Overview of Data Management

An EM program generates a large volume of data (a single facility may produce thousands of sampling results per month), so a computerized data tracking system is needed. TR13 emphasizes that:

  • Data must be reviewed and analyzed regularly to assess trends
  • Management must be aware of the state of environmental control in the facility
  • All database applications must be validated or qualified

This is consistent with the requirements of EU GMP Annex 11 (Computerised Systems) and FDA 21 CFR Part 11 (Electronic Records).

5.4.1 Data Integrity (ALCOA)

5.4.1 Data Integrity

The PDA Elements of a Code of Conduct for Data Integrity in the Pharmaceutical Industry and PDA Technical Report No. 80: Data Integrity Management Systems for Pharmaceutical Laboratories provide comprehensive information on this topic.

Collecting, analyzing, reporting and retention of EM data should be done under conditions that are designed to guarantee reliability and integrity of the data. The data are required to be attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, and accurate (ALCOA):

  • Attributable: Information is captured that identifies where the data came from
  • Legible: Data are readable and understandable by humans
  • Contemporaneous: Data are recorded at the time they are generated or observed
  • Original: Data are recorded for the first time
  • Accurate: Data are correct

Important elements to consider with respect to environmental monitoring are:

  • Integrity of the sampling site data by including a formal mechanism to enable full reconciliation of sample collection, incubation, and evaluation
  • EM data must be documented on controlled worksheets for paper records or entered into a validated computer system with appropriate security, audit trails, validation, and oversight
  • Employees must adhere to the established site documentation requirement and will not record raw data on unofficial forms, writing pads, or other uncontrolled media
  • Employees will not discard or destroy raw data or original records, other than at the end of the prescribed retention period

The deployment of a second-person verification should be considered to confirm the enumeration of microbial recovery. The main focus, however, should be to have in place a robust plate-reading process, reducing the risk for variability to the minimum, for instance, by applying appropriate lighting and/or magnification and a robust training of the technicians performing the readout. The utilization of QRM principles can aid in the determination of whether or not a secondary verification is required (e.g., Grade A EM). Automated systems for EM test readouts and data storage can eliminate bias and the need to perform a second verification.

Routine data are aligned into a source in a consistent record format. At a minimum, the record format should include the following:

  • Identification of the operator and analyst
  • Monitoring date and time
  • Equipment used
  • Specific sampling locations
  • Sampling methods including media used
  • Incubation conditions
  • Colony-forming units (CFU) or total airborne particulate count results
  • Identifications performed
  • Batch information (if applicable, e.g., for batch-related monitoring)
  • Current alert levels or action limits

Records must be signed and verified by the appropriate person, depending on the type of system used. Some alternative microbiological methods can use measurements other than CFUs, provided they have been properly validated before use, for example, relative light units, cells, etc.

Commentary: Data Integrity and the ALCOA Principles

Common data integrity risk points in EM:

  • End-to-end traceability of sampling and incubation: A formal mechanism must ensure that every sample is traceable from collection through incubation to plate reading, so that no sample can be "lost"
  • No uncontrolled media: Results must not first be recorded on sticky notes or in notebooks and then transcribed onto the official form
  • No destruction of original records: Records may be destroyed only after the specified retention period has expired

Second-person verification:

  • Consider whether a second person is needed to verify CFU counts
  • More importantly, establish a robust plate-reading procedure: appropriate lighting, magnifiers, and personnel training
  • QRM principles can be used to decide whether it is needed (for example, Grade A EM may require it)
  • Automated systems can eliminate bias and replace second-person verification

Minimum Record Elements for EM Records

Each EM data record should contain at least the following 10 items of information: operator/analyst identity, date and time of monitoring, equipment used, specific sampling location, sampling method including culture medium, incubation conditions, CFU or particle count result, identifications performed, batch information (if applicable), and current Alert/Action limits.

5.4.2 Data Trending Analysis

5.4.2 Data Trending Analysis

Histograms or tables, characterized by a number of data points that fall within a common frequency, are valuable tools to conduct the trending expected by regulatory agencies. Different room classifications with defined requirements will produce different histograms. For example, the CFU spread obtained across an ISO 8 data set will not be observed in a data set from an ISO 5 area. Therefore, each area, or area type, and its accompanying data set must be viewed as distinct. Data trends in counts, occurrences, and organism types can provide a holistic view of the cleanroom environment. They can also be used to evaluate if there is ingress from lower to higher grades. Cleanroom maps indicating sampling excursions are useful as well to show connectivity of various grades and potential ingress through equipment and personnel flows. While this does not require statistical tools, mathematical models could be applied. Examples of statistical methods and control charts can be found in PDA Technical Report 59: Utilization of Statistical Methods for Production Monitoring.

Commentary: Data Trending Analysis Tools

Histograms: These present CFU counts as a frequency distribution. Cleanrooms of different grades produce histograms of different shapes:

  • ISO 5: The vast majority of results are 0 CFU, with very few non-zero values
  • ISO 7: A skewed distribution, concentrated at 0-5 CFU
  • ISO 8: A wider distribution, which may reach 20+ CFU

Key principle: Each area or area type must be analyzed as a separate data set; data from different grades must not be mixed.

Three dimensions of trend analysis:

  • Counts — changes in CFU values over time
  • Occurrences — the frequency of excursion events
  • Organism Types — changes in the types of microorganisms recovered

Cleanroom Maps: Plotting excursion events on a floor plan gives a visual picture of the connectivity between different grades and the potential routes of microbial ingress (through equipment flow and personnel flow).

For more examples of statistical methods and control charts, see PDA TR59: Utilization of Statistical Methods for Production Monitoring.

5.4.3 Interpretation & Contamination Recovery Rates

5.4.3 Interpretation

Routine environmental microbial monitoring data should demonstrate that the classified area is operating under an adequate microbial control for the needs of the operations conducted in that area. Individual recoveries in critical areas (i.e., Grade A) must be adequately investigated. Compliant EM results do not guarantee the sterility assurance of the process, however, as EM incorporates multiple limitations (e.g., recovery efficiency of the applied methods, selected sample locations etc.). EM can only confirm to some extent the robustness of the design and processes.

Data generated should be summarized and evaluated to determine whether the EM process is in a state of control and documented in periodic reports. A variety of methods are available to perform this analysis, for example, the use of frequency distribution and control charts or statistical analysis.

Measurements for viable contamination, such as contamination excursion rates and/or recovery rates, can be used to determine the level of control in a given area. The excursion rate is related to the number of samples exceeding the defined alert levels and action limits, whereas the contamination recovery rate (expressed as a percentage) is related to the number of samples that are positive for microbial growth when compared to the total number of samples taken within the same classified area(s). Therefore, recovery rates differ from excursion rates. Given that microorganisms are not homogeneously distributed in the same environments, and the sensitivity and variability associated with microbial sampling methods, these rates can be useful approaches to trending results in controlled areas.

The combined excursion rates are calculated by determining the number of samples with excursions outside of established levels, dividing by the total number of samples collected, and converting to a percentage. For an ISO 5 environment, an excursion rate of not more than 1% is achievable due to stringent controls, and the expectation is that the results show no viable recoveries.

In addition to individual excursions, CRRs may be used for trending and control of the overall microbial load in the classified environment, equipment surfaces, material, and personnel. Companies can utilize the criteria from USP <1116> to develop their recovery rates for cleanroom environments depending on the activities and processes conducted in those areas. They should also establish a CRR for each monitoring attribute, such as surfaces, air, and garments, for each classified area. Any changes from the established calculated contamination rates should be investigated and corrected. However, any contamination in Grade A/ISO 5 areas must be investigated, since there should not be any microbial recovery within those areas. The CRR provides an alternative measurement of the effectiveness of the overall contamination control strategy.

Commentary: Interpreting Data: Excursion Rate vs Recovery Rate

Excursion Rate vs Recovery Rate (CRR)

Excursion Rate:

  • = number of samples exceeding the Alert/Action limit ÷ total number of samples taken × 100%
  • Focuses on the proportion of samples that "exceed the set limit"

Contamination Recovery Rate (CRR):

  • = number of samples in which any microorganism is detected ÷ total number of samples taken × 100%
  • Focuses on the proportion of samples with "microbial growth", whether or not a limit is exceeded

The two are different! A sample with 1 CFU (a positive recovery that does not exceed a limit) counts toward the CRR but not toward the Excursion Rate.

Inherent limitations of EM: TR13 specifically reminds us that compliant EM results do not guarantee sterility assurance of the process. EM has multiple limitations:

  • The recovery efficiency of sampling methods is limited
  • Sampling locations cover only a very small fraction of the space
  • EM can confirm the robustness of the design and processes only to a certain extent

Special requirements for Grade A/ISO 5: In Grade A areas, any microbial recovery must be investigated, because no viable recoveries are expected in this area.

Recommendations for applying CRR:

  • Establish a CRR separately for each monitoring attribute (surfaces, air, gowning)
  • Evaluate at least monthly (as recommended by USP <1116>)
  • Any change in CRR should be investigated and corrected

5.5 Rapid Microbiological Methods (RMM) in Environmental Monitoring

The industry should look toward utilizing innovative approaches for the detection and quantification of microorganisms as part of a holistic EM program. From a QRM perspective, the benefits of implementing rapid microbiological testing strategies can help to:

  • Design robust processes that prevent contamination
  • Ensure that a state of microbial control is maintained
  • Evaluate the impact of human interventions during aseptic filling
  • Establish recovery time after power shortages
  • Develop more effective strategies to correct a contamination problem
  • Continually improve processes and products
  • Quickly and efficiently assess the potential impact of failing results on the patient

Key applications of RMM within EM programs include:

  • Confirmation of microbial control in controlled areas prior to use (e.g., manufacturing suites, gowning rooms)
  • Rapid assessment of controlled areas during facility qualification activities (e.g., EM PQ)
  • Evaluation of contamination remediation efforts and root cause analysis

Additional detailed guidance may be found in PDA Technical Report No. 33: Evaluation, Validation and Implementation of Alternative and Rapid Microbiological Methods.

Commentary: Strategic Significance of Adopting RMM

Section 5.5 of TR13 formally brings RMM (Rapid Microbiological Methods) into the core discussion of the environmental monitoring (EM) program, marking an industry paradigm shift from "conventional plate counting" to "real-time sensing."

Why does RMM matter? The biggest weakness of conventional culture-based methods is delayed results: standard media need 3-7 days to give a result, and by the time an excursion is found, the batch has long since moved on to downstream processing or even shipment. The core value of RMM is shortening the time window between "problem occurs, detection, and intervention."

Four EM scenarios where RMM applies

  • Pre-use confirmation: rapidly confirm that the cleanroom bioburden meets requirements before production
  • Facility qualification: rapid assessment during EM PQ to accelerate the qualification timeline
  • Contamination source tracing: quickly pinpoint contamination hot spots during root cause investigation (RCA)
  • Disinfectant efficacy verification: assess the effect immediately after cleaning/disinfection

From a quality risk management (QRM) perspective, RMM improves the immediacy of microbial control and directly protects patient safety, which is why regulators actively encourage innovative methods.

5.5.1 Rapid Microbiological Method Scientific Principles Applied to Environmental Monitoring

Rapid methods appropriate for EM are based on a wide variety of scientific principles. Some methods still require growth of microorganisms using conventional microbiological media; however, detection and enumeration can be faster using novel detection techniques:

Growth-dependent RMM (faster detection via novel readout):

  • Digital imaging and autofluorescence of microcolonies
  • Fluorescent staining and laser excitation of microcolonies
  • Rapid detection of carbon dioxide (CO2)
  • Detection of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) after a brief microbial enrichment period

Growth-independent RMM (instantaneous or near real-time):

  • Flow and solid-phase cytometry using viability stains and laser detection systems
  • ATP bioluminescence (when enrichment is not required due to concentration of microorganisms in the sample)
  • Intrinsic fluorescence and optical spectroscopy detection of microorganisms in air or water
  • RAMAN spectroscopy coupled with viability staining
  • Nucleic acid amplification techniques (when enrichment is not required)

Technology selection factors:

  • Sample type and size required for analysis
  • Sample compatibility with RMM workflow and instrumentation
  • Desired time to result
  • Level of sensitivity required
  • Need to neutralize antimicrobial activity (for growth-based methods)
  • Ability to allow identification of recoveries (destructive vs. non-destructive assay)
  • Potential for false positives or negatives due to cleaning or disinfectant residues

Automated and high-throughput methods using conventional media are also available for faster results with better automation, data management, and data integrity.

Commentary: RMM Technology Classification and Selection Logic

TR13 divides RMM into two broad categories. Understanding this framework is the basis for choosing the right tool:

Type 1: Growth-Dependent

These methods use conventional culture media but shorten the wait with faster detection means.

  • Digital imaging: detects microcolonies and gives results within hours (conventionally 3 days)
  • Autofluorescence: uses the natural fluorescence of microorganisms, eliminating the staining step
  • CO2 detection: microbial metabolism releases CO2, allowing indirect quantification
  • ATP (after enrichment): microbial ATP serves as an indicator of viability and requires brief enrichment

Type 2: Growth-Independent

Microorganisms in the sample are detected directly, with results close to real time.

  • Flow cytometry: counts viable cells one by one, extremely fast
  • ATP bioluminescence: can detect directly without enrichment, commonly used for water systems
  • Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy: identifies organisms using their inherent fluorescence properties
  • Raman spectroscopy: combined with viability staining, provides a molecular fingerprint
  • Nucleic acid amplification (NAT): PCR and similar methods detect specific gene sequences

Key practical point: there is no universal RMM. Selection must consider the sample matrix (air, surface, water), whether subsequent organism identification is needed (destructive vs. non-destructive), and possible false positive/false negative interference from disinfectant residues.

5.5.2 Benefits of Using Rapid Microbiological Methods

The implementation of rapid methods for EM may present operational enhancements related to product quality, process knowledge, and regulatory acceptance, including:

  • Potential for reduced in-process microbiology testing and finished product release cycle times
  • Reduction in risks associated with forward processing
  • Elimination or reduction of off-line microbiology assays
  • Increases in laboratory automation and reductions in manual testing, sample handling, and data management
  • Reduced overhead and/or headcount for sampling and/or testing
  • Ability to make immediate quality decisions on the state of microbial control
  • Faster response to contamination events and initiation of investigations
  • Reduced repeat testing, lot rejection, reprocessing, and rework
  • Reduction in room or facility downtime and investigations
  • Faster confirmation of contamination remediation efforts

Commentary: Understanding the Ten Benefits of RMM

The benefits listed in TR13 can be understood along three dimensions:

Dimension 1: Time benefits (Speed)

  • Shorter batch release cycle time
  • Faster start of contamination investigations
  • Faster confirmation of the effect of disinfection remedial actions

Dimension 2: Risk benefits (Risk Reduction)

  • Lower forward processing risk: while waiting for conventional results, subsequent batches may already have entered aseptic filling and would be affected if an excursion is confirmed
  • Fewer retests, batch rejections and reprocessing events
  • Shorter facility downtime

Dimension 3: Operational benefits

  • Greater laboratory automation
  • Lower labor cost (sampling and testing personnel)
  • Improved data integrity

Audit tip: if a company has not yet adopted RMM, auditors may ask whether the feasibility of RMM has been evaluated and what the scientific rationale is for not adopting it. This benefits list in TR13 also provides strong support for a business case.

5.5.3 Validation of Rapid Microbiology Methods and Equipment

Rapid microbiology methods vary in complexity and in the associated equipment required:

  • Equipment/software validation follows the model found in USP <1058> (Analytical Instrument Qualification)
  • Method validation is discussed in compendial chapters: USP <1116> and Ph. Eur. 5.1.6
  • PDA TR No. 33 also provides industry-proven validation strategies

The most recent EU Annex 1 recommends that when a technology presents a result in a manner different from the CFU, the manufacturer should:

  • Scientifically justify the limits applied
  • Where possible, correlate them to the CFU
  • This applies to Grade A areas where "no growth" is expected

Key challenge — non-CFU signal correlation:

If the data signal is a CFU (growth-based RMM), results can be compared with existing alert levels and action limits directly. If the data signal is not a CFU, the quantitative result may not align with existing limits, even though a CFU correlation can be made. In this case, the end-user may:

  • Generate sufficient data to modify existing alert and action levels based on historical data
  • Rationalize and apply new levels using a risk-based analysis

Alternatively, trending EM values may be more appropriate than looking at individual counts — especially in well-controlled environments (e.g., Grade A / ISO 5 areas) where zero cell counts are expected.

Results using growth-independent RMM may be numerically higher than traditional methods, as viable but non-culturable (VBNC), physically stressed, damaged, or dormant organisms may be detected that would not grow on conventional media.

Commentary: Three-Tier Framework for RMM Validation and the Biggest Challenge

RMM validation is far more complex than conventional method validation, and TR13 clearly sets out three levels:

Validation hierarchy

  • Instrument/equipment qualification (USP <1058>): AIQ (IQ/OQ/PQ)
  • Method validation (USP <1116>, Ph. Eur. 5.1.6): sensitivity, specificity, linearity, precision
  • Industry strategy guidance (PDA TR33): a complete framework for evaluating, validating and implementing alternative methods

The biggest challenge: setting limits for non-CFU signals

Alert levels and action limits in conventional EM are both set in CFU (see EU Annex 1 Table 1). When an RMM produces a signal that is not CFU (for example "events/mL" from flow cytometry or "RLU" from ATP), the existing limits cannot be applied directly.

Two ways forward:

  • Establish a CFU correlation curve and convert to equivalent CFU for comparison (mathematically feasible, but statistically challenging)
  • Use sufficient historical data to set RMM-specific alert/action limits (more practical, but must be supported by a risk assessment)

The VBNC issue: RMMs that do not require culture can detect "viable but non-culturable" organisms, so counts are often higher than with conventional methods. This does not mean the environment has deteriorated; it reflects improved detection sensitivity.

Special considerations for Grade A: in an environment where "zero growth" is expected, trending is more meaningful than a single count, because any positive value should trigger an investigation without waiting to exceed an action limit.

5.6 Identification of Isolates

Identifying and strain-typing of microorganisms is not only important as part of cleanroom qualification or when related to EM excursions, but should also form an integral part of routine surveillance monitoring programs.

The identification program selected by the laboratory should be defined in writing, including:

  • The frequency of identification
  • Standard procedures for the methods used
  • Consistency with regulatory expectations for identifications

Companies relying on contract laboratories for microbial identification should qualify them per supplier qualification procedures and have a quality agreement in place.

The identification of microorganisms forms an important element to assess microbial state of control, allowing:

  • Detection of any microbial changes and potential seasonal variations of the flora
  • Definition of potential sources of isolates related to a detected contamination (e.g., as part of an investigation)
  • Assessment of the level of contamination control
  • Response upon recovery of undesirable organisms that might pose particular risks

Grade A/B considerations: The Grade A environment is expected to be completely free of viable organisms. In the surrounding Grade B environment, spore-forming organisms (mold, Bacillus spp.) and water-borne Gram-negative organisms are of particular concern. Gram-negative organisms may indicate water intrusion, increasing the risk of mold proliferation.

Grade C/D: Levels of undesirable organisms should be controlled to limit microbial stress on surrounding aseptic cleanrooms.

Commentary: Tiered Microbial Identification Strategy and Source Tracing

Microbial identification is one of the most scientifically in-depth parts of an EM program. Table 5.6-1 provides a clear three-tier strategy framework:

Three-tier identification strategy

  • Tier 1: Full identification to species level
    Applies to: all isolates from Grade A/B; Grade C/D on excursions; periodic monitoring samples
    Methods: MALDI-TOF MS, 16S rRNA sequencing, biochemical identification
  • Tier 2: Periodic identification
    Applies to: routine Grade C/D monitoring (to be performed periodically even without excursions)
    Purpose: build an environmental flora database and detect microflora shift
  • Tier 3: Strain typing
    Applies to: major product failures (media fill, sterility test failure, significant adverse trends)
    Methods: MLST (multilocus sequence typing), PFGE (pulsed-field gel electrophoresis), whole genome sequencing (WGS)

Analogy: identification is the organism's "ID card plus family DNA"

Identifying to species is like confirming a person's "identity"; strain typing is like a "family DNA comparison" that can determine whether organisms isolated from two places are the same strain (that is, the same contamination source). When a media fill fails, we need more than "this is a Staphylococcus"; we need to know "is it the same strain as the one on the operator's glove?"

Practical use of source attribution:

  • Pseudomonas spp. isolated: immediately suspect the water system (WFI, purified water) or cleaning water
  • Staphylococcus epidermidis isolated: focus on personnel gowning, gloves, masks
  • Aspergillus spp. (mold) isolated: check air filtration and ingress via outer packaging materials
  • Seasonal changes in microflora: may reflect outdoor air quality and the influence of pollen seasons

Regulatory requirements for outsourced identification: if a contract laboratory is used, supplier qualification must be completed and a quality agreement signed, to ensure the identification method and accuracy meet your own SOP requirements.

5.7 Investigations and Corrective Actions

Investigations are needed in response to an action-limit excursion and to address an adverse trend in order to determine a cause-and-effect relationship or most probable cause (i.e., sources of contamination). Investigative and corrective action steps should be specified in a written procedure.

The procedure should define the level of investigation required if there are multiple or sequential excursions. The investigation should include:

  • A product impact assessment
  • Evaluation of the risk to other batches of the same or related products manufactured in the same time frame
  • Critical investigations should be closed prior to product release

Corrective actions based on process knowledge can be implemented when sampling and laboratory failures have been ruled out. Commentary: Investigation Workflow and Root Cause Analysis Strategy

Section 5.7 is the most practically oriented part of the EM program. An investigation is not about "finding someone to blame" but about "finding the system problem."

Analogy: an EM investigation is like a criminal investigation

The detective (QA investigator) finds the crime scene (action limit exceeded) and must systematically work through all the suspects (contamination sources): the air handling system (HVAC), the water piping, operator error, or ageing of the facility itself? It cannot rely on intuition. There must be a written investigation SOP followed step by step, ending with the "most probable cause."

Investigation focus for the five systems:

Compressed gas system

The simplest and most direct investigation: retest immediately to confirm the result, perform a filter integrity test, and replace the filter if the excursion is confirmed. The key is to "act quickly" to prevent product impact.

HVAC system

The broadest scope, requiring a multi-faceted review: personnel activity (the largest source of microbial load), HEPA integrity, airflow patterns (static/dynamic visualization tests), differential pressure records, cleaning/disinfection records. An HVAC excursion often reflects the interaction of multiple factors.

Facility surfaces

Requires an on-site walkthrough: visually inspect walls/ceilings/floors for cracks and peeling paint (possible mold harborage). CCTV footage is often key evidence for reconstructing an "operational lapse."

High-purity water system (WFI/PW)

Distinguish "port contamination" from "system contamination." Review endotoxin data at the same time (a metabolic product of Gram-negative bacteria); dead legs, insufficient slope and hose management are common root causes.

Personnel gowning

Involves personal information and training records: interview the operator, retrieve gowning monitoring records, and check the gowning data of other operators on the same day, to determine whether it is an individual skill problem or a systemic training gap.

The product impact assessment must be completed within the investigation, and critical investigations should be closed before batch release. This is a frequent checkpoint in regulatory inspections.

Group 3: Analysis/testing records (items 13-19)

Records key laboratory information: test result with units (#13, e.g., CFU/plate), analyst (#14), reading date (#15), alert/action limits (#16), incubation temperature and time (#17), media lot traceability (#18), microbial characterization/identification (#19). Group 4: Review and approval (items 20-24)

Group 5: Investigation and CAPA (item 25)

The last item, the "investigation and corrective action system," is the most complex and includes five sub-elements: description of the deficiency, probable cause, responsible person, action steps and timeline, and CAPA effectiveness evaluation. This corresponds directly to the requirements of FDA 21 CFR Part 211 and EU GMP Annex 11.

Audit tip: auditors often ask to "take one EM record and check on the spot whether it contains these 25 elements." A checklist-based logbook is the most effective compliance tool.

6.0 Environmental Monitoring by Manufacturing Application

The levels and types of monitoring conducted may vary based on the type of product being manufactured, the attributes of the product, and the manufacturing areas and processes used.

6.1 Terminal Sterilization

Environmental monitoring in support of terminal sterilization monitors microflora from all sources — manufacturing environment, process gases, water used in product formulations, sterilizer feed and cooling water — that have the potential to contribute to product bioburden or endotoxin. This includes testing of air, surfaces, and water for pharmaceutical use.

Microbial levels of incoming containers and closures may also be monitored periodically (e.g., if the product is not terminally sterilized using an overkill cycle). While control of the environment in which the products are prepared is important, the most critical aspect of the program is the bioburden of the product and components to be sterilized, including both the number and type of microorganisms found.

Commentary: Manufacturing Application Determines Monitoring Depth

TR13 Section 6 describes EM requirements by process type, which makes it the most practice-oriented chapter of this report. The core logic: the higher the product risk, the wider the EM scope and the higher the frequency.

Fundamental Differences Between Terminal Sterilization and Aseptic Processing

Terminal sterilization: the product is sterilized in its final container. The purpose of EM is to monitor all sources that contribute to bioburden and endotoxin.

The most critical indicator is not the environment itself but the total amount and type of bioburden on the product and components to be sterilized, because this directly determines whether the sterilization process can achieve the required sterility assurance level (SAL).

If an overkill cycle is used, periodic microbiological monitoring of containers/closures can be handled flexibly; if the process is not an overkill process, the bioburden of containers/closures should be monitored periodically.

Analogy: Kitchen Ingredients vs. Surgical Operating Room

Terminal sterilization is like putting food into a pressure cooker: the contamination level of the ingredients themselves (bioburden) is what determines whether they can be cooked safely, and the cleanliness of the kitchen is secondary. Aseptic processing is like surgery: every step must be performed in an absolutely sterile environment, because there is no final "boiling" step.

6.2 Aseptic Processing

Environmental monitoring in support of aseptic processing is specifically designed to determine the number and type of microorganisms associated with the direct assembly or preparation of product prior to sealing the filled containers. The number of sample sites and frequency of monitoring are considerably greater than those for established terminal sterilization programs.

Air, water, personnel, compressed gases, machinery, and other surfaces within the filling room and associated support areas are routinely monitored. Microbial levels may be periodically checked on incoming containers and closures. Adequate environmental control is an integral part of the aseptic manufacturing process and is critical to sterility assurance.

EM data must be reviewed prior to product batch release and investigation of excursions closed. EM should also be conducted when operator interventions are required during aseptic processing.

6.2.1 Isolation Technology

The EM program for isolators differs from conventional aseptic filling operations and is based on the risks associated with isolator technology — especially the physical integrity of half-suits (if applicable) and gloves. Monitoring of air, surfaces, and personnel outside of the isolator environment is not required after each filling operation.

However, monitoring of isolator gloves and half-suits is required at the end of each filling operation or campaign (as applicable) to detect potential contamination. Product contact surfaces that are sterilized or changed out between batches in a campaign should be monitored at the end of the batch specific activities, prior to sterilization / change out.

Commentary: Comprehensive Monitoring Requirements for Aseptic Processing

The EM scope for aseptic processing covers air, water, personnel, compressed gases, mechanical equipment and all surfaces. This is because every contact point between filling and sealing carries a contamination risk to the product.

Key Regulatory Requirement: EM Data Must Be Reviewed Before Batch Release

TR13 clearly states: EM data must be reviewed before product batch release, and all out-of-limit investigations must be closed. This means EM is not merely a retrospective statistical tool but one of the prerequisites for batch release.

In addition, EM should also be performed during operator interventions to capture the contamination risk that intervention activities may introduce.

6.2.1 Special Monitoring Logic for Isolator Technology

An isolator physically separates operators from the product completely with a physical barrier, therefore:

  • Air, surface and personnel monitoring outside the isolator does not need to be performed after every filling operation
  • The isolator gloves and half-suits must be monitored at the end of each filling or each campaign, as this is the most critical contamination ingress route
  • Product contact surfaces should be monitored after batch-specific operations are completed, before they are sterilized or changed between batches

This difference reflects the risk-based principle of EM: the monitoring focus shifts according to the presence of a physical barrier.

6.3 Air Monitoring

Effectively designed air-handling units and use of HEPA filters or ultralow particulate air (ULPA) filters bring air of appropriate quality to cleanrooms. People are considered a major contributor of particulates, especially microorganisms, to the environment in aseptic areas.

Air monitoring determines if there are viable and/or total airborne particulates in locations that would allow them to settle on product-contact surfaces and thereby find their way into process intermediates or final product. The analysis of trends and frequencies is also vital to the understanding of the environmental impact of personnel behavior and process activities. Air-sampling methods and frequencies of monitoring will depend on manufacturing process needs and should be risk-based.

6.3.1 Total Airborne Particulate Monitoring

Monitoring of total airborne particulates is a necessary component of an EM program. It demonstrates control of potential contaminants in the environment to which the product is exposed during the manufacturing process. Classified areas must consistently meet particulate levels (see Section 3.0).

ISO Standard 14644-1 describes the classification of air cleanliness for cleanrooms based on specified concentrations of airborne particulates per volume of air sampled. The sample volume for routine monitoring does not need to be the same as that used for formal classification.

For the United States, FDA requires measurements of particles ≥0.5 μm. EU and PIC/S Annex 1 also include measurements for particles ≥5 μm for both classification and monitoring purposes.

For remote particle counter systems, the distance from the sample collection port to the detection technology should be considered for potential particle loss. The length of tubing should typically not exceed one meter and the radius should be greater than 15 cm, aligned with ISO 14644.

Commentary: The Dual Task of Air Monitoring

Air monitoring tracks two things at once: total particulates (non-viable + viable) and viable particulates. Only the two together give a complete assessment of environmental quality.

Personnel Are the Largest Source of Contamination

Even with HEPA filtration efficiency as high as 99.97%, personnel remain the main source of particles and microorganisms in a cleanroom. Each person can shed thousands to millions of particles per minute, and gowning that does not comply with requirements greatly increases this number.

This is why Personnel Behavior Monitoring and EM trend analysis must be combined: when EM shows an upward trend, the first thing to examine is often the personnel.

Important: Grade A (ISO 5) Requires Continuous Particle Monitoring

Regulatory requirement: Grade A (ISO 5) aseptic processing areas must be monitored continuously for particles (continuous monitoring) during operation.

Exception: if the operation itself generates a large number of product particles (such as powder filling) and these particles do not themselves pose a contamination risk, monitoring may be performed before and after exposure instead of continuously, because extraneous contamination cannot be distinguished from process-inherent particles in this case. Continuous monitoring may also damage the measuring equipment.

For remote counting systems, the tubing length must not exceed 1 meter and the bend radius must be greater than 15 cm (per ISO 14644).

Differences in Particle Size Requirements: US vs. EU/PIC/S

  • US FDA: ≥0.5 μm particle size (classification + routine monitoring)
  • EU Annex 1 / PIC/S: ≥0.5 μm and ≥5 μm (both must be monitored)

This is a market-specific design point; manufacturers in Taiwan that mainly export need to pay particular attention to the EU requirements.

6.3.2 Viable Particulates

Viable particulate determinations are critical in monitoring air quality in controlled environments. Traditional growth-based techniques for monitoring viable particulates in air are limited by:

  • Type of equipment
  • Growth media
  • Incubation time
  • Operator contamination of the capture media

6.3.2.2 Methods

Regulatory agencies currently require active air sampling of environments on a routine basis to demonstrate control of viable airborne particulates. Some agencies require that passive sampling methods such as settling plates should be performed, with justification provided if a company does not use passive sampling for select operations.

Active sampling methods have the benefit of sampling large homogeneous volumes of air in short amounts of time, but these methods may cause disruptions in the airflow of the environment being sampled. Therefore, these sampling methods should be included in airflow visualization studies.

Passive sampling methods can be used for prolonged periods (up to four hours) in close proximity to critical processes without affecting the airflow. An additional advantage of passive sampling is that it mimics the potential pathway of contamination to a critical process.

6.3.2.3 Equipment

Microbial air samplers are typically based on two principles — inertial or centrifugal impaction — to capture and deposit organisms onto the agar or filter surface. No microbial air samplers are 100% efficient at detection or collection of all viable particles present.

The physical efficiency of an air sampler is referred to as the "cutoff size" or "d50 value" — the size of a particle in microns that the sampler has a 50% chance of recovering — which is supplied by the equipment manufacturer.

Equipment user requirements include: calibration of the instrument and verification of volume accuracy.

Commentary: Comparison of Viable Air Sampling Methods

Meaning of the d50 Value

The d50 value is a physical efficiency indicator of an air sampler: it is the particle size that the sampler has a 50% probability of capturing (unit: μm). The smaller the d50 value, the smaller the particles the sampler can capture and the higher its efficiency.

This value is provided by the equipment manufacturer and is used to evaluate whether the equipment is suitable for a specific monitoring scenario. No air sampler can achieve 100% collection efficiency, which is one of the inherent limitations of conventional growth-based sampling.

Active samplers must be qualified, including instrument calibration and volume accuracy verification.

Active Sampling vs. Passive Sampling

Practical recommendation: use both together. Active sampling provides baseline quantitative data, while passive sampling reveals the dynamic risk of microbial fallout during operation.

6.4 Surface Monitoring

Surface monitoring is conducted to determine the bioburden of surfaces — walls, floors, ceilings, non-product-contact and product-contact surfaces of equipment — in the manufacturing area.

6.4.1 Monitoring Considerations

When periodic surface monitoring is performed, it should be done after the filling operation or a campaign has been completed so no extraneous contamination or residual growth media are introduced during filling activities. The basic methods provide surface monitoring data (qualitative or quantitative) that can be used to assess environmental quality.

Neutralizers are typically added to media to inactivate chemical disinfectant residues on surfaces. Recovery levels of surface-monitoring methods are typically low due to variability of surfaces, sampling procedures, and growth-based techniques.

6.4.1.1 Contact Plates

Contact plates are easy to use and can provide quantitative results (CFU per surface area). The plate is pressed against a flat surface by gently rolling from front to back. Prior to use, media and incubation conditions must be qualified.

The use of irradiated plates in ISO 5 areas is required and is recommended in ISO 7 and ISO 8 areas. Contact plates must be allowed to acclimate to room temperature before use. Contact plates are not suitable for irregular surfaces; swabs are recommended for that situation. Media residue must be removed after sampling.

6.4.1.2 Flexible Films

A flexible film containing media can be used similarly to a contact plate or to provide a defined sampling area. The film is pressed against a flat surface in a rolling motion. Like contact plates, flexible films leave a media residue that must be removed post-sampling.

6.4.1.3 Swabs

Swabs are employed for equipment and irregular surfaces not suitable for contact plates or flexible films. Swabbing may also be performed on flat surfaces using a template of approximately 2 in × 2 in (~25 cm²). Swabs are composed of Dacron® or nylon in flocked or spun format. Prior to plating, each swab is vortexed in an appropriate diluent (e.g., sodium chloride, phosphate buffer, or peptone solutions with neutralizers). Swabs may provide qualitative or quantitative results depending on how they are processed.

6.4.1.4 Surface Rinse Method

The surface rinse method is best used when a large surface area sample is required (e.g., kettles, equipment, trains, tanks). A sterile diluent is used as the rinsing fluid, collected from the interior surface, and tested by membrane filtration because of the large sample volume. The holding time of rinsing samples prior to plating should be qualified.

Commentary: Comparison of the Four Surface Monitoring Methods

Common Principles of Surface Monitoring

  • Timing: monitoring must be performed after the filling operation is finished, to avoid contaminating the product with growth medium
  • The medium must contain neutralizers to counteract the inhibitory effect of disinfectant residues
  • Recovery rates are generally low (affected by surface material, technique and analytical method), but consistent sampling technique produces data that can be trended
  • Trend analysis reveals the effectiveness of cleaning and disinfection procedures and the impact of manufacturing activities on the environment

Special Requirements for ISO 5 Areas

When using contact plates in ISO 5 (Grade A) areas, irradiated contact plates must be used; they are also recommended for ISO 7 and ISO 8 areas. Reason: irradiated plates are already sterile, which reduces the risk of introducing extraneous contamination through the contact plate itself.

6.5 Personnel Considerations

Personnel are the primary source of contamination, which is why all cleanroom personnel (including maintenance workers and others who enter periodically) must be carefully trained and qualified before entering an aseptic environment.

The qualification training should include:

  • Attention to personal hygiene
  • Basic microbiology education or background
  • Aseptic techniques
  • Appropriate cleanroom behavior
  • Patient safety hazards posed by a contaminated product
  • Gowning certification
  • Participation in aseptic processing simulation (APS or media fill) activities, where applicable

After initial training, personnel should participate regularly in an ongoing training and monitoring program. Routine microbiological monitoring of personnel garments and gloves should also be completed. Supervisory personnel should routinely evaluate each operator's conformance to written procedures. The quality unit should provide regular oversight.

6.5.1 Training and Certification of Personnel for Aseptic Manufacturing Areas

All training and certification activities should be documented and kept as part of the employee file.

6.5.2 Causes for Requalification or Retraining

Companies should have a policy stating when requalification or retraining is necessary, for example:

  • Retraining on general aseptic practices and techniques should be provided at least annually
  • Recertification should be required after extended absences (e.g., for illness or family leave)
  • Recertification and/or retraining should be considered as corrective action for trends in personnel monitoring excursions

Commentary: Personnel Control Is the Core of EM

Personnel Are the Cleanroom's Biggest "Contamination Generator"

TR13 clearly states that personnel are the primary source of contamination. This is not a theoretical assumption but the common root cause of countless contamination events.

Imagine a cleanroom as a hospital operating room: no matter how advanced the equipment is, the risk of infection rises immediately if even one member of the surgical team is careless with aseptic technique. Therefore, everyone entering the cleanroom, including maintenance personnel who enter only occasionally, must first complete training and qualification.

Seven Key Elements of Personnel Training

  1. Personal hygiene practices
  2. Basic microbiology education
  3. Aseptic technique
  4. Appropriate cleanroom behavior
  5. Awareness of the hazard that contaminated product poses to patient safety
  6. Gowning certification (Gowning Certification)
  7. Participation in aseptic process simulation (APS / Media Fill)

All training and qualification records must be kept in the employee's personal file.

Requalification Triggers

The company must establish a clear policy specifying the situations in which requalification or retraining is required:

  • At least once a year: refresher training in aseptic technique
  • After a prolonged absence (sick leave, parental leave, etc.): requalification before returning to work
  • Trend of personnel monitoring excursions: as part of corrective action (CAPA)

The third point is especially important: if personnel monitoring data show an upward trend, retraining is one of the most direct corrective actions.

6.6 Environmental Monitoring During Product Sterility Testing

Sterility-testing facilities should be monitored to demonstrate the absence of microbial contamination. Routine types of monitoring conducted are:

  • Viable air monitoring
  • Surface monitoring (including isolator gloves if applicable)
  • Personnel monitoring

Total airborne particulate monitoring is not mandatory in sterility testing environments; however, it may provide useful information regarding the HEPA filters in the ISO 5 testing environment or the integrity of isolators. Lab materials may generate particles that are not representative of actual manufacturing conditions, and there is no risk to marketed product in the testing environment.

Commentary: Special Considerations for Monitoring Sterility Test Environments

Why Is Particle Monitoring in Sterility Test Areas Not Mandatory?

The purpose of a sterility testing laboratory is to confirm whether a product is sterile, not to manufacture product, therefore:

  • Reagents, media and other materials used in the laboratory may themselves generate a large number of particles, but these particles do not represent actual manufacturing contamination
  • Even if particle counts are high, they pose no direct risk to marketed product
  • Therefore, although total particle monitoring is not mandatory, it is still useful for evaluating HEPA filter performance or isolator integrity

Viable air monitoring, surface monitoring (including isolator gloves) and personnel monitoring remain necessary, to ensure that the validity of test results is not compromised by environmental contamination.

6.7 Environmental Monitoring in Grade A Air Supply

The term "Grade A air supply" is introduced in Annex 1 and is specifically used to support the capping of fully stoppered vials until the cap has been crimped — providing additional protection without the requirement to meet all criteria related to a Grade A area.

Grade A air supply provides air passed through a filter qualified as capable of producing Grade A total particulate quality air, but where there is no requirement to perform continuous total particulate monitoring or meet Grade A viable monitoring limits, and the area itself is not classified.

As Grade A Air Supply is not a clean room category (e.g., according to ISO 14644), no guidelines for viable and total particulate limits exist. In order to confirm Grade A total particulate quality air is supplied, testing at the point of supply should be performed as a minimum during qualification and requalification.

At-rest viable (air and surfaces) and total particulate monitoring may be performed based upon a risk assessment. Routine monitoring, limits, and positioning should reflect the requirements for this "clean process" and be aligned with the contamination control strategy, meeting criteria applied for the background of the Grade A Air supply (usually Grade C, but minimum Grade D).

Commentary: A New Annex 1 Concept, Grade A Air Supply

What Is Grade A Air Supply?

EU Annex 1 (2022 revision) introduced the concept of "Grade A Air Supply", which is a functional concept rather than a classification grade. Its core purpose is to provide additional air quality protection during crimping of fully stoppered vials.

Key distinction:

  • Grade A Area: requires continuous particle monitoring + viable particle limits + room classification
  • Grade A Air Supply: the air quality meets Grade A particle standards, but continuous monitoring is not required, no viable limits are set, and the space itself is not classified

The background environment is usually Grade C, and must be at least Grade D.

Minimum Monitoring Requirements

Minimum confirmation requirement for Grade A Air Supply: testing at the point of air supply during qualification and requalification to confirm that Grade A total particle quality is achieved.

At-rest viable and total particle monitoring may be performed based on risk assessment but is not mandatory as routine monitoring. This gives manufacturers greater flexibility in compliant design without reducing the effectiveness of contamination control.

6.8 Water

This document addresses only the process monitoring of water for microbial attributes. Most countries have compendial requirements for evaluation of water quality. The recently published EMA Guideline for the Quality of Water for Pharmaceutical Use should be consulted as to the type of pharmaceutical-grade water used in manufacturing.

The type of water selected for manufacturing is based on the type of product and process. Water quality should be evaluated for microbial, bacterial endotoxin, and chemical attributes in accordance with applicable compendial or regulatory requirements. Water quality is critical for the manufacturing of parenteral drugs and selected devices because it is used in product formulation, component and equipment washing, and final rinsing.

The quality of laboratory water should meet the needs of analytical methods and technologies, which may require compendial-quality laboratory water.

Commentary: Monitoring Strategy for Pharmaceutical Water

Three Tiers of Pharmaceutical Water

  • Water for Injection (WFI): the highest quality requirement, used in the formulation of injectable products and for final rinsing of equipment. Bacterial endotoxin < 0.25 EU/mL, microbial limit NMT 10 CFU/100 mL.
  • Purified Water: used in the manufacture of non-injectable products, APIs and solid dosage forms. Microbial quality must be controlled to prevent contamination from spreading through the facility.
  • Clean Steam: used for sterilizing equipment and piping; it is tested by chemical and LAL tests, and microbiological testing is not recommended.

Main differences between USP and EP/JP:

  • USP: allows WFI to be produced by distillation or an equivalent or superior process (such as membrane filtration); EP traditionally allowed distillation only (since 2017, EP 10 also permits membrane filtration)
  • EP and ChP set a pH limit (5.0–7.0); USP has no pH requirement
  • EP sets limits for nitrate (0.2 ppm), nitrite (0.02 ppm), ammonia (0.2 ppm) and heavy metals (0.1 ppm); USP lists all as N/A
  • The ChP nitrate limit (0.06 ppm) is stricter than that of the EP

A Taiwanese manufacturer exporting to both the US (FDA) and Europe (EMA) must meet the stricter of the two sets of requirements.

6.8.1 Sample Site Selection and Frequency of Monitoring

The sampling and frequency of monitoring for water systems should be sufficient to ensure that water quality meets its intended use specifications and appropriate regulatory requirements. The frequency depends on the type of water, manufacturing process, system configuration, and facility design.

High-purity water systems should be validated to demonstrate that design requirements are consistently met and maintain a state of control. Site selection and frequency determination should be justified by thorough review of water system validation and risk-based approaches.

Once a water system is validated in a state of control, appropriate samples should be taken from:

  • Water source or generation system
  • Holding and storage tanks
  • Distribution system (all point-of-use sites for high-purity water systems)

Initial testing of water systems typically includes sampling after each process step.

6.8.2 Sample Collection and Testing

Water samples should be collected in a manner consistent with manufacturing practices. If manufacturing flushes use points prior to use, samples should be collected with the same flush cycle. All sample sites should be in a good state of repair and fully functional.

It is recommended to not use IPA on gloves or sample port prior to TOC sampling to avoid generating false positives. Microbiological examination of water should be initiated as soon as possible after sample collection. If immediate processing is not possible, samples should be refrigerated at 2–8°C. The time elapsed between collection and examination should be justified.

Three Core Principles of Sampling Strategy

  1. Representativeness: sampling points need to cover the full path from the source water through storage tanks to the distribution system. High-purity water systems (WFI) need to cover all points of use.
  2. Consistency with manufacturing practice: if the water lines are flushed before manufacturing operations, sampling should follow the same flushing steps. The reverse also applies.
  3. Risk-based frequency: the frequency must be sufficient to detect trend changes in chemical attributes, microbial flora and endotoxin.

Important Details of Sampling Operations

  • Do not use IPA before TOC sampling: alcohol residue on gloves or sampling ports can cause false-positive TOC results and lead to unnecessary investigations.
  • Process samples promptly: if they cannot be tested immediately, they should be refrigerated at 2–8°C, and the time interval from sampling to testing should be validated.
  • No microbiological testing of clean steam: a high-temperature steam environment does not support microbial growth, so only chemical and LAL testing are needed.

Differences in Media and Incubation Conditions Among Pharmacopoeias

R2A medium (Reasoner's 2A) is a low-nutrient medium that is particularly suitable for slow-growing, heat-sensitive oligotrophs in water, and better reflects the actual microbial composition of the water.

6.9 Compressed Gas Monitoring

The use of compressed air and compressed gas in the aseptic ISO 5 environment may adversely affect environmental conditions if appropriate precautions, routine testing, and critical assurances are not developed into a system.

Using a 0.2 μm filter on the compressed gas line prior to entry into the cleanroom is common practice. Integrity testing of the filter and sanitization of the line post-filtration may reduce the need for sampling.

The sampling equipment must be compatible for use with compressed gases. A pre-filter may be installed in the system to reduce the bioburden to the point-of-use filter, extending its life and reducing the possibility of higher contamination reaching the area.

The particulate or bioburden level of any gas or air expelled into the environment should be equal to or better than the classification level of the room.

Commentary: Key Control Points for Compressed Gas Monitoring

Contamination Risks of Compressed Gases

Compressed air and compressed gases (such as nitrogen and CO₂) are blown directly into ISO 5 aseptic areas and, if not properly filtered, can become a route for introducing particles and microorganisms.

Standard protective measures:

  • Install a 0.2 μm filter before the gas enters the cleanroom
  • Perform periodic filter integrity testing
  • Sanitize the piping downstream of the filter
  • Install a pre-filter to reduce the bioburden on the final filter and extend its service life

Core Regulatory Requirements

Gas or air blown into the environment must have particle or bioburden levels that are equal to or better than the classification grade of that room. For example, compressed gas used in an ISO 5 area must have a discharge quality of ISO 5 or better.

Sampling equipment must be compatible with compressed gases; some viable air samplers can also be used for compressed gas sampling through an adapter.

This section briefly describes the scope of prerequisites for EM. Successful environmental monitoring programs depend on extensive foundational work completed before routine monitoring begins.

Interpretation and Application

Why are the prerequisites so important?

Environmental monitoring is not as simple as installing the equipment and starting to sample. TR13 explicitly lists 16 prerequisite activities, stressing that the EM program must be built on a complete foundation of facility qualification, media qualification, personnel training and so on, so that the monitoring data are scientifically meaningful.

Analogy: the foundation of a building

The 16 prerequisites are like the foundation work of a building. If the foundation is unstable (for example, media that have not passed GPT, sampling equipment that has not been calibrated, or inadequately trained analysts), the EM data that follow are like a structure built on sand: there are data on the surface, but they cannot represent the true condition of the environment, and they will not withstand an inspection.

Color-coded interpretation

The 16 prerequisites can be grouped into four categories by nature:

  • Facility foundation (red): facility design, HVAC IQ/OQ, HEPA validation, airflow measurement, PQ (items 1, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16)
  • Media and strains (orange): supplier audit, media selection/release/shelf life, selection and maintenance of microbial strains (items 2–7)
  • Sampling workflow (green): sample holding and transport, validation of the incubation strategy (items 8–9)
  • Equipment and personnel (blue): equipment qualification, analyst training (items 12–13)

Common inspection findings

The most common FDA 483 observations related to EM prerequisites include:

  • Media GPT not performed with environmental isolates (only ATCC reference strains used)
  • Sample transport time/temperature not supported by a hold-time study
  • Sampling equipment (such as impaction air samplers) not calibrated for flow rate at defined intervals
  • Analyst qualification records incomplete, or analysts not periodically retrained

7.1 Facility Design

Separate or defined areas of operation in an aseptic processing facility should be appropriately controlled to attain different degrees of air quality depending on the nature of the operation. Design of a given area involves satisfying microbiological and particle criteria as defined by:

  • The equipment, components, and products exposed
  • The operational activities conducted in the area

Facility design is a crucial component of good environmental control.

ISPE Baseline Guide, Vol. 3: Sterile Product Manufacturing Facilities

Describes the design, construction, commissioning, and qualification of sterile manufacturing facilities. Similar baseline guides are available for other types of product manufacturing.

ISO 14698 (superseded by BS EN 17141:2020)

Two-part series providing extensive guidance on contamination control. ISO 14644-1 provides methods to assess control of air, surfaces, and liquid contamination.

Interpretation and Application

7.1 Facility design: the physical basis of EM

Facility design determines the upper limit of the environmental grade a cleanroom can achieve. No EM program, however good, can compensate for the structural contamination risk created by design flaws.

Important standard update

ISO 14698 (biocontamination control in cleanrooms) has been replaced by BS EN 17141:2020. The core monitoring principles of the old and new versions are the same, but 17141:2020 places more emphasis on risk-assessment-driven design of the sampling plan, which is consistent with the overall approach of TR13.

EM-related elements of facility design

  • Pressure differential cascade: positive pressure from higher grade to lower grade areas, to prevent ingress of external contamination
  • HEPA filter efficiency: ISO 5 / Grade A areas must achieve H14 class (≥99.995%)
  • Air changes per hour (ACH): determines the rate at which airborne contamination is diluted and removed
  • Airflow pattern: laminar flow vs. turbulent flow directly affects how representative the EM sampling locations are
  • Surface materials: cleanability affects the recovery rate of surface EM

Practical tip

Before a new facility is commissioned, the commissioning & qualification (C&Q) process of ISPE Baseline Guide Vol. 3 and the 16 prerequisites of TR13 form a closed loop: facility design documents → construction acceptance → IQ/OQ/PQ → baseline sampling → start of the formal EM program.

7.2 Selection of Microbiological Culture Media for EM Testing

Soybean-casein digest medium (SCDM) or trypticase soy agar (TSA) is the standard choice for EM, supporting growth of a wide range of bacteria, yeast, and molds.

Media Selection Decision Tree

  • SCDM/TSA — universal first choice; supports bacteria, yeast, and mold
  • + Neutralizing agents — add when surface monitoring is performed in areas exposed to sanitizing agents or antibiotics
  • Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) — use when fungal contamination is specifically suspected; note that SCDM already supports yeast/mold growth
  • Anaerobic media — consider only in special circumstances: risk assessment justification, prior recovery of anaerobes, or sterility / APS failure attributed to anaerobes
  • Tryptic soy broth (TSB) — used for swabs in Grade A environments (qualitative results only)

Initial Media Qualification Requirements

  • Validate fertility is not affected by desiccation at end of maximum allowed exposure period
  • Where neutralizers are required, confirm suitability for the applied disinfectants/agents
  • Confirm suitability over the supplier's full shelf life
  • Perform growth promotion testing (GPT) using low levels of microorganisms from the manufacturing environment and recognized culture collections
  • Document use of environmental isolates in an approved rationale

Routine Media Testing Requirements

  • Justify and document the QC strategy for EM media, including sterility checks and negative controls
  • Perform GPT on each delivery of a given batch
  • Consider reduced testing based on risk assessment, including supplier control confidence and supplier quality agreement (SQA)

Interpretation and Application

7.2 Media selection: the core of EM data quality

The selection and qualification of media directly determine which microorganisms EM can detect and at what recovery. Using inappropriate media is like monitoring the environment with a detector that has blind spots.

SCDM vs. SDA: when are both needed?

TR13 notes that SCDM by itself can already support the growth of yeasts and molds. SDA is therefore only a supplementary option, appropriate when a specific fungal contamination is suspected. If a firm routinely uses both SCDM and SDA, the SOP should provide the scientific rationale and state under which circumstances SDA is used.

Unless there is a specific reason, avoid using dual media at all EM sampling locations, because this greatly increases media cost and incubation workload.

Why neutralizers are necessary

In surface sampling (contact plates or swabs), residual disinfectants (such as IPA, quaternary ammonium, hydrogen peroxide) can inhibit microbial growth and lead to false negative results. Therefore:

  • Surface monitoring media should contain an appropriate neutralizer (for example, a lecithin + polysorbate 80 combination)
  • The neutralizer itself must be validated: it must not inhibit target microorganisms or leave toxic residues
  • The neutralizer should be selected to match the disinfectants actually used on site

Practical requirements for media GPT

TR13 emphasizes that GPT should use "low levels" of inoculum, typically <100 CFU, to simulate the very low microbial counts found in the actual environment. If GPT is performed with a high inoculum (>1000 CFU), the result may appear to pass even when the medium performs inadequately.

The GPT strain panel should include:

  • Pharmacopeial reference strains (ATCC/NCTC)
  • The site's own environmental isolates, which need to be refreshed periodically to reflect the current flora

7.3 Selection of Microorganisms for Growth Promotion

Selection of microorganisms for growth promotion should be established according to relevant pharmacopeial guidance, with consideration of USP <61>, based on the media to be evaluated.

Microorganisms selected should include:

  1. Those required by the appropriate compendial chapter (e.g., USP <61>)
  2. Representative environmental isolates — selected based on microbial profiling assessment using data from routine trending reports

For a new facility, organisms initially recovered from cleanroom qualification activities can be used as the environmental isolate component.

USP <1117> Microbiological Best Laboratory Practices

A microbiological culture maintenance program should be established that includes a procedure for testing and release with appropriate documentation of used passages.

Interpretation and Application

7.3 Selection of growth-promotion microorganisms

The GPT strain list is not set once and then left unchanged. TR13 explicitly requires that the representativeness of environmental isolates be re-evaluated periodically based on trending reports.

Lifecycle of GPT strain management

  • Establishment phase: use pharmacopeial reference strains plus isolates recovered during cleanroom qualification
  • Stable phase: based on routine EM trends, evaluate annually or quarterly whether the environmental isolates need to be updated
  • Update triggers: appearance of a new species, a change in the flora of environmental isolates, or investigation results from an Action Level excursion
  • Passage control: record the number of passages each time in accordance with USP <1117> to avoid strain drift

Common finding: using only pharmacopeial strains

The GPT SOPs of some firms list only ATCC reference strains (such as Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538 and Aspergillus brasiliensis ATCC 16404) and do not include environmental isolates.

This does not meet the expectations of TR13 and USP <61>, and is a common FDA 483 observation. The reason is that reference strains cannot represent the sensitivity of the environmental microorganisms actually present in the facility to the medium.

7.4 Sample Holding Studies and Transport

EM samples are sensitive to handling and storage conditions, which are deemed critical parameters. These conditions are affected by:

  • Time of sampling
  • Temperature of storage during transport

Sample hold studies should be performed to validate the sample handling from end of sampling through initial incubation.

Care should be used if transferring EM media using sanitizing airlocks (e.g., through sanitizing airlocks equipped with UV lights).

Interpretation and Application

7.4 Sample hold-time studies and transport

The period between the end of sampling and the start of incubation is a potential gap in EM data integrity. Without a hold-time study, there is no assurance that the microorganisms captured during sampling remain viable and countable until incubation.

Key design points of a hold-time study

  • Scope: the complete chain from the end of sampling to placement in the incubator, including waiting time inside the aseptic area, time through the airlock, and transport time to the laboratory
  • Temperature conditions: should simulate the worst case, such as summer room temperature or the high temperature of a particular production area
  • Maximum hold time: should equal or exceed the longest interval that could occur in actual operations
  • Acceptance criteria: counts after the hold should not be significantly lower than those of the control incubated immediately

Potential risk of UV airlocks

The material transfer airlocks in some facilities are fitted with UV lamps to sterilize the surfaces of materials entering and leaving. TR13 specifically warns that when EM media pass through such airlocks, UV radiation may kill the microorganisms already captured and lead to false negative results. An alternative transfer route should be considered, or the UV lamp should be switched off during transfer.

7.5 Environmental Monitoring Sample Incubation Strategy and Validation

Guidance documents and industry practices differ regarding incubation conditions. Information about appropriate incubation times and optimum temperature conditions is limited or sometimes conflicting.

Standard Incubation Conditions

Industry Survey Finding

Industry surveys have shown a majority of companies using a two-temperature incubation with the lower temperature first (i.e., incubate at 20–25°C first, then transfer to 30–35°C).

Multiple Incubation Schemes

Multiple incubation schemes can be employed if scientifically justified and validated. Validation studies typically use:

  • Laboratory-based in vitro studies with cultured microorganisms
  • ATCC reference strains combined with in-house environmental isolates
Studies such as those published by Gordon et al. demonstrate that there may be a difference between in vitro and in situ testing (Reference 37).

Interpretation and Application

7.5 Incubation strategy: one of the most controversial EM topics in the industry

The design of incubation temperature and time directly affects the sensitivity of EM detection. The industry currently follows a variety of practices, and TR13 does not mandate a single scheme but requires "scientific validation".

Dual-temperature incubation: why low temperature first, then high?

The industry survey shows that most companies use the sequence "20–25°C first (5–7 days), then 30–35°C (48–72 hours)". The scientific basis for this is:

  • In cleanroom environments, yeasts and molds are usually harder to detect than bacteria (slower growth, smaller colonies)
  • Allowing fungi to grow fully at the lower temperature first, and then raising the temperature to promote bacterial growth, reduces the risk of bacterial colonies overgrowing fungal colonies
  • If the higher temperature is used first, fast-growing bacteria may dominate the plate and mask fungal colonies

Important differences between in vitro and in situ

The work of Gordon et al. (Ref. 37) reminds us that results optimized under laboratory incubation conditions do not necessarily predict the performance of samples from the actual environment. The reasons include:

  • Environmental microorganisms may be in a damaged or sublethally injured state
  • Environmental conditions (disinfectant residues, desiccation) affect the vitality of strains
  • Laboratory strains (ATCC) are usually more vigorous than actual environmental isolates

Incubation validation studies are therefore advised to include "environmental isolates under stressed conditions" as challenges.

Documentation requirements for incubation strategy validation

Whichever incubation scheme is adopted, it must be supported by a validation study report and documented in the EM SOP or the method validation document:

  • Scientific rationale for the chosen temperature/time combination
  • Comparative data against alternative schemes
  • Sensitivity assessment (the lowest level of organisms that can be detected)
  • Change management: any change to incubation conditions requires revalidation

7.6 Environmental Monitoring Equipment Qualification

Assessment may be required to determine gaps in equipment qualification. The following items should be considered as prerequisite:

  • Equipment and instrument listing — comprehensive inventory of all EM equipment
  • Documentation requirements — qualification protocols, certificates, maintenance records
  • Maintenance and calibration — including air volume accuracy requirements for active air samplers
  • Tube length requirement — for total airborne particulate monitoring equipment (particle counters)
  • Data collection, transmission and storage — alarm conditions and audit trail requirements
  • Installation and Operational Qualification (IQ/OQ) of EM equipment

Interpretation and Application

7.6 Qualification of EM equipment

Qualification of EM equipment ensures that the data collected are reliable, reproducible and traceable to known standards.

Key qualification points for critical equipment

Active air samplers

  • The sampling flow rate (typically 100 L/min) must be calibrated periodically with a calibrated flow meter (NIST-traceable)
  • Calibration frequency should be set according to frequency of use and the manufacturer's recommendation (typically every 6–12 months)
  • Inaccurate flow = incorrect actual sample volume = distorted CFU/m³ values

Particle counters

  • The length of the sampling tubing (isokinetic probe) affects particle counting accuracy
  • Tubing that is too long or has too many bends causes deposition of large particles, resulting in under-counting
  • ISO 21501-4 specifies calibration requirements for particle counters

Data integrity requirements

Modern EM systems increasingly use electronic data collection and automatic transmission. TR13 specifically mentions data collection, transmission, storage and alarm conditions, echoing the expectations of the FDA data integrity guidance (2018):

  • Electronic records must not be deleted or overwritten (the audit trail must be enabled)
  • Alarm conditions (Alert/Action Level triggers) should be recorded automatically
  • The data backup strategy should be included in the scope of EM system validation

7.7 Microbiology Analyst or Operator Training and Qualification

Appropriate training curricula and qualification requirements should be established for EM analysts or operators. This entails:

  • Relevant, cohesive standard operating procedures (SOPs), including gowning and gowning qualification for aseptic areas
  • A good understanding of the procedures and their importance in aseptic manufacturing operations
  • Understanding of the impact or risk to quality for not adhering to these procedures
Analyst qualification is not merely procedural compliance — it reflects the analyst's understanding of why each step matters for product quality and patient safety.

Interpretation and Application

7.7 Analyst training and qualification

Personnel (analysts/operators) are the most important and also the most easily overlooked variable in EM. No matter how well media, equipment and incubation strategy are designed, they cannot make up for human error introduced by improper sampling technique.

EM analyst qualification process

  • Initial training: EM principles → SOPs for each sampling method → equipment operation → aseptic technique
  • Gowning qualification: before sampling in aseptic areas, personnel must pass an assessment of aseptic gowning technique (including fingertip / glove impression plates)
  • Sampling technique verification: observe actual sampling operations to confirm that technique conforms to the SOP (for example, the pressure applied to contact plates, the area sampled with swabs, and equipment placement)
  • Periodic retraining: annual reassessment is recommended, and retraining should also be triggered by procedure changes
  • Record retention: the training matrix must be completely documented and available for inspection and audit

The microbiologist's "risk awareness"

TR13 specifically stresses that analysts need to understand "the quality risks and impact of not following procedures". This goes beyond passively following the SOP to actively understanding the GMP background of every step, for example:

  • Why must media not be left at room temperature after sampling for longer than the specified time?
  • What contamination could improper aseptic technique introduce at the moment a contact plate is opened?
  • When an Action Level is exceeded, what action should be taken first?

9.0 Appendix 1: Qualification of Cleanrooms (Classified Areas)

Purpose, Scope & Procedure Overview — p43–p44

9.1 Purpose & 9.2 Scope

This appendix provides a consistent methodology for qualification of cleanrooms (classified areas) used for the manufacture, processing, packaging, holding, and testing of sterile drug products, biologics, and medical devices for human use.

EU and WHO designations for cleanroom classifications are used throughout (Grades A–D), with ISO equivalents added where appropriate.

Scope focus: Activities necessary to provide a high degree of assurance that cleanrooms meet requirements for product quality. Formal qualification (GMP) should center on:

  • Final HEPA filtration (including ductwork beyond the final filter)
  • Monitoring of area parameters to ensure cleanroom requirements are met

HVAC systems (air handlers, pre-filters, controls) have an indirect impact on product quality and should be commissioned rather than formally qualified under GMP protocols.

In scope: Isolators and unidirectional (laminar) airflow units providing Grade A air within a larger cleanroom.

Out of scope: Freezers, refrigerators, stability chambers, and sterilization tunnels (though test methods for airflow, filter testing, and air classification may be directly applied).

IQ — Installation Qualification
Installed and conforming to design specifications
↓
OQ — Operational Qualification
Functions correctly under at-rest conditions
↓
PQ — Performance Qualification
Continues to meet standards under dynamic (production) conditions

Commentary

The three-stage structure of cleanroom qualification

PDA TR13 Appendix 1 divides cleanroom qualification into three progressive stages, consistent with the general framework for pharmaceutical equipment validation:

  • IQ (Installation Qualification): confirms that the equipment has been installed to specification, the components are correct and the instruments are calibrated.
  • OQ (Operational Qualification): tests, under "at rest" conditions, whether the environmental parameters meet their targets (e.g., particle counts, differential pressure, temperature and humidity).
  • PQ (Performance Qualification): confirms that the environment continues to meet requirements under "in operation" (dynamic) conditions, with personnel, equipment and routine operations present.

Analogy: cleanroom qualification is like inspecting a newly built building

IQ is like confirming that the building materials match the construction drawings (Is the right rebar used? Are the pipes in the correct places?)

OQ is like testing the plumbing, electrical and air-conditioning systems in the empty building (With the air conditioner running in an empty room, can the temperature reach the set point?)

PQ is like confirming that everyday use is trouble-free once people have moved in (With people living there and appliances running, is the air conditioning still sufficient?)

The core of GMP is that quality cannot be assured by design drawings alone; it must be confirmed with measured data.

Key point: HVAC commissioning vs. formal GMP qualification

TR13 clearly distinguishes "commissioning" from "qualification":

  • Fan speed, pre-filters and coil performance → commissioning is sufficient
  • Terminal HEPA filters and particle monitoring → formal GMP qualification is required

This distinction reflects the difference in risk to product quality between "direct impact" and "indirect impact" systems.

9.3.1

9.3.1 Installation Qualification (IQ)

Critical Component Checklist — p44–p45

IQ Requirements Checklist

IQ verifies that critical system components meet user requirements and have been properly identified, installed, and calibrated. IQ requirements may be confirmed through inspection or by referencing commissioning/FAT documentation.

Items to address during IQ include:

  • Description, location, and use of cleanrooms/classified areas to be qualified
  • Verification of type, range, and placement of sensors and alarms for environmental parameters having a direct effect on product quality
  • Verification of location of final system filters for areas with airborne viable and/or total particulate limitations (Grades A–D)
  • Verification of material and proper installation of ductwork downstream of the final HEPA filter
  • Completion of a commissioning plan, including a test and balance report, approved by the system user and responsible engineering group
  • Verification of critical system components: terminal air filters, instruments, and alarms — identifying manufacturer, model number, size/range/efficiency, and materials
  • Verification of instrument calibration certifications in compliance with site calibration procedures
  • Software/firmware title, version, and installation verification (may be in separate RMS protocol)
  • Transfer of equipment/instrument information to computerized maintenance management system (CMMS)
  • Confirmation that maintenance procedures and/or manuals are in place

Note: Where a room monitoring system (RMS) includes monitoring sensors, recording, and alarm functionality, those components may be documented in a separate protocol.

Commentary

The core logic of IQ: documented evidence of "installed to specification"

The question IQ answers is: "Has this system been installed correctly according to the design specification?"

The checks fall into three categories:

  • Hardware checks: location of terminal HEPA filters, ductwork material, instrument models and specifications
  • Calibration checks: all measuring instruments have valid calibration certificates
  • Documentation checks: software versions, maintenance manuals and equipment lists are on file

Key detail: ductwork downstream of the terminal filter

TR13 specifically stresses verifying the material and installation method of the "ductwork downstream of the terminal HEPA filter".

Reason: even if the filter performs properly, unsuitable downstream ductwork (for example, poor-quality material that sheds particles) can still cause contamination. This is a frequently overlooked check point.

The RMS (room monitoring system) may have a separate protocol

Modern pharmaceutical plants typically deploy an RMS that integrates particle counters, temperature and humidity sensors, differential pressure gauges and alarm systems. TR13 allows the IQ of the RMS to be set up under its own protocol rather than being folded entirely into the cleanroom IQ document, giving more flexibility in document management.

Analogy: IQ is like a new-car delivery checklist

IQ is like the PDI (Pre-Delivery Inspection) when a car is handed over: checking item by item that all equipment is present, the odometer reads zero and every system works. It is not a road test (that is OQ/PQ); it first confirms that "everything was installed correctly."

Key logic for reading the OQ test matrix

The design of Table 9.3.2-1 embodies the "risk-proportionate principle": the higher the grade (Grade A is in direct contact with product), the stricter the requirements and the more tests are included:

  • Air Flow Velocity: required only for Grade A. Reason: Grade A is unidirectional flow, and velocity is critical to protecting open product; Grade B/C/D use turbulent flow, so velocity is not measured.

  • Recovery Time: N/A for Grade A, because Grade A uses unidirectional flow and, in theory, contamination is swept away immediately; Grade B/C/D use turbulent flow, so the system must be shown to "self-clean" within a defined time.
  • Smoke Study: mandatory for Grade A/B (to confirm that airflow does not create eddies in the product area); optional for Grade C/D (based on risk analysis).

OQ after changes: test only the affected items

TR13 emphasizes that re-confirmation after a change does not require the full test set to be repeated. For example:

  • Replacing a HEPA filter: only air flow velocity/distribution, filter differential pressure, leak testing and room classification need to be tested; temperature, humidity and differential pressure do not need to be retested.
  • Modifying the filling line geometry (within Grade A): the smoke visualization study must be repeated.

This reflects the core philosophy of TR13: "let risk analysis determine the scope of testing," avoiding unnecessary repeat testing.

Definition of the at-rest condition for OQ

"At rest" means: the HVAC equipment is installed and commissioned, other equipment is in place and operating, but there are no operating personnel (other than the necessary test personnel) and no product or materials.

This ensures that OQ results reflect the capability of the HVAC system itself, excluding interference from personnel activity (the effect of personnel is assessed in PQ).

Commentary

The core thinking of FMEA: predicting where failures will occur

The question FMEA asks is: "If this requirement is not met, what happens? How severe is it? How likely is it? Can we detect it?"

The EM FMEA in TR13 is specifically designed with three scoring dimensions:

  • SEV (Severity): the impact of a deviation on sterility and regulatory compliance
  • OCC (Occurrence): the likelihood that the location is actually contaminated with microorganisms
  • DET (Detectability): whether the existing EM program can detect potential contamination

- SEV is 3 in both cases (serious if anything goes wrong)

         well-established sampling procedure → low probability of contamination (1)
- DET: no monitoring → hard to detect (2)
         monitoring in place → easy to detect (1)

  • The stopper bowl is an indirect product contact surface (the stoppers go into the vials)
  • Operators perform multiple interventions nearby
  • Without active air sampling and surface monitoring, contamination events may be missed

This reminds us that even a "closed" RABS does not mean every location is already covered by the EM program; each high-risk location must be systematically reviewed using FMEA.

The BioPhorum model: "mapping" the filling line

The innovation of the BioPhorum model is to break the filling line down into a 4×4 grid (16 zones), turning risk assessment from a qualitative description into a quantifiable map (heat map).

This upgrades EM program design from "experience-based judgment" to a "systematic, defensible decision": the choice of monitoring locations is supported by data rather than intuition alone.

Why is "proximity to open product (O)" weighted ×2?

Of the six risk factors, only "Proximity to open product (O)" is given double weight. The reasoning:

  • The other factors (difficulty of cleaning, personnel traffic, etc.) are indirect risks
  • Airborne particles/microorganisms have an immediate chance of contaminating sterile product only when they are directly adjacent to open product or product contact surfaces
  • Therefore, even if an area has few interventions, its risk score rises significantly as long as it is right next to open product

What does the unusually high score of Zone 11 (512) tell us?

Zone 11 (conveyor belt) has a risk score as high as 512, far above the other zones, because:

  • It is difficult to clean (C=4, hard-to-reach dead spots)
  • It is less than 12 inches from open product (O=8, double weight)
  • It requires multiple manual interventions to clear stopper jams (CI=2, FI=4)

This indicates that the area needs the most rigorous EM monitoring, and may need to be redesigned to reduce the frequency of interventions.

The role of the EM table template (Table 10.0-3)

After risk scoring is complete, the EM table template converts the scores into concrete monitoring decisions:

  • At which time points monitoring is needed (during set-up, start/end of batch, surfaces after end of batch)
  • Which type of monitoring (total particulates vs. viable organisms)
  • The final decision on whether the zone needs monitoring at all

This forms a complete logic chain from "risk score" → "monitoring design" → "EM program execution."

10.1 Example of an HACCP Applied to EM Programs

HACCP Definition & Seven Principles (Figure 10.1-1)

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is a formal qualitative risk assessment technique designed to identify critical steps in a process that require control and monitoring to ensure hazards are not realized. For EM purposes, the hazards analyzed are:

  • Biological: viable monitoring (microbial contamination)
  • Physical: total airborne particulates

HACCP Seven Principles — Applied to EM Program Design

  1. Conduct a Hazard Analysis
    Identify all potential sources of microbial and particulate contamination in the manufacturing area; assess their likelihood and severity.
  2. Determine Critical Control Points (CCPs)
    Identify locations where monitoring is critical to prevent or detect contamination events. Not every location is a CCP — use a structured decision tree (Q1–Q4).
  3. Establish Critical Limits
    Define the EM alert levels and action limits (viable & non-viable) for each CCP location.
  4. Establish Monitoring Procedures
    Define what will be monitored, how often, and by whom at each CCP.
  5. Establish Corrective Actions
    Define the investigation and CAPA response when critical limits are exceeded (i.e., when alert or action levels are exceeded).
  6. Establish Verification Procedures
    Confirm that the HACCP system is working effectively — e.g., trend analysis, periodic HACCP review, and EM data review.
  7. Establish Record-Keeping and Documentation
    Maintain records of HACCP decisions, monitoring results, and CAPAs.

Commentary

The core question of HACCP: "Where are the critical control points?"

The essence of HACCP is using a "decision tree" to screen out the critical control points (CCPs). Not every potential hazard point is a CCP; HACCP screens them with four questions (Q1–Q4):

  • Q1: Is sampling at this location a cGMP requirement?
  • Q2: Is sampling at this location critical to protecting patient safety or product quality?
  • Q3: Can the monitoring data from this location support a robust deviation investigation?
  • Q4: Taking the above together, is this location a CCP?

In the Table 10.1-1 example of a Grade C cell culture room, all analyzed hazard points (Q4) were ultimately judged to be CCPs, but the value of HACCP lies in being able to identify which locations can have their monitoring frequency reduced (low risk + no history of problems).

Analogy: HACCP is like designing airport security

Airport security does not place security staff in every corner; it identifies "critical control points" (boarding gates, security checkpoints) and concentrates controls there. HACCP follows the same logic: rather than sampling everywhere, it applies the strictest monitoring where it truly matters.

FMEA, by contrast, is more like running a "what if...?" fault-finding exercise on the whole airport system, identifying all possible vulnerabilities and fixing the most severe first.
