Document Overview — PDA PtC-15

PDA Points to Consider No. 15 was published in 2025 by the Parenteral Drug Association. It addresses one of the most consequential shifts in pharmaceutical manufacturing: the move from permanently fixed, site-built facilities to deployable, relocatable manufacturing units.

The document is structured as a series of questions and considerations across three topics:

  • Topic 1 — Technology Considerations: Facility build options, MMU advantages and challenges, implementation, validation, and technology transfer
  • Topic 2 — Regulatory Considerations: Impact of current regulations; actions regulators can take to facilitate adoption
  • Topic 3 — Implementation: How to validate an MMU at a new location

Authors represent major industry stakeholders: G-CON Manufacturing, Lupin Pharmaceuticals, Genentech, On Demand Pharmaceuticals, Parexel, NIIMBL (Ex-FDA), Pfizer, Emergent BioSolutions — ensuring the document reflects both industry practice and regulatory perspectives.

"Mobile manufacturing is the ability to relocate manufacturing while maintaining an environment suitable for good manufacturing practice (GMP) production."
— PDA PtC-15, Introduction, p.1

Reading the Document in Context

PDA "Points to Consider" documents are guidance (not mandatory standards) that give industry a framework for thinking about specific technical topics. PtC-15 focuses on "mobile manufacturing", a manufacturing paradigm that has been seriously re-examined since the COVID-19 pandemic.

Why Does This Document Matter Especially for CDMOs?

  • The CDMO business model inherently requires flexible manufacturing capability, whether serving different clients and products or responding quickly to urgent demand
  • Building a traditional fixed facility is very expensive (often hundreds of millions of US dollars) and slow (3–5 years); mobile manufacturing offers an alternative path with a shorter capital cycle
  • Global supply chain risks (pandemics, geopolitical conflict, natural disasters) have turned "movable manufacturing capacity" from an ideal into a necessity

Why the Authorship Matters

The authors come from regulatory agencies (former FDA/NIIMBL), large multinational pharmaceutical companies (Pfizer, Genentech), a CDMO (Parexel), and a mobile manufacturing equipment supplier (G-CON Manufacturing). This mix ensures the document considers technical feasibility, regulatory compliance, and commercial applicability in full.

Introduction — Historical Context

The manufacturing of drugs has historically been fixed in the building in which it is processed and therefore, manufacturing was conceptualized as fixed in nature. Specifically, the conventional construction of such manufacturing facilities has been executed on the site and viewed as immobile. Such construction has often been termed stick-built or been called brick-and-mortar facilities.

This type of construction has been the dominant method in our industry throughout the past. However, the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the need for new thinking and a more flexible, modern, and scientifically sound approach to the platform design of the manufacturing process, thereby ensuring the essential manufacture, distribution, and availability of medicinal products.

While some concepts like modular facility construction have been implemented, only recently has a significant advancement in technologies allowed alternative methods, such as mobile manufacturing facilities, to be used in its place.

Mobile manufacturing is the ability to relocate manufacturing while maintaining an environment suitable for good manufacturing practice (GMP) production. This paper focuses exclusively on biotech and pharmaceutical manufacturing.

Historical Context

Analogy: Restaurant vs. Food Truck

A traditional pharmaceutical plant is like a fixed restaurant: the kitchen equipment is permanently installed in one building, and all infrastructure (power, water, ventilation, storage) is integrated into the building structure.

Mobile manufacturing is like a food truck: the kitchen equipment is movable and can be driven to wherever demand is, yet it must still meet the same food safety standards.

A closer analogy is the MASH field hospital (Mobile Army Surgical Hospital), which rapidly deploys complete surgical capability wherever it is needed. The pharmaceutical version of MASH is the MMU.

Stick-built facility (also called brick-and-mortar): a traditional facility constructed on site by various trades (carpenters, electricians, masons, etc.). Once built, it cannot be relocated.

COVID-19 was the key historical turning point. Vaccine demand jumped from zero to billions of doses within weeks, but fixed-facility construction timelines simply could not keep up, forcing industry to think seriously about "how to make manufacturing capacity deployable like software."

Introduction — Technology Evolution

The use of self-contained, mobile manufacturing units enables the relocation or replication of a manufacturing facility between different geographic locations, which allows manufacturers to rapidly supply and scale up product manufacturing.

Examples of manufacturing strategic improvements over time include the adoption of cleanrooms (first introduced in 1962), which are now a requirement for GMP manufacturing. Prior to that time, it was difficult to control the manufacturing environment, and risks from particulates and adventitious agents were common. Since then, cleanrooms have become the status quo and are a requirement for GMP manufacturing.

The development of prefabricated elements presaged mobile manufacturing. The preassembled, prefabricated solutions which altered how manufacturing facilities were built on-site also seeded the concept of manufacturing mobility as it became common to ship these semi-completed facilities across the world.

Within the past 20 years, prefabricated technologies have been introduced that can be easily prequalified, disassembled, and relocated elsewhere. These are the technologies evaluated within this document.

The process equipment used to manufacture pharmaceuticals is prefabricated and prequalified (via factory acceptance testing) off-site prior to delivery at the facility, which lends support to the overall concept of mobile manufacturing.

Technology Evolution

PDA uses a clear technology evolution timeline to show that mobile manufacturing did not appear suddenly but is the natural convergence of several maturing technologies:

The Central Role of FAT/SAT in Mobile Manufacturing

Factory Acceptance Test (FAT): the initial qualification testing of equipment performed at the manufacturer's premises.

Site Acceptance Test (SAT): confirmation testing performed after the equipment arrives at its final installation site.

Conventional thinking holds that qualification must take place in the "final facility." One core idea of the MMU is to transfer FAT results portably to a new location, avoiding a full repeat of validation activities. This directly challenges the existing regulatory framework and is the central question for the later sections of PtC-15.

Introduction — Key Drivers

Another major advancement in the evolution of mobile manufacturing feasibility was the introduction in the 1970s of single-use components, which have matured dramatically since that time and provided greater flexibility in facility design.

Accompanying the advances in technology, the size of manufacturing facilities and equipment has decreased over time, driven both by:

  • Process intensification — achieving the same or greater output with smaller equipment footprints
  • Personalized medicines — targeting smaller patient populations, reducing the need for large-scale batch production

Geopolitical concerns have also been a driver of distributed manufacturing, as many countries look for in-country manufacturing. Similarly, pharmaceutical and biotechnical companies want to mitigate the risk to their assets by having the ability to move their production capabilities in the event of wars, natural disasters, or other crises.

Lastly, regulatory barriers within the drug production landscape have also lessened over time to accommodate the concept of manufacturing mobility.

The Three Drivers in Depth

Driver 1: Single-Use Technology (SUT)

The revolutionary aspect of single-use technology is that it removes cleaning validation from the manufacturing process. Traditional stainless steel equipment requires extensive cleaning, sterilization, and re-qualification; single-use equipment is disposable after use, greatly simplifying cross-contamination control between batches and between sites.

For an MMU, this means cleaning validation does not need to be repeated for each deployment, which lowers the regulatory complexity of mobile manufacturing.

Driver 2: Geopolitical Risk

After COVID-19, governments realized that production of critical medicines cannot be highly concentrated in a single country or region. Demand for "In-Country Manufacturing" has risen sharply.

For a CDMO, being able to offer an "MMU that can be rapidly deployed within the client's own country" will become an important competitive differentiator.

Driver 3: Process Intensification and Personalized Medicine

Traditional high-volume production (such as billions of vaccine doses a year) requires massive facilities. But gene therapies, cell therapies, and personalized cancer vaccines often target patient populations of only dozens to thousands. Small batches, high flexibility, and high variety are the new production requirements, which fits the MMU's capabilities well.

Scope of PDA PtC-15

This document aims to assess the feasibility and requirements for designing and qualifying a self-contained manufacturing process that, once validated, can be relocated and operated in a different location for the same process, without the requirement for regulatory preapproval or revalidation.

The scope includes:

  • Exploring the technology supporting mobile manufacturing in greater detail
  • The regulatory requirements and risks associated with moving a manufacturing process from one location to another

While mobile manufacturing can be considered an enabling technology for distributed manufacturing or point-of-care manufacturing, the scope of this paper excludes identifying or elaborating on those requirements.

"This document aims to assess the feasibility and requirements for designing and qualifying a self-contained manufacturing process that, once validated, can be relocated and operated in a different location for the same process, without the requirement for regulatory preapproval or revalidation."
— PDA PtC-15, Scope, p.2

Why Scope Definition Matters

One key phrase in the PtC-15 scope statement deserves repeated attention:

Core objective: "without the need for new pre-approval or revalidation"

Traditional regulatory requirement: when a validated process is moved to a new site, it usually requires a new approval filing to the regulatory authority and full revalidation at the new site.

The question PtC-15 explores is: can a system be designed so that manufacturing capability validated at Site A can be moved to Site B and continue production without new approval?

This is a very bold goal that touches the fundamental assumptions of current GMP regulations, and it is the core question that the entire Topic 2 (regulatory considerations) chapter addresses.

Metaphor: Standardization of the ISBU (Shipping Container)

The revolutionary aspect of the ISO shipping container unit (ISBU) is not that it "holds a lot of cargo" but its standardization: at any port, the dock equipment can handle containers of the same specification.

The ideal state of the MMU is similar: a standardized manufacturing unit designed to perform as expected at any qualified location, without redoing "customized" qualification work for each site.

Excluded scope: "Distributed Manufacturing" and "Point-of-Care Manufacturing" are broader concepts involving more complex regulatory frameworks. PtC-15 deliberately limits its scope to "movable, fully qualified manufacturing units" to keep the discussion focused.

Decision Hierarchy for Mobile Manufacturing

Although not stated explicitly as a numbered hierarchy in PtC-15, the document's structure and emphasis make clear that decisions in mobile manufacturing must follow a priority order. Any business case for mobility must be subordinate to product quality and regulatory compliance:

  1. Product Quality & Patient Safety — GMP compliance must be maintained regardless of whether the manufacturing environment is fixed or mobile. The cleanroom, utilities, process controls, and environmental monitoring must all perform identically whether the unit is in Site A or Site B.
  2. Regulatory Compliance — Validation portability, multi-jurisdiction requirements, and the ability to satisfy different national competent authorities are non-negotiable prerequisites before deploying to a new location.
  3. Business Flexibility — Speed-to-market, pandemic response capability, CDMO capacity management, and new business models are legitimate and important drivers — but they are pursued within the constraints established by levels 1 and 2.

Decision Priority Framework

Level 1: Product Quality and Patient Safety
GMP compliance is never compromised at any location
↓
Level 2: Regulatory Compliance
Portability of validation, multi-jurisdiction requirements
↓
Level 3: Business Flexibility
Speed, cost, CDMO business model

What this priority framework means in practice:

  • Site qualification steps cannot be skipped because "business needs are urgent"
  • Environmental monitoring standards after relocation cannot be lowered because of "cost considerations"
  • Regulatory uncertainty must be considered at the technical design stage (not fixed after the fact)

Implications for CDMOs

A common mistake when a CDMO pitches MMU services is to lead with "fast and low cost" (Level 3) and overlook what clients care about most: "Will my product quality be the same after the move?" (Level 1) and "Will regulators accept it?" (Level 2).

The right communication order is to establish the quality assurance framework first, and only then discuss business value.

Q1.1

What are the available options to build a facility?

What Are the Facility Construction Options? | PDA PtC-15, p. 9

Original Text — PDA PtC-15 (pp. 5, 9)

The means to fabricate or build a GMP-compliant facility can be categorized into two main groups: on-site construction and prefabricated solutions.

One method for on-site construction involves conventional stick-built or brick-and-mortar facilities. This involves various trades (e.g., carpenters, electricians, masons) being on-site and using stock materials cut or manipulated on-site based on build plans. Another approach to on-site construction is modular components, where materials are built off-site but assembled on-site. Both options provide a fixed facility which cannot be moved to an alternate location if needed.

Prefabricated solutions allow for the build-out of completed components assembled off-site. Being built off-site entails certain aspects of mobility, and the level of completeness or percentage of work done off-site typically lends itself to the ability to redeploy the facility elsewhere. However, certain prefabricated solutions, once installed, become fixed in place and are not easily relocated.

To be considered a mobile manufacturing platform, the ease of redeployment becomes a prerequisite. The two prefabricated units that are inherently mobile will be referred to as mobile manufacturing units (MMUs).

"Mobile manufacturing is the ability to relocate manufacturing while maintaining an environment suitable for good manufacturing practice (GMP) production."

— PDA PtC-15 (2025), Introduction

Manufacturing has historically been conceptualized as fixed in nature — stick-built or brick-and-mortar was the dominant model. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the need for a more flexible, modern, and scientifically sound approach to facility design, ensuring the manufacture, distribution, and availability of medicinal products under crisis conditions.

Key enabling factors for mobile manufacturing include:

  • Prefabricated elements — shipped semi-complete across the world
  • Single-use components (introduced in the 1970s, matured significantly since)
  • Process intensification — smaller equipment footprints
  • Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) — qualification off-site prior to delivery
  • Reduced geopolitical risk through distributed manufacturing

Commentary - Overview of Facility Construction Options

Three Construction Approaches - A One-Line Comparison

Stick-built (traditional): Workers build on site from raw materials, piece by piece. The most common approach, but the slowest to build, and it cannot be moved.

Modular: Modules are prefabricated in a factory and assembled on site. Faster, but once fixed in place they cannot be relocated.

MMU (Mobile Manufacturing Unit): The whole facility can be dismantled, transported, and reassembled while maintaining its GMP classification. This is the focus of the document.

Analogy: Building a Plant Is Like Building a House

Stick-built = a traditional brick house: once built, it stays there forever.

Modular = a prefab house: pre-made panels are assembled on site, but once the foundation is laid it is fixed.

MMU = a luxury shipping-container home: fully equipped inside and able to be dismantled; in Taiwan today, in Singapore next year, with exactly the same function.

The MMU concept comes from the same idea: a standardized shipping container can be handled at any port in the world, and a pharmaceutical MMU should likewise be deployable at any GMP-compliant site.

Key Criterion: The Capability for "Ease of Redeployment"

The PDA document states explicitly that the prerequisite for being a "mobile manufacturing platform" is ease of redeployment.

Practical meaning: if your prefabricated module needs major civil works to be removed after installation, it is not an MMU; it is just a faster conventional plant.

COVID-19 as a Catalyst

The pandemic clearly exposed the fragility of fixed manufacturing: the shutdown of a single plant can disrupt supply globally.

One design philosophy behind the MMU is "distributed supply chain resilience": when a crisis hits one location, the same GMP manufacturing capability can be deployed rapidly at another location without building a new plant.

Q1.2

What are the advantages and challenges associated with building a mobile manufacturing unit?

Advantages and Challenges of the MMU | PDA PtC-15, pp. 5-8

Advantages of Mobile Manufacturing Units

The PDA PtC-15 document identifies multiple strategic advantages driven by technological evolution:

1. Faster Construction Timeline
Because MMUs are fabricated off-site in parallel with site preparation activities, overall project timelines are compressed significantly compared to conventional construction. Equipment qualification via Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) occurs before delivery, reducing on-site commissioning burden.

2. Pre-Qualification Off-Site (FAT)
Process equipment is prefabricated and prequalified off-site prior to delivery at the facility. This is described as a major lend-support to the overall concept of mobile manufacturing. FAT at the vendor's facility reduces uncertainty at the deployment site.

3. Relocatability
The core value proposition: a qualified MMU can be disassembled and reassembled into its functional state without incorporating any major rework. Geopolitical concerns — in-country manufacturing requirements, natural disasters, wars — are directly addressed by this feature.

4. Scalability
Additional MMU modules can be added to increase manufacturing capacity without constructing a new facility. The distributed manufacturing (DM) model networks multiple units operating under a central remote Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS).

5. Lower Capital Risk
Compared to a full stick-built facility investment, MMU capital expenditure is more modular and can be phased. For personalized medicines targeting smaller patient populations — a growing segment — smaller footprints are appropriate.

6. Single-Use Technology Integration
The maturation of single-use components (introduced in the 1970s) enables MMUs to avoid the complexity of permanent stainless steel CIP/SIP infrastructure, reducing the footprint and turnaround time between products.

Challenges of Mobile Manufacturing Units

1. Limited Space Constraints
MMUs are inherently compact — typically shipping-container-based configurations. Equipment integration complexity increases when fitting full GMP-grade operations (cleanroom, HVAC, utilities, personnel flow) into a confined volume. Worker ergonomics in confined space is a distinct challenge versus traditional facilities.

2. Utility Hook-Up Complexity
At each deployment site, the MMU must connect to site utilities: water for injection (WFI), clean steam, compressed air, electrical power, chilled water, nitrogen. The quality and reliability of these supplies varies by site and must be independently assessed each time.

3. Multi-Site Validation and Regulatory Submissions
While the goal is to avoid revalidation, each new location creates regulatory change events. The strategy for whether a move triggers a site change supplement or can be managed under a prior approval supplement must be defined in advance and agreed with regulatory agencies.

4. Environmental Control Variability
External temperature and humidity fluctuations at each new deployment site affect the performance of the MMU's HVAC systems. A unit qualified in a temperate climate may require HVAC requalification when moved to a tropical or arctic environment.

5. Worker Ergonomics
The confined working environment of an MMU poses challenges for personnel performing manual operations. GMP-compliant gowning, material flow, and waste removal all become more complex in a compressed physical footprint.

Commentary - Analysis of MMU Strengths and Weaknesses

Core Advantages: Why Is the MMU Being Taken Seriously Now?

This is not just a technological breakthrough, but the result of three long-term trends maturing at the same time:

  • Single-use technology (emerging in the 1970s, mature by the 2010s) removed the need for large stainless steel infrastructure
  • Process intensification shrinks the equipment footprint needed for the same output
  • Personalized medicine makes small-batch, multi-site manufacturing commercially viable

Together, these three trends have turned "fitting an entire GMP plant into a container" from science fiction into an engineering problem.

Analogy: A MASH Field Hospital vs. a Fixed Hospital

The MASH (Mobile Army Surgical Hospital) of the Korean War was the military's "mobile manufacturing": a full surgical suite could be set up anywhere within 24 hours, delivering care close to the quality of a fixed hospital.

Its challenges map exactly onto those of the MMU:

  • Limited space: equipment and workflows must be highly streamlined
  • Unstable local power and water: utilities must be self-supplied or the local utilities assessed
  • Changing environmental conditions (desert, cold regions): temperature control capability must be re-evaluated

A pharmaceutical MMU faces the same problems, except that the "sterile surgical environment" is replaced by a "GMP aseptic manufacturing environment."

Biggest Challenge: Regulatory Filing Strategy - Early Planning Is Essential

The most complex issue for the MMU is not engineering but regulatory. Every time an MMU is moved to a new location, it may trigger a "site change," and under the FDA 21 CFR 314/601 framework a site change may require:

  • Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): FDA approval is required before the move, and the wait may be 6-18 months
  • Changes Being Effected (CBE-30): The change may be implemented 30 days after notification
  • Annual Report: The lowest-impact category of change, reported after the fact

Ideally, the MMU is designed so that each move falls under CBE-30 or even Annual Report. This requires designing the validation strategy in advance, at the time of the ANDA/BLA submission, rather than fixing it afterward.

Analogy: The Challenge of a Pop-Up Restaurant

A three-Michelin-star chef opens a pop-up restaurant: the same team of cooks, the same equipment, and the same recipes deliver exactly the same quality of food in different cities.

The list of challenges is almost identical to the MMU's:

  • The kitchen is smaller than a fixed restaurant: workflow and staffing must be redesigned
  • Local water quality differs: additional filtration or treatment is needed
  • Local voltage and gas pressure differ: equipment needs adjustment
  • Local health authorities have different requirements: additional applications are needed

The best pop-up chefs perform a "new site assessment" before every move; the MMU likewise needs a standardized Site Readiness Assessment.

Q1.3

How does one implement and validate a mobile manufacturing unit?

How Do You Implement and Validate an MMU? | PDA PtC-15, pp. 6-8

Validation Strategy for MMUs

The MMU validation framework builds on established pharmaceutical qualification principles while addressing the unique challenge of portability. The PDA PtC-15 document articulates a risk-based approach to validation that distinguishes between elements that are stable across relocations and elements that must be re-assessed at each new site.

Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) — Primary Qualification Strategy

FAT is described as the primary off-site qualification strategy for MMUs. The vendor performs FAT at their facility before delivering the MMU to the initially qualified site. Key activities at FAT include:

  • Verification of equipment installation against design specifications (IQ scope)
  • Functional testing of all systems under representative conditions (OQ scope)
  • HVAC performance testing: air change rates, pressure differentials, temperature/humidity uniformity
  • Utility systems: WFI, clean steam, compressed air, clean-in-place (CIP) and steam-in-place (SIP) where applicable
  • Documentation completeness review: calibration records, instrument qualification, SOPs

The MMU vendor performs initial qualification of major facility systems (water, air), but not product- or process-specific aspects of validation. Those remain the responsibility of the pharmaceutical manufacturer.

Site Acceptance Testing (SAT) — At Each New Location

Upon arrival at each new deployment site, a SAT verifies that the MMU has survived transport without damage and that site utilities are correctly connected and performing to specification. SAT scope is typically a subset of the original FAT, focused on:

  • Physical integrity check after transport
  • Utility connection verification (correct connections, correct supply quality)
  • Critical system re-verification: HVAC, WFI, clean steam
  • Environmental monitoring baseline at the new location
  • Personnel qualification for site-specific procedures

Risk-Based Re-Qualification: What Re-Validates at Each New Site?

The central question for MMU deployment is: what must be repeated, and what carries over? A risk-based approach evaluates each element based on whether relocation plausibly affects it:

  • Carries over (no re-qualification needed): Equipment-level IQ/OQ for systems with no site-dependent parameters; process validation data for parameters not affected by environmental conditions
  • Must re-qualify: HVAC performance in new climate; environmental monitoring data (microbial and particulate baseline); site utility quality (water, steam); personnel gowning qualification for new facility layout

Family Approach to Equipment Qualification

The family approach concept applies when multiple identical or very similar MMUs are operated. After the first unit is fully qualified, subsequent units of the same design can undergo reduced qualification, demonstrating functional equivalence rather than repeating all protocols in full.

Continuous Process Verification and Regulatory Filing

After initial process validation at the on-site (initially qualified) location, continued process verification (CPV) provides the ongoing data stream to demonstrate the process remains in control across sites. The regulatory filing strategy must be established before first deployment — specifically, whether a move to a new site constitutes a site change supplement requiring prior approval, or can be filed as a lower-impact change.

Commentary - MMU Validation Strategy

The Core Validation Question for an MMU: "What Is Still Valid After the Move?"

The logic of traditional pharmaceutical validation is: at a fixed location, validate completely once, then maintain the validated state.

The MMU upsets this assumption: the facility itself moves. The validation strategy must answer one fundamental question:

"Does this validation conclusion still hold at the new site?"

Where the answer is "yes" -> carry it over (validation portability)
Where re-confirmation is needed -> repeat it on site (SAT scope)

Analogy: "Phone Factory Testing" vs. "Swapping the SIM Card"

Imagine the MMU as a high-end smartphone:

  • FAT = factory testing: every function is fully tested at the factory to confirm it meets specifications
  • SAT = switching it on at a new location: you confirm the SIM card is inserted correctly, the WiFi connects to the right network, the voltage is stable, and calls are free of noise

You do not need to retest every chip in the phone, but you must confirm that "overall function is normal" in this new environment. The FAT results can be carried over, while the SAT confirms that it "still works properly once connected to the new environment."

Family Approach - Validation Relief for Identical MMU Models

If your company deploys 3 MMUs of identical specification, then once the first is fully validated, the second and third can undergo "reduced qualification":

  • Only functional equivalence needs to be confirmed
  • There is no need to repeat all of the full qualification protocols
  • Precondition: design, construction, materials, and configuration must be truly identical

This concept comes directly from the Family Approach in equipment qualification, and PDA PtC-15 explicitly applies it at the MMU level. For companies deploying many MMUs at scale, this is a significant saving in time and resources.

Risk Assessment Framework: Criteria for "Site Dependency"

To decide whether a validation item must be repeated at the new site, ask this question:

"Could the performance of this parameter be affected by geographic location, climate, local utilities, or personnel?"

  • The answer is "no" -> the validation conclusion can be carried over
  • The answer is "yes, but the impact is predictable and within the validated design range" -> on-site confirmation is needed, but a pass is expected
  • The answer is "yes, and the impact exceeds the original design boundaries" -> a full repeat is needed and a regulatory filing may be triggered

HVAC Is the Most Commonly Repeated Item - Why?

HVAC system performance is highly dependent on external environmental conditions:

  • Taiwan summer at 35°C and humid vs. German winter at -5°C and dry: for the same HVAC system, the dehumidification and heating loads in the two environments are completely different
  • If the original design did not consider this temperature and humidity range, the HVAC may be unable to maintain the cleanroom specification
  • This means an MMU that "follows the molecule" must consider the harshest deployment environment at the design stage and use it as the basis for HVAC sizing

Q1.4

How can mobile manufacturing facilitate technology transfer?

How Does Mobile Manufacturing Facilitate Technology Transfer? | PDA PtC-15, pp. 7-8

MMU as a Technology Transfer Vehicle

One of the most compelling applications of mobile manufacturing is its use as a technology transfer vehicle. In conventional pharmaceutical development, technology transfer from a developer's facility to a CDMO or commercial manufacturing site involves extensive documentation, equipment qualification, and process re-demonstration — all conducted on equipment at the receiving site that may differ from the originator's.

The MMU model fundamentally changes this dynamic: the manufacturing equipment travels with the process. The same hardware, the same SOPs, the same validated parameters are physically moved to the new location, eliminating the equipment-related sources of variation that conventionally complicate technology transfers.

CDMO Application: Run the Process at the Client Site

A CDMO operating an MMU can deploy its qualified manufacturing unit to the client's site, producing product under the CDMO's validated process within the client's facility boundary. This model offers:

  • Reduced intellectual property (IP) risk for the innovator — the CDMO's process "comes to them" rather than requiring disclosure of proprietary formulations to a third-party facility
  • Client oversight of manufacturing without requiring the client to build or license their own facility
  • The CDMO maintains control of its quality system and equipment, reducing the risk of process deviation during transfer

Phase I to Phase II to Commercial: Same MMU Follows the Molecule

Perhaps the most transformative application is following a drug candidate through its development lifecycle with the same MMU:

  • Phase I: MMU deployed at clinical trial site or investigator facility; small batch sizes; emphasis on process characterization
  • Phase II: MMU relocated to larger clinical center or CDMO hub; same equipment, expanded batch sizes, continued process understanding building
  • Phase III / Commercial: MMU moved to commercial manufacturing location; process validation executed; no equipment-change-driven re-validation of process parameters already established

The key regulatory advantage: because the equipment itself has not changed, the data generated in Phase I and Phase II remains directly relevant to the commercial filing. There is no need to re-demonstrate that the process works on new equipment — only that the new site conditions do not perturb the established process.

Knowledge Management and Documentation Portability

Technology transfer success depends heavily on knowledge transfer. The MMU model supports this through:

  • Documentation portability: Batch records, SOPs, deviation history, and qualification packages travel with the unit — there is no "receiving site SOP translation" exercise
  • Personnel continuity: Operators trained on the MMU at the originating site can travel with the unit to the receiving site, at least for the initial production campaigns
  • Process history integrity: Since the same equipment is used throughout development, the process history is continuous and unbroken — a significant advantage in regulatory submissions that require a coherent process narrative
"The self-contained, mobile manufacturing unit enables the relocation or replication of a manufacturing facility between different geographic locations, which allows manufacturers to rapidly supply and scale up product manufacturing."

— PDA PtC-15 (2025), p. 5

Distributed Manufacturing and Point-of-Care Considerations

While PDA PtC-15 explicitly excludes point-of-care (POC) manufacturing from its scope, it acknowledges MMU as an enabling technology for POC and distributed manufacturing (DM). In a DM model, multiple MMUs are networked under a central remote PQS, potentially allowing a product to be manufactured simultaneously or sequentially at multiple geographic locations — a direct response to geopolitical supply chain fragility.

Commentary - The MMU as a Technology Transfer Vehicle

The Core Pain Point of Traditional Technology Transfer

One of the leading causes of failed traditional technology transfer is "rebuilding the process on new equipment."

Even if you hand over the formulation, process parameters, and SOPs in full, the receiving site's equipment still differs from the equipment used in the original development:

  • Different mixing tank geometry -> different flow fields -> mixing performance may differ
  • Different filtration system -> compatibility testing must be redone
  • Different filling machine model -> filling parameters must be revalidated

The MMU's solution is straightforward: take the equipment along, eliminating equipment differences as a variable.

Analogy: A Chef Brings Their Own Knives to the Client's Kitchen

When a top chef works in a different kitchen, they usually bring their own knife set. The reason is that all of their cutting technique and force habits are calibrated to that set; with unfamiliar knives, even with an identical recipe, the precision of the knife work declines.

Technology transfer is the same: all of the process development engineer's process knowledge was built on specific equipment. Letting that equipment follow the process gives technology transfer its highest chance of success.

The CDMO's MMU is this "personal knife set": instead of handing the recipe to the client, you bring the chef and the cookware together into the client's kitchen.

The Development Advantage of "Following the Molecule"

This is the key insight of Q1.4 and is worth understanding in depth:

Traditional development path (equipment changes):

  • Phase I: small laboratory equipment (lab scale)
  • Phase II: pilot-scale equipment, requiring scale-up bridging studies
  • Phase III: commercial equipment, requiring bridging again, often raising new regulatory questions

MMU development path (equipment unchanged):

  • Phase I: the MMU produces small batches next to the clinical trial center
  • Phase II: the same MMU moves to a larger clinical center and scales up the batch size
  • Phase III: the same MMU moves to the commercial manufacturing site, with no equipment bridging needed

Regulatory advantage: the Phase I and Phase II data directly support the commercial application, because the equipment has not changed.

IP Protection: "Equipment Goes to the Client" vs. "Technology Shown to the Client"

For many drug developers (innovators), one of the biggest concerns is: when I hand the process over to a CDMO, is there a risk that my core IP (formulation, critical process parameters) will leak?

The MMU-based CDMO model offers an interesting solution:

  • The CDMO brings the entire MMU to the client's site and manufactures under the client's oversight
  • The client can participate throughout, ensuring process fidelity
  • The CDMO maintains its own quality system and equipment, without handing every detail over to the client's facility
  • The innovator does not need to build its own plant, nor fully disclose the formulation to a third party

This model is especially attractive for orphan drugs and personalized medicine, where batches are small and sites are dispersed.

Analogy: Restoring a Phone Backup vs. Setting Up a New Phone

Traditional technology transfer is like getting a new phone and then manually reinstalling every app and setting: in theory you can end up with exactly the same result, but the process is time-consuming, error-prone, and often leaves things out.

MMU technology transfer is like restoring a complete iCloud backup from the old phone to the new location: everything is there, and you only need to confirm that the network and power connections at the new site are working.

Note, however: just as a new SIM card number needs to be set up again, the MMU still needs a SAT at the new site to confirm the "local connections." Eliminating on-site work entirely is not a realistic goal.

Knowledge Management: Documents and People Move Together

Knowledge management for MMU technology transfer has three dimensions:

1. Explicit Knowledge - batch records, SOPs, validation reports -> move in full with the MMU's drives/systems, with no translation or rewriting needed

2. Tacit Knowledge - the operator's feel for the equipment and early awareness of equipment abnormalities -> addressed by sending people along, with the original site's staff leading initial production

3. Process History - all batch, deviation, and CAPA records are in the same system, so a regulatory review can be given a complete, coherent narrative of how the process evolved

Key Terms & Glossary

Official Definitions (PDA PtC-15)

Mobile Manufacturing
The manufacturing of pharmaceutical products at off-site locations via qualified manufacturing units.
Mobile Manufacturing Unit (MMU)
A deployable manufacturing unit that has been qualified to make a specific set of pharmaceutical products, regardless of location. It is designed so that it can be easily disassembled and reassembled into its functional state without incorporating any major rework.
Distributed Manufacturing (DM)
A decentralized manufacturing strategy consisting of a manufacturing platform comprising manufacturing units deployed to multiple locations.
Initially Qualified Site
The first site where a mobile unit is qualified for producing a specific pharmaceutical product or set of products prior to deployment to other locations. Usually a fixed facility of the pharma company that developed the product.
MMU Vendor
The company that assembles the mobile manufacturing units and provides them to the initially qualified site. Performs initial qualification of major facility systems (water and air), but not product- or process-specific aspects of validation.
Family Approach
A concept applied to equipment qualification where a set of identical or very similar pieces of equipment are subjected to reduced qualification after an initial member of the set is fully qualified.
On-site
The site where the MMU is fully qualified (initially qualified site) for manufacture of a pharmaceutical product. Usually a fixed site at a pharma company.
Off-site
The site where the MMU is deployed for product manufacture after initial qualification.

Glossary - English

Mobile Manufacturing

The ability to manufacture drug products at an off-site location using a qualified manufacturing unit. The emphasis is on being "off-site" while maintaining a GMP-compliant environment.

Mobile Manufacturing Unit (MMU)

A deployable manufacturing unit that has been qualified and can manufacture a specific drug product at any location. Key design requirement: it can be dismantled and reassembled to a functional state without major modification.

Distributed Manufacturing

A decentralized manufacturing strategy in which multiple manufacturing units are deployed at different locations, typically managed centrally by a remote PQS.

On-site vs. Off-site - Easily Confused Terms

In the PDA document, On-site refers to the location where the MMU was initially fully validated (usually the pharmaceutical company's fixed facility), while Off-site is any location where the MMU is subsequently deployed to manufacture.

Note: here "off-site" does not carry its everyday meaning of "away from the plant"; it means "any location other than the initial validation site." Every new deployment site is off-site, even if it is itself a full pharmaceutical plant.

Responsibility Boundary of the MMU Vendor

The vendor (MMU Vendor) is responsible for:

  • Assembling the MMU
  • Initial validation of the water systems (WFI, purified water)
  • Initial validation of the HVAC system

The vendor is not responsible for the following (these are the pharmaceutical company's responsibility):

  • Product-related validation (PQ, process validation)
  • Process development and validation for the specific drug product

This division of responsibility is very important, as it determines who bears ultimate responsibility for product quality.

Q 2.1

How do current regulations impact the adoption of mobile manufacturing?

Original Text — PDA PtC-15 (pp. 14–15)

Current regulatory expectations with respect to facilities used in the manufacturing of drugs and biologics is that they are required to be designed in compliance with cGMPs. At a high level, processing should permit a smooth flow of materials through the facility. Space must be provided for receiving, quarantine, sampling, and storage of all manufacturing materials, including intermediate and finished bulk drug substance.

More specifically, facility builds or modifications need to ensure compliance with a host of regulations, such as the U.S. FDA Title 21, Code of Federal Regulations, the EMA Commission Decision 2021/1240 of 13 July 2021, and the equivalent requirements of other global regulators. Appendix A provides examples of applicable compliance expectations.

As a requirement of licensure, marketing authorization holders (MAHs) must provide information on the manufacturing locations, such as the address (and potentially GPS coordinates), that will allow the relevant regulatory agencies to identify the manufacturing location. The location needs to hold a manufacturing license and a GMP certificate. In some jurisdictions, the facility locations and specific GMP activities (e.g., analytical testing, storage, manufacturing) must be registered and assigned specific identification and status by the regulators.

For example, the FDA assigns a Firm Establishment Identifier (FEI) number to all facilities, and the identification of the facility or site is linked to an address and specific business entity (e.g., DUN & Bradstreet number). If multiple locations are listed in the dossier (i.e., the application), then this is required for each location. The requirements to file and register facility-specific information, including addresses and site contacts, are in large part linked to the regulators, enabling them to triage inspections.

In an ideal case of mobile manufacturing, the MMU could move from one physical location to another, and the license and certificate would only be required once. However, there are no provisions in current regulations for such a scenario. Moreover, in the United States, many state boards of pharmacy require registration for in-state drug manufacturers, presenting additional complexity of rapidly moving across state lines.

In addition, the movement of the MMU could create logistical challenges for regulators when making decisions about where and when they would need to deploy resources to conduct GMP inspections. Also, the full manufacturing process may be segmented in different locations (e.g., warehousing and the quality control of raw materials may be centrally located for stocking of one or a set of MMUs).

Finally, some countries have multiple agencies that would first need to agree to accept the license and certificate from another agency within their country. Typically, countries will not have provisions to accept such licenses from other agencies. The situation becomes even more convoluted and complicated if the MMU is expected to cross country borders.

Key Points Explained: Study Guide

Core barrier: regulations were designed for "fixed facilities"

The foundational assumption of current cGMP is that a manufacturing facility is a permanent building at a fixed address. From FDA's 21 CFR 210/211 to the EU GMP Directive of the EMA, everything is built around an "address-bound" licensing model.

The essence of a mobile manufacturing unit (MMU) is that its location can change. This directly conflicts with the fundamental assumption of current regulations and creates a systemic barrier.

Key Regulatory Concepts

  • MAH (Marketing Authorization Holder): the company that holds the marketing authorization for a medicinal product and bears ultimate responsibility for product quality.
  • FEI number: a unique identifier FDA assigns to each manufacturing facility, tied to its physical address and the company's D&B number. An MMU would need a new FEI every time it moves, which is extremely complicated in practice.
  • Manufacturing license + GMP certificate: a dual certification requirement. In the EU, a GMP certificate is issued after an inspection, and one is needed for each site.
  • State Boards of Pharmacy: a state-level regulatory layer unique to the US. An MMU moving across states would face the requirements of 50 different state regulations.

Analogy: Driver's License vs. Vehicle License Plate

Imagine a foreign chef who brings a complete set of kitchen equipment to open pop-up restaurants in different cities. His chef qualifications (technical competence) are recognized, but in every city he must apply again for a "restaurant business license", because the license is tied to the address, not to the chef or the kitchen equipment.

MMUs face exactly the same regulatory problem: GMP certification is issued to "this building at this address", not to "this manufacturing system".

Checklist of Multi-Layer Regulatory Barriers

  • Federal level: a new FDA FEI number must be requested (for every relocation)
  • State level: each of the 50 states has different pharmacy regulations
  • International level: separate GMP certification by EMA, PMDA, MHRA and others
  • Multi-agency problem: in some countries, several authorities must coordinate before a license is recognized
  • Inspection resource problem: authorities find it hard to schedule GMP inspections because the site location is not fixed

These five layers of barriers compound one another, which explains why the regulatory pathway for MMUs remains unclear in practice.

Site Registration Mechanics

FDA Dossier Filing Requirements (US)

  • NDA / BLA / ANDA: All marketing applications must list each manufacturing site with its physical address. Any site change requires a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS), Changes Being Effected (CBE-30), or annual report, depending on risk level.
  • Drug Establishment Registration: Required annually under 21 CFR 207. Each domestic establishment must register before manufacturing.
  • Foreign Establishment Registration: Foreign manufacturers must also register and pay fees under the PDUFA/GDUFA framework.
  • FEI Number Assignment: Linked to a specific physical address. Relocation triggers a new FEI application process.

EMA Site Change Variation Procedures (EU)

  • Type IA / IB / II Variations: Manufacturing site changes are classified as variations. A new primary manufacturing site is typically a Type II Major Variation, requiring advance approval before implementation.
  • Manufacturing Authorisation: Each EU member state issues a Manufacturing Authorisation (MA) for each site. Cross-border MMU movement requires new MAs in each country.
  • GMP Certificate: Issued after successful inspection by national competent authority (e.g., MHRA, BfArM, ANSM). A new site requires a new inspection and new certificate.
  • Annex 1 (2023): Updated sterile manufacturing guidance does not contemplate MMU scenarios.

ICH Q10 — Pharmaceutical Quality System

ICH Q10 establishes the Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS) framework. Its site independence requirements mean:

  • Each site must have its own fully functional QMS.
  • Quality oversight, batch release authority, and deviation management must be site-specific.
  • For an MMU, replicating a complete QMS in a compact mobile unit and maintaining site independence at each new location is operationally challenging.

If an MMU relocates 4 times a year across 3 different regulatory jurisdictions:

Number of filings = 4 relocations x 3 jurisdictions x 1-2 filing types
                  = 12-24 regulatory filings / year

Analogy: Chain Restaurant Franchise Standardization

McDonald's can quickly open a new branch in any city in the world because its "standardized equipment + standardized processes" have already been certified. But if every new branch required a fresh 12-month food safety review, the McDonald's business model would collapse completely.

The ideal vision for MMUs is exactly this kind of "standardized platform certification", but the current regulations do not yet have such a framework.

Practical Implications of ICH Q10 for CDMOs

For a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization), the "site independence" requirement of ICH Q10 means:

  • Every MMU needs a complete QMS infrastructure (SOP system, deviation management, CAPA system).
  • Batch release authority needs a clearly defined authorization structure.
  • Maintaining a complete, "inspectable" QMS inside a resource-limited mobile unit is a major technical and organizational challenge.

Comparison of US FDA Filing Types

If an MMU relocates frequently, each move may trigger a PAS, and the 6-12 month review time completely cancels out the commercial advantage of "rapid deployment".

Q 2.2

What actions can global regulators take to help facilitate mobile manufacturing?

Original Text — PDA PtC-15 (pp. 15–16)

Regulators can facilitate mobile manufacturing both by adjusting regulatory/administrative procedures and modifying validation and qualification expectations.

A prime example of where regulatory requirements could be revisited is the FDA approach to the FEI number. It would not be practical to give each MMU an FEI number: there would be too many units in circulation and the MMU manufacturer would need to reapply for a new one each time an MMU relocated, potentially every month. Not only would it be burdensome for the manufacturer but for FDA, with little public health benefit.

Alternatively, an approach could be to have an FEI number for the site where the MMUs are produced and then have that site's Quality Unit track the unit numbers from there. This would ensure the traceability expected by regulators, and regulators could revisit the concept of 100% "facility" inspection. If the MMU was viewed as analogous with a "facility," as set forth in 21 CFR 201.1, it would be impractical — a strict interpretation may require that each and every mobile unit be inspected after they are produced.

Novel validation and qualification approaches would also ease otherwise insurmountable regulatory burdens. For example, implementing a "family approach" towards validation and qualification of MMUs would streamline mobile unit qualification, without lowering quality standards. In this approach, once one unit is qualified and validated, identical follow-on units would be subject to reduced testing.

As another streamlining concept, the bulk release of raw materials or water could take place at one mobile manufacturing site, with portions of the pre-released material distributed to each unit. This would avoid unit-specific testing each time an MMU is made and stocked with material.

Recent draft guidances issued by the FDA on the Platform Technology Designation Program and the Advanced Manufacturing Technologies Designation Program potentially offer a new opportunity for MMUs. Specifically, these draft guidances propose that once a platform technology has been designated as such, it may be leveraged for other new or future products from the same sponsor or if a full right of reference for the same platform technology is provided to a third party.

As currently written, these draft guidances are limited to technology incorporated into a drug or biological product which is essential to its structure or function. Expansion of the guidances, or additional guidance to include the methods of manufacturing and the production platforms themselves, could prove useful to accelerate this paradigm.

The designation of a platform manufacturing technology should be considered early in development during the clinical phase. Other regulators, including Health Canada and the MHRA, are creating frameworks for advanced therapeutic products that take into account scientific and technological advances leading to distributed and point-of-care manufacturing, both of which could take advantage of mobile manufacturing facilities.

Key Points Explained: Study Guide

Two Directions for Regulatory Action

PDA states clearly that regulators can move forward along two dimensions:

  • Administrative/procedural: revise the FEI numbering system and allow facility licenses to "follow the system" rather than being "bound to an address"
  • Technical/validation: introduce a "Family Approach" to reduce repeated validation, and allow centralized batch release of raw materials and water systems

Analysis of the FEI Number Reform Proposal

Current problem: FEI = address-bound -> relocating an MMU = new FEI application

Alternative proposed by PDA:

  • The FEI number is issued only to the "MMU manufacturer (the plant that builds the MMUs)"
  • The manufacturer's quality unit is responsible for tracking the machine serial number of each MMU
  • FDA can inspect "the facility that manufactures the MMUs" rather than each individual MMU

The essence of this proposal is to shift the inspection focus from "location" to "system design and manufacturing quality".

Analogy: Vehicle Type Approval vs. Inspecting Every Car

A specific car model (for example, the 2026 Toyota Corolla) does not need every single vehicle to pass crash testing again. Only "that model" needs to be certified, and after that each car leaving the factory needs only a standard end-of-line inspection.

The logic of the "Family Approach" is exactly the same: certify an MMU model once, and later machines of the same model need only reduced validation (reduced testing) rather than starting from scratch.

FDA Platform Technology Designation Program

Two new FDA initiatives are potentially beneficial to MMU developers:

  • Platform Technology Designation: once a platform technology is designated, it can be reused across products, reducing duplicate reviews
  • Advanced Manufacturing Technologies (AMT) Designation: emerging manufacturing technologies can apply for program-specific designation and receive proactive technical support from FDA

At present both programs still focus on "the core technology of the drug itself" and have not yet been extended to "the manufacturing platform itself". PDA recommends that FDA broaden the scope so that the MMU manufacturing platform itself can also be designated a "platform technology".

Industry Advocacy Channels

Industry associations such as PDA and ISPE play a key role in advancing a regulatory framework for MMUs:

  • Pre-submission Meeting: first-of-its-kind MMU projects should proactively request a meeting with FDA to clarify the filing strategy in advance
  • Emerging Technology Program (ETP): FDA runs an emerging technology program that encourages innovative manufacturing technology developers to engage early
  • MHRA / Health Canada: the UK and Canada have built more flexible frameworks for advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), which can serve as a regulatory entry point for MMUs

CDMO Regulatory Strategy Recommendations: Modular Layout for Mobile Manufacturing

For a CDMO planning to adopt MMUs, the most practical regulatory strategies at this stage include:

  • Engage regulators early: arrange a Pre-Sub or INTERACT meeting (FDA) during the MMU design phase to clarify the filing pathway.
  • Choose a regulator-friendly launch market: the UK (MHRA) and Canada (Health Canada) currently have more flexible regulatory frameworks and can serve as pilot markets for the first MMU deployment.
  • Build a platform designation strategy: if the MMU uses continuous manufacturing or another emerging technology, actively apply for FDA AMT Designation to build up regulatory capital.
  • Family Approach documentation architecture: starting with the first MMU, build a standard machine "Validation Master Package" to pave the way for later machines of the same model.
  • Centralized QMS architecture: design a cloud-based, portable QMS infrastructure so that every site can meet ICH Q10 requirements in real time.

Summary of Regulatory Actions: Key Points Explained

A Two-Track Approach to Regulatory Progress

From a practitioner's perspective, regulatory progress must run on two tracks at the same time:

  • Short term (within the current framework): use the FDA Emerging Technology Program and Pre-Sub meetings to obtain case-specific guidance; choose jurisdictions that already have flexible frameworks (UK, Canada) as the first deployment sites.
  • Medium to long term (framework reform): through policy advocacy by organizations such as PDA and ISPE, push for FEI reform, official guidance on the Family Approach, and the development of an ICH guideline on cross-regional validation.

Regulatory Risk Warnings

In any MMU project, watch out for the following filing strategy pitfalls:

  • Do not assume that "approval in one country = valid in other countries"; each regulatory jurisdiction must be assessed independently.
  • Do not relocate across states or countries before obtaining the regulator's agreement; this may trigger a Consent Decree or a production stop order.
  • Do not underestimate the review time for a Type II Variation (EMA); it averages 60-90 days and in some cases exceeds 12 months.
  • Simultaneous multi-jurisdiction filings can shorten timelines but require substantial coordination resources; setting up a dedicated "Regulatory Roadmap" workstream is recommended.

Q 3.1

How would one validate a mobile manufacturing unit at a new location?

Original Text — PDA PtC-15 (p. 16)

Assuming the initial validation was fully conducted at the initially qualified site, validation of an MMU should be done in stages, with as many elements completed at the initially qualified site as possible (see Question 1.3). The initial stage occurs at the manufacturing site of the MMU (vendor), where critical systems like water and air are qualified via installation qualification/operational qualification (IQ/OQ). Mobility over various terrains would be part of this exercise as well.

Some systems could be tested at the initially qualified site of the receiving company (i.e., the drug manufacturer). For example, testing and qualification of raw materials should occur at the receiving company's fixed site, as could the initial process validation. These systems would need to be further qualified in field testing, through performance qualification (PQ) or confirmatory runs and, potentially, process validation.

This is analogous to "static" and "dynamic" testing of air systems where use in the field would be analogous to "dynamic" testing. In the case of process validation (PV), if the MMU is intended for multiproduct use, it may be challenging to perform PV for every potential product. The choice of the model process for PV should consider its complexity and robustness in order to cover a range of product types.

Key Validation Elements from Q1.3

Building on Q1.3, elements to address for MMU validation include:

  • Identify the need for an MMU and the initially qualified site
  • Determine what unit operations will be performed
  • List systems requiring qualification: utilities, manufacturing systems, computer systems, servers, access control, unit operation validation, method validation, cleaning validation, shipping validation
  • Describe procurement strategy and identify training requirements
  • Define quality oversight structure

Key Points Explained: Study Guide

Core Logic of Phased Validation

The basic principle of MMU validation is: complete as much validation as possible in a controlled environment up front, minimizing the scope that must be repeated on site at a new location.

This principle reflects risk management thinking: the earlier validation is completed, the better the quality of data that can be obtained under controlled conditions; field testing should focus on "site-specific uncertainties".

Analogy: Static Testing vs. Dynamic Testing

PDA uses static/dynamic testing of HVAC systems as an analogy, a concept familiar to pharma professionals:

  • Static testing (at-rest): testing cleanroom performance with no personnel and no operating activity, analogous to IQ/OQ performed at the vendor's plant or at the initial qualification site.
  • Dynamic testing (in-operation): testing under simulated production conditions, analogous to PQ and confirmation batches performed at the actual deployment site.

This analogy is very practical: static validation establishes "baseline performance", while dynamic validation verifies "robustness under actual operating conditions".

Process Validation Challenges for Multiproduct MMUs

If an MMU is designed for multiproduct use, a practical dilemma arises:

  • In theory, every product needs a full process validation (PV).
  • Performing PV for every potential product in a mobile unit is unrealistic.
  • PDA recommendation: select one "most representative model process" for PV. That process should be complex and robust enough to cover the range of products planned for manufacture.

The regulatory basis for this strategy should be discussed with the regulator in advance to confirm that the rationale for selecting the "model process" is accepted.

The Role of Continued Process Verification (CPV)

ICH Q10 requires Continued Process Verification (CPV) during commercial manufacturing. For an MMU, the CPV plan after each relocation should pay particular attention to the following:

  • Re-establish the baseline of the process control charts, because a change of site may introduce new sources of variation
  • Increase the CPV sampling frequency for the first several batches after the relocation
  • Ensure that CPV data can be compared with pre-relocation data through bridging batches

Commissioning at New Site — Practical Checklist

Site Commissioning Before Qualification

Before formal qualification activities begin at the new location, a comprehensive commissioning check must confirm:

  • Utility Hook-Ups: Electrical (voltage, frequency, phase), compressed air quality, nitrogen supply, steam or hot water, drainage systems
  • Water System: If Purified Water or WFI is generated onboard the MMU, flush and sanitize the system before use; test water quality per Ph. Eur. / USP specifications
  • HVAC Performance: Verify differential pressure cascades, air change rates, temperature/humidity setpoints at the new location (climate may differ significantly)
  • Environmental Monitoring Baseline: Run EM program for a defined period before production to establish baseline particulate and microbial counts at the new site
  • Vibration / Shock Assessment: Inspect critical equipment for damage sustained during transport (especially precision instruments, filling needles, sensors)
  • Computer System Revalidation: Confirm software, network connectivity, and data integrity systems function correctly at new site

Shipping Validation for MMU Relocation

Shipping the MMU itself is a validation exercise. Key elements:

  • Define transport conditions (temperature, humidity, vibration, shock limits)
  • Use data loggers to record actual transport conditions
  • Compare actual conditions against validated transport profiles
  • Inspect all critical equipment upon arrival per SOP
  • Document any excursions and evaluate impact on system qualification status

On-Site Start-up: Study Guide

Practical Key Points for Utility Connections

Utility connection is the area most likely to cause problems after an MMU relocation, especially:

  • Power quality: differences in voltage (110V/220V/380V) and frequency (50Hz/60Hz) between countries. Even within Taiwan, the grid quality of an industrial park can affect precision instruments.
  • Compressed air/nitrogen: confirm that the purity grade meets pharmaceutical manufacturing requirements (ISO 8573).
  • Drainage/wastewater: pharmaceutical wastewater treatment requirements may vary by location, so local environmental regulations must be confirmed in advance.

Establishing the Environmental Monitoring Baseline

After every relocation to a new site, the EM baseline must be re-established:

  • Before production starts, collect at least 2-4 weeks of environmental monitoring data
  • Assess the background microbiological profile of the new site, since the common contaminating organisms may differ between geographic locations
  • Update the Alert Levels and Action Levels based on the EM data from the new site
  • This step is an explicit requirement of Annex 1 (2023) and must not be omitted

Transport Validation: The Most Easily Overlooked Step

Relocating an MMU is in itself the "transport of a manufacturing facility" and requires complete transport validation documentation:

  • Temperature, humidity, vibration and shock data during transport (using data loggers)
  • A visual inspection SOP for equipment on arrival (especially filling needles, precision sensors and valves)
  • Any deviation in transport conditions requires an impact assessment to decide whether additional validation activities are needed

Validation Strategy Recommendations for CDMOs

To maximize the commercial flexibility of an MMU, a CDMO should build a "Portable Validation Package" during the initial validation phase:

  • A standard Validation Master Plan (VMP) covering all anticipated relocation scenarios
  • A predefined "Trigger Criteria List" stating when a full PQ is required and when only bridging validation is needed
  • A pre-formatted "Site-Specific VP (validation protocol) Template" to reduce preparation time for each relocation
  • Electronic change control and validation record systems to ensure documents can move with the MMU

Core Findings from the Multi-Jurisdiction Regulatory Comparison

Across all major regulatory jurisdictions, the core finding of Appendix A can be summarized in one common theme:

All current GMP regulatory systems assume that a manufacturing facility is fixed at a specific address, and no major regulatory jurisdiction has provisions written specifically for MMUs.

This shared gap is the core reason PDA PtC-15 exists.

Warnings on Multi-Jurisdiction Complexity

When planning cross-border MMU deployment, the following are the most commonly underestimated complexities:

  • Bilateral recognition: even when two countries have an MRA (Mutual Recognition Agreement), it usually applies only to the sharing of inspection reports and does not mean licenses are accepted automatically.
  • Language and documentation requirements: Japan requires filing documents in Japanese, China requires a Chinese CTD, and Brazil requires Portuguese. Language alone adds time and cost.
  • Local representative requirements: markets such as Japan and China require a local holder (MAH) in order to file, so a CDMO acting alone may be restricted.
  • Unpredictable inspection timing: authorities in each country have discretion over when to inspect, so an MMU may face an immediate inspection request after relocating.

Analogy: International Driving Permit vs. Local Licenses in Each Country

You hold a Taiwan driver's license, and when traveling in Japan you can drive legally for a limited period with an "International Driving Permit". But if you want to live in Japan long term and drive for a living, you must pass the Japanese driving test and obtain a local Japanese license.

This is exactly the reality of cross-border MMU operation: a short-term emergency deployment may obtain a "temporary exemption" or an emergency use authorization (EUL), but long-term commercial operation still requires completing the full local authorization process in each market.

Recommended Multi-Jurisdiction Filing Strategies for CDMOs

For a CDMO planning to deploy MMUs in multiple markets, the following strategic framework is recommended:

  • Phased Market Entry: in the first wave, choose MHRA and Health Canada (flexible regulators) to establish regulatory precedent for MMUs; in the second wave, enter FDA jurisdiction (leveraging the precedent data); in the third wave, consider other regions.
  • Parallel Submissions: run Pre-Sub / Scientific Advice with FDA and EMA at the same time. This requires substantial resources but can shorten the timeline gap by 12-18 months.
  • WHO prequalification as a bridge: in LMIC (low- and middle-income country) markets, WHO prequalification can replace most local filing requirements.
  • Build a regulatory database: systematically track MMU-related regulatory developments in each jurisdiction and set up a quarterly regulatory intelligence reporting mechanism.

Key Takeaways for CDMO Regulatory Strategy

The fundamental challenge of MMU regulation is structural, not technical: every major regulatory framework globally was designed for fixed-address facilities, and there are currently zero jurisdictions with dedicated MMU regulatory pathways. The short-term strategy for any CDMO is to use existing innovation dialogue channels (FDA ETP, MHRA Innovation Office, Health Canada Pre-submission) to negotiate individual project pathways. The medium-term opportunity lies in building a "portable validation package" and using the family approach to dramatically reduce requalification burden per relocation. The long-term play is advocacy through PDA and ISPE to establish ICH-level guidance for multi-site MMU validation and a global consensus on address-independent manufacturing authorization.
