Analogy: Three Levels of Kitchen

Think of ATMP manufacturing equipment as three levels of kitchen:

  • Open BSC = a home kitchen with a range hood: it protects the cook (the operator), but outside air can still enter
  • Open isolator = the glass partition of a restaurant open kitchen: there is a physical separation, but openings allow items to be passed through

The higher the degree of separation, the better the protection of product and operator, but the lower the flexibility and the higher the cost.

ATMP Definition and Basic Characteristics

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p7)

Introduction

Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) are unique because they are based on cells, genes, or tissues from animals and human beings. ATMPs are produced either in low-batch volumes, such as products produced for an individual patient (autologous), or (relatively) larger batch volumes for products produced for multiple patients (allogeneic).

ATMPs are classified into three main types.

  1. Gene Therapy Medicines: These contain genes that lead to a therapeutic, prophylactic, or diagnostic effect. They work by inserting "recombinant" genes into the body, usually to treat a variety of diseases, including genetic disorders, cancer, or other long-term diseases. A recombinant gene is a segment of DNA that is created in the laboratory, bringing together DNA from different sources.
  2. Somatic-Cell Therapy Medicines: These contain cells or tissues that have been manipulated to change their biological characteristics, or cells or tissues not intended to be used for the same essential functions in the body. They can be used to cure, diagnose, or prevent diseases.
  3. Tissue-Engineered Medicines: These contain cells or tissues that have been modified so they can be used to repair, regenerate, or replace human tissue.

"Combined ATMPs" are ATMPs that combine with one or more medical device(s) as an integral part of the medicine.

In general, ATMPs should adhere to the same manufacturing requirements as sterile parenteral drugs for their upstream, downstream, and fill-and-finish requirements. The minimum expectations for the manufacture of ATMPs are essentially the same as for all biological process considerations, with some specific nuances depending on the starting material, end product, intended administration window, and intended recipient(s). The use of ATMPs for clinical trials must also be manufactured under equivalent quality standards to the manufacturing of ATMPs with a marketing authorization.

Commentary

Core Definition of ATMPs

Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) are unique in that their starting materials are cells, genes or tissues from animals or humans.

Batch size determines the process type:

  • Autologous: cells/tissues taken from the patient themselves and returned to the same person after manufacture. Each batch is very small (sometimes a single dose for one person), the process is highly personalized, and strict patient identity tracking (Chain of Identity) is required.
  • Allogeneic: derived from healthy donors and can be manufactured for multiple patients. Batches are larger and closer to the scale-up production of conventional pharmaceuticals.

Analogy: Made-to-Measure Suit vs. Ready-to-Wear

  • Autologous: like a "made-to-measure suit": each one is tailored for a specific customer, with size and fabric fully personalized. A ruined suit cannot be replaced, and delivery pressure is high.
  • Allogeneic: like "premium ready-to-wear": standardized sizes and large production volumes; the starting material from one donor can serve dozens to hundreds of patients.

This analogy directly affects facility design: autologous processes need strict prevention of mix-up, whereas allogeneic processes need more emphasis on preventing cross-contamination.

Why Regulatory Requirements Matter

The document states clearly that ATMP investigational medicinal products (IMPs) for clinical trials must meet the same quality standards as products with a marketing authorization.

This differs from small-molecule drugs. Many companies wrongly assume that GMP requirements can be relaxed for ATMPs in the clinical trial stage, but both EMA and FDA require an equivalent standard, starting from the first Phase I batch.

The Three Pillars of GMP: Design, Control and Monitoring

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p7–p8)

The basic good manufacturing practice (GMP) principles of all processes include design, control, and monitoring. For ATMPs, these are:

Design

  • An appropriate Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS)
  • A detailed design of the process (open, closed, or hybrid, for autologous and/or allogeneic therapies)
  • Facilities, utilities, and process equipment that support the process design
  • Prevention measures to avoid extraneous contamination, cross-contamination, error, or mix-ups
  • Material, personnel, and waste flows that support the process and prevention measures

Control and Mitigation

  • A formal contamination control strategy (CCS) specific to the process
  • Control of starting materials and their source/donation and critical raw materials that are appropriately sourced and controlled for quality
  • Manufacturing environment controlled in a manner that supports the CCS
  • Laboratory practices and personnel training that support the PQS
  • Process (Safety) equipment controlled in a manner that supports both the PQS and CCS
  • Effective Pharmaceutical Quality System (including a facility risk assessment)
  • Traceability requirements that support batch identity and traceability from manufacture through distribution
  • Warehousing, distribution, and temperature controls that support product quality throughout all phases of production and distribution

Monitoring

  • Process-environment-related attributes in accordance with CCS
  • Cleanliness of facility and equipment to support fulfillment of CCS
  • Testing (including microbial, particulate, airflow, and HVAC) to confirm integrity of environmental controls
  • Release-testing strategy to ensure fulfillment of the quality target product profile (QTPP)
Commentary

How the Three GMP Pillars Are Interpreted in ATMP Manufacturing

The three pillars of GMP (Design/Control/Monitoring) are not specific to ATMPs, but the particular nature of ATMPs gives each pillar its own challenges:

  • Design: whether the process will be open, closed or hybrid must be decided at the very start of design. This decision determines the BSL requirements, cleanroom grades, personnel flow design and even the architecture of the whole building.
  • Control and Mitigation: the CCS is the core tool, integrating comprehensive control from raw materials, the manufacturing environment, equipment and personnel to waste.
  • Monitoring: meeting the QTPP requires continuous monitoring throughout the process, including microbial, particulate, airflow and HVAC performance.

Analogy: Three Layers of Safeguards in a Hospital Operating Room

Compare the three GMP pillars to the design of a hospital operating room:

  • Design = the architectural design of the operating room: positive-pressure airflow, double-door air locks, dedicated drainage. These are decided before construction starts, and rebuilding is extremely costly.
  • Control = pre-operative medication preparation, sterilization of surgical instruments, gowning rules for staff. However good the design, poor execution will still lead to failure.
  • Monitoring = intra-operative vital sign monitors, air particle counters, blood pressure monitoring. These confirm in real time that everything is working as expected, so that any abnormality is dealt with immediately.

The Status of the CCS (Contamination Control Strategy)

In ATMP manufacturing, the CCS is not optional but a mandatory GMP requirement (the concept was formally introduced in the 2022 revision of EudraLex Annex 1).

The CCS for ATMPs is particularly complex because it must address several things at once:

  • Adventitious microbial contamination (the main challenge of conventional aseptic processing)
  • Cross-contamination by biologically active materials (viral vectors, GMOs, cells)
  • Identity mix-up between batches (especially in autologous processes)

CDMO Application Scenario

Scenario: Your CDMO takes on contract manufacturing of both autologous CAR-T (20 patients/month) and allogeneic NK cells (200 doses per batch).

Challenges in applying the three GMP pillars:

  • Design: Do the two processes share a facility? If so, is the segregation physical or temporal? Are the BSL requirements of each the same?
  • Control: How should the Chain of Identity SOP for the autologous product be designed? How do batch release criteria for the allogeneic product differ from the autologous one?
  • Monitoring: Can the HVAC monitoring system serve the different pressure cascade requirements of both processes at the same time?

These questions have no standard answers, but the later sections of PDA PtC-14 provide a decision framework.

BSL Biosafety Levels: The First Consideration in Design

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p8)

This document considers the facility, utilities, and process equipment designs necessary to support the manufacture and testing of ATMPs (autologous and allogeneic). Ideally, the design considerations should be weighed upfront to ensure the process meets the respective requirements.

ATMP processes are still an emerging science. As a consequence, there are a variety of groups that are involved in their development and commercialization including academia, research organizations, hospitals, and biopharmaceutical companies. At the time of compilation of this Points to Consider (PtC) document, various manufacturing environment technologies are employed in the manufacture of ATMPs. Products are commonly manipulated in biosafety cabinets (BSCs), isolators, or closed systems with the required cleanroom surroundings. The stage of commercialization does not always dictate the type of facility or type of technology through which the products are produced; rather, the environment or technology is often selected based on the flexibility required for the process.

These technologies all have controls to ensure the environment where product manipulations occur is maintained in a manner that prevents contamination. These cell-, gene-, or tissue-engineering-based products are classified from occupational safety requirements to have a working environment determined by its Biological Safety Level (BSL).

ATMP manufacturing includes some products that are meant to replace, repair, or modify genes in a cell. To achieve this, certain viral vectors (e.g., adeno-associated virus, retrovirus, lentivirus, herpesvirus) are used for that purpose. Viral vector-based products can vary in their BSL depending on the type of virus and its potential to cause disease in humans. These are commonly classified as BSL-1 or BSL-2 and, in rare circumstances, even higher (e.g., BSL-3), depending on the virus load and type of virus. This Points to Consider document will focus on BSL-1 and BSL-2 requirements only, as BSL-3 and BSL-4 facilities are rare for the manufacture of ATMPs.

Commentary

Definition of Biological Safety Level (BSL)

BSL grades the requirements for the working environment according to the degree of biological hazard:

  • BSL-1: microorganisms with no known hazard to healthy adults (e.g., non-pathogenic E. coli). Standard laboratory practice is sufficient; no special equipment is needed.
  • BSL-2: moderate potential hazard; may cause human disease but the risk is manageable (e.g., many viral vectors such as AAV and lentivirus). Requires a Class II BSC and restricted personnel access.
  • BSL-3: may cause serious disease, but treatment exists (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, West Nile virus). This level is not covered by this document.
  • BSL-4: can be lethal with no treatment available (e.g., Ebola virus). This level is not covered by this document.

BSL vs. GMP Cleanroom Grades: Two Independent Systems

This is the most common point of confusion in ATMP facility design:

BSL (Biological Safety Level) = an occupational safety requirement that protects the operator from biological hazards

GMP cleanroom grades (Grade A/B/C/D) = a pharmaceutical regulatory requirement that protects the product from microbial contamination

The two may conflict:

  • BSL-2 favors negative pressure (to prevent hazards from escaping)
  • Aseptic areas (Grade A/B) favor positive pressure (to prevent contamination from entering)

Solution: use a closed isolator, with positive pressure inside the isolator (protecting the product) and negative pressure outside the isolator/in the building (protecting the operator). This is the core design logic of the later sections of PtC-14.

Analogy: The Dual-Barrier Principle of a Nuclear Power Plant

The dual BSL + GMP requirements of an ATMP facility are similar to the design logic of a nuclear power plant:

  • First barrier (BSL): prevents radioactive material (viral vectors/GMOs) from escaping into the environment, protecting workers and the community
  • Second barrier (GMP): prevents outside contamination from reaching the fuel rods (the product), ensuring the quality and reliability of the nuclear reaction

The two act in completely opposite directions, yet the designer must satisfy both. The closed isolator is the engineering solution to this "two-way barrier".

Logic for Choosing Among the Three Main Technologies

The document states that the choice of technology is determined not by the "stage of commercialization" but by the "need for process flexibility":

  • BSC (Biosafety Cabinet): the most flexible, suitable for multi-step open operations; but requires a higher-grade cleanroom background (Grade B)
  • Isolator: the highest sterility assurance (SAL 10⁻⁶) and permits a lower background grade (Grade C/D); but offers low operational flexibility and high initial equipment cost
  • Closed System: sits between the two; suitable for specific steps such as continuous culture and filtration; requires sterilization validation

In practice, ATMP processes often use a hybrid approach, with different technologies for different process steps.

QRM (Quality Risk Management) and the Purpose of the Document

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p9)

Product and process characterization is performed using quality risk management (QRM) principles to help the sponsor understand the importance of each aspect of the developed process/product. This information includes requirements for environmental conditions, production equipment/systems, product quality, and occupational safety. These elements have been reflected in GMP guidelines, norms, and practices for decades. Additional recommendations on these concepts specific to ATMPs are addressed in this technical document.

Given the novelty of these products and the relative scarcity of comprehensive technical guidance in support of manufacturing, it can become difficult to find resources to help determine appropriate strategies to ensure the successful manufacture of ATMPs. This Points to Consider (PtC) is an initiative of the PDA ATMP Advisory Board and was compiled by a diverse team of PDA subject matter experts to address some of the common questions within the industry regarding the manufacture of these products.

The manufacture of ATMPs should generally comply with the GMP and regulatory guidance for other biologic and aseptic products. Therefore, many of the topics that are included in this document may overlap with the conventional handling of biologics. However, the scarcity of comprehensive technical guidance and common questions from the industry seeking help to understand how various requirements apply to ATMP processes necessitate this guidance exhaustively addressing those design, control, and monitoring requirements, even when those requirements overlap with conventional processing.

As technology advances and regulatory expectations for continuous process improvement continue to grow, pressures may increase within the industry to further minimize the risk of our processes by moving to greater containment, greater sterility assurance, and reduction of personnel involved in the process. This is not always possible with the current state of the art for these novel processes. Hence, this PtC addresses the common technologies employed today and presents the risk-benefit analysis for each so that manufacturers can realistically assess their current process while simultaneously understanding alternate designs and controls that may be available for implementation.

This document is organized into topics and various subtopics that address the questions raised on the subject of ATMP manufacture. Each question first presents an overall recommendation, which may be followed by a rationale for the recommendation and/or appropriate references for further reading.

Commentary

Application of QRM (Quality Risk Management) in ATMPs

Quality Risk Management (QRM) is a systematic approach established by ICH Q9 and plays a central role in ATMP manufacturing:

  • Identify sources of risk at each process step (raw materials, environment, operators)
  • Assess the impact of risks on CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes)
  • Decide which risks need to be controlled through facility design and which can be handled through process controls
  • Establish the QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile)

The facility design recommendations in this document are, in essence, concrete outputs of applying QRM.

Why PDA PtC-14 Is Needed: The Gap in Regulatory Guidance

The core challenge in ATMP manufacturing is insufficient regulatory guidance.

Limitations of the main existing guidance:

  • EudraLex Annex 2 (2023): addresses biological products, with limited guidance on ATMPs
  • FDA 21 CFR Part 1271: mainly addresses HCT/Ps (human cells, tissues, and cellular and tissue-based products) and does not fully apply to all ATMPs
  • EMA ATMP guidance: scattered across several documents, with no integrated guidance on facility design

PDA PtC-14 fills this specific gap in facility design, offering practical industry recommendations in a Q&A format.

High-Risk Terms: RCV vs. Non-RCV

In viral vector manufacturing, the distinction between RCV and Non-RCV is critical:

Non-RCV (non-replication-competent virus): designed so that it cannot replicate autonomously in host cells. Used for most gene therapies; key replication genes are deleted, leaving only the ability to enter cells and express the target gene. The BSL risk is relatively manageable.

RCV (replication-competent virus): can replicate autonomously in host cells and may continue to spread. It is usually an unintended contaminant of the production process (e.g., through recombination events). The appearance of RCV means the BSL risk has escalated; containment measures must be upgraded immediately and the regulatory authority notified.

Key monitoring requirement: all viral vector batches must be tested for RCV (as one of the release criteria).

Functional Differences Between RTP (Rapid Transfer Port) and MAL/PAL

All three terms relate to the "safe transfer of materials/personnel between areas of different grades":

  • RTP (Rapid Transfer Port): a small, fast transfer device on the isolator wall, used to pass small items (e.g., culture dishes, syringes) without breaching isolator integrity. The alpha port (isolator side) and the beta port (container side) are used together.
  • MAL (Material Air Lock): a room-level air lock area for moving large equipment or materials in and out, which maintains separation between different cleanliness grades through pressure differential and time delay.
  • PAL (Personnel Air Lock): a transition area for personnel entering and leaving higher-grade cleanrooms, used together with gowning and air shower procedures.

Analogy: Three Kinds of "Passage" in a Hospital

  • RTP = a small pass-through window on the operating room door: surgical instruments are passed through without opening the main door
  • MAL = the equipment entry/exit passage of the operating room: large equipment (e.g., a C-arm) goes in and out under a full disinfection procedure
  • PAL = the changing room of the operating room: staff change into sterile garments before entering the operating room

The Role of VPHP (Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide) in ATMPs

VPHP (Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide) is the most commonly used equipment decontaminant in ATMP facilities:

  • Uses: routine decontamination of isolators (after each batch or periodically); facility surface decontamination; deep cleaning of BSCs
  • Advantages: strong penetration, no residue (breaks down into water and oxygen), effective against a broad spectrum of microorganisms (including spores)
  • Cautions: H₂O₂ is corrosive to materials, so compatibility must be considered when selecting equipment materials; operator exposure limits must be strictly controlled (OSHA: 1 ppm TWA)
  • ATMP-specific consideration: after decontamination, adequate aeration is needed so that residual H₂O₂ does not affect cell viability

Application of CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) in Facility Design

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is an increasingly important tool in ATMP facility design:

  • Use: simulate airflow distribution inside a facility or isolator by computer before construction

Q1.1

How can a risk assessment be created for an ATMP facility?

How is an ATMP facility risk assessment established? | Recommendation + Rationale (p10–p13)

Recommendation

The manufacture of any product, including an ATMP, necessarily entails some degree of risk. The degree of risk often correlates with such factors as the number of manual interventions, the type of environment in which those interventions occur, and the open or closed nature of the process.

For example, autologous therapies produced in small batches (small-scale) often have a higher risk per unit produced when manual manipulations are numerous. The facility design element of a formal facility risk assessment should always be included as part of the Design Review process in the organization, but it is even more important when processes have varying levels of risk.

Based on the process design principles and nature of the work to be performed in the facility, the organization can define the risk profile of the facility and work to mitigate those risks through appropriate design controls associated with the equipment (e.g., isolators, BSCs) and appropriate process improvements (e.g., single-use systems, closed processing, automation, improved intervention design).

As part of the same facility risk assessment, design controls can also be considered to manage risks related to biological hazards.

Core Concepts Explained

Why is risk especially complex for ATMPs?

Risk assessment for conventional drugs focuses mainly on chemical and microbial contamination. ATMPs add further risks:

  • Biosafety risk: viral vectors may infect operators (a BSL issue)
  • Genetic modification risk: GMO regulations require containment (negative-pressure containment)
  • Cross-contamination risk: each autologous batch is unique and must never be mixed up
  • Patient-specific risk: immunocompromised patients have zero tolerance for contamination

Analogy: the risk scale is like a kitchen safety assessment

A general kitchen assessment covers knife management and separate storage of foods (to prevent cross-contamination).

An ATMP facility assessment covers the same, plus: whether the cook could be infected by the ingredients (BSL), whether waste could contaminate the surrounding environment (GMO containment), and the fact that the ingredients of each dish may be served to only one specific person (the chain of identity of an autologous therapy).

Key judgment: open vs. closed systems

  • Open system (open processing): highest risk; requires the highest environmental grade (ISO 5 / grade A)
  • Closed system (closed processing): lowest risk; can be operated in an environment of lower cleanliness
  • Hybrid system: risk depends on the exposure time and extent of the open steps

Facility design goal: convert open steps to closed wherever possible, or protect exposure points with a BSC or isolator.

Discussion question

An autologous CAR-T cell therapy process includes cell collection, activation, gene transduction (viral vector), expansion, harvest and formulation. Which step is the most open? Which step has the strictest BSL requirement? Are they the same step?

Interdisciplinary Team Requirement

Facility risk assessment should be undertaken by an interdisciplinary team and should be guided by a risk-assessment facilitator of appropriate experience along with a scribe to record the risk assessment.

The scope of the facility risk assessment should include both facility and equipment design and selection, as well as product, personnel, and environmental risk categories. Including personnel and environmental safety aspects in the risk assessment is recommended as it is likely that safety-related controls could also have an impact on product quality.

Control measures for one risk category should be assessed for impact on other risk categories. For example, the use of a virus in cell therapy manufacturing may pose health risks to personnel while facilities producing the viral vectors have additional safety considerations due to the active replication of viral vectors, albeit in a controlled process and environment.

Where applicable, specialized safety personnel, such as those with biosafety, toxicology, microbiology, and virology expertise, should be involved in the performance of the risk assessment and in the design of the mitigations of the identified risks.

Why a multidisciplinary team is needed

Key principle: cross-category impact

PtC-14 specifically emphasizes that the impact of a risk control measure on other categories must be evaluated. This is the pitfall most easily overlooked in ATMP facility design:

  • BSC placement affects personnel, material and waste flows
  • The waste decontamination system affects the CCS
  • Residues from facility decontamination affect product quality and patient safety
  • GMO negative-pressure containment conflicts with sterile positive-pressure design

Risk Assessment Elements — 1 & 2

Facility risk assessments should consider, but are not limited to, the following elements:

1. Material

  1. Presence of adventitious agents (e.g., potential presence of replication competent agents).
  2. Material handling and transfer into and out of critical process areas (e.g., appropriate design and classification of pass-through containers, surface decontamination/disinfection during transfer of frozen materials, transfer of sterile parts/consumables).
  3. Controls and material-flow requirements when viruses, cellular materials, or GMOs are present in the facility (including raw material, product, sample, and waste flows).
  4. Incoming control of materials for quality, microorganisms, and adventitious agents (e.g., vendor qualification, vendor audit, vendor certification, in-house sampling and testing, controls at loading dock, warehouse).

2. Facility and Equipment

  1. Equipment, material, and waste flow (e.g., material or equipment moving to a higher-grade area, cross-contamination due to non-unidirectional flow).
  2. Personnel flow (e.g., cross-contamination risk due to personnel working or supporting different batches or different products at the same time).
  3. Cleanability of equipment and facility, including contact materials (e.g., selection of materials of construction that are nonadditive, non-absorptive, or non-reactive, including primary equipment, gaskets, filters, and selection of product-/process-compatible lubricants).
  4. Need for dedicated, segregated, self-contained areas and separation by time (e.g., manufacturing process using infectious viral vectors).
  5. Containment adequacy (e.g., ability to contain potential aerosol release, examination of open processing for possible routes of contamination).
  6. Radiation shielding (e.g., use of x-ray irradiation equipment in process may require additional consideration directed by local regulations).
  7. Aspects of commissioning, qualification, maintenance, and environmental monitoring (EM) (e.g., handling of pass-through containers, process gases).

In-Depth Analysis of Material and Facility Risks

ATMP-specific features of material risk

Adventitious agents are the core of ATMP material risk. Sources include:

  • Starting materials (the patient's own autologous cells may carry latent viruses)
  • Viral vectors used in the process (even if non-replicating, they remain infectious)
  • Animal-derived ancillary materials (serum, feeder cells)
  • Supplier cross-contamination

Vendor qualification is therefore more critical for ATMPs than for conventional drugs.

In-Depth Analysis of Multi-Product, Cleaning, Airflow and External Environment

The most critical conflict: the pressure differential dilemma

This is the central technical challenge in ATMP facility design, and PtC-14 states it explicitly in the Rationale:

  • Aseptic processing requires positive pressure: airflow in the clean area pushes outward to keep external contamination out
  • BSL/GMO containment requires negative pressure: airflow in the containment area is drawn inward to keep biological material from escaping

When a process requires aseptic operation AND biological containment at the same time (e.g., cell transduction with a BSL-2 viral vector), the two requirements directly conflict. Solution: use a class II BSC or an isolator to achieve positive-pressure sterility and negative-pressure containment simultaneously within a small area.

Air recirculation vs. single-pass air

Recirculation: saves energy, but if the facility handles GMOs the air must be HEPA-filtered before recirculation; processes involving viral vectors usually require HEPA H14 or higher.

Single-pass air: all exhaust air leaves the building directly (after HEPA filtration) with no recirculation. Used where biosafety requirements are highest; energy consumption is high but containment is the most reliable.

ATMP facility airflow design must balance BSL requirements, GMP cleanliness requirements and energy cost.

An allogeneic therapy is like standard sizes mass-produced in a factory: managed by batch, but the risk of cross-contamination between batches still needs tight control.

A CDMO running both needs physical segregation or strict separation in time (campaign manufacturing), plus a robust chain of identity SOP.

Rationale — Why Cross-Category Assessment Matters

A facility risk assessment can help determine the most appropriate design of facilities and selection of equipment. In particular, it is important to consider not only quality risks but also environmental and safety risks. Examples of how hazards can affect both safety and quality aspects include, but are not limited to:

  1. Certification and qualification test for a BSC covers product, personnel, and environmental risk but placement and layout of BSC should also be verified according to manufacturer and/or standard requirements (e.g., NSF/ANSI 49). The layout will then likely impact personnel, material, sample, and waste flows and potentially affect the cross-contamination control strategy. If the risk cannot be adequately controlled with the use of a BSC, a closed system or isolators should be used.
  2. Waste treatment is part of the biological risk management in the facility, and the type of waste treatment will impact personnel and material flow and potentially affect the cross-contamination control strategy.
  3. Facility decontamination processes may leave residues that subsequently impact product quality and patient safety but may also represent a risk to operator safety.
  4. Cross-contamination between manufacturing areas of different products (e.g., live vs. inactivated microorganisms) will impact product quality and patient safety, as well as put operators at risk.
  5. Design requirements for BSL containment of GMOs or other highly potent products may conflict with aseptic processing requirements. For example, a negative-pressure cascade may be recommended to contain GMO within the facility, but aseptic processes rely on a positive-pressure cascade to prevent contamination from entering the facility.

In-Depth Analysis of the Five Rationale Examples

Example 1: system-wide impact of BSC placement

NSF/ANSI 49 certification of a BSC (biological safety cabinet) only confirms that "the machine itself is safe", not that "it is safe in this location". A wrong location can:

  • Leave the operator with their back to the waste route while working, carrying contamination when moving
  • Cause the door-opening airflow to conflict with the HVAC supply direction and disrupt laminar flow
  • Restrict material entry and exit paths, forcing operators onto non-compliant routes

Conclusion: BSC location must be verified through CFD (computational fluid dynamics) simulation of the facility's overall airflow.

Example 3: the double-edged sword of decontamination residues

VPHP (vapor phase hydrogen peroxide) is the most commonly used facility decontaminant in ATMP facilities, but note that:

  • Impact on the product: VPHP residues may oxidize cell culture medium components and affect cell viability
  • Impact on the patient: hydrogen peroxide remaining on equipment surfaces may enter the product
  • Impact on the operator: VPHP concentrations above 1 ppm are harmful to humans, so complete dissipation must be ensured

After decontamination there must therefore be a sufficient aeration cycle, with indicators used to confirm that the residual concentration is below the safe threshold before personnel may enter.

Example 5: engineering solutions to the positive/negative pressure conflict

Problem: GMO containment (negative pressure) vs. aseptic protection (positive pressure)

Solution tiers:
1. Use a Class II Type B2 BSC
   → 100% exhaust to outdoors, HEPA filtered
   → Work area under positive pressure (product protection)
   → Cabinet under negative pressure relative to the room (containment)

2. Use a negative pressure isolator
   → Interior kept sterile
   → Negative pressure relative to the room
   → Glove-box operation, fully enclosed

3. Buffer airlocks at the building design level
   → Intermediate-pressure airlock between the containment area and the sterile area
   → Complex but achieves both

4. Campaign manufacturing (separation in time)
   → Containment and aseptic processes are not run at the same time
   → Last line of defense, sacrifices efficiency

How does the ATMP risk assessment determine the biosafety level requirement?

How does an ATMP risk assessment determine the biosafety level (BSL) requirement? | p14

Risk Group Classification (WHO / FDA)

A list of biological materials, including GMOs, should be maintained and reviewed regularly against local requirements as part of a risk management program. The list serves as a basis for the organization to determine the risk group classification of each material, and the risk group is determined by the hazard posed by the biological agents and toxins (if any) used in the manufacturing process.

Although risk group classification depends on localities, the definition is generally in line with those from the World Health Organization (WHO) and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA):

  • Risk Group 1 (no or low individual and community risk): A microorganism that is unlikely to cause human or animal disease.
  • Risk Group 2 (moderate individual risk, low community risk): A pathogen that can cause human or animal disease but is unlikely to be a serious hazard to laboratory personnel, the community, livestock, or the environment. Laboratory exposures may cause serious infection, but effective treatment and preventive measures are available, and the risk of the spread of infection is limited.
  • Risk Group 3 (high individual risk, low community risk): A pathogen that usually causes serious human or animal diseases but does not ordinarily spread from one infected individual to another. Effective treatment and preventive measures are available (not considered in this document).
  • Risk Group 4 (high individual and community risk): A pathogen that usually causes serious human or animal disease and that can be readily transmitted from one individual to another, directly or indirectly. Effective treatment and preventive measures are not usually available (not considered in this document).

Risk Group Classification in Detail

Why regional differences matter

PtC-14 specifically emphasizes: "risk group classification depends on localities". In practice, the same viral vector may have different BSL requirements in different countries:

  • United States: NIH Guidelines + CDC/USDA regulations
  • European Union: EU Directive 2000/54/EC (protection of workers from biological agents)
  • Taiwan: pathogen classification table of the Taiwan CDC, Ministry of Health and Welfare
  • Japan: infectious disease regulations of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare

A CDMO serving clients worldwide must design its facility to the strictest applicable regulation.

Analogy: risk groups are like food safety levels

RG 1 → ordinary food (hand washing is enough)

RG 2 → raw meat (needs a separate chopping board, dedicated knives, cooking until done)

RG 3 → cultures of pathogenic bacteria (needs specialized laboratory isolation)

RG 4 → highly lethal pathogens (needs the highest-level biosafety laboratory)

Most ATMP viral vectors are RG 2, equivalent to "raw meat level": careful handling is required, but standard protective procedures exist.

Determining BSL Requirements & Heightened Risk Situations

Upon understanding the risk group classification of the materials involved in the product, the organization should consult with the local environmental health and safety regulatory bodies and the inspecting regulatory bodies of the countries in which the product is licensed to determine the BSL requirements for the manufacturing facility.

The organization can then determine the controls required to manage the risk using facility risk assessment. Typically, risk group classification and control requirements are heightened for the following situations:

  • Novel biological materials (e.g., new gene insertion or deletion, novel virus)
  • Biological material that is replication competent as opposed to biological material known to be no longer capable of replication
  • Viral vectors and their risk to the operator and cross-contamination

The organization should also consider the requirements related to the use of GMOs. Many countries maintain specific facility and procedural requirements for facility-handling of GMOs, and the containment requirements are typically applied in addition to the typical pharmaceutical facility cleanliness requirements.

Risk management principles should be applied when determining the control points. The risk assessment and mitigations should aim to minimize the risk to as low as is reasonably practical.

Analysis of the three aggravating scenarios

1. Novel biological materials

A completely new gene insert or a new virus has no precedent, so existing safety data cannot be cited. In this case the risk group is often provisionally raised by one level until sufficient safety data have accumulated.

2. Replication competency

RCV (Replication-Competent Virus) vs. Non-RCV is one of the most important determinants of BSL:

  • Non-RCV (replication-deficient): can infect only once and cannot replicate and spread in the body, so the risk is lower
  • RCV (replication-competent): can replicate in humans and poses a transmission risk, so BSL requirements must be raised
  • The process must include RCV screening (RCA assay) to confirm that the vector is Non-RCV

3. The dual risk of viral vectors

Viral vectors carry risk in two dimensions at the same time:

  • Operator infection risk: if a vector carries a therapeutic gene, accidental infection of an employee may cause unintended gene expression
  • Cross-contamination risk: viral vector remaining in the environment may contaminate other batches or other products

BSL-1 vs. BSL-2 — Facility Requirements Overview

PtC-14 focuses on facilities operating at BSL-1 and BSL-2, which cover the majority of ATMP manufacturing operations. The following provides a comparative overview based on WHO Laboratory Biosafety Manual and EU Directive 2000/54/EC requirements:

BSL-1 (Risk Group 1 agents):

  • Standard microbiological practices apply
  • No special primary or secondary barriers required beyond standard laboratory design
  • Handwashing sink required
  • Personal protective equipment: laboratory coat, gloves, eye protection
  • No specific HVAC requirements beyond general ventilation
  • Waste decontamination not required before disposal (though good practice)

BSL-2 (Risk Group 2 agents):

  • BSL-1 practices plus enhanced controls
  • Access restricted to trained, authorized personnel
  • Biohazard signage required on all access points
  • Class II Biological Safety Cabinet (BSC) required for aerosol-generating procedures
  • Autoclave available within facility or building
  • Decontamination of all infectious materials before disposal
  • HVAC: inward directional airflow (negative pressure) recommended
  • Emergency eyewash and safety shower available
  • Defined emergency and spill response procedures

Layering of GMO Regulations and the ALARP Principle

GMO regulations layered on top of GMP (dual compliance requirement)

This is a key point an ATMP CDMO must understand: GMO containment regulations and GMP regulations are parallel and cumulative; neither can substitute for the other:

  • GMP regulations require: assurance of product quality, sterility assurance, documentation integrity
  • GMO regulations require: facility containment level, waste inactivation, environmental release authorization
  • Both sets of requirements must be met at the same time, and they sometimes conflict (such as the positive/negative pressure problem described above).

Regulatory inspections of ATMP facilities may involve both GMP inspectors and biosafety inspectors, so you must have an answer for both frameworks.

Applying the ALARP principle in ATMP facilities

ALARP = As Low As Reasonably Practicable

This principle comes from the field of occupational safety, and PtC-14 brings it into ATMP facility risk management:

  • It is not "zero risk" (which is impossible)
  • It is not the lowest risk technically possible (which may be prohibitively costly)
  • It is the lowest risk within reasonable cost and technical feasibility

In practice: when the cost of an engineering improvement (such as upgrading a BSL-2 facility to BSL-3) far exceeds the additional safety benefit, ALARP allows the facility to remain at BSL-2, but stronger procedural controls and compensating PPE are required.

The facility risk assessment (Q1.1) identifies all risks across six categories — material, facility, multiproduct, cleaning, airflow, external environment — while the BSL determination (Q1.2) specifically addresses the biological hazard dimension that is unique to ATMP manufacturing.

The integrated outcome is a Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) that simultaneously addresses:

  • Operator safety through appropriate BSL containment design
  • Patient safety through aseptic processing and cross-contamination prevention
  • Product quality through environmental monitoring and process controls
  • Regulatory compliance through documented risk assessment and BSL determination
  • Business flexibility through scalable and multi-product capable facility design

This integrated approach aligns with ICH Q9 quality risk management principles, applying systematic risk identification, analysis, evaluation, and control across the full spectrum of ATMP manufacturing hazards.

Q2.1 — Process Flow Design: Contamination & Cross-Contamination Prevention

How do we design the process flow to reduce extraneous contamination and cross-contamination and to prevent operational errors? | Pages 15–18

Recommendation — Contamination Vectors

Facility design plays an essential role in minimizing contamination, cross-contamination, and prevention of errors. The possible risk factors should be evaluated and defined in the CCS (Contamination Control Strategy) document.

Contamination and cross-contamination occur through several different vectors, including:

Physical

  1. Contaminated packaging or containers — Packaging formats of raw or starting materials that cannot be adequately decontaminated upon transfer.
  2. Mechanical transfers — Spillages, improperly sealed transfer devices, or risks such as the "ring of concern" — an area on a rapid transfer port (RTP) that cannot be adequately disinfected and may be exposed during transfer procedures.
  3. Personnel — Contamination from non-process-dedicated clothing regimes.
  4. Waste processes.
  5. Airborne sources — Aerosols, failure of air-handling units in the Grade A environment, or volatile constituents of contaminated waste.

Commentary

CCS: The Contamination Control Strategy Document

The CCS (Contamination Control Strategy) is the core document of ATMP facility design. It requires you to identify and document all contamination risks already at the facility design stage.

EU GMP Annex 1 requires the CCS to be maintained across the whole product lifecycle, not just assessed once.

Analogy: Restaurant kitchen zoning

Picture an ATMP facility as the kitchen of a Michelin-starred restaurant:

  • Raw-food area = high-risk BSL area (viral vector production room)
  • Cooked-food area = aseptic filling area (Grade A/B)
  • Pass-through window = MAL material airlock
  • Chefs' changing room = PAL personnel airlock

Core principle: ingredients (materials), chefs (personnel) and kitchen waste (waste) must never share the same route.

"Ring of Concern": a high-risk area of transfer ports

The RTP (Rapid Transfer Port) connects an isolator to the outside. The "Ring of Concern" is the annular contact surface of the port, which cannot be effectively disinfected. If handled improperly, this area can be directly exposed during transfer and become a route for contamination to enter.

Practical countermeasure: use a VPHP chamber, or redesign the RTP transfer so that it is performed inside a MAL with automated surface decontamination.

Containment Strategy — Inside-Out Principle

To minimize these risks, a containment strategy should be developed from the inside-out (from critical operations to less-critical operations) based on a risk assessment.

Based on the primary protection measures, a secondary means of protection should be planned, such as the PAL (Personnel Air Lock) and MAL (Material Air Lock) pressure cascades — from production room to air lock and from air lock to corridor.

Personnel, product, sample, and waste paths should be considered to avoid possible contamination and minimize cross-contamination risk.

Commentary

Inside-out protection design logic

The "inside-out" design mindset:

  • Innermost layer: Grade A core process zone (highest protection need)
  • Next layer out: Grade B/C background environment
  • Airlock layer: PAL / MAL (segregation and buffering)
  • Outermost layer: corridors and support areas

First determine the risk of the most critical operation, then design the protection layer by layer outward, rather than designing from the external environment inward.

Four-Flow Separation Matrix

Flow          Entry route          Exit route             May it be shared?
─────────────────────────────────────────
Personnel     PAL entry            PAL exit               Not with materials
Material      MAL material in      MAL material out       Not with waste
Sample        Dedicated container  Separate sampling port Strictly labelled
Waste         Waste MAL            Removed after inactivation  Not simultaneously with clean materials

Rule: the four flows must not intersect. When designing, draw the flow diagram and make sure there are no crossing points.

Important Considerations (1–4): Closed Process & Material Handling

  1. Is the overall process closed throughout the whole procedure? Any process which can be performed closed should be designed and executed closed. Items required within the Grade A space should be sterilized. Intrinsic sterile connectors should be used when possible.
  2. How are transfers in and out of the BSC or isolator performed? Use of RTPs (Rapid Transfer Ports) or MALs with automated surface decontamination is recommended.
  3. Isolators or BSCs should use appropriate technologies like RTPs for transferring sterilized or disinfected material to reduce the risk of physical transfer of contaminants.
  4. How is the pharmaceutical product handled inside the BSC or isolator?
    • First-air protection for any open-handled processes.
    • Greatest possible separation of personnel from the process through use of barriers and sterile implements.

Commentary

First-Air Protection Principle

In a BSC (biological safety cabinet) or isolator, "first air" is the clean air that comes directly out of the HEPA filter and has not yet touched any surface or person.

Principle: open manipulation must be performed directly under the first air, and no object or hand may obstruct this airflow path.

Violating this principle = loss of Grade A environmental protection.

Analogy: sterile wound dressing in hospital

When changing a dressing, a nurse never reaches over the sterile pack (which would block the first air). The same applies in ATMP processing: the movement paths of the operator's hands, tools and packaging materials must avoid "shadowing" the first air coming down from the HEPA filter.

The importance of intrinsic sterile connectors

Every aseptic connection is an opportunity for contamination. Intrinsic sterile connectors keep the connection step itself sterile, reducing the risk of exposure.

The number of aseptic connections between different lines should be minimized: every added connection point adds a risk of CCI (container closure integrity) failure.

Important Considerations (5–9): Packaging, Waste, Spills

  1. Can the packaging material be externally contaminated before leaving the isolator? Use VPHP (Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide) or other appropriate decontamination air locks, or RTPs, to transfer material to a safe area.
  2. What are the methods to manage pharmaceutical product residues or waste?
    • Waste should be handled in a contained manner through a separate pathway from product or product samples.
    • If the product exit is not separate from waste exit, removal-of-waste should be performed at a dedicated time separate from product manufacture.
  3. How will the processing area be cleaned to eliminate cross-contamination risk? Any inactivation step of residual pharmaceutical product should be performed before cleaning.
  4. Are single-use closed systems used? Contamination can occur at interfaces (e.g., single-use bioreactors, single-use bags) during additions, sampling, or dismantling. Tightness/integrity should be tested and validated.
  5. How will process leaks and spills be handled?
    • Containment measures to stop and prevent spread.
    • Inactivation/decontamination steps to protect product and packaging.
    • Procedures for gowning contaminated by spills.
    • Deviation and investigation procedures.

Commentary

VPHP: Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide decontamination

VPHP (Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide) is currently the most common whole-room decontamination method in ATMP facilities. Its advantages:

  • Can deliver a 6-log inactivation for an entire room or airlock
  • Penetrates corners, crevices and other areas that are difficult to wipe
  • Leaves no residue (breaks down into water and oxygen)

At the design stage, material compatibility must be confirmed (VPHP resistance of metals, plastics and electronic equipment).

Inactivate first, then clean: an order to remember

Wrong order: clean first, then inactivate (cleaning has already spread the virus over a larger area)

Correct order: inactivate first (inactivate the virus in place), then clean (remove residues)

This order is especially critical for ATMPs (particularly viral vector products); getting it wrong can cause a biocontamination event across the entire facility.

CDMO practical application: designing a spill response procedure

In your CDMO environment, the spill/leak response procedure (Spill SOP) should include:

  • Immediate action list: steps that can be performed immediately during processing vs. steps performed only after processing is complete
  • Personal protection escalation criteria: when a contaminated gown/gloves must be changed
  • Waste classification: distinguishing virus-contaminated waste from general process waste
  • Deviation trigger points: the conditions that automatically open a deviation investigation

Analogy: interface risk of single-use systems

A single-use system is like a sealed food container: the box itself is sealed, but every time you open the lid to add something (sampling, topping up medium), that is a risk point. The design focus is not "use a sealed box" but "how to keep every lid opening sterile as well".

Thinking exercise

Have the flow paths of materials, personnel, samples and waste in your ATMP facility been drawn as flow diagrams? Are there any crossing points? If so, are the current controls sufficient?

Warehouse & Kitting Requirements

To reduce the risk of intrinsic particulate contamination and maintain low mold in support areas, kitting of consumables should be performed in, at minimum, an ISO-8 cleanroom or controlled non-classified area to prevent introduction of particulate matter from the warehouse.

Warehouses should be maintained free of cardboard and paperboard by transferring materials into totes prior to storage. This lower-particulate warehouse for materials destined for Grade B cell-processing suites should be separated from the main warehouse where non-product-contact items are stored, ideally with air-wash curtains.

Rationale: An appropriate closed process, with a cleaning and inactivation strategy, minimizes the chance of extraneous contamination and cross-contamination. Well-documented procedures and training also contribute to prevention.

Commentary

Kitting: why does the warehouse also need contamination control?

Kitting means preparation work, such as dispensing and removing outer packaging, performed in a lower-grade controlled environment before materials enter a higher-grade cleanroom.

Cardboard is a major source of mold spores and particulate contamination. If cartons go directly into the warehouse, every picking action may stir up large numbers of particles that eventually reach the production area.

Air-wash curtain: how it works

An air-wash curtain creates a continuous airflow barrier at the interface between warehouse zones:

  • Blocks external particles and spores from entering the low-particulate warehouse area
  • Needs no physical partition wall, so carts and forklifts can pass through easily
  • Used together with the material procedure "remove cardboard, then load into totes (storage bins)"

This is a low-cost, high-benefit contamination control measure, especially important in multi-product CDMO manufacturing environments.

CDMO practical application: warehouse zoning design

We recommend establishing a graded material-entry system in your CDMO facility:

  • External warehouse: original cartons, general environmental control
  • De-cardboarding area (ISO 8 or CNC): remove outer packaging, transfer into totes, start recording
  • Low-particulate staging area: materials waiting to enter Grade C/D
  • Grade C/D MAL: enter the production area after surface disinfection

Q2.2 — Open vs. Closed Systems: Product & Operator Protection

Choosing between open and closed systems: how to achieve both product protection and operator protection? | Pages 18–19

Open System

An open system is one where the material is exposed to the manufacturing environment for even a short time. Due to this exposure, the environment is required to be of Grade A cleanliness.

The manufacturing environment is a total sum of events that occur during the process — any particulate, bioburden, pyrogenic, or viral contamination present in the area has the potential to contaminate the product. This is why environmental controls and monitoring, operator movement, effectiveness of material transfers, and sanitization steps are of key importance.

Open systems are often used for early-phase/proof-of-concept trials when the required manipulations are still being defined and refined.

Commentary

Three layers of meaning of an open system

An open system does not only mean "a container with the lid off"; more broadly it means:

  • Product/material is exposed to the surrounding environment at any step of the process
  • The exposure can be very short (even only a few seconds)
  • Once exposed, any contamination in the environment can enter the product

Requirement: open manipulation must be performed in a Grade A environment (in the first-air zone under a BSC, or inside an isolator).

Analogy: surgery outdoors vs. in an operating room

Open system = changing a wound dressing in a hospital corridor (high risk, uncontrolled environment). Grade A BSC = changing the dressing in a positive-pressure operating room (environment controlled to the lowest risk). Closed system = giving the drug with a prefilled syringe (no exposure to the environment at all, safest).

System choice in early development vs. commercialization

Open systems are mostly used in early-phase/proof-of-concept (PoC) studies, because the process steps are not yet fixed in early development and flexible adjustment is needed. For commercial production, however, every step that can be closed should be converted to a closed system as far as possible to reduce the risk of batch failure.

This is a technical upgrade path that a CDMO needs to explain specifically to early-stage clients.

Closed System

A closed system is hermetically sealed from the manufacturing environment, inside which the product pathway is considered sterile. Examples include single-use systems that have been gamma-irradiated using intrinsic sterile connectors.

Systems are only considered closed when they have been integrity-tested and demonstrated to be hermetically sealed. Fully closed systems can be deployed in a Grade C environment if adequate control measures are implemented.

Hybrid System

Hybrid processes — such as those requiring an open addition into a closed system, or that are open during initial assembly but closed thereafter — are required to be performed within:

  • Grade A cleanroom with Grade B background when using a BSC
  • Grade D background when using a closed isolator (depending on CCS requirements)

The increased cleanliness requirement is due to the fact that hybrid processes may create the opportunity for exposure of the product pathway to the environment.

Commentary

Proving closure: integrity testing is a prerequisite

A system cannot simply "claim" to be closed; it must:

  • Pass integrity testing (pressure testing, dye ingress testing, etc.)
  • Demonstrate that it maintains its seal under operating conditions
  • Supplier qualification + in-house testing where necessary

Only systems that have passed testing and are "proven" to be sealed can qualify for the lower requirements of a Grade C environment.

System Selection Decision Tree

Are materials exposed in the process step?
├── No (fully closed) → closed system → at least Grade C
├── Partly (open during assembly/additions) → hybrid system
│   ├── Using a BSC → Grade A + Grade B background
│   └── Using a closed isolator → Grade D background
└── Yes (open throughout the process) → open system → Grade A BSC + Grade B background

CDMO practical application: commercial considerations in system selection

In a CDMO, the choice of system directly affects facility capability and the range of clients that can be taken on:

  • Closed systems first: lower environmental grade requirements and save facility construction and maintenance cost
  • Flexibility of hybrid systems: can take on early-development clients (process steps not yet fixed), but a Grade B background must be prepared
  • Open systems: retain the greatest operational flexibility, but require the strictest environmental control and have the highest cost

For your current or planned ATMP process, which steps are open, which are closed and which are hybrid? Are the environmental grade requirements for each step clearly documented in the CCS?

Q2.3 — Pressure Cascade Concepts: Containment & Classification

Positive pressure, negative pressure, bubble and sink pressure concepts: how does an ATMP facility protect both the operator and the product? | Pages 19–21

Regulatory Background

The leading regulatory standards for aseptic manufacturing include:

  • FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing — cGMP
  • EU GMP Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products (EudraLex Volume 4)

These guidances state that pressure differentials for supporting clean areas must be maintained to keep sterile processing areas free from any contaminant (viable or nonviable).

EU GMP Annex 1 states that a positive pressure differential of a minimum of 10 Pascals should be maintained between each adjacent space.

Commentary

Basic principle of pressure differential cascades

The logic of using positive pressure in cleanrooms is very intuitive:

  • Air in the higher-pressure area can only flow outward
  • "Unclean" outside air cannot flow back in
  • This creates a "pressure cascade" from the cleanest to the least clean area

EU GMP Annex 1 requires a minimum pressure differential of 10 Pa between adjacent spaces. In practice, 10–15 Pa is usually designed in to provide a safety margin.

A special ATMP challenge: the conflict between positive pressure and BSL negative pressure

Conventional aseptic processing = positive pressure protects the product (clean air blows outward)

BSL-2 viral vector production = negative pressure protects the environment/operator (virus-containing aerosols are drawn inward)

The conflict: you cannot make the same room both positive and negative.

The solution: use a "Bubble + Sink combination design" (see the formula box below).

Pressure Concepts: Bubble, Sink, and Cascade

Cleanrooms are designed to maintain positive pressure, ensuring filtered air always flows from the cleanest to less-clean spaces — a pressure cascade.

In a multichambered cleanroom, the cleanest room is kept at the highest pressure. Air locks can act as a "bubble" (higher pressure than both adjacent rooms) or as a "sink" (lower pressure than both adjacent rooms).

For potent products or viral vectors, the pressure concept should be adapted to prevent spreading into surrounding areas using negative and positive adjacent areas.

The BSC is an open system; therefore the pressure differential cannot be regulated in the same way as an isolator. Instead, the surrounding environment is controlled with respect to the air locks and corridors.

Commentary

Bubble vs. Sink airlock design diagram

[Bubble airlock] Suited to multi-product facilities to prevent cross-contamination
  Corridor (low pressure) ← airlock (highest pressure) → production room (medium pressure)
  Air flows out of the airlock to both sides, preventing contamination from entering the airlock from either direction

[Sink airlock] Suited to containment of high-potency or viral products
  Corridor (medium pressure) → airlock (lowest pressure) ← production room (high-potency product area)
  Air flows from both sides into the airlock, preventing high-potency substances in the production room from spilling out

[BSC pressure configuration] The BSC itself is an open system and cannot be pressurized directly
  Grade B room around the BSC (positive pressure)
  ↑ BSC laminar flow head (downward positive-pressure airflow)
  → Exhaust air is discharged after HEPA filtration (the BSC under-cabinet exhaust can be designed as negative-pressure extraction)

Decision principle: high-potency/viral products → use a Sink first to prevent leakage; multi-product facilities preventing cross-contamination → use a Bubble first

The special nature of BSC pressure management

Because a biological safety cabinet (BSC) has an "open" front interface, a pressure differential cannot be set inside it as it can in an isolator. BSC protection relies on:

  • Downflow: HEPA-filtered air blows downward, forming a positive-pressure layer in the work area
  • Front inflow: negative pressure draws in air at the front, preventing aerosols from escaping
  • Positive-pressure surrounding room: the Grade B environment outside the BSC provides an additional protective layer

MAL/PAL Design Requirements & Suite Fumigation

MALs and PALs should be designed with interlocks that prohibit opening both doors at once. They should also have an integrated timer that enforces the required time to ensure at least 4–5 room air-changes occur within the MAL to reduce particles introduced into the higher-classified side.

Cleanroom suites should be designed for VPHP (Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide) or ClO₂ (Chlorine Dioxide) compatibility. For product changeover (different vectors) and for RCV (Replication-Competent Virus) to non-RCV, whole-suite fumigation should be carried out periodically.

RCV is a crucial safety concern in gene therapy. Vectors are engineered to be replication-defective, but there is a risk that the virus could revert to a RCV during manufacturing due to recombination events. If this happens, the RCV could replicate uncontrollably in the patient's body.

Commentary

Airlock interlock and air change requirements

Interlocks ensure that the doors on both sides of an airlock cannot be opened at the same time; this is the physical safeguard that maintains the pressure differential.

The timing requirement of 4–5 air changes: the air needs enough time to be exchanged so that particles brought in from the lower-grade side are diluted to an acceptable level before the door to the higher-grade side may be opened.

This design requirement must be stated explicitly in the MAL equipment specification, and the timer function must be confirmed during qualification.

RCV: one of the most serious safety risks in gene therapy

RCV (Replication-Competent Virus) is a key safety issue in gene therapy:

  • Viral vectors are designed to be "replication-deficient" (they can infect only once and cannot multiply)
  • During manufacturing, however, recombination may restore replication competence
  • If a product containing RCV is injected into a patient, the virus may replicate without limit in the body and cause serious harm

Facility implication: before changing products (a different viral vector), whole-suite fumigation must be performed to ensure that no RCV remains.

Analogy: RCV vs. replication-deficient virus

Imagine a USB flash drive (the viral vector) designed in "read-only mode": it can only "copy" the gene drug into cells but cannot "copy itself". RCV is like a USB drive whose read-only lock has been removed, turning it into a self-replicating virus. That is why residues must be strictly monitored and cleared by whole-suite fumigation for every batch of product.

CDMO practical application: designing the changeover cleaning procedure

In a multi-client / multi-product CDMO environment, changeover cleaning must include:

  • Whole-suite fumigation plan: confirm the contact time and concentration when using VPHP or ClO₂
  • Efficacy confirmation: use biological indicators (BI) for 6-log kill verification
  • HVAC clearance time: after fumigation, ensure complete breakdown to avoid residue affecting the next batch
  • Enhanced EM monitoring: increase the environmental monitoring frequency for the first 3 batches after changeover

Q2.4 — Cleanroom, HVAC, Air Locks & Corridors for Viral Vector Handling

Hardware design of viral vector ATMP facilities: a complete view of cleanrooms, HVAC, airlocks and corridors | Pages 22–24

Design Principles

When designing a production site for ATMPs, essential design considerations include:

  • Fulfilling a minimum of Grade D background (closed isolator) or Grade B background (BSC) with appropriate graded air locks.
  • Designing all cleanrooms to be easily cleaned and disinfected; as some ATMPs require BSL-1 or higher, rooms should accommodate specific cleaning/inactivation procedures required by the specific BSL.
  • Designing all equipment and room features to be hygienic and chemically compatible with cleaning, sanitizing, and inactivation agents used. Chemical compatibility should be considered early in the design stage.
  • Installing appropriate decontamination/deactivation methods (e.g., autoclaves) based on the required BSL, to deactivate/decontaminate waste or leftover raw materials/products before leaving the area.
  • Consider locating emergency response equipment (e.g., emergency shower, eye shower) outside the cleanroom, while ensuring there are no open drains within classified environments.

Commentary

Chemical compatibility: consider it from the start of design

ATMP facilities use many types of disinfectants/decontaminants (VPHP, ClO₂, sodium hypochlorite, IPA, etc.), each corrosive to materials in a different way. At the start of design, confirm:

  • Chemical resistance of wall coatings, flooring and gaskets
  • Equipment surfaces (stainless steel grade, coatings)
  • Protection ratings of electronic components and sensors
  • Pipe connectors and sealing materials

If this is overlooked in design, later retrofitting is very costly and may affect the facility qualification timeline.

No open drains in cleanrooms

"No open drains" is a basic requirement in GMP cleanrooms:

  • Open drains are a breeding ground for microorganisms (especially mold)
  • In BSL-2 areas, drain water may contain viral liquid waste, which must be inactivated first
  • Emergency showers/eyewash stations must be located outside the cleanroom, but positioned so that personnel can reach them quickly

Analogy: the design logic of a hospital operating room

The design requirements of an ATMP cleanroom are very similar to those of a hospital operating room:

  • Easy-to-clean surfaces (coved corners, no crevices to collect dust) = sterile operating table
  • High-efficiency air filtration (HEPA) = the operating room's laminar flow ceiling
  • No drains = operating room floors that prevent pooling water and contamination
  • Emergency eyewash outside the room = first-aid equipment must not compromise the sterile environment

HVAC Requirements

  • Design the HVAC system appropriate for the product potency and/or the required BSL.
  • Use HEPA filtration to obtain the required cleanroom classification.
  • Design the air supply for each space to promote appropriate air pressure differentials between rooms of varying classification or required operations (for maintenance of cleanliness or containment).
  • Design HEPA filter installation and replacement so they can be easily maintained in a way that minimizes cross-contamination and permits periodic integrity testing.

Commentary

Integrity testing of HEPA filters

HEPA filters must undergo periodic DOP/PAO integrity testing (Dispersed Oil Particulate or Polyalphaolefin testing) to confirm there are no leak points.

In ATMP facilities (especially BSL-2), HEPA filter replacement and testing are high-risk operations: operators may come into contact with filters containing live virus. The design must consider a "Bag-in/Bag-out" (BIBO) filter replacement mechanism so that filters can be changed without exposing operators.

CDMO practical application: HVAC zoning and segregation

The HVAC design of a multi-client CDMO must consider:

  • Independent HVAC system for each production suite, to prevent airflow crossover between different clients' products
  • Independent exhaust system: exhaust from BSL-2 areas must not be shared with general areas and must be discharged after separate HEPA treatment
  • Pressure differential protection after power loss: UPS backup power keeps the pressure differential in place, preventing contamination from escaping during a power outage

Air Lock Requirements

  • Position air locks to separate spaces of different classifications and/or avoid spreading contaminants in adjacent areas.
  • Design PALs and changing rooms so that exiting operators do not contaminate entering operators — providing separate routes of entry and exit.
  • Separate MALs from PALs.
  • Design MALs to allow for decontamination procedures within their environment, permitting the movement of materials from a lower grade to the higher grade.
  • Restrict personnel from crossing through a MAL to enter the processing cleanrooms. Instead, design a procedure for personnel to deliver material to one side of the air lock and, after decontamination, retrieve it from the other side.
  • Allow waste materials to pass through MALs/PALs only when clean materials or personnel are not present.
  • Separate waste flow from personnel and material based on potency and BSL requirements.

Commentary

Personnel must not pass through a MAL: why is this rule so important?

A MAL is designed for surface disinfection of materials, and spray disinfection, VPHP treatment and similar procedures take place inside it. If personnel pass through a MAL:

  • Personnel may be exposed to the disinfectant
  • Personnel clothing may carry in biological contamination
  • The disinfection procedure is disrupted by people passing through and becomes ineffective

Correct practice: the person places the material on one side of the MAL and walks away, letting the MAL complete disinfection; then a person on the other side (or the same person going around through a PAL) retrieves the material.

Analogy: the material pass box at a nuclear power plant

When someone outside the controlled area of a nuclear power plant needs to pass items in, a "pass-through box" is used: the item is put in, the outer door closes, radiation decontamination takes place, the inner door opens, and personnel inside the controlled area take the item out. People themselves never pass through this box. The MAL logic in an ATMP facility is exactly the same.

Corridor Design

  • Plot personnel, material, samples, and waste flows throughout the facility to ensure corridors promote unidirectional flow where possible (cleanest to "dirtiest") and avoid risks from intersecting flow paths.
  • Design the facility to employ clean corridors with positive pressure from the corridor to the MAL and PAL, or MAL and PAL with positive pressure to the corridor (air locks as bubbles), if the product is highly potent or if multiproduct manufacture will occur.
  • Use air locks as sinks when containment of potent products is the primary concern, based on the CCS.

Rationale: The design of ATMP facilities requires careful understanding of all personnel, material, waste, and sample flows. The adjacent rooms, corridors, air locks, and HVAC all play a part in ensuring product is free from contamination and providing operator safety. Additional safety requirements should be based on risk assessment and captured within the CCS.

Commentary

Framework for choosing corridor pressure design

[Scenario A] Multi-product facility, preventing cross-contamination between products
  Corridor (high pressure) → MAL (positive-pressure bubble) → individual production rooms
  The corridor is kept at the highest pressure, so any leakage from a product is pushed back into its production room by the corridor's positive pressure

[Scenario B] High-potency/viral products, preventing release to the environment
  Corridor (medium pressure) → MAL (negative-pressure sink) ← production room (high potency, low pressure)
  The production room is kept at a relatively low pressure, so any leakage is drawn into the MAL and does not spread into the corridor

[Scenario C] Typical configuration of a BSC facility
  Corridor (medium pressure, 15 Pa) → PAL/MAL (+10 Pa) → Grade B production room (+5 Pa)
  The BSC laminar flow head itself maintains the Grade A environment

Principles of one-way corridor design

An ideal corridor design avoids "crossing" of flows:

  • Direction of clean material entry ≠ direction of waste exit
  • Corridor for gowned personnel ≠ corridor for decontaminating personnel
  • If complete separation is not possible, at least design for separation in time (passage at different times)

During facility design review, drawing a "flow conflict matrix" is a practical tool for identifying potential problems.

CDMO practical application: documenting corridor pressure design in the CCS

The CCS should include the pressure design rationale for each corridor segment:

  • List corridor segment → adjacent space → pressure differential setpoint → design basis (preventing contamination or preventing release)
  • Draw a facility pressure map (Pressure Map) as the baseline for Commissioning & Qualification
  • Define the upper and lower pressure differential alarm limits (Alert/Action limits) and connect them to the BMS (Building Management System)

Q2.5 — External Environmental Risks: GMO & Highly Potent Materials

Environmental risks beyond the manufacturing facility: control of GMOs and high-potency substances in neighboring facilities, hospitals and the home | Pages 24–25

Recommendation — External Environmental Risks

Highly potent materials and GMOs are considered major environmental risk factors in facility design since there are often additional local regulatory requirements to prevent unwanted release to the environment.

The organization should:

  • Consult local regulatory requirements, including assessing all biological materials handled in the facility against such regulations, including GMOs where appropriate.
  • Work with the point-of-administration facility (e.g., hospital) to determine any containment control requirements (e.g., administration precautions, patient waste, hospital waste).
  • Engage in a discussion with local regulatory authorities — a discussion is recommended since ATMP technology is developing rapidly, and novel technology may result in novel products with novel risks.

Commentary

GMO regulatory framework: the manufacturer's responsibility extends beyond the hospital

GMOs (Genetically Modified Organisms) are regulated in many countries by dedicated environmental legislation, not only by pharmaceutical regulations:

  • Taiwan: governed by the Regulations on Field Trial Management of Genetically Modified Organisms and related Environmental Protection Administration rules
  • EU: governed by Directive 2001/18/EC (deliberate release of genetically modified organisms)
  • US: NIH Guidelines for Research Involving Recombinant DNA Molecules

At the facility design stage, manufacturers need to identify which materials are GMOs and confirm the corresponding regulatory requirements.

The need to work with hospitals

The "product lifecycle" of an ATMP does not end at the factory but extends to:

  • Hospital administration room: personnel protection during thawing, preparation and administration
  • Hospital ward: handling of patient waste for 30 days after administration
  • Home: exposure prevention measures for family members after the patient is discharged

The manufacturer is responsible for providing hospitals and patients' families with adequate handling guidance; this is not merely a service but a regulatory compliance requirement.

Healthcare Setting & Home Environment

Research laboratories manage biosafety risk through the oversight of an Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC). The use of ATMPs with biosafety concerns in healthcare settings has resulted in the extension of the IBC's role into patient administration settings, which is not within the traditional purview of conventional IBCs.

For gene therapy products in healthcare facilities:

  • A contact precaution over a 30-day period post-product administration, including waste handling, is recommended.
  • When the patient has returned home, families are recommended to take similar precautions for the same duration.
  • Disinfection and cleaning procedures, including relevant disinfectant contact time, must be shown appropriate for the organisms involved within the post-administration setting (e.g., the home).

Commentary

IBC: extending the role of the Institutional Biosafety Committee

The IBC (Institutional Biosafety Committee) has traditionally been responsible for biosafety oversight of research laboratories. Once ATMPs enter the clinic, however, its role has extended to:

  • Biosafety assessment of the hospital administration environment
  • Handling requirements for patient post-treatment waste (urine, feces, blood)
  • Personal protection requirements for healthcare staff within 30 days of administration

For many hospitals this is an entirely new challenge: traditional IBCs are not familiar with the risks of gene therapy products. ATMP manufacturers need to proactively provide supporting documentation.

Analogy: home-control guidance for radiopharmaceuticals

Radiopharmaceuticals used in nuclear medicine come with standard "home radiation protection guidance": for X days after treatment, the patient must not sleep with infants or young children, must flush the toilet twice after use, and so on.

The ATMP "30-day exposure precautions" concept is similar, but the implementation framework is still developing. ATMP manufacturers must proactively fill this guidance gap.

Disinfectant contact time: the challenge in the home setting

In hospitals, standard disinfection procedures can be enforced, but once the patient returns home, the use of disinfectants and the contact time depend entirely on the understanding and cooperation of the patient/family:

  • The disinfectant chosen must be an ordinary product available for purchase by households
  • The contact time must be long enough to kill the target virus but not so complicated that families cannot carry it out
  • Instructions must be written in clear, non-technical language

Healthcare Personnel Training Challenges

One particular risk in healthcare settings is related to handling competency. Healthcare settings tend to include:

  • Use of floating staff where the personnel assigned are not always involved in one particular activity.
  • Training management for healthcare personnel (e.g., physician, nurse) is managed differently than for production workers. Use of a Learning Management System (LMS) may not be common in a healthcare setting, making tracking specific training competency difficult.

Rationale: Safety concerns for highly potent materials or GMOs must be addressed regardless of the setting, to protect patients, healthcare workers, home caregivers, and others who may come into contact with the patient. The evolving nature of ATMPs means that the systems designed to control these extended risks are not fully mature. Therefore, it is incumbent upon the manufacturer to provide appropriate guidance for the necessary controls.

Commentary

Training risks of the floating staff system

Hospitals widely use a "floating" system in which nurses are assigned to different wards as needed. This creates a particular challenge for ATMP administration management:

  • Knowledge of post-administration exposure precautions for ATMPs is not part of every nurse's routine training
  • Without an LMS, it is difficult to track who has been trained
  • Handover at shift change may cause critical protective measures to be missed

This is part of the facility risk of ATMPs after marketing authorization (post-marketing), which manufacturers and hospitals must resolve together.

Extension of the manufacturer's regulatory responsibility

The final sentence of PtC-14 carries deep significance:

"it is incumbent upon the manufacturer to provide appropriate guidance for the necessary controls."

Manufacturers must proactively provide handling guidance to hospitals and home settings; this is not an optional service but a component of ATMP regulatory compliance. At the facility design stage, this "off-site risk management" should already be built into the overall CCS documentation framework.

CDMO practical application: the CDMO view of GMO risk management

As a CDMO, when taking on ATMP clients you need to confirm:

  • GMO classification: is the client's product a GMO? Which country/region's GMO regulations apply?
  • GMO material inventory: all GMO-containing materials in the facility must be registered, with periodic review of regulatory compliance
  • Waste notification: does GMO-containing waste need to be reported to the environmental authority?
  • Off-site guidance support: help the client draft SOPs for the hospital administration environment as part of the technical service

Q3.1

Are there any specific requirements for designing utilities in an ATMP facility?

PDA PtC-14, p25–28 | Topic 3: Utilities — General Principles

Original Text — Recommendation (p25)

There are no specific design requirements for utilities used for the manufacture of ATMPs compared to traditional small-molecule or large-molecule manufacture. However, specific requirements pertaining to containment and BSL facilities may apply according to local and international regulations.

EU GMP Annex 1 and other suggested readings provide recommendations of utilities for product contact. Although the design requirements are generally consistent with other types of manufacture, the following good principles apply to clean utilities:

"The design requirements are generally consistent with other types of manufacture — but containment and BSL requirements add a critical overlay."

— PDA PtC-14, Topic 3 Preamble

The six clean utility systems addressed in Q3.1 are:

  1. Gaseous disinfection system
  2. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) generation and distribution
  3. Purified water generation and distribution
  4. Water for Injection (WFI) generation and distribution
  5. Liquid nitrogen (LN₂) generation and distribution
  6. Nitrogen (N₂) distribution

Commentary: Why Is ATMP Utility Design Unique?

Core principle: the same foundation, plus a layer of BSL protection

PDA PtC-14 states clearly that utility design for ATMPs has no entirely new special requirements; the GMP foundation is the same as for conventional small-molecule and large-molecule manufacturing.

The difference is that ATMPs involve living cells, viral vectors and GMOs, so biosafety level (BSL) requirements add a layer of "containment logic" to the design of every utility system.

  • Conventional plant: utilities mainly serve product quality and sterility assurance
  • ATMP facility: utilities must also maintain BSL containment integrity

Analogy: ventilation in a hospital isolation room

Both ordinary wards and isolation rooms (such as COVID-19 negative-pressure rooms) need heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC). But an isolation room's HVAC has one extra core requirement: negative-pressure design that ensures contaminants do not escape.

The utility logic of an ATMP facility is exactly the same: the basic GMP requirements do not change, and BSL containment requirements are an additional protective layer on top.

Decision priority: the core principle for utility penetrations through walls and floors

Every pipe, every sensor and every power socket that passes through a cleanroom wall or a BSL containment boundary must first be asked:

#1 Does this penetration maintain BSL containment integrity? (operator safety)

If it cannot, the design must be changed, regardless of cost.

Gaseous Disinfection System

PDA PtC-14, p25 | Risk-based commissioning, airtightness validation, residual removal

Gaseous Disinfection — Key Requirements

a. A risk-based commissioning approach for gaseous disinfection systems should be applied.

b. Airtightness of the space needs to be demonstrated if gaseous disinfection is designed into an ATMP facility to decontaminate the facility/rooms. All surfaces and/or penetrations (e.g., sensors, electrical sockets) and openings that cannot be sealed in routine operation (e.g., the gap around the door, leak-tight isolation dampers in the HVAC duct) should be adequately sealed to prevent the escape of the decontaminating agent(s).

c. The performance of the gaseous-disinfection system should be demonstrated through validation and appropriate Environmental Monitoring Performance Qualification.

d. The residual level of disinfectant post-disinfection process should be validated using trace-limit-detection methods to demonstrate that the residuals are removed to levels that will have no adverse impact on the manufactured pharmaceutical product.

e. Pipes should be marked with gas identity signs and should indicate the flow direction.

Gaseous Decontamination Systems: Key Analysis for ATMP Facilities

What is a gaseous decontamination system?

Gaseous disinfection (biodecontamination) means using a sporicidal agent in gas or vapor form to sterilize/decontaminate an enclosed space. The most common agents are:

  • VHP (Vaporized Hydrogen Peroxide): the most widely used
  • Chlorine dioxide (ClO₂): suitable for large spaces
  • Formaldehyde gas: being phased out (toxicity concerns)

In an ATMP facility, gaseous decontamination is especially important because after handling live virus or GMOs, the entire room may need thorough decontamination, not just surface cleaning.

Key warning: airtightness is the lifeblood of BSL containment

Point (b) stresses in particular that all penetrations, including sensors, power sockets, door gaps and HVAC isolation dampers, must be effectively sealed during gaseous decontamination.

This maps directly to decision priority #1 (operator safety): if VHP or ClO₂ leaks into the corridor, BSL containment has failed and operators face a double risk (chemical exposure plus release of biological material).

Three key quantitative metrics for validating gaseous decontamination

1. Airtightness test (Room Integrity Test)
   - Usually performed by the pressure decay method
   - Acceptance criterion: typically < 0.5%/hr pressure decay (depending on the agent used)
   - BSL 2+ facilities may require a stricter standard

2. Biodecontamination efficacy
   - Use biological indicators (BI), usually Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores
   - Target: ≥ 6-log reduction (D-value × number of cycles)

3. Residual level validation
   - VHP residual analysis (trace-limit-detection method)
   - Acceptance criterion: residual concentration with no adverse effect on the product
   - Usually < 1 ppm VHP (depending on product sensitivity)

Analogy: checking a hot air balloon for leaks before inflating it

Gaseous decontamination is like filling an airtight space with a "gas" that has a germicidal effect. Just as a hot air balloon must be checked for holes before inflation, an ATMP facility must first verify the airtightness of the whole room before VHP decontamination.

The difference is that a leaking balloon simply will not fly, whereas a loss of airtightness in an ATMP facility can release BSL pathogens or toxic agents, with completely different consequences.

Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) Generation and Distribution System

PDA PtC-14, p25–26 | Cell culture supply, asphyxiation risk, O₂ monitoring

CO₂ System — Key Requirements

a. Any CO₂ distribution lines installed should be tested to confirm that the microbial and particulate levels are suitable for the intended use. The quality classification of the distribution line generally depends on the intended use (e.g., the classification of the air space within the equipment for which the CO₂ is used).

b. The design of the distribution system should consider the pressure requirements of the intended use including any possible future expansion needs. Appropriate pressure regulation should be included in the design if equipment with different pressure requirements is connected to the distribution system.

c. The design should include the necessary controls to provide a continuous supply of CO₂ gas (e.g., source tank switchover) at the appropriate flow rate and such features should be commissioned and/or qualified.

d. Branched-distribution system designs serving different processes should consider how cross-contamination risk through the piping can be prevented.

e. Pipes must be marked with CO₂ signs and should indicate the flow direction.

f. An oxygen (O₂) sensor with visual and audible alarm should be installed to notify personnel of a low-oxygen condition. Personnel should also be equipped with personal O₂ sensors. A buddy system and/or man-down alarms should also be considered.

g. A risk-based approach should be used for the qualification.

CO₂ Systems: the "Lifeline" of Cell Culture

Why do ATMP facilities especially need a CO₂ system?

The main use of CO₂ in ATMP manufacturing is cell culture. The culture environment for mammalian cells (including T cells, NK cells and iPSCs) requires:

  • Temperature: 37°C (simulating body temperature)
  • CO₂ concentration: 5% (maintains culture medium pH at 7.2–7.4)
  • Humidity: high (prevents evaporation of the culture medium)

Conventional small-molecule plants hardly need a CO₂ system at all. In an ATMP facility, if the CO₂ supply is interrupted, cells can die within hours from loss of pH control, resulting in batch loss.

Life-safety warning: CO₂ asphyxiation risk

CO₂ is a colorless, odorless gas. When its concentration rises in an enclosed space, operators may become incapacitated before they notice.

The three-layer protection required by point (f):

  • Room O₂ monitoring system: fixed sensors with visual and audible alarms
  • Personal O₂ sensors: carried by operators
  • Buddy system / man-down alarm: triggers emergency rescue if a person stops responding

These three layers correspond to decision priority #1 (operator safety) and are non-negotiable mandatory requirements.

Principle for classifying CO₂ system quality

CO₂ quality classification depends on the classification of the environment at the point of contact:

Use scenario                           CO₂ line quality requirement
─────────────────────────────────────────
CO₂ into an ISO 5 laminar flow hood  →  must meet the equivalent cleanroom standard
CO₂ into an ISO 7 incubator          →  meets ISO 7 particulate/microbial requirements
CO₂ for non-product-contact use      →  general industrial grade may be acceptable
(e.g. HVAC pressure balancing)

Key principle: CO₂ line quality ≥ the most stringent classification of the environment it enters

Analogy: the natural gas supply in a restaurant kitchen

Picture the natural gas system of a restaurant:

  • It needs a continuous supply (switching to a backup cylinder = source tank switchover)
  • Different burners have different pressure requirements (pressure regulation is needed)
  • Natural gas carries an explosion/asphyxiation risk (leak detection is needed)

The logic of a CO₂ system is exactly the same; the kitchen becomes a cell culture room, natural gas becomes CO₂, and the consequence changes from a kitchen explosion to cell death plus operator asphyxiation.

Purified Water (PW) Generation and Distribution System

PDA PtC-14, p26 | Biofilm prevention, disinfectant preparation, pressure differential

Purified Water — Key Requirements

a. Any purified water generation and distribution system should be qualified and commissioned for the intended use. Routine sampling of both the generation and distribution system should be conducted to ensure the ongoing quality of the purified water.

b. The use of purified water in cleaning activities should consider the impact of water quality on any surface-disinfectant preparation and on the ability to maintain room classification, or WFI should be considered as an alternative.

c. The design and maintenance (e.g., temperature, flow rate, routine disinfection) of the distribution system should ensure no biofilm formation.

d. Distribution systems serving different processes or different equipment within the process should ensure that the process, including the cleaning process, operates at a lower pressure than the purified distribution system to prevent a cross-contamination risk through the piping, or the valve design should prevent any possible mixing (e.g., double-block and bleed).

e. Pipes must be marked with "Purified Water" signs and should indicate the flow direction.

Purified Water Systems: the Clean Water Strategy of an ATMP Facility

Definition of purified water (PW) and its scope in ATMP facilities

Purified water is produced to Ph. Eur. / USP standards, and is mainly used for:

  • Cleaning water (washing equipment surfaces)
  • Preparing non-injectable disinfectants (such as 70% IPA and sodium hypochlorite solution)
  • Non-direct product-contact uses

Point (b) raises an important ATMP-specific consideration: if purified water quality is unstable, it may affect the efficacy of the disinfectants prepared with it (hardness and pH have an effect), thereby jeopardizing the ability to maintain the BSL environment. In that case, switching to WFI for disinfectant preparation should be considered.

Special challenge of PW in ATMP facilities: biofilm

Conventional plants already have mature biofilm control measures for purified water (hot circulation, periodic ozone sanitization, etc.), but ATMP facilities face additional risk:

  • If the facility is small (for example an autologous ATMP facility), the water system may be used less frequently
  • The risk of biofilm formation rises during periods of low flow velocity
  • ATMP immunotherapy patients are often severely immunocompromised (after chemotherapy) and have zero tolerance for microbial contamination in water

The "no biofilm" requirement in point (c) is, in an ATMP facility, a direct requirement of decision priority #2 (patient safety).

Pressure differential principle for preventing cross-contamination

Double-block and bleed (DBB) design:

High-pressure side (PW main header)
       |
    [Block Valve 1]  ← normally closed
       |
    [Bleed Valve]    ← any leakage is vented from here to a safe location
       |
    [Block Valve 2]  ← normally closed
       |
Low-pressure side (process equipment / cleaning use)

Principle: equipment-side pressure < PW distribution line pressure
      Even if a valve fails, contamination cannot flow back into the main header

Alternative: independent piping design (no shared connection)

Analogy: the backflow preventer in a city water supply

When a city water network connects to a user, a "backflow preventer" is installed to ensure the water pressure from the waterworks is always higher than on the user side, preventing contaminants from flowing back from the user to the waterworks.

The DBB design for an ATMP facility's PW system works on exactly the same principle: it ensures the clean water source is never contaminated by the process side, and the flow can only go "water source → process", never backward.

Water for Injection (WFI) Generation and Distribution System

PDA PtC-14, p26 | Distillation requirement, product-contact validation, biofilm prevention

WFI System — Key Requirements

a. Any WFI generation and distribution system should be qualified and validated to consistently produce water meeting the specification as it is intended to be used. Sampling locations close to the intended points of use should be defined according to the Control of Contamination Strategy (CCS). Routine sampling of both the generation and distribution system should be conducted to ensure the ongoing quality of the WFI.

b. WFI should be produced by distillation or by a purification process that is equivalent to distillation if WFI is used for direct product-contact purposes.

c. The design and maintenance (e.g., temperature, flow rate, routine disinfection) of the distribution system should ensure no biofilm formation.

d. Distribution systems serving different processes or different equipment within the process should ensure that the process, including the cleaning process, operates at a lower pressure than the WFI distribution system to prevent cross-contamination risk through the piping, or the valve design should prevent any possible mixing (e.g., double block and bleed).

e. Pipes must be marked with "WFI" signs and should indicate the flow direction.

Water for Injection Systems: the Highest Water Quality Requirement in ATMP Manufacturing

Uses of WFI in ATMP manufacturing

Water for Injection (WFI) is the water grade used for direct product contact in ATMP manufacturing, including:

  • Final formulation: diluting the cell suspension
  • Buffer preparation
  • Sterile rinsing (final rinse after CIP)
  • Autoclave feed water (if pure steam is used)

CCS (contamination control strategy) and WFI sampling point design

Point (a) specifically mentions defining sampling locations based on the CCS, a core concept of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022 version).

The CCS requires that sampling not be done only at the outlet of the WFI generator; it must be done at the location closest to the actual point of use, because the distribution loop itself can also be a source of contamination.

In small-batch ATMP production, the WFI system may be smaller in scale and sampling point design is especially important, because infrequently used branches are more likely to accumulate microorganisms.

Analogy: a hospital drinking fountain versus operating room water

A hospital corridor water fountain (purified water grade) and the sterile saline in the operating room (WFI grade) follow different water quality standards, because the latter goes directly into the patient's body.

The logic is the same in an ATMP facility: "the water must meet the quality required where it is used", which is the core spirit of CCS-driven design.

Liquid Nitrogen (LN₂) Generation and Distribution System

PDA PtC-14, p26–27 | Ultra-low temperature storage, cryogenic burn risk, cell bank backup

Liquid Nitrogen — Key Requirements

a. Installation of a liquid nitrogen (LN₂) generation and distribution system should consider the asphyxiation risk. A room O₂-level monitoring system with visual and audible alarms should be available, and personnel should also be equipped with O₂ personal-monitoring instruments. A buddy system and/or man-down alarms should also be considered. Means to detect and isolate any leak in the system should be available, including PPE for routine use and emergency-rescue purposes.

b. LN₂ is extremely cold and contact to bare skin causes severe frostbite. Signage should be posted throughout the space. PPE-donning stations should be prominently positioned within the floor plan. First-aid stations should also be available for accidental exposures.

c. The materials that require storage in LN₂ (ultra-low temperature storage) are commonly the most critical materials in the process (e.g., cell or viral banks). Therefore, it is common in designs to consider the inclusion of back-up equipment (e.g., LN₂ freezers) in case of equipment failure. The back-up equipment should be qualified for use and subject to routine maintenance so it is ready for deployment.

d. Piping for distributing LN₂ should be adequately insulated both due to the cold-burn risk and to avoid condensation on external surfaces.

e. Pipes should be marked with "LN₂" signs and should indicate the flow direction.

f. Due to the risk of testing LN₂ and its stability, LN₂ quality through the distribution system is typically confirmed by the use of a Certificate of Compliance or Certificate of Analysis provided by LN₂ supplier.

g. Design should include controls to provide continuous supply of LN₂ as required (e.g., source-tank switchover), and such features should be commissioned and/or qualified accordingly.

Liquid Nitrogen Systems: the Most Critical Storage Medium for ATMPs

Why is LN₂ so critical for ATMPs?

Liquid nitrogen (LN₂, boiling point -196°C) is the medium used in ATMP manufacturing to store the following highest-value materials:

  • Cell banks: Master Cell Bank / Working Cell Bank, the source of the entire product line
  • Viral banks: viral vector seed banks for gene therapy products
  • Patient cells: the starting material for autologous CAR-T therapy, which cannot be regenerated

Point (c) states explicitly that these are the "most critical materials in the process", so backup equipment must be considered. In the decision priorities this involves both #2 (patient safety) and #3 (product quality).

Dual hazards: asphyxiation plus cryogenic burns

LN₂ systems are among the most dangerous utility systems in an ATMP facility, with two entirely different hazard mechanisms:

Hazard 1: asphyxiation risk

  • When LN₂ vaporizes it expands about 700-fold, rapidly displacing O₂ from the space
  • O₂ concentration < 19.5%: danger alert; < 16%: impaired judgment; < 10%: immediate loss of consciousness
  • The whole process can occur within minutes, with no noticeable warning sensation

Hazard 2: cryogenic burns (frostbite)

  • LN₂ at -196°C causes severe frostbite on skin contact within 0.1 second
  • LN₂ splashes can cause permanent eye damage
  • PPE stations and first-aid stations must be clearly marked on the floor plan

Design considerations for LN₂ backup systems

Three levels of backup design:

Level 1: backup tank (backup dewar / tank)
  - Ensures a switchover before the main tank is depleted
  - Supply switchover must be verified through commissioning
  - Usually requires additional floor-loading design

Level 2: backup LN₂ freezer
  - If the main equipment fails, critical materials are transferred immediately
  - The backup equipment also needs IQ/OQ/PQ qualification
  - The transfer procedure (SOP) must be rehearsed regularly

Level 3: off-site storage
  - Cell banks and viral banks should usually be backed up at two physically separate locations
  - This is industry best practice but is beyond the scope of Q3.1

Note: backup design increases:
  + Facility floor footprint
  + Capital expenditure (CapEx)
  + Complexity of the LN₂ supply piping

Analogy: the uninterruptible power supply (UPS) in a hospital ICU

Life-support equipment in an intensive care unit (ventilators, cardiac monitors) must never be interrupted by a power outage, so hospitals design dual backup with a UPS plus a generator.

The LN₂ backup logic of an ATMP facility is exactly the same: the cell bank is the patient's "life support", and if the LN₂ supply is interrupted without a backup, the patient may lose their only chance of treatment, with irreversible consequences.

Nitrogen (N₂) Distribution System

PDA PtC-14, p27 | Inert overlay, sterile filtration, asphyxiation risk, classification

Nitrogen (N₂) — Key Requirements

a. Any nitrogen (N₂) distribution line installed should be classified. The classification of the distribution line generally depends on the intended use (e.g., classified environment within the equipment where the N₂ is used, criticality of the material in contact with the N₂ gas). Examples of N₂ use include, but are not limited to, N₂ overlay in the product container head space and N₂ overlaying in the bulk-material containers (i.e., displacement of oxygen to reduce oxidation of materials and/or maintain the stability of raw materials).

b. Installation of an N₂ generation and distribution system should consider the asphyxiation risk. An O₂-level monitoring system room with visual and audible alarms should be available, and personnel should also be equipped with O₂ personal-monitoring instruments. Personnel in the room, or intending to enter the room, should be trained to exit (or not enter) the room when the alarm is active. A buddy system and/or man-down alarms should also be considered. The means to detect and isolate any leak in the system should be available, including the availability of PPE for emergency-rescue purposes.

c. Appropriately qualified source and terminal sterile filtration should be considered if N₂ is used for product-contact purposes.

d. Distribution systems serving different processes or different equipment within the process should ensure that the design prevents the risk of cross-contamination.

e. Pipes must be marked with "N₂" signs and should indicate the flow direction.

Nitrogen Systems: Inert Protection and Product Stability

Two main uses of N₂ in ATMP manufacturing

Use 1: inert overlay / blanket

  • Cover the headspace of product containers (vials, bags) with N₂ to exclude O₂
  • Prevent oxidation of the product (especially cells, proteins and lipid nanoparticles)
  • Maintain the stability of raw materials (such as sensitive buffer reagents)

Use 2: line purging

  • Purge lines with N₂ before filling to remove O₂ and moisture from the air
  • Confirm line cleanliness before returning equipment to service after maintenance

N₂ sterile filtration design: source vs. terminal filtration

When N₂ comes into direct contact with the product, two-stage filtration is needed:

Source filtration:
  Location: outlet of the N₂ supply station (gas manifold)
  Function: remove particles and microorganisms coming from the gas source
  Validation: validated according to the environment classification at the point of use

Terminal filtration:
  Location: closest to the point of use
  Function: ensure the N₂ entering the product-contact area meets the sterility standard
  Validation: 0.22 μm sterilizing filtration, with integrity testing required

Why are two filters needed?
  Source filtration protects the lines from external contamination
  Terminal filtration is the last line of defense, preventing contamination arising inside the lines
  Together, the two filters ensure the N₂ reaching the product is sterile

Analogy: the medical gas system in an operating room

Every medical gas terminal on the operating room ceiling (O₂, N₂O, compressed air) has color coding and mix-up-proof connectors, just like the pipe labeling required by Q3.1(e).

If the O₂ in an operating room were contaminated with N₂, the patient would be in immediate danger of death. If the N₂ in an ATMP facility were contaminated by biological contaminants flowing back, the safety of subsequent patients' products could be jeopardized. The purpose of labeling in both cases is to prevent the catastrophic consequence of "connecting the wrong pipe".

Reading the comparison table: four common principles of ATMP utility design

Principle 1: every utility system requires a "classification" decision

Every utility system in Q3.1 emphasizes classification by "intended use". This is a core principle running through the whole document:

  • CO₂ entering an ISO 5 environment = must meet ISO 5 quality
  • N₂ in direct contact with the product = requires sterile filtration
  • WFI used for final formulation = the most stringent validation requirements

This is fully consistent with the risk-based approach of EU GMP Annex 1.

Principle 2: gas systems = three-layer protection against asphyxiation

The CO₂, LN₂ and N₂ systems all need exactly the same three-layer safety architecture:

  1. Fixed room O₂ monitoring (visual and audible dual alarms)
  2. Personal portable O₂ sensors
  3. Buddy system / man-down alarm

These three layers derive from the same decision priority, #1 (operator safety), and are a non-negotiable hard requirement in any ATMP facility design.

Principle 3: preventing cross-contamination = pressure differential or double-block and bleed valves

The PW, WFI, CO₂ and N₂ systems all stress the risk of cross-contamination. There are two preventive approaches:

  • Pressure differential method: distribution header pressure > process equipment pressure, so contamination cannot flow back
  • Valve design method: double-block and bleed (DBB), so that even if one valve fails there is a second layer of protection

In an ATMP facility, the consequences of cross-contamination can include mix-up of patient cells between batches, which falls in the highest risk category of decision priority #2 (patient safety).

Principle 4: pipe labeling is a GMP basic and the first line of defense for safety

In Q3.1, all six utility systems require pipe labeling of flow direction. This looks basic, but it is especially important in an ATMP facility:

  • Several high-risk gases (CO₂, N₂, LN₂) are present in the same facility at the same time
  • Connecting the wrong pipe can cause an asphyxiation accident or product contamination
  • In an emergency, maintenance personnel need to identify the pipe type quickly

CDMO Scenario 1: converting a conventional biotech plant into an ATMP CDMO

Background: A Taiwanese CDMO currently produces mainly recombinant proteins and mAbs and plans to add CAR-T cell therapy contract services. The existing plant already has complete WFI, purified water and CDA (clean dry air) systems and holds EU GMP certification.

Which utility systems need to be added or modified?

  • Must add: CO₂ system — conventional protein production does not need CO₂, whereas CAR-T cell culture needs a constant supply of 5% CO₂. A CO₂ distribution line, room O₂ monitoring and personal sensors must be installed.
  • Must add: LN₂ system — patient apheresis products and cultured CAR-T cells need long-term storage in LN₂. A backup storage system must be planned at the same time.
  • Needs assessment for upgrade: gaseous decontamination system — if a BSL-2 facility needs VHP or ClO₂ decontamination, airtightness testing must be performed and the sealing of all wall and floor penetrations confirmed.
  • Existing systems that may need confirmation: N₂ line quality classification — if used for inert overlay of cell products, confirm whether terminal sterile filtration has been installed.
  • The WFI system may be reusable — but the sampling point layout for the new production areas must be re-evaluated against the CCS.

CDMO Scenario 2: designing a new multi-modal ATMP facility

Background: A start-up ATMP CDMO plans to build a multi-modal facility supporting simultaneously: Line A (autologous CAR-T, BSL-2), Line B (AAV gene therapy, BSL-2) and Line C (allogeneic iPSC-derived NK cells, BSL-1).

Key decision points in utility design:

  • Should the CO₂ system share a main header?
    Q3.1 point (d) warns of the cross-contamination risk of branched systems. The CO₂ lines for autologous and allogeneic production lines should be laid out independently, or a backflow preventer should be installed at each take-off point, to ensure Line A patient cells cannot contaminate Line B through the CO₂ lines.
  • How should LN₂ backup be designed?
    Should the three lines share one LN₂ storage system or each have its own? Sharing saves cost, but the risk level of Line A autologous cells (which cannot be replicated) is far higher than that of Lines B/C, so it is recommended that Line A be equipped with its own independent backup tank.
  • BSL boundary management for the gaseous decontamination system:
    The three lines have different BSL boundaries, so the VHP decontamination cycle must be designed separately for each. BSL-2 Lines A/B need stricter airtightness requirements, and Line C may adopt a different decontamination strategy.
  • Pressure design of WFI vs. PW distribution lines:
    When several lines share a WFI main header, the DBB design must ensure that the process pressure of every branch is lower than the main header pressure. The pressure requirements need to be confirmed jointly by the facility engineers and the process development team at the design stage.

CDMO Scenario 3: inspection readiness, common FDA/EMA inspection focus areas for utilities

Based on the Q3.1 principles, the questions inspectors are most likely to focus on:

  • Are WFI sampling points designed according to the CCS?
    The most common deficiency: sampling only at the WFI generator outlet and not at the location closest to the point of use. Inspectors will ask to see the CCS document and check that the actual sampling point locations are consistent with the drawings.
  • Has the LN₂ backup equipment been qualified?
    Many facilities buy a backup LN₂ freezer but never perform IQ/OQ/PQ, so in an emergency the backup equipment may be in an "unqualified state" and cannot be used in compliance.
  • Is the validation of gaseous decontamination residuals complete?
    Inspectors will ask to see the validation report for VHP residual analysis to confirm that the residual level has no adverse effect on the product. A missing validation of the trace-limit detection method is a typical 483 observation.
  • What is the validation status of the O₂ monitoring system?
    The O₂ monitoring systems in the CO₂, LN₂ and N₂ rooms are safety-critical equipment and must have periodic calibration records and alarm test records. Inspectors will check the equipment calibration logs.
  • Are there sealing records for all wall-penetrating lines?
    Especially all penetrations in gaseous decontamination facilities. Inspectors may ask for on-site confirmation or review the fire/chemical compatibility certification of the sealing materials.

Q3.1 in one sentence

The foundation of ATMP utility design is the same as for conventional plants (GMP standards), but there are two additional layers on top:

  • Negative-pressure ventilation (prevents outside contamination from entering) → corresponds to gaseous decontamination plus BSL containment

Q4.1 — Biosafety Cabinet (BSC) Selection for ATMP Manufacturing

PDA PtC-14 p29–p33 | BSC Classes, Airflow, Grade Requirements, Operational Constraints

Recommendation — BSC Overview

A BSC is flexible in its use, although it typically does not have the same levels of integrated control and monitoring systems as an isolator. Instead, it relies on operators to manually decontaminate the Grade A space, maintain it through good aseptic practice, and appropriately monitor the environment.

This increased reliance upon the operator means that operations should be carefully planned with an understanding of the barrier system limitations of the BSC and should incorporate appropriate risk mitigations to safeguard the aseptic operations.

According to EU GMP Annex 1, the BSC is not classified as barrier technology.

The room environment where the BSC is located should support the maintenance of the Grade A environment within the operational zone. This dictates the need for a Grade B environment to surround the BSC.

BSCs are widely used in ATMP manufacturing as they are easy to install and maintain. They generally occupy a modest footprint that permits highly flexible operations. BSCs allow access to the inner working zone via an open sash.

Note: BSC Class III is the sole exception to the open-sash design.

Key Concepts Explained

The Role of the BSC in ATMP Manufacturing

The BSC is the most common equipment choice for early development and small-batch ATMP production: it installs quickly, takes little space, and is flexible to operate. The trade-off is that sterility control depends almost entirely on personnel technique rather than on automated barriers built into the equipment.

EU GMP Annex 1 states explicitly that a BSC is not barrier technology. In regulators' eyes, a BSC is therefore a "highly operator-dependent" piece of equipment, and its ceiling for sterility assurance is lower than that of an isolator.

The Logic Behind the Grade B Requirement

The Grade A work zone of a BSC is separated from the surrounding room only by an open front sash, with no physical sealed barrier. The background environment must therefore be Grade B so that Grade A conditions can be maintained when the sash is open during operations.

By contrast, a closed isolator has a fully sealed physical enclosure, so its background can be as low as Grade D. This difference directly affects facility construction and operating costs.

BSC Classes — Figure 4.1-1🔍 & Classification

Figure 4.1-1🔍 — Biosafety Cabinet (textual description): A typical Class II BSC shows a rectangular enclosure with a sloped glass sash at the front. Downflow HEPA-filtered air enters from the top plenum and flows vertically downward across the work surface. An inflow of room air is drawn in through the front opening at the sash base, forming an air curtain that protects the operator. Exhaust air is drawn through front and rear grilles, passes through a rear plenum, filtered through a HEPA exhaust filter, and either recirculated or exhausted to the building HVAC depending on type.

Class I BSC: Provides personnel and environmental protection via HEPA exhaust. Does not protect the product. Never use for ATMP manufacturing.

Class II BSC — designed to protect product, personnel, and environment. Acceptable for ATMP aseptic operations. Multiple subtypes:

  • Type A1: Min. inflow 75 ft/min; 70% recirculated, 30% exhausted. No volatile chemicals. Development labs only (non-clinical).
  • Type A2: Min. inflow 100 ft/min. Can handle minute volatile chemicals with thimble (canopy) HVAC connection.
  • Type B1: 70% exhausted / 30% recirculated. For minute quantities of toxic chemicals exhausted toward the rear.
  • Type B2 (Total Exhaust): Min. inflow 100 ft/min; 100% exhaust, zero recirculation. For significant toxic chemical quantities.
  • Type C1: Switchable between Type A and Type B modes — flexible for labs handling both biologics and chemicals.

Class III BSC: Gas-tight enclosure; highest protection level. Operations via attached gloves. Used with high-risk microbiological agents.

Classification and Selection Logic

Analogy: the "exhaust logic" of a hospital operating room

Imagine stir-frying chilies in your kitchen. A Type A1 cabinet recirculates part of the fumes inside the kitchen (the food is protected, but the smoke stays in the room). A Type B2 cabinet is like a powerful range hood that vents all the fumes straight outdoors.

In an ATMP facility, if the product contains a viral vector or is cytotoxic, you must choose the B2 total-exhaust type, because no volatile organic compounds can be allowed to recirculate in the room.

Airflow Pattern — Figure 4.1-2🔍

Figure 4.1-2🔍 — Airflow Pattern in a Biosafety Cabinet (textual description): The diagram illustrates vertical laminar downflow from a supply HEPA filter in the top plenum. Room air is drawn inward through the front sash opening (inflow velocity arrow pointing inward). Inside the cabinet, downflow air splits at the work surface: a portion is drawn into front grille slots, another portion into rear grille slots. Combined air is filtered through a rear HEPA (for recirculated types) or exhausted through a top HEPA (for B-type). The key principle: the inward airflow at the sash creates a protective air curtain preventing room-air contamination from entering the work zone, while HEPA downflow keeps the product zone clean.

Operating Considerations for the BSC

  • Easy-to-perform manual processes through the sash opening
  • Flexibility to adjust how the BSC is used based on process needs
  • Rapid changeover from process to process

Constraint Considerations for the BSC

  • Material transfers can be difficult due to the need for manual sanitizing upon entry to the Grade A zone
  • An assistant is often needed to conduct material transfer so the operator can continuously maintain hands within the Grade A zone
  • Wrist rests should be purchased to avoid the operator covering the exhaust and effectively bringing contaminated air into the Grade A environment
  • Long gloves are required — gowns cannot be sanitized upon entry to Grade A and should not enter this zone

Surfaces inside BSCs are typically cleaned and disinfected manually, requiring intensive training to validate this procedure. Hence, BSCs require comprehensive QRM to ensure maintenance of aseptic conditions during all activities within the critical zone.

The open-sash operation means personnel gowning is directly exposed to the product being handled. Gowning requirements are more stringent and expensive than for isolators, translating into a more complex and costlier EM program.

Operating Points and Risk Analysis

Analogy: the BSC air curtain is like the air curtain at a doorway

The air curtain at a large supermarket entrance keeps outdoor dust out, but if you reach your arm in and stir, the curtain breaks down. The inflow air curtain at the BSC front opening works on the same principle: whenever the operator's arms move at the front opening, they disturb the protective airflow.

This is why a wrist rest matters so much. It holds the arms in a proper position and prevents them from blocking the exhaust grille or disrupting the airflow pattern.

Key risk: material transfer is the BSC's biggest weakness

Materials entering a BSC must be wiped down with disinfectant by hand. This takes two people: one working inside the BSC and one outside passing in and disinfecting the materials. If there is only one person, the operator's hands must repeatedly go in and out of the BSC, and each pass can disturb the integrity of the Grade A airflow.

By contrast, in an isolator, materials enter through an RTP (rapid transfer port) or MAL (material air lock) with fully automated decontamination, so there is no risk from manual wiping.

Why Long Gloves Are Necessary

BSC operation requires long gloves (extending above the wrist) because the operator's sleeves and wrists cannot be disinfected to a qualified level before entering Grade A. This is completely different from the integrated gloves of an isolator, which are part of the equipment and can be integrity tested. Personal gloves for a BSC are consumables that, before each use, rely on hand hygiene and procedural controls.

Q4.2 — Isolator Selection for ATMP Manufacturing

PDA PtC-14 p33–p36 | Open vs. Closed Design, VHP, MAL/RTP, CFD, Pressure Management

Recommendation — Isolator Overview

An isolator typically has integrated monitoring of the Grade A environment using viable and non-viable (total particulate) monitoring in addition to air-velocity and pressure monitoring. This provides the ability to control and monitor the environment while the isolator is in use.

Isolators have the capability of undergoing an automatic decontamination cycle as they can be fully closed from the external environment. This ability during operations also provides effective separation from operators who can only interact with the interior through glove ports. Maintenance of Grade A conditions within an isolator is made simpler through this separation.

Isolators are considered closed barriers to the surrounding environment and may be placed in a Grade C or Grade D environment based on the CCS and market requirements.

In EU GMP Annex 1:

  • Closed isolators require a background of Grade D or higher
  • Open isolators with small openings for continuous/semicontinuous infeed/outfeed require a Grade C background

The isolator system provides a Grade A work zone and should be equipped with an integrated, automated, and validated decontamination system (e.g., vaporized or nebulized H₂O₂).

Core Advantages of Isolators

Isolator vs BSC: the Essential Difference in Monitoring Capability

A BSC relies on the operator's visual judgment and procedural discipline to maintain Grade A, whereas an isolator monitors Grade A status automatically in real time through integrated sensors (particle counter, differential pressure gauge, anemometer).

An isolator alarms immediately on any abnormality (pressure drop, particle excursion). In a BSC, the operator may only learn that contamination has occurred after the operation is over and the sampling results come back.

The Strategic Significance of a Grade D Background

A closed isolator needs only a Grade D background at minimum, which is a significant cost advantage for a CDMO:

  • Grade D room: lower gowning requirements, lower EM frequency, lighter training burden
  • Grade B room (required for a BSC): equivalent to the environmental grade of the core aseptic area, with substantially higher construction and operating costs

Over the long term, the high CapEx of an isolator (equipment cost) can be offset by low OpEx (Grade D background, less gowning, less EM).

Closed Isolator Design — Figure 4.2-1

Figure 4.2-1 — Closed Isolator with Material Air Lock, Centrifuge, and Incubator (textual description): The figure shows a modular closed isolator system. The main central chamber is a large rectangular enclosure with glove ports mounted on the front glass panel — operators interact with contents exclusively through these integrated gloves. During operation, the operator's only access to an isolator is through the gloves mounted on the glass panels. The quality of the gloves, set up, and design of the workspace are important and offer flexibility. Working with gloves through the barrier might be more challenging and could require enhanced training.

According to EU GMP Annex 1, isolators are classified as a barrier technology. Open or closed isolators may be attached to adjacent chambers, permitting the closed transfer of materials in and out via a MAL (Material Air Lock).

The VPHP used in the MAL decontamination cycle might pose a risk to the transferred material and should be carefully assessed.

Additional process technologies — such as an incubator, centrifuge, or robotic system — can be attached and integrated within an isolator system to provide a closed and continuous working environment.

MAL Design and VHP Risks

Analogy: the airlock of a submarine

When a submarine needs to let someone out underwater, it uses an airlock: the person enters, the inner door is sealed, water is flooded in, and once the pressure is equalized the outer door is opened. The MAL works on exactly the same logic, except that the "water" is replaced by the "VHP decontaminant":

  1. Materials enter the MAL and the outer door is closed
  2. The MAL is filled with VHP and the decontamination cycle is completed
  3. The VHP is aerated (purged) out
  4. The inner door is opened and the materials enter the Grade A zone of the isolator

Throughout the process the main chamber of the isolator is never opened to the outside, so Grade A integrity is fully maintained.

Potential Damage from VHP to Materials

VHP (vaporized hydrogen peroxide) is a strong oxidizer and can damage the following materials:

  • Consumables containing natural rubber or silicone (may degrade)
  • Living cells or tissue (residual VHP concentration must be assessed)
  • Some metal surfaces (long-term exposure may cause oxidation)

In practice, a compatibility study and residual level validation are needed to confirm that the VHP has been aerated down to a safe level after decontamination before the inner door may be opened.

The Strategic Value of Integrated Equipment

Integrating a centrifuge and incubator inside the isolator brings several cell therapy process steps (separation, culture, filling) into one closed Grade A environment, with no need to transfer repeatedly between pieces of equipment, since every transfer is a contamination risk. For high-value ATMPs such as CAR-T, this closed, continuous workflow secures both quality and efficiency.

Leak Rate Management and Risk Implications

Leak Rate Calculation Framework

Maximum leak rate = 5% v/h (volume/hour)
Typical actual value = 3–5% v/h

If isolator volume = 200 L
Maximum allowable leakage = 200 L × 5% = 10 L/h

Dual risk by leak direction:
- Exfiltration: product/viral vector escapes → operator exposure risk
- Infiltration: external contaminants enter → loss of sterility assurance

Glove Integrity Testing: The Most Often Overlooked Critical Point

Gloves are the weakest link of an isolator. They are stretched and rubbed every day, and pinhole breaches are almost invisible. If a glove breaks:

  • Positive-pressure isolator: the product is exposed to outside air (contamination risk)
  • Negative-pressure isolator (containing toxic ATMP): the operator is exposed to the product (safety risk)

PDA therefore recommends regular glove integrity testing (pressure decay method or dye penetration method), with the frequency determined by QRM, typically before each batch or at least weekly.

For highly potent or biologically active ATMPs (e.g., viral vectors, gene therapy products), the diagram indicates that additional HEPA filters — a double-HEPA configuration — may be required to ensure no particles or aerosols escape the contained environment.

CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) studies are powerful tools to predict the spreading of particles and aerosols. Particles and aerosols should be transferred to air-return duct filters in the fastest and shortest pathway possible.

The design process should use CFD to demonstrate control of airflow towards the safety filters within the air-return ducts. The criticality of the ATMP should always drive the necessary design attributes for both tightness (leaks) and airflow controls.

For potent products, additional HEPA filters should be fitted before the air-return ducts to avoid spreading particles and aerosols outside the isolator chamber.

The Importance of CFD and Safety Filtration

Analogy: CFD is like running a wind tunnel test before designing a smoke exhaust system

When an architect designs office ventilation, a computer simulation of airflow is used to find which corners will have dead zones (no airflow) and where air speeds will be high enough to cause discomfort. CFD does the same job in isolator design, except that the "air" becomes "aerosol containing viral vector" and the consequence changes from "discomfort" to "operator infection" or "product contamination".

A good CFD design should ensure that the path of all aerosols from the point of generation to the safety filter is shortest and fastest, with no indirect path that bypasses the filter.

Where Double HEPA Applies

Standard isolator: a single HEPA safety filter is sufficient.

ATMPs containing high-titer viral vectors (such as AAV, lentivirus): consider a double HEPA design, in which the first stage captures the vast majority of particles and the second stage acts as the final barrier. This is the same exhaust treatment logic used in BSL-2+ or BSL-3 biosafety facilities.

Key regulatory point: integrity testing of the safety filter (filter integrity test, such as the DOP/PAO test) should be included in the isolator's periodic maintenance program.

Operating Considerations of Isolators

  • Provides a controlled closed environment to protect the product and the operator
  • Surface decontamination with VPHP (vaporized hydrogen peroxide) or other decontamination agent systems is automated and validated
  • Controlled material transfer in and out via RTPs (Rapid Transfer Ports) or MALs with automated, validated decontamination cycles
  • Isolation of the pharmaceutical product within a small, closed environment that avoids spreading highly potent pharmaceutical products to surrounding areas
  • Higher BSL requirements can be accommodated due to the closed environment, automated decontamination, and the possibility of inactivation
  • More sustainable due to lower surrounding cleanroom environment (Grade D): less room EM, less gowning, and less related training

Constraints of Isolators

  • Isolators require careful design to ensure that all required aspects of the process can be executed through the designated glove ports and that all required material transfers can occur through designated RTPs or MALs
  • Isolators may, in some circumstances, be less flexible in terms of significant process or equipment changes
  • Isolators require routine leak-testing of the chamber(s) and integrity-testing of the gloves, sleeves, and glove ports to ensure that the Grade A environment is maintained

Dual Pressure Challenges and CDMO Equipment Selection

The Key Conflict: the Positive vs Negative Pressure Dilemma

For an ATMP isolator, pressure design faces a fundamental conflict:

  • Positive pressure: protects the product by preventing external contaminants from entering (the standard practice in traditional aseptic pharmaceutical manufacturing)
  • Negative pressure: protects the operator and the environment by preventing aerosols containing viral vectors or cytotoxic substances from escaping (a biosafety requirement)

An ATMP needs both at once, which makes this one of the most complex engineering challenges in BSC and isolator design. The solution is usually a pressure gradient design (for example, positive pressure in the core chamber and negative pressure in the exhaust path) or double HEPA safety filtration.

CDMO Decision Framework for ATMP Equipment Selection

When evaluating whether to use a BSC or an isolator for a new ATMP client, think through the following in order:

  1. BSL level: What is the biosafety level of the product? Is containment at BSL-2 or higher required? → Determines whether a closed isolator is mandatory
  2. Batch size and process complexity: Is it for a single small-batch clinical trial, or for commercial production? → Affects the CapEx vs OpEx judgment
  3. Process flexibility needs: Will the process change significantly over the next 2–3 years? → A BSC is more flexible, while an isolator needs precise up-front planning
  4. Regulatory market: Is the main market the EU (under strong EU GMP Annex 1 pressure) or the US FDA? → Affects the expectations for barrier technology

Background Grade Difference: Grade B vs. Grade D

This is one of the most fundamental differences between a BSC and an isolator. A BSC requires a grade B background room, whereas an isolator needs only a grade D background (the lowest requirement).

The Regulatory Position Is Clear: BSCs Are High-Risk Equipment

Here PDA directly cites the regulators' view: a BSC is regarded as "high risk" equipment because the operator's body (arms, gloves) enters the grade A zone and becomes the largest source of contamination risk.

EU GMP Annex 1 expressly does not classify a BSC as barrier technology, which is a fundamental difference in regulatory standing.

Analogy: A Door That Is Locked vs. Not Locked

A BSC is like a door with a gap: the inside may be clean, but the operator's arms must pass through the gap to work, so there is no true "complete separation." An isolator is like a fully sealed glove box, where the operator can only reach the interior through fixed gloves. The two are fundamentally different in risk level.

Operational Comparison of Biosafety Cabinets and Isolators

BSCs and isolators are typically equipped with a filter of at least a HEPA classification. The type of HEPA filter installed is expected, at a minimum, to have particulate removal of 99.99% when tested, in accordance with internationally acceptable standards. The filtration capability of HEPA filters in BSCs and isolators should provide a Grade A operating work zone.

Since a BSC is operated via an open sash compared with the closed operating mode of an isolator, there is a greater need to manage particulates and contaminants through good aseptic practice. Products in the work zone of the BSC may be exposed to particulates from gowning worn by personnel since personnel are required to manipulate the products directly via gloved hands.

In contrast, an isolator provides a closed environment where personnel indirectly access the work zone through integrated gloves. The use of gloves attached to the isolator may make product-handling inconvenient (i.e., due to the loss of some physical dexterity) in an isolator compared to a BSC. However, indirect access not only protects the product and process but also protects the operator as there is no risk of transfer of contamination to the operator's gowning.

Production within isolators also reduces the gowning requirement for operators since they may be gowned in accordance with the requirements of the background environment. This means operators working with isolators may be gowned for Grade D (or C) operations, while operators working with BSC must gown for Grade A operations.

Material transfers in a BSC are performed with the open sash, as one operator transfers items to the operator working in the BSC. Transfer technologies such as RTPs can provide lower-risk transfers. Isolators use transfer systems like MALs and/or RTPs to provide a safe and rapid transfer after a vaporized or sprayed decontamination agent cycle.

Commentary: Key Operational Comparison Points

HEPA Filtration: The Common Foundation

Both BSCs and isolators rely on HEPA filters as the basis of the grade A environment (removal efficiency ≥ 99.99%). This is a prerequisite, not a factor that distinguishes the two.

The Practical Impact of Gowning Grade

Operators using a BSC must wear grade A gowning (sterile gown, double gloves, face mask, etc.), whereas operators using an isolator need only grade D gowning (basic work garments).

Practical impact: each grade A gowning set can cost tens of US dollars, and it requires intensive training and environmental monitoring, so the cumulative long-term cost is very high.

Material Transfer: A High-Risk Node

Material transfer into a BSC is the highest-risk operation: the sash must be opened and an assistant hands in the materials while sterility is maintained. This requires two operators working together, and every transfer is a contamination risk point.

An isolator transfers materials in through a MAL (material airlock) or RTP (rapid transfer port) after VPHP decontamination, which greatly reduces the risk, but the effect of H₂O₂ on the materials must be evaluated.

Commentary: Pressure Cascade and Airlock Design

Two Pressure Control Strategies

A BSC uses a pressure cascade: across the facility, a continuous pressure differential is established from grade A → B → C → D, with each grade 10-15 Pa higher than the surrounding area to prevent contaminated air from flowing in the reverse direction. This requires precise coordination of the HVAC across the whole facility.

An isolator uses a pressure bubble: the isolator itself is an independent high-pressure bubble and does not depend on the facility-wide pressure differential system. The grade D background room is relatively simple, and the isolator maintains positive pressure internally on its own.

The Key Role of the MAL (Material Airlock)

The MAL is one of the most critical design components of an isolator system. Before any material enters the isolator, the MAL must run a full VPHP decontamination cycle (typically 30-60 minutes) to ensure that the grade A sterile integrity inside the isolator is not compromised.

Design focus: the MAL interlock must prevent both doors from being open at the same time, following the same design logic as a spacecraft airlock.

Decision Framework: When to Choose BSC vs. Isolator

Autologous vs. Allogeneic Considerations

Autologous ATMPs (patient-specific) typically involve:

  • Small batch sizes (one patient = one batch)
  • High product diversity and process variability
  • Frequent rapid changeovers between different patient materials
  • Shorter manufacturing campaigns

Allogeneic ATMPs typically involve:

  • Larger batch sizes from a single donor source
  • More standardized, repetitive processes
  • Longer manufacturing campaigns
  • Commercial-scale throughput potential

Phase-Appropriate Approach

Clinical phase (Phase I/II): BSC may be pragmatic given low batch frequency, limited capital, and need for rapid process adjustments. Regulatory agencies generally accept BSC at early clinical stages if appropriate risk mitigation is in place.

Late clinical / Commercial phase: Regulators increasingly expect isolator-level containment for licensed products. The investment in isolator infrastructure becomes justified by the higher production volumes and regulatory expectation for robust sterility assurance.

Commentary: Decision Framework

Autologous vs. Allogeneic: Different Equipment Logic

Autologous ATMPs: one batch per patient, very small batches, frequent changeovers, and a process that may differ each time. The flexibility of a BSC is an advantage here, and the complex changeover procedures of an isolator may actually reduce efficiency.

Allogeneic ATMPs: one cell line can serve many patients, with larger batches and a standardized, repeatable process. This is the isolator's home ground: the fixed investment is amortized over each batch, giving a better ROI.

The Evolution of Regulatory Expectations: When Must You Upgrade?

The current regulatory position:

  • Phase I/II: a BSC is acceptable (with adequate additional risk mitigation measures)
  • Phase III / marketing application: higher sterility assurance begins to be expected, and the isolator is gradually becoming the standard
  • Commercial licensure: in the spirit of Annex 1, the isolator has become the de facto industry standard

Practical advice: even if you use a BSC now, reserve space and HVAC capacity in the facility design for a future isolator installation.

Q5.1 — What is important to consider in a mock-up for biosafety cabinets and isolators?

Key considerations for BSC and isolator mock-ups | pp. 43–45

Recommendation — PDA Guidance

A mock-up for the BSC or isolator at the vendor's site should be built to ensure that all reasonably practicable steps have been taken to ensure good aseptic practice when performing the necessary motions for the process, ergonomics for the operators, and the ability to perform the required maintenance.

With an isolator system, the vendor normally performs a mock-up with wood, cardboard, or similar rigid materials because, after the isolator system is installed, the glove position can only be changed with significant effort and cost. With BSCs, the designs are normally standard and vary only with the size of the BSC.

The mock-up exercise should consider the following key elements:

  • Working position — seated vs. standing
  • Positions of all equipment within the Grade A area
  • Positions of all components and materials (hangers, trays, stands)
  • Position of process waste
  • Location of product-contact tools continuously bathed in first air
  • Process sequence/flow and all required manipulations and interventions
  • Location of critical surfaces relative to first air and operator movements
  • Locations of continuous viable and nonviable sampling in Grade A
  • Operator hand/arm/glove movements — hand must not pass over critical surfaces
  • Manipulations tested by both right- and left-handed individuals, varying heights
  • Frequency and duration of operations; weight/force for each action
  • Setup and performance of cleaning and disinfection actions

Commentary: Core Concepts

Why Do We Need a Mock-Up?

A mock-up uses wood, cardboard or full-scale physical models to simulate how operators will actually perform aseptic operations inside an isolator or biosafety cabinet, before the real equipment is manufactured or purchased.

ATMP processes involve a great deal of manual manipulation (cell culture transfers, syringe filling, opening and closing of containers). These actions must be performed in a grade A environment without disrupting first air (the first unidirectional flow of sterile air). Once the equipment has been built, correcting any design flaw is extremely expensive.

  • Isolators: glove port positions can hardly be changed once installed, so all working angles must be confirmed through a mock-up at the design stage
  • BSCs: the design is more standardized, but you still need to confirm that the selected size suits the process requirements

Analogy: a surgical training simulator

Before performing real laparoscopic surgery, a surgeon first practices instrument angles and force on a simulator, and operates on a patient only once the feel is right. A mock-up is the "surgical simulator" of pharmaceutical equipment: before the real equipment is fabricated, a low-cost model is used to verify that every movement is feasible.

A single misplaced glove position can force the operator to reach over an open container during a critical step. This directly violates the first air principle and creates a risk of microbial contamination.

ATMP-Specific Considerations

ATMP processes usually run much longer than those for conventional small-molecule drugs, and operators may need to work continuously in the glove box for hours. The mock-up must evaluate:

  • Fatigue and ergonomics during prolonged operation
  • Individual variation: whether operators of different heights and handedness can all work safely
  • The continuity of complex operation sequences (such as cell counting, dispensing and closing)

Operator Involvement & Mock-Up Process

For the mock-up, it is highly recommended to involve the operator(s) responsible for handling the equipment in routine production. The mock-up process should be conducted by a minimum of two trained operators so the second person can observe and record actions or violations that may not be apparent to the person conducting the process.

The mock-up may be performed using simplified representations manufactured from basic materials to aid in rapid, low-cost iteration — especially during the initial design phase when there may be multiple iterations. However, for the final mock-up testing, the selected representations must be adequate to challenge the weight/force that may be required for the actual manipulations.

Besides rigid mock-up studies, computerized mock-ups and virtual-reality mock-ups can also support the physical mock-up (see Figures 5.1-1, 5.1-2, 5.1-3). Physical, computerized, or virtual mock-ups are often retained by the operating company to:

  • Test new process movements or interventions before incorporating them into the process
  • Provide preliminary training to new operators

Cost spent on making the model realistic is an investment in process maintenance over time.

Commentary: Operator Involvement and Iteration

Analogy: car crash-test dummies

Car safety tests use dummies instead of people in crash tests, so that design flaws are found before the vehicle goes into mass production. A physical mock-up plays the same role: a low-cost model (wood, cardboard) stands in for expensive stainless steel equipment, so that all ergonomic issues are caught before the design is fixed.

Once an isolator has been built, changing a glove port position can mean weeks of downtime and hundreds of thousands of dollars. A mock-up is the tool that "finds the problem before the welding is done."

Key point: why at least two operators are needed

The person performing the operation often cannot notice their own non-compliant movements (for example, an arm unconsciously passing over an open container). The second person, the observer, is equivalent to the observer/recorder in an aseptic process simulation (APS) and serves the same purpose: to catch the first person's blind spots. This principle runs through the entire aseptic manufacturing lifecycle.

Q5.2 — Differences in chamber leak-integrity testing between isolator and BSC?

Differences in leak integrity test frequency between isolator and BSC chambers | pp. 46–47

Recommendation — Isolator Leak Testing

For isolators, the leak test should be performed frequently with the interval established based on the frequency of intended operations in an appropriate risk assessment.

The leak-testing frequency for isolators should not be limited to periodic evaluation, but rather should include the following situations:

  • After changing gloves and sleeves (documented in the glove change management protocol)
  • Following any maintenance or repair that has breached the sealed structure
  • Supporting failure investigations, especially those involving viral and/or bioburden contaminations

PDA Points to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Sterile Pharmaceutical Products in Isolators (2020) recommends that leak-testing be performed at defined periods, at a minimum, at the beginning and end of each batch or campaign and following any intervention that would affect the integrity of the unit.

The testing procedures are described in Annex E.3 of ISO EN 14644-7. Methods include:

  • Oxygen method (only for negative pressure)
  • Pressure-change method
  • Parjo method (very rare; mainly atomic industries)
  • Constant-pressure method (ISO 10648-2)
  • Tracer gas method (PDA TR No. 34)

Commentary: Isolator Leak Testing

Why do isolators need frequent leak testing?

An isolator is a closed system: its sterility depends on the physical integrity of the chamber. If any part leaks, external microorganisms or particles can enter the grade A environment and directly jeopardize product sterility. Integrity must therefore be reconfirmed after every operation that could affect it.

When isolator leak testing is triggered:

  • Routine: before the start and after the end of each batch
  • After maintenance: any opening, part replacement or repair
  • After glove changes: gloves are the most common integrity weak point
  • Investigation support: when unexplained microbial or viral contamination occurs

Overview of the Main Test Methods

Pressure-change method:
  - Principle: pressurize the chamber (positive-pressure isolator) or evacuate it (negative pressure),
    then observe the pressure change over a defined time
  - Acceptance criterion: usually pressure change ≤ 0.25% / min (per ISO 14644-7)
  - Advantages: no additional reagents, simple to perform, can be run frequently

Oxygen method (negative-pressure isolators):
  - Principle: monitor the change in oxygen concentration inside the chamber; when outside air
    leaks in, the O₂ concentration rises, indicating a leak

Tracer gas method:
  - Principle: release a tracer gas (e.g. He) inside the chamber and use an external detector
    to measure whether any gas escapes
  - Advantages: high sensitivity, can locate the leak point
  - Disadvantages: higher cost, requires dedicated equipment

BSC — Containment Testing vs. Leak Test

As BSCs are an open system, a leak test cannot be performed before each batch as is done with an isolator system. A containment test method could be used instead of the leak test.

This testing is typically performed:

  • Upon first commissioning and validation for use
  • Repeated periodically (e.g., annually or biannually) to check continued performance
  • When required for significant investigations into the system's operation

Containment test methods referenced include the KI-Discus test and the segregation test (described further in Q5.3).

Commentary: The Fundamental Difference of the BSC

Open system vs. closed system: a fundamental difference

A BSC (biosafety cabinet) is an open system: it has an opening at the front (the sash opening) through which the operator's arms enter. This means it is physically impossible to seal completely, so conventional pressure-hold leak testing cannot be applied.

The BSC's protection principle is airflow, not a physical barrier:

  • Downward HEPA-filtered airflow protects the product from particulate contamination
  • Inward airflow (inflow) prevents hazardous materials inside from escaping into the operator's area

Therefore, "integrity" testing of a BSC tests the effectiveness of the airflow, not the physical tightness.

Q5.3 — Similarities and differences in airflow visualization (smoke study) between BSC and isolator?

Differences and similarities in airflow visualization (smoke studies) between BSCs and isolators | pp. 47–50

Airflow Visualization — Common Principles

The airflow visualization, or smoke study, is a method used to aid in the evaluation of the CGMP-required airflow generated by a BSC, isolator system, or containment enclosure.

Common criteria include:

  • Unidirectional airflow in Grade A areas
  • Airflow around the apertures between Grade A and lower-grade areas

Isolator — Airflow Visualization Objectives

For isolator systems, airflow is designed to ensure that first air from the HEPA filter flows over the sterile product. The objective is to verify that airflows create a "sweeping action over and away from the product" under dynamic conditions (FDA, EU GMP Annex 1, PIC/S Annex 1, WHO TRS 1044 - Annex 2).

The study visualizes flow at:

  • Downflow across the chamber/work zone at the working position
  • Airflow with sweeping action over and away from the product
  • Airflow around large objects that may generate turbulence
  • Airflow during manual operations, interventions, and transfers
  • Airflow during processing of open units — no turbulence enters open units
  • Airflow at apertures — flow from critical area outward without reflux or entrainment

Studies performed at rest AND in operation, including during interventions. Air must not ingress from lower-grade to higher-grade zones.

Commentary: Core Concepts of Smoke Studies

Why are smoke studies needed?

Airflow is invisible under normal conditions. A HEPA filter supplies clean air, but whether this air actually flows across the product surfaces in the right direction and at the right velocity cannot be judged by eye.

A smoke study (airflow visualization) uses a harmless smoke tracer to visualize the direction, velocity and disturbance of the airflow, confirming that the equipment meets grade A requirements in both its design and operating states.

Smoke studies are a mandatory requirement of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) and must be performed in both the at-rest and in-operation states.

Core Objectives of Isolator Smoke Studies

The purpose of an isolator is to protect the product (product protection). The core questions of the smoke study are therefore:

  • Does the first clean airflow from the HEPA directly cover all product-contact surfaces?
  • Does the airflow form a "sweeping action", moving from above the product downward and then toward the exhaust, carrying any particles away from the product?
  • Do the operator's hand movements disrupt the direction of first air?
  • With containers open, is any turbulent air entering them?

Analogy: a fume extraction hood

A kitchen range hood is designed so that cooking fumes rise into the exhaust duct rather than drifting toward the cook's face. If you light a match and watch the path of the smoke, you can immediately tell whether the hood is working properly.

A smoke study is the pharmaceutical version of this principle: smoke replaces cooking fumes and the HEPA filter replaces the exhaust duct, to confirm that "the clean airflow really moves in the right direction, protecting the product rather than contaminating it."

BSC — Airflow Visualization Requirements

For BSCs, the airflow visualization mandated by NSF/ANSI 49 – 2019 requires the following to be performed at least annually:

  • Airflow along the centerline of the work surface is downward
  • Airflow along the entire perimeter of the sash at the outside of the cabinet is inward into the grill — does not intrude into the work zone
  • Airflow behind the sash, on top of the access opening, does not escape from the BSC
  • Airflow behind the window, at the sides and top, is not leaking out of the BSC

For BSCs as containment enclosures, the primary purpose is to ensure that any hazardous product material liberated in the airflow is transported away from the operator. Visualization focuses on:

  • Airflow around large objects that may generate turbulence
  • Airflow around continuous viable and nonviable sampling equipment
  • Airflow around hand movement and equipment during required operations
  • Airflow during interventions
  • Airflow remaining undisturbed by external airflow from surroundings

Documentation Requirements

The documentation of the airflow visualization should be similar regardless of the equipment type. The FDA and EMA recommend providing:

  • Visual recordings from as many angles as necessary
  • Detailed summary of equipment and supplies present
  • Number of personnel present
  • List of all activities simulated

Commentary: BSC Differences and Containment Testing

Containment testing methods

In addition to smoke studies, a BSC can be assessed with quantitative containment tests:

  • KI-Discus test (EN 12469): potassium iodide (KI) salt particles are released inside the BSC and the concentration outside the BSC (in the operator's area) is measured to calculate the protection factor. This is the gold-standard test of a BSC's biosafety performance.
  • Segregation test (ISO 14644-3 §4.2.11): an aerosol of constant concentration is generated in the lower-classified area, and the concentration difference in the protected area is measured to calculate the protection factor
  • Alcohol dispersion test (fume cupboard): a constant-concentration alcohol vapor replaces KI, and the concentration difference on either side of an anthropomorphic dummy is measured

Calculating the Protection Factor

Protection factor (PF) = concentration inside the cabinet / concentration outside the cabinet (operator side)

Example:
  KI concentration inside the cabinet: 10,000 particles/m³
  Concentration measured outside the cabinet: 10 particles/m³
  PF = 10,000 / 10 = 1,000

EN 12469 requires a PF ≥ 1×10⁵ for a Class II BSC
(meaning operator exposure ≤ 1/100,000 of the concentration inside the cabinet)

Significance: higher PF = better operator protection
ATMP applications (highly potent biological products) usually require a higher PF

Common failure modes of smoke studies

  • Turbulence: large equipment (centrifuges, culture flasks) inside the chamber creates dead zones or backflow in the airflow, so particles accumulate near the product
  • Intervention disturbance: when the operator moves an arm quickly into the BSC, non-clean outside air is drawn in (room air intrusion)
  • Shadowing: a larger object shades the product beneath it, so HEPA first air cannot reach the product-contact surfaces directly
  • Short-circuiting: airflow near the HEPA outlet goes straight into the return grille and does not adequately cover the work zone
  • External disturbance: airflow produced by adjacent equipment (fans, personnel movement) disturbs the BSC's inflow air curtain

Q5.4 — What methods are used to maintain facility cascade/pressure stability?

Methods for maintaining facility pressure cascade/pressure stability | pp. 52–53

Recommendation — Pressure Measurement Methods

Pressure cascades can be measured using two methods:

Method 1 — Direct Differential Measurement

Directly measure the pressure difference between two adjacent areas where common areas exist. It is recommended that individual tubing be used between each high- and low-pressure sample-point location (although a manifold is possible but more complex). Advantage: a direct response to a pressure cascade change of state.

Method 2 — Absolute Pressure with Calculation

Measure the absolute pressure of each space and calculate the differential. Advantage: typically uses fewer sensors and provides a wider view of pressure comparison across the facility. Disadvantage: wider error range as two sensors are required for each differential calculation. In either case, a loss of local pressure would be identified.

For a negative-pressure isolator, P2 (chamber) would be negative relative to the surrounding room, and both relative to atmospheric reference.

Commentary: Pressure Measurement Methods

Basic principle of the pressure cascade

The pressure cascade of a cleanroom facility is designed so that air always flows from the higher-grade (cleaner) area to the lower-grade (less clean) area, preventing cross-contamination.

  • Positive bubble: the core aseptic area is at positive pressure relative to its surroundings, ensuring that any leakage runs from the aseptic area outward, not inward
  • Negative sink: the hazardous-material handling area is at negative pressure relative to its surroundings, ensuring that hazardous materials do not escape into adjacent areas
  • Cascade: multiple rooms form a sequence of successively decreasing pressures, creating a "one-way escape path"

Comparison of Two Measurement Methods

Method 1: direct differential pressure measurement
  Measures: the direct pressure difference between two adjacent spaces (ΔP)
  Formula: ΔP = P_high-pressure area - P_low-pressure area (measured directly by a single sensor)
  Advantages: fast response, directly reflects the pressure differential status
  Disadvantages: each adjacent pair needs its own sensor

  Typical acceptance criteria:
  Grade B vs. C: ≥ 10–15 Pa
  Grade C vs. D: ≥ 10–15 Pa
  Grade D vs. outside: ≥ 5–10 Pa

Method 2: differential pressure calculated from absolute pressures
  Measures: the absolute pressure P_abs of each space
  Formula: ΔP_calculated = P_abs(A) - P_abs(B)
  Advantages: fewer sensors, can monitor the overall pressure status of multiple rooms
  Disadvantages: the two sensors each have their own error, so the error of the calculated differential
         is the sum of both
         → the error has a larger effect when measuring small differential pressures (e.g. 10 Pa)

Figure 5.4-1: Differential Pressure Measurement Above the Supply HEPA and in the Isolator Chamber

This figure shows two key pressure measurement points in an isolator system:

  • P1 (above the HEPA): the static pressure of the air before it passes through the supply HEPA filter, reflecting the driving pressure of the air supply system
  • P2 (inside the isolator chamber): the static pressure of the filtered clean air as it enters the working chamber

Measuring the difference P1 - P2 (i.e. the HEPA pressure drop) allows the loading state of the HEPA filter to be monitored: a gradually rising pressure drop indicates increasing HEPA loading and the need for replacement. It is also a key monitoring parameter for keeping the isolator chamber at its designed pressure.

Alarm Response and Pressure Control

A suitable measurement interval for pressure readings should provide a balance between:

  • Small variances caused by internal fluctuations within a controlled space
  • Loss of control parameters
  • Enabling the averaging of results accurately over longer periods

The process and use of the cleanrooms (entries, exits, frequency of use, movements) should be used to determine measurement and recording frequency as this may be different throughout each zone.

If a pressure loss is observed:

  • The air-handling unit (AHU) should react immediately to balance the pressure loss
  • Pressure loss should be recorded
  • An alarm should signal
  • The reason for the pressure loss must be investigated
  • Alarm signals should be timed to avoid signaling for routine operational events (entries and exits)

Manual operation in BSCs and isolators, or the opening of a door at a PAL (Personnel Air Lock) or MAL (Material Air Lock), are the most critical factors influencing air stability. Fast movements should be avoided.

Alarm delays should be minimal (less than 3 minutes) and should be justified as part of the CCS (Contamination Control Strategy).

For cleanrooms: air locks with door interlocks on all doors and pressure stabilization are recommended to avoid pressure fluctuations. Disruption of the differential pressure between air locks and adjacent cleanrooms will disrupt the function as:

  • Pressure sink — negative relative to surrounding spaces
  • Air bubble — positive relative to surrounding spaces
  • Pressure cascade — positive on one side and negative on the other

Commentary: Alarm Design and Pressure Control Strategy

Why is pressure stability particularly important for ATMPs?

An ATMP facility often has to handle both of the following at the same time:

  • Positive-pressure aseptic areas: protect the cell product (a highly potent drug) from environmental contamination
  • Negative-pressure containment areas: prevent viral vectors (adenovirus, lentivirus) from escaping and harming operators

These two types of area coexist in the same facility, and the pressure management system must ensure that their pressure differentials are maintained in every operating state (including door openings and personnel entry and exit). If the pressure cascade collapses, it can jeopardize both operator safety (#1) and product sterility (#2).

Rationale for the alarm delay design

Opening a door inevitably causes a brief pressure fluctuation. If the alarm is triggered the instant each door opens, operators will ignore genuine pressure excursions because of "alarm fatigue".

The logic behind the alarm delay design is therefore:

  • Delay = the time allowed for brief fluctuations caused by normal operations to dissipate
  • PDA PtC-14 sets an upper limit: delay < 3 minutes
  • The delay setting must be supported by a risk assessment and included in the CCS (contamination control strategy) documentation

If the delay exceeds 3 minutes, the window between the loss of pressure control and the alarm is too long, and a contamination event that is difficult to detect may already have occurred in the aseptic area.

Analogy: the differential pressure system of a building's fire doors

In a high-rise fire, the escape stairwell is usually kept at positive pressure to keep smoke out. This pressurization system must start within seconds of the fire alarm and must withstand the pressure shocks caused by people frequently opening the doors.

ATMP cleanroom pressure control follows the same principle: it needs fast response (fast-acting pressure control dampers), tolerates brief fluctuations (doors opening and closing), but cannot tolerate sustained loss of pressure control.

Pressure Control System Design Framework

Typical CCS (contamination control strategy) pressure parameter settings:

  Normal operating range (before the action level):
    Target differential pressure: 15 Pa (Grade B vs. Grade C)
    Alert limit: 10 Pa
    Action limit: <8 Pa (sustained beyond the alarm delay time)

  Alarm delay time:
    Recommended: 30 s to 120 s (adjusted to the frequency of door openings)
    Upper limit: < 3 minutes (PDA PtC-14 requirement)

  AHU response time:
    Pressure deviation detected → AHU adjusts → pressure recovers
    Requirement: AHU response time < alarm delay time (to ensure automatic recovery)

  Airlock door interlock:
    Only one door can be open at a time
    → prevents the pressure cascade from collapsing completely during personnel entry and exit
