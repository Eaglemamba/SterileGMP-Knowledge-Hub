1.0 Introduction

Distribution scope, Last Mile, purpose, and stakeholder framework

1.0 Introduction

The distribution of life science products is extremely diverse, ranging from raw materials to finished product through the supply chain to the end user. Typically, as the distribution process moves from shipments on pallets to individual packages to delivery to the end patient, the term "Last Mile" is applied. The Last Mile defines the point at which products have reached their final destination (e.g., pharmacy, medical practice, private residence). Product temperature must continue to be maintained within registered storage and/or transport conditions.

This may include shipments from a wholesale distributor to a pharmacy where the products are held awaiting customer pick-up or, as the Last Mile process has become an even more complex system, home delivery of products that also include cell and gene therapy.

Key Points

What Is the "Last Mile"?

The Last Mile is the final leg of a medicinal product's journey after it leaves the wholesaler or distribution center, until it reaches its final destination (pharmacy, clinic or the patient's home). This leg is the hardest to control for temperature because:

  • Packaging shrinks from pallets to individual parcels, so thermal protection is reduced
  • Environmental conditions are hard to predict (vehicle cabin temperature, weather)
  • Cell & Gene Therapy products require transport at -80°C on dry ice or in liquid nitrogen

TWi's CDMO Context

As a CDMO, TWi is upstream of the customer's Last Mile once product is shipped. We must ensure that temperature records at delivery are complete and that shipping documents meet GDP, so that the customer can pass TFDA or FDA inspections smoothly.

1.1 Purpose

This technical report provides guidance for establishing a quality management system for distribution of all types of temperature-sensitive medicinal and pharmaceutical products, from preparation of products for transportation at shipping sites to the shipment of products to receiving sites for storage or distribution to patients (end users).

The examples accompanying the points to consider provided herein on how to protect the quality of the product are for demonstrational purposes only.

Purpose

The core purpose of TR39 is to establish a Quality Management System (QMS) for the entire distribution supply chain. The scope covers:

  • Packing preparation at the shipping site
  • Temperature control during transport
  • Acceptance and storage at the receiving site

Note

The examples in the document are for illustration only and are not the only correct approach. Each company should develop SOPs that fit its own product characteristics and regulatory requirements.

1.2 Scope

This technical report offers a resource for all stakeholders involved in the pharmaceutical supply chain and provides a model for qualifications from the process design through implementation and operation to verification. The stakeholders include, but are not limited to:

  • Pharmaceutical and biotech companies of commercial and clinical medicinal products, including manufacturers, contract manufacture organizations (CMOs), and repackagers
  • Health care providers (HCPs)
  • Logistic service providers
  • Wholesalers
  • Distributors (including subcontractors)
  • Pharmacies, including mail order, infusion, specialty, and compounding pharmacies
  • 503B pharmacies currently in the United States
  • Nongovernmental organizations such as UNICEF and PAHO

The stakeholders are responsible and accountable for the processes that control the handling, transport, and storage of the product from the point of manufacture to distribution to the patient. They need to work closely with manufacturers who have all the stability data and knowledge necessary to evaluate excursions.

Applicability & CDMO Positioning

TR39 explicitly lists the CMO (contract manufacturing organization) as one of the stakeholders with responsibility. As a pharmaceutical CDMO, TWi acts as the shipper in cold chain compliance and must ensure that:

  • The URS (User Requirements Specification) is confirmed with the customer (the product owner) before shipment
  • A qualified packaging system is used
  • A Quality Agreement is signed with the LSP (logistics service provider)
  • Stability data are provided to support temperature excursion assessments

What Is a 503B Pharmacy?

503B is a special designation created by the US FDA for outsourcing facilities (large-scale compounding), positioned between a traditional pharmacy and a pharmaceutical manufacturer. A 503B facility must comply with Current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP), unlike a conventional retail pharmacy (503A).

The Three-Stage Framework Explained

Stage 1: Process Design

Before formal testing, define the product's temperature control requirements, select the packaging solution and assess transport route risks. The URS (User Requirements Specification) is the cornerstone of the whole framework: all later qualification testing is based on the URS.

Stage 2: Process Qualification

Execute DQ → IQ → OQ → PQ in sequence to confirm that the design meets requirements, the equipment is installed correctly, operation is consistent, and performance is achieved in the real distribution environment.

Stage 3: Ongoing Process Verification (OPV)

Once qualification is complete, cold chain quality is maintained through the QMS (SOPs, monitoring, training, CAPA). Change control ensures that any change of supplier or route is re-evaluated.

Relationship to TR60 (Process Validation Lifecycle)

The three-stage structure of TR39 closely mirrors the TR60 process validation lifecycle (Stages 1-3): design = Stage 1, qualification = Stage 2, ongoing verification = Stage 3.

2.0 Glossary & 2.1 Abbreviations

Industry definitions and key abbreviations for cold chain qualification

The terms and definitions below are in common use within the industry and represent industry best practices in the experiences of the technical report team.

Glossary of Terms

Key Terms in Depth

URS (User Requirements Specification)

User Requirements Specifications are the starting point of the entire cold chain qualification. In TWi's context, the URS should clearly define: the product's temperature range (e.g., 2-8°C or -20°C), packaging format, transport mode and expected worst-case environmental conditions. If the URS is poorly defined, all later DQ/OQ/PQ work loses its baseline.

The Four Qualification Steps: DQ / IQ / OQ / PQ

These four steps are the full qualification path for a cold chain system (such as an insulated shipper or a temperature-controlled truck):

  • DQ (Design Qualification): Do the drawings and specifications meet the URS?
  • IQ (Installation Qualification): Is the equipment installed correctly according to specification?
  • OQ (Operational Qualification): Under simulated conditions, does the system operate as expected? (Requires a validated test chamber + data loggers)
  • PQ (Performance Qualification): Performed by the personnel who carry out routine shipping, in the real transport environment, to confirm the system is effective and repeatable.

Key Differences Between PQ and OQ

OQ is performed in a controlled laboratory environment (test chamber); PQ is performed in the real distribution environment (actual transport lanes) by the operators responsible for routine shipping. PQ is closer to proof in the "real world".

CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action)

CAPA has two parts: Corrective Action addresses deviations that have already occurred, eliminating the root cause to prevent recurrence; Preventive Action addresses potential risks, removing latent hazards before a problem occurs. In the cold chain, a temperature excursion is the most common trigger for CAPA.

LSP (Logistics Service Provider)

LSPs include carriers, integrators, forwarders and customs brokers. TR39 requires the CDMO to sign a formal Quality Agreement and Service Level Agreement (SLA) with the LSP and to include it in an annual performance review; this is a core GDP compliance requirement.

Active vs. Passive TPS

Understanding with an Everyday Example

An active TPS is like a refrigerator with a compressor, actively maintaining temperature continuously; it suits long-distance sea freight or large pallets. A passive TPS is like an insulated box with dry ice, relying on phase change materials (PCM) absorbing and releasing heat to hold temperature; it suits small-volume courier shipments or Last Mile delivery. The choice of system is a decision output of the URS and the risk assessment.

Stability Budget

This is a key tool for assessing the impact of a temperature excursion. The manufacturer's stability study results determine how long the product can tolerate conditions outside its labeled storage conditions. When TWi ships, the customer (the product owner) should provide Stability Budget data as the scientific basis for excursion assessment.

Placebo Product

In OQ/PQ testing, inexpensive placebo (mock) product is used in place of the real, costly product, but its size and thermal behavior must simulate the real product as closely as possible. This is both a cost control measure and standard qualification practice.

Seasonal Differences in Pack-Out

Hot summer and cold winter environments call for very different pre-conditioning of the insulated shipper. A seasonal pack-out is validated separately for each season; an all-season pack-out uses the same configuration year-round (usually with a more conservative design). When TWi ships to external parties, it should confirm which pack-out version the customer has specified.

Section 1-2 Summary

  • TR39 applies to all stakeholders in temperature-controlled medicinal product distribution, including pharmaceutical manufacturers, CMOs (TWi), logistics service providers (LSPs), pharmacies and healthcare facilities; every party is responsible for temperature control compliance.
  • The "Last Mile" is the most vulnerable link in the cold chain, and the ultra-low-temperature needs (below -80°C) of cell and gene therapy products make the problem even more complex.
  • The TR39 three-stage framework (Process Design → Process Qualification → Ongoing Process Verification) provides a complete cold chain quality management path from URS to CAPA.

Process Design

3.0 Process Design — Overview

Process design begins with defining the requirements for shipping a product from the origin to the destination, which will require multiple inputs from development, logistics, etc. The outcomes of all requirements are a proposed thermal protection system (TPS) and the inputs for the transportation process qualification.

3.0 Process Design: Overview

Process design starts by defining all the requirements for moving a product from the origin to the destination, with inputs from multiple departments such as R&D and logistics. The output of all these requirements is a proposed Thermal Protection System (TPS), which also provides the input for the subsequent shipping process qualification.

Core Concept

TPS = the collective term for the entire transport thermal solution. It can be a foam box plus ice packs (passive) or a refrigerated container (active); either way, the aim is to maintain the required product temperature during transport.

3.1 User Requirements Specification (URS)

The best practice for an organization is to complete a cross-functionally reviewed and approved User Requirements Specification (URS) before starting a qualification process. The URS defines the requirements of the product, packaging, and transport system for the qualification process. Examples of considerations may include but are not limited to:

  • Product storage and transportation temperature
  • Transportation method and/or lanes
  • Performance duration required to perform activities such as pick, pack, ship, or put away
  • Expected ambient temperature exposures or ambient temperature profiles
  • Minimum and maximum expected shipping quantities
  • Product load thermal characteristics and shapes (i.e., payload capacity)
  • Temperature monitoring strategy
  • Operational, safety, and logistical capabilities, including handling and material conditioning
  • Environmental considerations (i.e., reusable, disposable, compostable, or recyclable)

Note: Shock, vibration, and atmospheric pressure considerations at the primary- or secondary-packaging level are outside the scope of this document.

3.1 User Requirements Specification (URS)

Best practice calls for a cross-functionally reviewed and approved URS (User Requirements Specification) to be completed before the qualification program starts. The URS defines all requirements for the product, packaging, and transport system during qualification.

Main Items Covered by the URS

  • Product storage and transport temperature: e.g., 2–8°C, -20°C, -80°C
  • Mode of transport and lanes: air/sea freight, Taiwan→US, Taiwan→EU
  • Performance duration: the time budget for each step of picking, packing, transport, and receipt into storage
  • Ambient temperature exposure: the expected ambient temperature range or temperature profile
  • Shipping quantity range: minimum and maximum load
  • Product thermal characteristics: payload, shape, and thermal mass
  • Temperature monitoring strategy: number and placement of data loggers
  • Operational and logistics capabilities: including material conditioning
  • Environmental considerations: reusable, recyclable, compostable

Analogy

The URS is like the design brief before building a house: you first state how many floors, what earthquake rating, and what insulation standards are needed, so the designer can draw plans that meet the requirements. Without a URS, the subsequent qualification has no clear acceptance criteria.

CDMO Practice Tip

For TWi, with 2–8°C biologics and potential -20°C/-80°C ATMP products, each lane (e.g., Taiwan→US East Coast, Taiwan→EU) should have its own URS that clearly documents the temperature range, maximum transit time, and expected worst-season ambient temperatures.

3.2 Quality Risk Assessment

Risk assessment is the process of identifying, evaluating, and mitigating risks that have the potential to negatively affect product quality, processes, and/or user/patient safety. It requires proactively identifying risks that have the potential to occur, evaluating their relative risk impact (using qualitative or quantitative scoring), and implementing effective design, process, and/or operator/user controls to minimize or eliminate those risks to product quality and user/patient safety.

Risk management procedures should be established to provide a standardized process to conduct, review, and update a risk assessment. A risk assessment is paramount to ensure that patient safety and product quality have been comprehensively considered and met through internal qualification testing, or equivalent vendor qualification testing.

Risk review sessions should be ongoing as well as event-driven (e.g., additional requirements, event-based failures, nonconformances, corrective/preventive actions (CAPA), changes). This will ensure previously evaluated risk levels are still representative of, or updated to reflect, the current state of a product and/or process.

The outcome of the risk assessment should be documented. Plans to handle, store, and distribute the product should be made accordingly. For more information, see PDA TR54, TR58, and ICH Q9.

3.2 Quality Risk Assessment

Risk assessment is the process of identifying, evaluating, and reducing risks that could adversely affect product quality, processes, or patient safety. Potential risks must be identified proactively, scored qualitatively or quantitatively, and reduced or eliminated through design, process, or personnel controls.

Three Key Characteristics of Risk Assessment

  • Proactive: identify risks before problems occur, rather than responding only after an incident
  • Ongoing: review periodically to ensure the assessment reflects the current state
  • Event-driven: a deviation, CAPA, or change triggers reassessment

Common Risk Scenarios (Temperature-Controlled Transport)

  • Summer heat causes the PCM (phase change material) to melt prematurely, so the TPS loses its cooling effect
  • Freight delays exceed the performance duration the TPS was designed for
  • Excessively long layovers at transit airports cause accumulated ambient temperature exposure
  • A refrigerated vehicle breaks down, and the product is exposed to room temperature on the loading dock

Reference Documents

PDA TR54 (quality risk management tools), TR58 (risk assessment practice), and ICH Q9 (quality risk management guideline) are all extended references for the topics mentioned here.

3.3 Design Qualification (DQ)

Design qualification (DQ) is the process of obtaining and documenting evidence that the TPS has been designed in accordance with user requirements. Principles discussed in this section apply to both active and passive TPS solutions.

DQ testing may not be necessary for every process qualification, especially if the shipping system has already been developed and qualified for similar applications. If the risk analysis confirms the need for a DQ, a protocol should be written and results documented.

When evaluating a supplier's prequalified TPS, the supplier's DQ does not need to be part of the evaluation package but may be leveraged to evaluate process parameters, as long as they are technically justifiable and representative of the intended use environment.

DQ may be performed to ensure that the URS are met by the proposed solution; it is performed prior to OQ and PQ. The outcome of a successful DQ assures a high confidence level for successful OQ.

Design testing must consider both physical and thermal protection. Thermal qualification should demonstrate that a TPS can maintain the required temperature range and duration described in the URS.

When a placebo product is used for testing, understanding its thermal dynamic compared to real products is crucial. Assumptions used to design the placebo should be well explained with preliminary test results or thermal calculations.

DQ testing results are also used to define the components to be used as part of the TPS and in the OQ. Qualification results are only valid for the specific components tested. Component specifications and diagrams should be written for each component critical to the TPS performance.

For reusable temperature-controlled shippers, the strategy should additionally include assessment on re-certification, recovery, and refurbishment services.

3.3 Design Qualification (DQ)

DQ (Design Qualification) is the process of obtaining and documenting evidence that the TPS is designed in accordance with the user requirements; it applies to both active and passive systems.

Core Purposes of DQ

  • Confirm that the design solution meets all URS requirements
  • Define the test conditions and the worst-case/best-case locations for OQ and PQ
  • Confirm the specification of each component (a "similar" component cannot be substituted for one that has already been qualified)

Key Principle: Component Specificity

A refrigerant pack of the same volume but a different shape cannot be considered equivalent! A change in shape alters the heat conduction path, and the qualification result is valid only for the specific component used in testing.

Analogy

DQ is like the design verification testing a car undergoes before leaving the factory: crash simulations and fuel consumption measurements are run in the lab to confirm the design meets regulatory requirements before the car enters mass production. Going straight to OQ without DQ is like taking a driving test without ever having seen the design drawings.

Passive TPS

Definition: an insulated container combined with PCM (phase change material, such as ice packs, dry ice, or gel packs). It needs no power and is relatively simple in structure, but its temperature control capability is time-limited (it fails once the PCM has melted).

Typical application: air shipment of small batches of biologics (2–8°C), using pre-chilled gel packs to hold temperature for 48–96 hours.

Active TPS

Definition: a container with a powered refrigeration/heating mechanism, such as a reefer container or an active controlled container. It can maintain temperature for longer but requires power infrastructure.

Hybrid Systems

For hybrid systems (active + passive), all parameters of both types may apply and must be evaluated one by one.

Process Qualification

4.0 Process Qualification — Overview

The qualification exercise can be regarded as the deliberate and purposeful collection and evaluation of data from a process in order to establish evidence that the process can consistently satisfy the user requirements.

The subtle difference between OQ and PQ is that the former is intended to examine the process at the extremes, and the latter is intended to ensure process technical transfer to the process owner. The OQ of a TPS is typically executed in a climate chamber at the anticipated extreme conditions, whereas the PQ is executed in real life according to the Validation Master Plan.

A risk assessment should examine events that are exogenous to the process yet may impact product quality, such as equipment failures or unplanned shipping delays.

All qualification processes require a protocol and a report with a clear and precise description of how the tests were done. The methods section must:

  • Describe the materials used in the tests
  • Explain the conditioning procedure
  • Describe the testing protocol
  • Explain how measurements and calculations were achieved
  • Describe, if necessary, which statistical analyses were used

The Validation Master Plan (VMP) encompasses all aspects of validation required by an organization, including manufacturing processes, equipment, storage, and distribution.

Thermal qualification should not use a macroscopic approach. Rather, it should target the detection of product temperature excursions at critical locations, such as corners and points nearest or farthest from a cold pack (passive) or refrigeration unit (active). In most cases, more than one temperature measuring point is needed.

4.0 Process Qualification: Overview

The Role of the VMP (Validation Master Plan)

The VMP is the organization-level validation overview document, covering everything that requires validation in manufacturing, equipment, storage, and distribution. PQ must be executed in accordance with the VMP to ensure that all temperature management activities are identified, documented, and yield acceptable results.

Analogy: OQ vs. PQ

OQ is like pushing a new car to its limits on the factory test track, while PQ is the break-in drive of 1,000 km on real roads after delivery. The former tests the limits of the system; the latter confirms stability in everyday use.

Principles for Selecting Temperature Measurement Locations

Do not measure temperature at the center point only! Probes must be placed at critical points such as the corners, the location farthest from the refrigerant, and the location closest to the refrigerant. Use the DQ results first to determine which are the high-risk locations, then plan the OQ/PQ probe layout accordingly.

4.1 Operational Qualification (OQ)

The goal of an OQ is to provide documented testing that demonstrates, with a high degree of assurance, that a process or system will function to meet its predetermined acceptance criteria when exposed to anticipated environmental extreme conditions (for example temperature, shock, and vibration).

The location of products or placebo products within the shipper must be clearly documented. Including only the number of products or items is not sufficient; describing their orientation and positions with respect to one another (loading configuration) is essential.

The protocol may include but is not limited to a description of:

  • Scope and activities required to complete an OQ test
  • Clear acceptance criteria
  • Preconditioning of the test materials
  • Temperature-monitoring methodology
  • Ambient temperature profile to stress the active or passive solution
  • Product payloads
  • Shipper orientation
  • Physical protective properties (often tested separately)
  • Rationale of all tested conditions explained and documented

Mechanical performance testing should also be performed as part of the OQ unless otherwise justified.

Not all environmental chambers will provide the same testing conditions, even though they can replicate the same temperature profile. Such parameters as product placement and stacking pattern, airspeed, shelf surface types, and temperature accuracy will have a significant impact on test results.

Most small parcel shipments will experience multiple orientations during handling and should be tested to at least identify potential temperature excursions that were not observed during the original design orientation.

4.1 Operational Qualification (OQ)

The goal of OQ is to provide documented testing that demonstrates, with a high degree of assurance, that the system can meet the predetermined acceptance criteria under expected environmental extremes.

Required Elements of an OQ Protocol

  • Acceptance Criteria: clearly define the temperature range and permitted time, e.g., "product temperature must remain within 2–8°C throughout"
  • Material Preconditioning: to what temperature, and for how many hours, are gel packs pre-chilled?
  • Temperature monitoring method: number of probes, calibration status, recording interval
  • Ambient temperature profile: worst-case thermal profile (e.g., prolonged exposure to +43°C in summer)
  • Load configuration: the specific position and orientation of each product/dummy unit; it is not enough to write "30 vials loaded"
  • Shipper orientation: different orientations such as upright, on its side, and inverted must all be tested

Considerations When Selecting a Climatic Chamber

Even with the same temperature profile set, different climatic chambers may produce different actual test conditions! Contributing factors include airflow velocity, shelf material (metal/wood/plastic), product placement, and temperature uniformity. When changing the climatic chamber, assess whether requalification is needed.

CDMO Application: OQ Strategy for TWi Lanes

For the Taiwan→US lane, OQ must cover the following worst-case conditions:

  • Summer maximum temperature profile (e.g., the ISTA 7D summer thermal profile)
  • Winter minimum temperature profile (to prevent freezing; 2–8°C products also face a freezing risk in winter)
  • Maximum cargo delay time (e.g., normal 36 hours + 12 hours buffer = 48 hours)
  • Various package orientations (upright, on its side)

4.2 Performance Qualification (PQ)

Performance qualification (PQ) is done using the actual transportation or distribution environment and is performed by those who will be executing the day-to-day shipping process. The PQ serves to demonstrate that the qualified system is effective and reproducible in real-world application.

Testing should be performed using typical parameters including product loads, operational handling, transportation routes, and conditioning procedures.

PQ testing should be performed following a preapproved protocol and the results should be presented in a report. The test plan should incorporate standard operating procedures (SOPs) that include:

  • Actual ambient temperature variances, including seasonal changes and cross-hemisphere lanes, if applicable; data should be available to support the lane
  • Representative transportation load configurations
  • Defined packaging configurations
  • Calibrated temperature monitors
  • Sufficient number of tests to assure reliable results
  • List of SOPs relevant to the process
  • Evidence of operator training on the relevant SOPs (extending to all subcontractors)

Other procedures may be used and, if so, they need to be justified. For further understanding, see Section 8.0 Case Studies.

4.2 Performance Qualification (PQ)

PQ is conducted in the actual transport or distribution environment by the operators who handle shipping day to day. Its purpose is to confirm that the qualified system is effective and repeatable in the real world.

Core Elements of the PQ SOP

  • Seasonal temperature variation: PQ must be executed in different seasons, or historical weather data must be used to support the representativeness of the worst-case season
  • Cross-hemisphere lanes: Taiwan→EU may cross from subtropical to temperate zones, so ambient temperatures in different climate zones must be considered
  • Calibrated temperature recorders: use validly calibrated data loggers throughout to ensure the temperature data are reliable
  • Sufficient number of test runs: statistically sufficient to represent the reliability of routine operations
  • Operator training records: including evidence of SOP training for all subcontractors (e.g., third-party logistics providers)

CDMO Application: Seasonal Strategy for Lane PQ

For the Taiwan→US and Taiwan→EU lanes, TWi should plan the following seasonal bracketing tests:

  • Summer (June–September): Taiwan is hot and humid, and the US West/East Coast transit airports are also in their hot period, representing the highest-temperature exposure
  • Winter (December–February): a freezing risk may be encountered (especially for 2–8°C products), representing the lowest-temperature exposure
  • If the lane characteristics are stable, historical data can be used to justify testing in only one season, but the rationale must be fully explained

Analogy: PQ Is "Running It for Real"

OQ is running a marathon distance on a treadmill; PQ is actually running a road race. The real environment has wind, slopes, and crowds jostling, none of which a treadmill can simulate. PQ confirms that you can finish in a real race too.

4.3 Qualification Plan Complete

To close the qualification of the TPS, all completed documentation and the deviations should be summarized in one report, sometimes called Qualification Assurance Summary Report or Validation Summary Report. The conclusion should indicate the applicability and shortcomings of the TPS and list the deviations from the URS. The report should be approved according to established procedures.

4.3 Qualification Plan Complete

To formally close out the qualification of a TPS, all completed documents and deviations must be compiled into a summary report, commonly called a:

  • Qualification Assurance Summary Report
  • Validation Summary Report

The Summary Report Should Cover

  • Applicability and shortcomings of the TPS
  • Deviations from the URS
  • A list of all completed documents: URS, risk assessment, DQ, OQ, PQ, mechanical testing
  • Approval in accordance with established procedures

4.4 Shipping Control Strategy

A shipping control strategy (or quality risk control strategy), which is the output of the quality risk assessment, for drug product in the supply chain should define the measures to implement the qualified TPS within the operational environment to ensure that the product is shipped under the intended conditions within the stability budget. This means anything that can increase the degradation rate of the medicinal product must be controlled.

A control strategy should include the implementation or update of the VMP, SOPs, contracts (e.g., quality agreement, technical agreement), training, and maintenance protocols including calibrations.

In addition, a control strategy may include, in part, allowable times and temperature exposures expressed in a tabular form that reflects product stability, citing the stability budget for the product. This will ensure the qualified TPS will be implemented and used according to the established qualified conditions.

4.4 Shipping Control Strategy

The shipping control strategy is an output of the quality risk assessment. It defines the specific measures for implementing the qualified TPS in the operating environment, ensuring that the product is shipped under the predetermined conditions within the Stability Budget.

Stability Budget

The upper limit of the cumulative temperature excursion time a product can tolerate, for example: "this biologic allows a maximum cumulative exposure of 12 hours below 25°C." The shipping control strategy must ensure that cumulative exposure across the entire supply chain does not exceed this budget.

Components of the Control Strategy

  • VMP: update or implement the Validation Master Plan
  • SOPs: standard operating procedures for packing, shipping, and temperature monitoring
  • Contracts: Quality Agreement, Technical Agreement
  • Training: ensure that all operators (including subcontractors) receive the relevant training
  • Maintenance and calibration: periodic calibration program for temperature recorders and refrigeration equipment
  • Allowable exposure time table: a table listing the maximum allowable exposure time for each temperature band

CDMO Application: Key Points of the TWi Control Strategy

As a CDMO, TWi ships its clients' drug products, so the control strategy must be linked to the stability data provided by the client. Recommendations:

  • Clearly state in the Quality Agreement the client's responsibility to provide the stability budget
  • Establish an "allowable cumulative excursion time table" for each lane so operators can make on-the-spot judgments
  • Include third-party logistics providers (3PLs) in the scope of training and contract management

Process Design & Process Qualification: Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways — Process Design (3.0)

  • URS first: Define all requirements — temperature, lane, duration, payload — before starting any qualification. Cross-functional review and approval is mandatory best practice.

  • Passive vs. Active TPS: Passive (insulated container + PCM) is simpler but time-limited. Active (powered refrigeration) handles longer durations but requires infrastructure. Hybrid systems combine both.
  • Component specificity: A qualification is valid only for the exact components tested. Shape changes in PCM invalidate the result, even if thermal mass is identical.

Key Takeaways — Process Qualification (4.0)

  • OQ in the lab, PQ in real life: OQ uses a climate chamber to test extreme conditions; PQ executes the real lane under real-world operating conditions.
  • More than one temperature probe: Macroscopic (single-point) thermal qualification is misleading. Critical locations (corners, nearest/farthest from cold source) must be monitored.
  • Seasonal bracketing for PQ: Cross-hemisphere and cross-season lanes require hot and cold season testing, or robust data-based justification for bracketing.
  • Stability budget governs control strategy: The Shipping Control Strategy must ensure cumulative temperature excursions remain within the product's stability budget throughout the supply chain.
  • Training extends to subcontractors: All parties executing the shipping process — including 3PLs — must be trained on relevant SOPs.

Key Takeaways: Process Qualification (4.0)

5.0

Ongoing Process Verification — Overview

OPV Overview: Maintaining and Improving After Qualification

5.0 Ongoing Process Verification

After process design and process qualification of a TPS (Temperature-Controlled Packaging System), the shipping organization should implement the TPS in operations according to the control strategy. By applying continuous process verification, this will ensure that the TPS will protect the product throughout transit—from its preparation at the shipping site, during transport, and until its arrival at the receiving site—as intended during the qualification for quality, effectiveness, and safety.

Continuous process verification applies to all transportation modes and to all parties involved in the transportation of medicinal products and its related activities, such as (but not limited to):

  • Preparing the goods for transport
  • Loading/unloading goods into shipping equipment
  • Loading/unloading from one piece of shipping equipment to another
  • In-transit handling between transportation (e.g., airport transit, harbor transit)
  • Receipt of goods

Each participant in the distribution process has an obligation to support the goal of protecting the product, minimizing opportunities for exposure to temperatures outside the required range, and to assess and respond appropriately based on the impact of such exposures, should they occur.

Participants in the transportation process can be categorized as shippers, logistics service providers (LSP) and receivers. LSP refers to the providers that contribute to the transportation and storage–distribution process, including carriers, integrators, forwarders, and customs brokers.

In order to support implementation, shippers must thoroughly document the processes used and improvement modifications made, as well as the reasoning for those changes, during the qualification process. This documentation is the critical reference for the creation of a process SOP, which should include procedures for process monitoring, environmental monitoring, nonconformance management, training, and the application of these procedures by the managing stakeholders.

Process monitoring must demonstrate that the state of control is being maintained where the set of controls consistently provides assurance of continued process performance and product quality. Monitoring tools, control systems, and regular trend analysis must be in place to measure performance and establish the level of distribution process capability.

OPV Overview

After the initial process design and qualification are complete, the organization enters the OPV phase, a continuous cycle of "maintain and improve." OPV ensures that the temperature-controlled packaging system (TPS) keeps delivering, on every actual shipment, the protection that was demonstrated during qualification.

Analogy: OPV is like the driving record after you get your license

Getting a driver's license (qualification) only shows that you are able to drive. OPV is like the dashcam footage from every trip: it continuously confirms that you really are driving by the rules, and lets you trace the cause right away when a deviation occurs.

OPV covers every role in the supply chain, including:

  • Shipper: responsible for preparing, packing, and dispatching the goods
  • Logistics service provider (LSP): handles the intermediate steps such as transport, warehousing, and customs clearance
  • Receiver: confirms the condition on arrival and records acceptance data

CDMO Practical Points

As a contract manufacturer, TWi relies on OPV data as key evidence to show customers (and FDA/EMA inspectors) that the cold chain is consistently under control. These data should be compiled regularly into the Annual Product Review (APR) and used as the basis for supplier performance evaluation.

The core goal of process monitoring is to maintain a "state of control," meaning that all controls continue to ensure that process performance and product quality meet expectations. Trend analysis, KPI tracking, and periodic review are the three main tools for achieving this.

5.1

Standard Operating Procedures (SOP)

Standard Operating Procedures: The Documentation Backbone of OPV

5.1 Standard Operating Procedures (SOP)

SOPs should be in place to describe in detail all steps of the process execution. SOPs should be reviewed and revised periodically according to the organization's policies and guidelines.

A key part of an SOP implementation would include training and a comprehension evaluation section to assess the knowledge of the SOP users (e.g., shippers, logistic service providers, carriers). A summary of changes and versioning is required for easier tracking.

The review and approval system requires a defined process of retrieval and removal of obsolete SOP versions.

Transfer of the qualified process to the operational areas and the involvement of service providers (stakeholders) is formally accomplished by means of approved procedures. When those procedures are followed, the controls of critical process parameters will ensure repeatable, successful transport of medicinal products.

The written, approved procedures should be in place, and responsible personnel should be trained prior to implementation of the process. Consideration should be given to the procedures attached to the OQ and PQ reports.

Relevant personnel and organizations participating in the temperature-controlled process should be provided instruction concerning the principles of packaging, qualifying, and transporting temperature-sensitive medicinal products.

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)

SOPs are the documentation backbone of OPV and specify in detail how each process step is performed. A complete temperature-controlled transport SOP should cover at least process monitoring, environmental monitoring, nonconformance management, personnel training, and the responsibilities of each stakeholder.

Four Key Elements of SOP Management

  • Versioning: every revision needs a summary of changes, and obsolete versions should be systematically retrieved and archived
  • Training verification: training must be not only completed but also assessed for understanding (comprehension evaluation)
  • Periodic review: update at intervals set by organizational policy to reflect the latest regulations and practices
  • Link to qualification reports: procedures from the OQ/PQ reports should be incorporated into, or referenced by, the SOP

Common Inspection Findings

One of the problems inspectors find most often: an SOP has been revised but frontline staff are still using the old version, or training records show that employees were not trained on the latest version. The rigor of version control directly reflects the maturity of the QMS.

5.2

Monitoring — Data Loggers, TTIs, and Placement Strategy

Monitoring Tools and Placement Strategy: The Science of Data Recording

5.2 Monitoring

Data loggers and indicators, commonly referred to as monitors, are instruments that use microprocessors with sensors and internal memory capacity to collect data on predefined attributes, such as temperature and/or humidity.

In different circumstances, monitors can be connected wirelessly to a modem, device, or cloud-based system, or monitors can operate independently as stand-alone instruments. Data loggers can record and transmit predefined attributes in a real-time environment providing certain predispositions are fulfilled, such as connectivity to a GSM/Wi-Fi/Bluetooth signal operated within the restrictions of airfreight transport.

Unlike temperature, humidity monitoring and control is predominately reserved for storage premises. Like shock and vibration, humidity monitoring during transport is a product-specific consideration, not a standard attribute.

When supported by the risk assessment, a time–temperature indicator (TTI) can be used as an alternative to data loggers. A TTI is a device or smart label that shows the accumulated time–temperature history of a product. TTIs are usually smaller than data loggers and can assist with immediate decision-making due to a clear symbol or color visible on the device.

The number and position of monitors used in a shipment should depend on the qualification studies and control strategy. Conducting a risk assessment is recommended to determine the use of monitors on each shipment. The monitor position in a TPS should be determined by conducting a worst-case monitor-position study according to the temperature-mapping qualification data.

The frequency of monitoring or measurement interval should be based on a risk assessment that encompasses:

  • Shipment duration
  • Interval or frequency mandated by country regulations
  • Memory capacity
  • Stability budget of the product

Monitors should be started, at the latest, as the container is closed and, in turn, should be stopped when the container is opened at the destination. Each monitor is required to have a certificate of calibration and must not be used beyond the date specified on the certificate.

Monitoring Tools and Placement Strategy

Monitoring is the core means of collecting OPV data. The main tools fall into two categories:

Key Principles of Placement Strategy

  • The number and position of monitors should be determined from the qualification study results and the control strategy
  • Positions should be confirmed through a worst-case monitor-position study, corresponding to the most vulnerable points in the temperature mapping
  • External data loggers can support root cause analysis of nonconformances

Risk Considerations for Measurement Frequency

The recording interval (for example, every 5 minutes vs. every 15 minutes) must balance the product's stability budget, regulatory requirements, memory capacity, and transit duration. An interval that is too long may miss brief temperature spikes, while one that is too short increases the data-handling burden.

Calibration analogy: an expired thermometer is the same as no thermometer

Every monitor needs a valid certificate of calibration and must not be used beyond its calibration due date. It is like an expired blood pressure monitor: a reading is displayed but cannot be trusted, and the data collected may also be unacceptable from a regulatory standpoint.

Training

Personnel Training: Controlling the Human Factor in OPV Execution

5.3 Training

Training on all SOPs should be completed by all those who will be responsible for implementing the task the SOPs describe. Training records should be maintained and updated promptly so they are complete and available for internal Quality Unit audits and external regulatory inspections.

Relevant personnel and organizations participating in the temperature-controlled process should be provided instruction concerning the principles of packaging, qualifying, and transporting temperature-sensitive medicinal products. The purpose of the training is to ensure that the shippers, transport service providers, and their subcontractors maintain the integrity of the transport chain for medicinal products.

Personnel Training

Whether OPV works effectively depends largely on whether everyone who carries it out has been adequately trained. Training should extend beyond internal employees to include:

  • Transport service providers
  • Subcontractors
  • Operators of logistics service providers (LSPs)

Training Record Management Requirements

  • Records must be updated in real time and not filled in retrospectively
  • They should be able to support the needs of internal quality unit audits and external regulatory inspections
  • Training scope covers: principles of temperature-controlled packaging, qualification procedures, and maintaining transport integrity

Training is not just a matter of signing off. Effective training requires confirming that personnel truly understand and can perform correctly, which is exactly the point of the "comprehension evaluation" mentioned in Section 5.1.

5.4

Stakeholder Management — Vendor Qualification and KPIs

Stakeholder Management: Supplier Audits, Quality Agreements, and Performance Indicators

5.4 Stakeholder Management

The management process for vendors or service providers is an important component of a quality management system (QMS). Vendors, for the purpose of this document, are defined as qualified contract acceptors and their approved subcontractors.

The contract-acceptor approval process comprises verification of the vendor's QMS system and level of compliance to good distribution practices (GDP). Verification for direct contract acceptors is typically performed through an audit or comparable assessment process based on risk assessment.

The most important elements of the vendor approval process are:

  • Audit
  • Quality Agreement or Quality Service Level Agreement
  • Key performance indicators (KPI) or performance metrics for continuous improvement verification

A review of KPIs should be conducted periodically. Ad-hoc or for-cause audits could be required based on poor performance or significant changes related to services offered in accordance with an organization's internal procedures.

The main areas of a vendor's QMS verification, typically covered in a quality or GDP audit, include:

  • Organization, staff, training
  • Quality management system
  • Deviations/nonconformances
  • Supplier assessment/auditing program
  • Record management (paper and electronic)
  • Change control
  • Annual reports
  • Facilities, vehicles, and equipment, including environmental monitoring and qualification
  • Cleaning, sanitation, hygiene, pest control
  • Maintenance, including calibration
  • Deliveries
  • Warehouse operations and qualification
  • Safety

Stakeholder Management

In a temperature-controlled supply chain, LSP performance directly affects product quality. Vendor management is an important part of the QMS, and its three core elements are:

Three Elements of Supplier Approval

  • Audit: performed at initial approval and repeated periodically, with higher frequency for high-risk suppliers. Third-party audit reports (such as IATA CEIV) may be accepted as a supplement
  • Quality Agreement: clearly defines each party's responsibilities for temperature control, deviation reporting, CAPA, and so on
  • Periodic KPI review: common indicators include temperature excursion rate, on-time delivery, and damage rate

Practical Significance of KPI Monitoring

KPIs are more than numbers; they are an early warning system. If the temperature excursion rate on a lane rises for three consecutive months, then even if each result is within the acceptable range, the trend itself should trigger a supplier performance discussion, or even a for-cause audit.

Audit scope analogy: a full-body checkup, not a single-specialty exam

A GDP audit covers 13 areas, from organizational structure and facilities and equipment to warehouse qualification, which amounts to a "full-body checkup" of the supplier. You cannot look only at temperature control records; you must also confirm that the entire QMS is sound.

Audit frequency should be tied to the risk level: LSPs providing critical temperature-control services need closer oversight, while suppliers providing only administrative services carry relatively lower risk.

5.5

Management of Nonconformances

Nonconformance Management: Classification, Assessment, CAPA, and Product Disposition Decisions

5.5 Management of Nonconformances

The distribution process is a complex and dynamic process, where product is exposed to the external environment, less controllable than a typical drug manufacturing process, not managed directly by the product manufacturer and exposed to various entities in the supply chain. Due to the inherent variability of the external environment, nonconformances should be expected and documented according to the level of impact on the product.

Written procedures should be in place so that nonconformances are defined, their impact on product quality is assessed, and their root cause investigated, determined and, as applicable, eliminated.

5.5.1 Categorization of Nonconformances

Nonconformances can be categorized by their type (e.g., temperature excursions outside the defined limits, logistic process deviation, equipment breakdown, theft/loss, anomalous trends, noncompliance with a good-practices requirement), as well as their criticality (e.g., no impact or potential or determined minor or major impact on product quality or patient safety).

The categorization applied by each company must be described in a written procedure.

Nonconformance Management

Cold chain transport is not as fully controlled as a cleanroom. The inherent variability of the external environment means that temperature excursions are to be expected. What matters is whether the organization has sound procedures to identify, classify, assess, and respond to them.

Classification criteria must be set out in a written procedure. This ensures that different people facing the same event reach a consistent classification, and it is also one of the first documents an inspector asks to see when reviewing a nonconformance system.

5.5.2 Impact Assessment

An impact assessment must be performed following written procedures. The impact assessment must also consider and define the boundaries of the nonconformance in terms of which products may have been affected and which are out of scope.

The level of impact determines the level of investigation and correction needed. A prompt correction of the nonconformance to limit its immediate impact is required, and all actions undertaken in that respect must be properly documented.

All products impacted (or potentially impacted) must be individually assessed. An investigation should be conducted involving the appropriate subject matter experts (SME) for the product(s) to determine the impact on product quality. Stability data might be used to assess temperature excursions. Nevertheless, the cumulative impact of any possible previous excursion must be assessed.

All investigations including temperature excursions should be properly documented and approved by the Quality Unit.

When product quality, compliance, or patient safety is not impacted, it is considered acceptable to close the nonconformance with minimal or no root cause investigation (as applicable and described by the procedure).

Impact Assessment

Impact assessment is the core step of a nonconformance investigation and must establish:

  • Which product batches are confirmed as affected (in-scope)
  • Which batches are excluded (out-of-scope, with justification)
  • The degree of impact (assessed using stability data and the history of cumulative excursions)

Cumulative Impact Assessment: Do Not Look at a Single Event Alone

This is the point most often overlooked: when assessing the impact of a temperature excursion, you must include the cumulative effect of all previous temperature excursion records for that batch. If the product has already used up part of its stability budget in earlier shipments, the acceptable threshold for the current excursion is lowered accordingly.

Analogy: the stability budget is like a credit limit

A product's stability budget is like a credit limit: every temperature excursion draws it down. A single small excursion (for example, 2°C outside the cold chain range for 1 hour) may be within budget, but if this is already the third excursion for the batch, the cumulative consumption may be close to the limit, and the feasibility of release must be reassessed.

The investigation must involve appropriate subject matter experts (SMEs) and be formally approved by the Quality Unit before it can be closed.

5.5.3 Corrective and Preventive Actions (CAPA)

If a root cause of a nonconformance is determined, a CAPA should be considered and implemented in order to remove the root cause of the nonconformance. A corrective action is taken to prevent recurrence, whereas a preventive action is taken to prevent occurrence.

Depending on the CAPA, the implementation should be monitored for its timeliness and effectiveness. The thoroughness of a CAPA must be commensurate with the risk posed by the nonconformance.

A CAPA should result in process improvements and process understanding. It should be used in conjunction with the root cause analysis. To facilitate trend analysis, a root cause should be identified according to:

  • The route where the nonconformance occurred
  • Root cause location (where it happened)
  • Root cause owner (who or what is at the origin)

The level of effort, formality, and documentation of the actions should be commensurate with the level of impact of the issue on product quality. The development, implementation, and verification of the CAPA's effectiveness will often require collaboration with the LSP(s) and/or packaging/container manufacturers.

Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA)

CAPA is the key to closing the loop in nonconformance management: after the root cause is found, actions are taken to ensure the problem does not happen again:

Corrective Action vs. Preventive Action

  • Corrective Action: addresses a problem that has already occurred, eliminating the root cause to prevent it from happening again
  • Preventive Action: addresses a potential risk, acting proactively before the problem has yet occurred

Verifying CAPA Effectiveness

A CAPA is not complete once it is "written up." Timeliness of implementation and actual effectiveness must be tracked. Common verification methods include: checking whether the same problem recurs, whether subsequent KPIs improve, and whether the deficiency has been eliminated at the next audit.

Structured recording of the root cause (lane, location of occurrence, responsible party) serves not only the handling of a single event but also forms the data basis for trend analysis, for example identifying systemic patterns such as "Lane A consistently shows excursions in summer."

The Principle of Proportionate CAPA

The strength of a CAPA should be commensurate with the risk: a minor excursion with no impact may need only an update to operating instructions, but a major deviation involving patient safety may require changing the carrier, redesigning the packaging solution, or requalifying the entire transport lane.

5.5.4 Product Control and Disposition Decision

Should an absence of impact for products be determined, the product can be distributed after that no-impact determination has been confirmed, according to product specifications and local procedures.

Should an impact be determined, product(s) must be held and can be distributed only after a correction of the impact, if possible, has been successfully executed and product quality impact no longer exists.

When correction of the impact is not possible, impacted product(s) must not be released.

5.5.5 Approval and Closure

The responsibilities for approving nonconformances and their investigations must be described in a local procedure. The Quality Unit is typically responsible for approving all nonconformances as described.

The person responsible for the approval and closure of a nonconformance must have access to all information and investigation elements required to perform the closure. When more information or investigations are needed, these must be requested from and provided by the investigation team.

The approval can be granted only after all investigative elements are available and formalized and all applicable CAPAs have been satisfactorily identified and approved.

Product Release Decisions and Case Closure

Three Product Disposition Scenarios

  • No impact confirmed: the impact assessment confirms no quality impact → the product can be released and distributed normally
  • Impact, but correctable: the product is held until corrective actions have been successfully implemented and the impact eliminated, and only then can it be released
  • Impact, and not correctable: the product must not be released and should be destroyed or returned

Strict Closure Conditions

Closure of a nonconformance requires that all investigation elements be fully documented, all applicable CAPAs be identified and approved, and the Quality Unit formally review the case. It must not be closed early before CAPAs are completed; this is a common inspection finding.

In a CDMO setting, the customer's quality agreement typically requires that significant temperature deviations be reported within a specified time (for example, within 24–48 hours) and that the investigation report be completed within a defined period (for example, within 30 days). These timelines should be clearly specified in the quality agreement.

5.6

Complaints

Complaint Management: Systematic Handling of External Feedback

5.6 Complaints

Similar to nonconformances discovered during distribution operations, procedures should be documented that describe the processes for receiving, evaluating, investigating, addressing, and trending of complaints from customers.

A CAPA should be considered to remove root causes of complaints as appropriate.

Products returned due to complaints must be segregated and prevented from subsequent distribution. A comprehensive product quality impact assessment must always be performed by the manufacturer, if product is planned to be further distributed, as defined by local procedure and allowed by local regulation.

Complaint Management

Customer complaints are another important source of external data. The handling process is similar to that for nonconformances, but has its own particular requirements:

  • A written procedure must be established covering receipt, evaluation, investigation, handling, and trend analysis
  • Where applicable, CAPA should be initiated to eliminate the root cause
  • Product returned because of a complaint must be stored in quarantine and must not be redistributed
  • If the manufacturer concludes after evaluation that redistribution is possible, a full product quality impact assessment must be completed first

The Value of Complaint Trend Analysis

A single complaint may be an isolated event, but if trend analysis shows that a certain type of complaint (such as "packaging already damaged on arrival") clusters in a particular season, it may reveal a systemic problem, for example summer shipping temperature peaks exceeding the TPS design capacity, which would trigger a requalification assessment.

5.7

Periodic Review — KPIs, Trend Analysis, and Management Review

Periodic Review: KPI Tracking, Trend Analysis, and Management Review

5.7 Periodic Review

Distribution performance must be monitored and reviewed periodically to ensure the state of control is maintained as expected by the qualifications. The extent and frequency of the distribution process capability should be reviewed at regular time intervals using a risk-based approach.

Distribution nonconformances (e.g., number, trends) and the system for the management of nonconformances must be periodically reviewed. The aim of the review is identifying negative patterns or trends in the processes and highlighting areas for improvements.

Implementing a process to monitor the occurrence of nonconformances, by tracking appropriate metrics and KPIs, is necessary. Mechanisms for escalation should be included to discuss the trends.

Aspects to be reviewed include, but not limit to:

  • Number or frequency of nonconformances for each process or product, as applicable
  • Adequacy (effectiveness) of CAPAs (e.g., by measuring recurrent nonconformances)
  • Adherence to the standards required by the local procedure (e.g., timeliness for investigating and approving nonconformances)

For nonconformances classified as minor, trending should be performed to ensure they do not represent (or hide) a major nonconformance that would warrant more incisive actions.

This periodic review must be an integral part of the management review.

Periodic Review

Periodic review is the "review and improve" step in the OPV closed loop, ensuring that the distribution system stays in a state of control.

Three Core Questions of Periodic Review

  • Nonconformance trends: is the number rising? Are events concentrated on particular lanes or seasons?
  • CAPA effectiveness: have implemented CAPAs really solved the problem (measured by the repeat deviation rate)?
  • Procedural compliance: were nonconformance investigations and approvals completed within the required timeframes?

The Trend Trap: "Small Deviations Accumulate into Big Risks"

This is the most important reminder in Section 5.7: nonconformances classified as minor must undergo trend analysis to confirm that they are not masking a systemic, more serious problem that needs more active handling. For example, a "minor" excursion occurring once a month may show up in trend analysis as a progressively worsening systemic failure.

Integrating Periodic Review with the APR

For a CDMO, periodic review data on cold chain OPV should be compiled into the customer's Annual Product Review (APR). This is not only a regulatory requirement but also an important opportunity to demonstrate supply chain management capability to the customer. A quality culture in which the data speak for themselves is one of the differentiators that sets TWi apart from an ordinary CDMO.

Periodic review must feed into Management Review, ensuring that senior management understands the overall performance of the distribution process and has a basis for decisions on any necessary resource investment.

5.8

Change Control — Triggers for Re-qualification

Change Control: A Decision Framework for Triggering Requalification

5.8 Change Control

An effective change control system must be in place to control changes made to the qualified systems. Each change to a qualified system must be described and documented.

In distribution, a good practice is to maintain a list with all the routes/shipping lanes and their description (e.g., shipping/receiving site, LSP, airlines/haulers, cold chain solution).

Changes made to the system may render the distribution process no longer qualified. All changes (planned or unplanned) and their potential impact on the qualification status must be documented and approved by the Quality Unit.

Examples of criteria that could trigger a change control process might include:

  • New transportation lane with a specific transportation mode (e.g., air, ocean, rail, road) or major modification of an existing qualified transportation lane (e.g., shift from air to ocean)
  • New type of data logger
  • New passive TPS

Minor changes can be followed by a periodic report shared during management reviews:

  • Minor modification of an existing qualified transportation lane (for example change of the transit airport)
  • Change of the alarm/alert limits of an existing data logger

In contingency situations, such as an element for pandemic, proper risk assessment and change control should be assessed.

Change Control

Change control ensures that any modification to a validated system is assessed, preventing uncontrolled changes from causing the whole distribution system to lose its qualified status.

Lane List: The Foundation Document for Change Control

Maintaining a complete list of transport lanes (including origin/destination, LSP, route/carrier, and cold chain solution) is good practice. This list lets the organization immediately assess the impact on qualification status whenever a change occurs, rather than discovering problems after the fact.

Change Control in Emergencies

Emergencies such as pandemics and natural disasters may force the use of unqualified alternative lanes or carriers. Even in an emergency, risk assessment and change control should still be performed. The process can be expedited as an "emergency change," but it cannot be bypassed altogether. Afterwards, complete documentation should be added and the need for requalification assessed.

Other common triggers for requalification (not explicitly listed in TR39 but a matter of practical consensus): addition of a product or a change in specifications, seasonal extreme weather conditions, and persistently nonconforming KPI performance on a particular lane.

Section Summary: OPV Is the Engine of Continual Improvement

Key Takeaways

Ongoing Process Verification (OPV) is the "maintain and improve" phase that follows initial qualification. It transforms one-time validation into a living quality system through systematic data collection, analysis, and response.

The core elements of OPV are interconnected:

  • SOPs and Training ensure consistent execution by all parties in the distribution chain.
  • Monitoring (data loggers, TTIs, real-time systems) provides the objective evidence that the TPS continues to perform as qualified.
  • Nonconformance Management (categorization → impact assessment → CAPA → disposition) is the structured response system for deviations.
  • Stakeholder Management extends quality oversight to LSPs through audits, quality agreements, and KPI reviews.
  • Periodic Review aggregates performance data into management-level insight and connects to APR.
  • Change Control protects qualification status by evaluating every system change before implementation.

OPV data collected across these elements feeds directly into Annual Product Reviews (APR) and supports regulatory inspection readiness.

OPV is the ongoing quality assurance engine of cold chain management. It turns a one-time qualification into a dynamic quality management system.

  • Monitoring: data recorded for every batch, with valid monitor calibration

6.0 Conclusion

This technical report provides guidance for establishing a process for the qualification of distribution of temperature-sensitive medicinal and pharmaceutical products. It offers a resource for all involved stakeholders in the pharmaceutical supply chain, providing a model for qualifications from the process design through implementation and operation to verification including the shipment preparation of the products and thermal protection systems (TPS), their transportation and arrival at the receiving site for storage and further distribution to the patient (end user).

The core logic of "What vs. How": TR39 deliberately defines only what must be done, and leaves how to do it to each organization to decide according to local regulations and product characteristics. The full lifecycle covered by TR39

  • Process Design: selecting a suitable thermal protection system
  • Implementation & Operation: packing and shipment execution
  • Verification: OQ / PQ confirm that the system is effective
  • End point: ensuring that the product still meets quality requirements when it reaches the patient

What this means for a CDMO

TWi provides contract manufacturing services, and cold chain distribution is the last mile of service delivery. 8.1 Case Study 1: Distribution Incident Trends Analysis

8.1 Case Study 1: Distribution Incident Trends Analysis

CAPAs (Corrective and Preventive Actions) should be created for major and critical distribution deviations that have an impact on the product quality, for example, temperature excursions with product impact or product damages.

For events and minor deviations that happen in the distribution environment, CAPAs are not mandatory, as there is no impact on product quality and integrity as defined by the documented procedure.

The objective of monthly trend analysis is to confirm CAPAs opened for major and critical deviations and to prioritize CAPAs according to destinations with the highest reported number of minor deviations.

Step 1 — Classify the Deviation
  • Determine if the reported deviation is relative to damage, temperature, or another reason (e.g., logistic split)
  • Determine the root cause based on gathered information
Step 2 — Document Root Cause by Category
  • Type of freight (air / sea / road / rail)
  • Site of origin
  • Site of destination
  • Logistic service provider
  • Root cause owner (e.g., handling agent, hauler, airline, cold chain solution supplier)
  • Root cause location (e.g., storage or tarmac time at transit airport, motorway rest area, aircraft)
Step 3 — Monthly Review
  • Perform a monthly review based on a rolling 12-month period of distribution deviations
  • Calculate per site destination the percentage of deviations (= number of deviations / total shipments)
  • Sort the deviation percentage (by decreasing order)
  • Identify the site destinations with the highest deviation percentage
  • Based on a fixed percentile (e.g., Percentile 95 — the 5% of destinations with the highest deviation percentage), count the occurrence for same root cause location and owner
  • If more than one occurrence, identify as a cluster of a potential issue to investigate further
  • If occurrence is more than two within the 12-month rolling period (destination / root cause location / owner), investigate further and create a CAPA accordingly
Step 4 — Biannual Review
  • Perform a biannual review based on the last 12 months of distribution deviations
  • Calculate per root cause owner and per root cause location the number of deviations / shipments
  • Sort the deviation percentage by decreasing order
  • Identify the root cause owner and root cause location with the highest incident percentage
  • For the top three root cause owners and root cause locations, investigate further and create a CAPA accordingly

Case 1 in depth: the trend analysis framework

Three-tier deviation classification logic

  • Major/Critical deviations: impact on product quality → CAPA is mandatory
  • Minor deviations: no impact on product quality → CAPA not mandatory, but they must be included in the trend analysis
  • Trend trigger: more than 2 occurrences for the same destination/root cause within 12 months → escalate to CAPA

This "tiering + trend trigger" mechanism avoids the waste of resources of opening a CAPA for every minor event, while still not missing systemic problems.

Analogy: understanding the Percentile 95 rule

Imagine you have 100 distribution lanes. Percentile 95 means you focus only on the 5 lanes with the highest deviation rates (the worst 5%). This is an application of the Pareto principle (80/20 rule): concentrate resources on the few highest-risk lanes rather than spreading attention evenly.

The importance of root cause classification

TR39 requires recording both the "root cause owner" and the "root cause location." These two dimensions let you answer:

  • Who caused it? (airline, freight forwarder, truck driver, cold chain supplier)
  • Where did it happen? (tarmac waiting time, transit airport, highway rest stop)

Without these two dimensions, a CAPA cannot point to the right target for improvement.

TWi CDMO application: annual cold chain KPI report

  • Build a quarterly distribution deviation statistics table (classified by customer, lane and shipment type)
  • Use 12-month rolling data to calculate the deviation rate for each destination and identify high-risk lanes
  • Include the trend analysis results in the customer's Annual Product Quality Review (APQR) to demonstrate proactive management capability
  • For lanes with more than 2 events of the same type, proactively propose lane re-qualification instead of waiting for the customer to ask
8.2 Case Study 2: Passive Shipper SOPs

8.2 Case Study 2: Passive Shipper SOPs

When using passive shippers, the assembly diagram, photos, and a description should be included within either the SOP or protocol. As with a puzzle, the multiple pieces must be assembled correctly. A written description of the process to assemble a passive shipper must accompany the diagram. Training should be completed and documented for all employees who will assemble passive shippers. Retraining schedules should be developed and maintained. Audits need to be performed on the accuracy of the passive shipper assembly. Random checks may be done after a passive shipper has been assembled and finalized. The Quality Unit should randomly select shippers and open them to verify proper assembly.

Transport modes need to be defined prior to the execution of any qualification. A carrier quality agreement may be helpful to understand exactly how logistic service providers and freight forwarders will consistently handle each shipment. Things to consider are the route and the risks with that route related to time in transit and/or security.

Gel packs and phase-change materials (PCM) are used inside passive shippers to maintain temperature. Frozen gel packs and phase-change materials can be at different temperatures, such as −15 °C to −10 °C. Knowing at what temperature these materials should be, according to the passive shipper requirements, is essential to understand. Consistency is needed to assure that equipment used to condition and store gel packs and phase-change materials are capable of producing gel packs and phase-change materials at the proper temperature. The next step is to determine how long gel packs and phase-change materials need to stay inside the equipment to ensure the proper temperature. In most cases, it may require many days to condition these products depending if they are stored on a pallet, in a box, or individually.

The goal is to make sure the materials are at the correct temperature prior to use. Using gel packs and phase-change materials prior to being at the proper temperature could result in failed shipments. If gel packs and phase-change materials are used frozen, it is imperative to determine whether or not they will cause irreversible damages to the payload. Gel packs and phase-change materials may freeze if they come in direct contact with products affected by freezing (thermal shock and denaturing the product); this should have been tested during prior qualification processes. Thermal shock can occur unintentionally if the cold bank moves during handling and ends up touching the payload, or if the shipper is placed in a cold room prior to shipping. The layout and written plan should include a process to warn users that the cold bank may require material to separate it from the payload. Introducing gel packs and phase-change materials at the wrong temperature into a pack-out or improperly placing them can result in adverse temperatures.

Case 2 in depth: key SOP elements

The six core elements of a passive shipper SOP

  1. Assembly Diagram & Photos: the written description must be accompanied by visual material; like a puzzle, every part has its correct position.
  2. Training Documentation: all assembly personnel must complete training and leave a written record; a periodic retraining program must be established and maintained.
  3. Random Audits: the Quality Unit must open shippers at random to verify correct assembly; this is the last line of defense in quality assurance.
  4. Transport Mode Definition: before any qualification is carried out, the mode of transport (air / road / sea) must be clearly defined.
  5. Conditioning Procedure for gel packs/PCM: includes the target temperature, conditioning time and storage method (pallet / box / individual).
  6. Separation Material: if the PCM is frozen, the SOP must describe the separation layer that prevents direct contact with the product.

Key details of PCM conditioning time

TR39 specifically stresses that PCM conditioning may take several days, and differs significantly depending on how it is stored (whole pallet vs. in a box vs. individually). This is a detail many organizations overlook: an SOP that only says "place in a 2-8°C refrigerator" without specifying the time is equivalent to not fully documenting a critical parameter.

Analogy: the risk of thermal shock

Imagine you take a frozen PCM straight out of −20°C and place it right next to a biologic in a glass vial. The PCM surface temperature can instantly drive the temperature at the contact point below freezing, causing protein denaturation. The product may look normal, but its biological activity has been irreversibly lost. This is why the "separation material" cannot be omitted from the SOP.

Carrier Quality Agreement

TR39 recommends entering into a quality agreement with the logistics service provider that clearly specifies:

  • Standard procedures for handling temperature-sensitive cargo
  • Maximum permitted transit waiting time
  • Requirements for the use of temperature monitoring devices
  • Reporting mechanism for abnormal events

This agreement is a commonly checked item in GDP audits.

TWi CDMO application: SOP design that passes GDP audits

  • SOP structure: create a separate SOP for each passive shipper model, including (1) assembly step diagram (2) PCM conditioning matrix (model × storage method × required time) (3) release checklist
  • Training records: build an electronic training matrix that shows in real time which personnel have completed certification, with automatic expiry reminders
  • GDP audit highlight: random check records (Quality Unit opening shippers for verification) are the documents auditors like to check most; it is recommended to keep a separate check log and to track the check pass rate periodically as a KPI
  • Customer delivery: a first-time customer may ask for a PCM conditioning validation report as evidence of technical capability
8.3 Case Study 3: Qualification Master Plan

8.3 Case Study 3: Qualification Master Plan (QMP)

The following case study is an example of how to implement the information contained in this technical report. Risk analysis and planning for its mitigation should be the first step.

To make validation terminology applicable to a good distribution practices (GDP) environment, a qualification master plan (QMP) is written to define the qualification strategy. For each qualification step — design, operational, and performance qualifications — a rationale is written to support the qualification program according to the type (active or passive) of thermal protection system (TPS).

More specifically, when the qualification program addresses winter and summer conditions in the operational qualification (OQ) and performance qualification (PQ), particular attention is given to the PQ done with commercial batches that can last one year or more.

Performing a TPS PQ with consecutive shipments of the product is considered a best practice, because it mimics the real situation and therefore confirms the representative OQ result. However, considering the recommendations from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the World Health Organization, which advocate the PQ be completed before commencing commercial distribution of the product, it is necessary to document and develop a rationale to ensure the product quality used during the PQ.

After the OQ, an intermediate qualification summary report is written, based on the OQ conclusion, to justify the decision to use commercial product for the PQ. The output of the OQ performed considering winter and summer conditions is to evaluate temperature risk during static conditions (thermal chamber temperature profiles) and performance of the solution in order to define the number of probes and locations that have to be used for routine operations.

In order to demonstrate that the OQ results are effective and reproducible over the years, PQ field transportation tests are performed in real shipping conditions (dynamic condition), during which the temperature is monitored and documented in a PQ winter and PQ summer report. PQ will consist of replicated live transportation tests done as defined in the intermediate validation report. Based on a matrix approach, multiple destinations and product loads may be considered. The number of replicate(s) should be governed by the regulatory requirements of the specific country or region. Studies should be done in winter and summer conditions at the time of the year where the weather and route are challenging to assure the results are representative. Regional anomalies should be taken into consideration, e.g., during extreme weather conditions; monsoon.

Another option to consider is having a continuous performance verification program in place, where all shipments are monitored, and the performance of a temperature-controlled shipper is verified based on temperature data and the number of nonconformances experienced on regular quarterly/annual basis.

The output of the PQ is to evaluate temperature during real shipment and performance of logistics processes with the TPS.

Until the final Quality Assurance Summary Report is approved, in addition to the PQ shipments, real transportation shipments will be made based on the output from the OQ validation results. There is no product risk, as every shipment is temperature-monitored in a location defined by the analysis of the thermal mapping results obtained during the OQ phase. In addition, any temperature nonconformance will be documented.

A final Quality Assurance Summary Report is issued once the PQ is completed and the qualification process is closed.

Case 3 in depth: QMP structure

The three-stage logic chain of the QMP

DQ - Design Qualification
Select a suitable TPS type (active/passive)
↓
OQ - Operational Qualification
Static conditions / thermal chamber testing / winter and summer seasons
↓
PQ - Performance Qualification
Dynamic conditions / real shipments / multi-destination matrix

A report is issued on completion of each stage, and between OQ and PQ an "Intermediate Qualification Summary Report" is needed as a bridge.

Analogy: OQ is the "practice match" and PQ is the "real match"

OQ confirms in a controlled thermal chamber that the system design works; PQ uses consecutive shipments on real lanes to confirm that the design still holds under real-world conditions. It is like a pilot who first trains in a simulator (OQ) and then flies a real mission (PQ). The "Intermediate Report" in between is the simulator training report card, which decides whether you can move on to real flights.

Seasonal and regional anomalies

TR39 specifically stresses that PQ testing must be conducted at the most challenging times: winter testing should be done in the coldest period, and summer testing in the hottest. In addition, regional weather anomalies (such as monsoons and extreme cold snaps) must also be considered. This principle ensures that the qualification results cover a "representative worst case."

Continuous Performance Verification program

TR39 presents an advanced option: monitor the temperature of all shipments and periodically (quarterly/annually) count the number of non-conforming events, using this in place of periodic re-qualification. This is a lifecycle approach, the same philosophy as the plant process validation of PDA TR60: replacing one-time validation with continuous monitoring.

TWi CDMO application: QMP template for customer delivery

  • QMP document structure: create a standardized QMP template with the following sections: (1) TPS selection rationale (2) lane matrix (destination × season × product type) (3) OQ plan (4) Intermediate Report framework (5) PQ plan (6) continuous monitoring framework
  • Matrix approach: if multiple destinations / product types share the same TPS, use a matrix approach to reduce duplicate testing: group them sensibly so that one qualification covers multiple scenarios
  • Customer reporting highlight: in the customer's annual technical review, provide a "cold chain qualification status matrix" (showing which lanes have completed OQ/PQ, which are in progress, and which need re-qualification)
  • Regulatory cross-reference: it is recommended that the QMP map to both the FDA (21 CFR Part 211) and the EU GDP Guidelines, so that customers in all regions can accept it
