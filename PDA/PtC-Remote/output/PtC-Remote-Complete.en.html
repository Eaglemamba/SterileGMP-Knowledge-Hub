Introduction

Original Text

The COVID-19 pandemic has driven the world to find new technological tools to do business safely without sacrificing quality, efficiency, and effectiveness. This is especially true for the biopharmaceutical industry. While many consider on-site inspections to be the optimal way of assessing compliance, remote inspection tools increasingly are seen as useful ways of obtaining meaningful insights into a site's operations. But what factors should companies and health authorities consider when adjusting to the inspection format selected? How do you determine, address, and plan for the technological hurdles and considerations?

This document examines advantages and best practices for planning and implementing all types of remote inspections, including desktop, virtual, and hybrid inspections. It describes best practices that will help all participants engage efficiently and avoid unnecessary or unexpected delays, stressors, or complications in the remote inspection process. While good planning and preparation by both the inspectors and the inspected entity are important for any inspection, they are vital to an efficient and effective remote inspection. This document therefore presents the topics in order, from planning and notification through closing and response, as illustrated in Figure 1. The suggestions in this document may be useful to sites that are the target of inspections and to health authorities.

If inspections conducted using remote tools can provide thorough and reliable information, health authorities may continue to use them in certain situations in the future. Remote tools present advantages, as they allow health authorities to make optimal use of resources based on risk, allow for the involvement of specialized experts in a way that has not traditionally been possible on-site, and are more environmentally friendly. Issues currently seen as constraints may become less problematic as sites and health authorities gain experience.

In addition, Appendix 2 presents the complete results of a PDA global survey on biopharmaceutical manufacturing experiences related to remote inspections. These results may provide insights about industry's needs and concerns.

"Because health authorities around the world may take different approaches or be governed by specific legal requirements, sites should always consider local laws and practices... This document does not represent a standard or regulatory guidance."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Spectrum of Three Remote Inspection Types

The three forms of remote inspection defined in this document can be viewed as a continuous spectrum of increasing complexity:

  • Desktop Inspection: Document review only, with written questions and answers and no physical contact. This is the least complex form, and preparation centers on written materials.
  • Virtual Inspection: Desktop inspection plus live video streaming, screen sharing or a virtual site tour. The inspector can "see in but cannot step in".
  • Hybrid Inspection: Part remote and part on-site visit. The two can be combined flexibly, run concurrently or in phases, and may result in a single report or separate reports.

Understanding this spectrum is very important: the inspection format determines your preparation strategy, staff deployment and the level of investment in technical infrastructure.

Analogy

Remote inspection is like choosing between a video consultation and an in-person clinic visit:

  • Desktop inspection = You send your medical records and test reports to the doctor, who replies to questions in writing. The doctor never sees you in person.
  • Virtual inspection = A video consultation. The doctor looks at your throat through the camera and listens to you describe your symptoms. This gives more information than writing alone, but the doctor still cannot examine you by touch.
  • Hybrid inspection = A video first consultation, followed by a clinic visit for detailed tests once the need is confirmed. The two approaches complement each other.
  • On-site inspection = Going to the clinic in person. This is the most comprehensive, but also the most time-consuming and resource-intensive.

Key point: The goal is the same (assessing compliance / diagnosing the condition), but the tools and depth of information differ. Likewise, remote inspection does not lower compliance standards; it uses different tools to achieve the same purpose.

Key Notes: Key Limitation of This Document

Pay particular attention to this sentence in the document: "This document does not represent a standard or regulatory guidance."

This means:

  • This document is a set of Points to Consider. It reflects PDA best practice recommendations and is not a regulatory requirement.
  • Health authorities in each country still act within their own legal frameworks; the recommendations in this document cannot replace local regulatory requirements.
  • When a CDMO faces remote inspections by different authorities (FDA, EMA, PMDA, TFDA, etc.), the specific requirements of each authority take precedence, and the recommendations in this document should be used to supplement them.

Scope

Original Text

This document applies only to regulatory inspections concerning good manufacturing practices (GMP) and good distribution practices (GDP) and provides information relevant to both health authorities and the industry. The recommendations described in this document may be applied to other types of GxP inspections, although those were not specifically considered in the development of this document.

This document is not intended to address remote auditing programs, such as remote supplier audits, vendor audits, internal audits, or self-inspections, although similar challenges apply.

Glossary

Original Text

This document uses the following definitions. Because of the diversity of terminology used by inspectorates around the world, however, sites should be aware that inspectors may use a different term or definition than stated here.

Desktop Inspection

A type of remote inspection in which documentation (e.g., filing commitments, site description, and key procedures) is requested by the inspecting body that is not physically present at the site(s). Requests and responses to questions are shared in written form (e.g., via email or on a document-sharing platform). There can be several iterations of the requests and review of documentation, questions, and answers. Phone calls and videoconferences take place only rarely to provide clarification about the documents requested or provided.

Note: Some regulatory authorities may use documentation requests and review in advance of an inspection or to update a company's risk profile.

Document-Sharing Platform

A secure software application that facilitates file transfer. Document-sharing platforms allow end users to upload files to a shared storage space and designate who may access the files.

GMP/GDP Inspection

Assessment of the compliance of a site with the applicable GMP/GDP regulations and filing commitments performed by officials of a competent (local/international) health authority.

Hybrid Inspection

An inspection that is conducted remotely in part and also includes an in-person presence and on-site visit by the inspecting body. The remote and on-site aspects may be concurrent or at different times (e.g., a portion of the inspection is performed virtually or desktop, followed by a second portion on-site). The combination of remote and on-site components may be captured in a single inspection report or in separate reports.

Remote Inspection

An inspection in which the inspector is not physically present at the site(s) where the activities subject to the assessment take or have taken place. Remote inspections may take several forms, including desktop, virtual, and hybrid.

Video Communication Platform

Web-conferencing software that allows participants to conduct or attend meetings via the internet. This is also known as online meeting software or videoconferencing. The video communication platform enables remote meetings based on voice over internet protocol (VoIP), online video, instant messaging, file-sharing, and screen-sharing.

Virtual Inspection

In addition to sharing documentation as in any other remote inspection, virtual inspections utilize technologies such as live or real-time streaming video, screen-sharing, or other means of real-time communication. Virtual inspections may include virtual tours of aspects of the inspected site. These inspections also may be referred to as distant assessments or remote interactive evaluations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Terminology Differences: Virtual = Distant Assessment = Remote Interactive Evaluation

The document points out that the terminology used by inspectorates around the world varies, which matters a great deal in practice:

  • Virtual Inspection: The standard term used in this PDA document, emphasizing the use of real-time communication technology.
  • Distant Assessment: A term preferred by some authorities (such as the UK MHRA), emphasizing assessment carried out at a distance.
  • Remote Interactive Evaluation: Another expression, emphasizing "interactivity", in contrast to the one-way written questions and answers of a Desktop inspection.

Practical advice: When you receive an inspection notice from an authority, always confirm which actual inspection format the term they use refers to, so that you do not prepare on a wrong assumption. For example, if an authority in an EU country says "Distant Assessment", you must confirm whether this is equivalent to a Virtual Inspection as defined in this document (which requires video equipment) or is limited to a desktop document review.

Topic A.1 — Types of Inspections Performed Remotely | p4

Original Text

Problem Statement

What factors might health authorities consider in determining whether a remote inspection of a site is appropriate?

Recommendation

Health authorities can use risk assessments to determine the suitable use of remote inspections, including desktop and virtual inspections, on a case-by-case basis. Authorities will determine the level of risk based on several factors including results of previous inspections, availability of historical information and inspection reports by other trusted inspectorates, complexity of the manufacturing processes, and criticality of the products being manufactured. Desktop inspections often are more suitable for less critical assessments, while on-site inspections may be necessary for more critical inspections. External factors also may be considered, and an inspectorate may decide to use remote inspection tools for products or sites related to a public health need when an on-site inspection is not possible (e.g., during a pandemic or due to prioritization of regulatory resources).

In determining the appropriate format and tools for an inspection, the inspectorate also should consider any technological limitations and the processes, activities, or sites that can be assessed remotely with the technological tools available. For instance, inspectors may not be able to see the "full scene" virtually due to technological, security, and/or safety constraints. Wi-Fi network coverage may be limited in certain parts of the inspected facility, and some communication devices cannot be used in areas that present a risk of explosion.

Health authorities may determine that some inspections cannot be conducted remotely. For instance, some health authorities may determine that for-cause inspections, some follow-up inspections (e.g., following a warning letter or equivalent outcome), or some pre-approval inspections (PAIs) might not be suitable candidates for remote inspection at this time.

If some inspection objectives can be achieved remotely, a hybrid inspection may be used. In this scenario, the remote portion of the inspection, whether desktop or virtual, can partially address the inspection scope and a later on-site inspection can be used to complete the assessment.

The legal and regulatory framework in some countries may not allow remote inspections.

Rationale

While, theoretically, each type of inspection can be performed remotely, the level of scrutiny possible in a remote inspection may not always be equivalent to an on-site inspection. Remote inspections are heavily dependent on the technology available and scope of the inspection (e.g., physical manufacturing facility versus quality management system (QMS) documentation review).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes — For-Cause Inspections Are Almost Never Suitable for Remote Conduct

For-Cause Inspection: An inspection triggered by a specific problem or a serious compliance concern (for example a Warning Letter or a serious adverse event). Its purpose is in-depth investigation rather than routine surveillance.

This type of inspection requires the inspector to "see with their own eyes" the actual situation on site, for example:

  • Observing process operations and operator behavior in person
  • Directly reviewing original paper records
  • Interviewing the people involved and observing their reactions

Likewise, some PAIs (Pre-Approval Inspections) are not suitable for remote conduct either, especially for the first application involving a sterile product facility. When a CDMO takes on a new client's drug application, it should watch whether the HA requires an on-site PAI.

Analogy — Like Deciding Whether to View a House in Person

When buying a house, you can first look at online photos and floor plans (desktop inspection), watch a live video tour (virtual inspection), or do the video tour first and then go in person to sign (hybrid inspection).

But if the house has been reported for serious structural problems (for-cause), you would never decide on video alone; you would definitely go in person. The same applies to remote inspections.

Topic B.1 — Notification | p5

Original Text

Problem Statement

How can an inspectorate provide notification of a remote inspection? Should this be provided in advance?

Recommendation

For a remote inspection, the health authority may provide notification through emails, phone calls, or periodic teleconferences and meetings.

Because both parties must prepare and test the document-sharing platform and, in the case of virtual inspections, the video communication platform, the inspector is strongly recommended to provide adequate advance notification of each remote inspection. Advance notification also is necessary to allow the inspected site to prepare documentation as well as on-site and remote resources and logistics. This preparation time will allow for a smoother and more effective inspection and will help prevent frustration and lost time over technical issues during the inspection. For virtual inspections, in particular, the inspector should notify the site of the anticipated starting date as early as possible.

For a virtual inspection, additional pre-inspection requests may be sent to the inspected site, in addition to or embedded in the usual documentation request, to be completed within a specified timeframe (e.g., within two calendar weeks of receipt of the request). This additional information will assist in determining if the site is ready for a virtual inspection.

"For desktop inspections, the request for documentation signals the beginning of the inspection, whereas the beginning of a virtual inspection is denoted by the opening meeting."

Rationale

A key difference between a remote inspection and an on-site inspection is the sharing of data and documentation with inspectors using electronic methods. In an on-site inspection, binders of paper documents can be easily provided to the inspectors for their review, and tours of the facility do not require the preparation and sharing of videos. To facilitate electronic sharing of information in a remote inspection, appropriate communication channels must be established and tested. Data and documentation may need to be prepared in advance for electronic exchange. For example, paper documents may need to be scanned, documents may need to be translated into the language of the inspectorate if requested, and data may need to be extracted from computerized databases.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Application — 2-Week Preparation Checklist After Receiving Notification

After the HA issues a virtual inspection notice, it is recommended to complete the following preparation within 2 weeks:

  • Days 1–2: Confirm the Remote Inspection Host, form the core team, confirm the list of HA inspectors
  • Days 3–5: Set up and test the document-sharing platform; prepare the accounts or access links the inspectors need
  • Days 5–8: Assemble the routine inspection document package (SOP list, CAPA, APR, deviation reports, etc.) and complete PDF digitization
  • Days 9–12: Run a 30-minute technology test (video, document sharing, backup phone line)
  • Days 13–14: Finalize the agenda, assign backup personnel, and confirm legal advice (if recording authorization is involved)

Analogy — Preparing for a Remote vs. an In-Person Interview

For an in-person interview you only need to print a few copies of your resume and go. For a video interview you need to test the computer, check the internet connection, prepare the background and check the audio. Remote inspection works the same way: the technology test is the key prerequisite for success.

Topic B.2 — Verification of an Inspector's Identity | p6

Original Text

Problem Statement

In the remote setting, how can a site verify the identity and affiliation of a person presenting him/herself as a regulatory inspector?

Recommendation

Verifying the identity and affiliation of any inspector conducting a remote inspection is critical. The remote context makes this verification more difficult than it is during an on-site visit, where the inspected site personnel can view the visitor's official credentials in person.

The inspected site should ensure that the regulatory inspector or other representative is legitimately employed by the health authority he/she represents. Companies can take the following steps:

  1. Become familiar with published policies and procedures followed by relevant health authorities. Check websites or inquire directly about the normal process and sequence of events that the health authority uses to initiate each type of remote inspection. Determine whether the health authority has defined a protocol for inspectors to validate their affiliation.
  2. If personnel at the inspected site have any reason to doubt the authenticity of the source of an inspection notification, contact the health authority by email or telephone to confirm it.
  3. Report any suspected impersonation of a health authority official directly to the health authority's management, providing as much detail as possible.

Rationale

While reported instances of impersonation of regulatory inspectors are rare, they have occurred. For example, in December 2009, the U.S. FDA issued a press release warning of an impersonation scam. By impersonating an inspector, a person could engage in industrial espionage and gain access to records, trade secrets, and confidential information.

The company can protect itself by verifying the legitimacy of the inspector. While it may be more challenging to confirm the inspector's legitimacy in the remote context, steps should be taken to verify identity and authenticate affiliation before releasing records, answering questions, or enabling views of premises, equipment, and processes.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes — The Industrial Espionage Risk of Inspector Impersonation

This is not a theoretical threat. In 2009 the FDA publicly warned that people were impersonating FDA inspectors in order to commit fraud, with the aim of obtaining manufacturers' confidential records, trade secrets and formulation information.

In a remote inspection the risk is higher, because:

  • Physical credentials (the official ID card) cannot be checked on site
  • A video feed can fake the identity background
  • Email notifications can be spoofed (phishing)

For a CDMO, clients' process secrets, CMC data and batch records are highly sensitive information, and a leak would cause enormous losses. Any unusual inspection notice should first be confirmed through the HA's official channels.

Analogy — Like Vetting a Contractor Before Letting Them onto the Site

A responsible plant always checks a contractor's business license and personnel qualification certificates before letting them into a controlled area. It does not wave someone through just because they say "I'm from such-and-such company".

The same applies to video inspections: seeing an FDA badge in the background of the other party's screen does not mean they are really an FDA inspector. Calling the HA's official number to confirm is the correct approach.

Topic C.1 — Remote Inspection Team Formation | p7–8

Original Text

Problem Statement

What team should the inspected site assemble to prepare for a remote inspection? What is the role of the Remote Inspection Host?

Recommendation

The first point of contact for the health authority usually will be the same person that would be notified of an on-site inspection. The first point of contact should promptly involve the personnel responsible for establishing a suitable communication channel for the exchange of confidential information.

To ease the communication process, the inspected site should identify one person to serve as the "Remote Inspection Host" / Single Point of Contact (SPOC). The inspectors should use the Remote Inspection Host as the single point of contact for all inspection-related communication before, during, and after the inspection. This person must have or be supported by adequate authority to ensure that the remote inspection can proceed smoothly. For instance, the Remote Inspection Host must be able to quickly obtain support from the Information Technology (IT) or similar department to solve any problems that arise during the inspection.

If several inspectors will be involved, it may be useful to have multiple Remote Inspection Hosts. The inspection team should indicate the number of inspectors that will be involved or the number of workstreams that will be necessary.

The team that manages the remote inspection for the inspected site should be the same as for an on-site inspection. The team should include the inspection management personnel, the site leadership team (at least for opening and wrap up/closing meetings), subject matter experts (SMEs), local operating company representatives as applicable, IT support, and representatives of global functions when required.

During the remote inspection, a core operational team, including a designated leader, Remote Inspection Host, and IT support, should be present at the inspected site. The on-site core operational team will be responsible for document and data management during the inspection. In a virtual inspection, this team also will manage video requests and virtual facility tours.

The core operational team must fully respect required controls for health and safety, e.g., during a pandemic virus outbreak. When health and safety rules limit the number of people that may be present at a site or limit access to the manufacturing areas, the inspected site should mention such constraints up front.

The inspected site should provide the inspector a list of anticipated participants in the different steps of the inspection. A complete list of all personnel that participated should be provided by both parties at the conclusion of the inspection.

Rationale

The remote inspection format inherently allows more people to participate from diverse locations on behalf of both the health authority and the inspected site. With this increase in the number of participants, personnel roles and responsibilities should be clarified at the beginning of the process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept — The Role and Authority of the Remote Inspection Host

The Remote Inspection Host is the central hub of the whole remote inspection process. Core responsibilities include:

  • Acting as the Single Point of Contact (SPOC) between the HA and the company
  • Making sure technical problems (platform crashes, connection drops) can be resolved immediately by IT support
  • Coordinating the receipt, handling and upload of document requests
  • Managing staff deployment across multiple workstreams
  • Maintaining consistent communication before, during and after the inspection

This role requires adequate authority, meaning the Host must be able to mobilize resources directly rather than seek approval layer by layer. In many companies this role is filled by a senior manager from the quality unit.

Analogy — The Host Is Like an Air Traffic Controller

Imagine a virtual inspection as a complex piece of air traffic: the HA inspectors are the aircraft that need to land, the SMEs are the runways, the documents are the fuel, and IT is the ground support.

The Remote Inspection Host is the air traffic controller. He does not fly the planes himself, but he directs who lands, when, and on which runway, keeping the whole airspace from descending into chaos. Without good air traffic control, you get a crash.

Practical Application — Remote Inspection Team Composition Checklist for a CDMO

The following is the recommended remote inspection team configuration for a CDMO:

  • Remote Inspection Host (with authority to mobilize IT and QA resources)
  • Designated Leader (responsible for strategic decisions)
  • IT support staff (on standby on site to resolve platform problems)
  • QA manager (responsible for confirming document review)
  • Production/process SMEs (from each relevant department)
  • Regulatory affairs representative (to help interpret HA questions)
  • Document control staff (searching, scanning, uploading)
  • Backup personnel (every key role should have a backup in case of a network outage)

Special note for multi-client CDMOs: If products of different clients are being inspected at the same time, confirm whether the client confidentiality agreements (NDAs) allow SMEs to take part across products in the same inspection.

Topic C.2 — Planning the Agenda | p9–10

Original Text

Problem Statement

How might an inspection agenda be different in the desktop or virtual setting?

Recommendation

Virtual inspections generally range in duration from a few days to 2 weeks, with intense support required during this period. The overall time span to complete a virtual inspection can be double or triple that of an on-site inspection if the amount of time spent in a virtual inspection on any day is less (e.g., 4 hours instead of 7). In contrast, the timeframe for desktop inspections tends to be especially long, as they are iterative in nature. Desktop inspections can take more than a month to complete.

The agenda for a virtual inspection may be different from an on-site inspection agenda. An agenda that alternates short live conversations with periods of off-line document preparation and review can help prevent fatigue and inattention. Hourly short breaks allowing participants to move around and/or turn away from the computer screen also are important to maintain well-being.

Time zones also must be considered when inspectors and inspected facilities are in different areas of the world. Video communications and interviews should be conducted during the normal working hours of both the inspected facility and the inspectors, to the extent possible. If this cannot be done, the inspectors should consider shifting their working hours to those of the inspected site.

It is helpful for the inspector to share the date on which the virtual or hybrid inspection will start and end, if possible. A daily wrap-up may be built into the agenda to allow the parties to discuss any technical issues or necessary adjustments to the agenda.

Rationale (Key Challenges)

  • The review process might last longer than it would in an on-site inspection
  • Additional time may be required for clarification of some topics
  • Less time is available for informal "hallway" conversation, making it more difficult to build rapport
  • Communication style and cultural differences are more difficult to understand remotely, and the lack of body language is a further complication
  • Potential connection issues may impact the quality of audio and video communications
  • Being seated and using the screen continuously can be challenging and tiresome

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes — The "2–3 Times the Duration" Reality of Remote Inspections

Many companies are surprised during their first remote inspection: an on-site inspection originally expected to take 4 days needs 10–14 days in its virtual version.

The reason is that effective working hours per day are shorter (4 hours vs. 7 hours), plus:

  • Digitizing and uploading documents takes extra time
  • Connection quality problems cause explanations to be repeated
  • Time differences (for cross-border inspections) limit the effective working window
  • Written confirmation replaces spontaneous conversation, causing back-and-forth iterations

Planning recommendation for CDMOs: When communicating the expected duration of a remote inspection to clients, use 2.5 times the number of on-site inspection days as a conservative estimate, and reserve a buffer to handle extended requests.

Key Notes — The Challenge of Cultural Differences and the Loss of Body Language

This is a "soft" factor that is often overlooked but does in practice affect inspection outcomes. In an on-site inspection, experienced inspectors and the inspected party both use body language, facial expression and tone of voice to judge the other side's confidence and the credibility of the information.

In a remote inspection:

  • Video quality limits how well facial expressions can be read
  • Network latency may be misread as a "deliberate pause"
  • Different cultures interpret "silence" differently (in East Asian cultures silence may signal respect rather than evasion)
  • Language interpretation makes it harder to confirm understanding in real time in a virtual environment

Recommendation: Explain the language and cultural background proactively in the opening meeting, and set up a clear "confirmation of understanding" mechanism (for example, after each topic the Host summarizes what both sides have agreed).

Analogy — The "Marathon" Effect of Video Meetings

An on-site inspection is like a 10 km run: high intensity but over quickly. A remote inspection is more like a marathon: long in duration, requiring pacing and physical reserves.

A short break every hour, an agenda of no more than 4–5 hours per day, and the last 30 minutes before close of day reserved for a daily wrap-up: these are not "slacking off" but strategies for sustaining performance over the long run.

Problem Statement

What hardware is required or recommended for a virtual inspection?

Recommendation — Basic Requirements

  • Computer: All participants must have a computer with a reliable internet connection of a speed that matches the requirements of the technological tools and communications platforms to be used.
  • Webcam or conference camera: A videoconferencing camera or a camera built-in to the computer can be used.
  • Audio: Computer audio must be suitable. The speakers and microphone for each participant should be of appropriate quality.
  • Intranet or virtual private network (VPN) connectivity at inspected site: The inspected site should test connectivity from all locations involved in the inspection.

Suggested Items

  • Telephone line: A separate telephone line should be designated as a backup means of communication in case of an internet or computer software outage.
  • Larger monitor: A 20-inch or larger monitor is highly recommended. Participation in an inspection by tablet or handheld device is strongly discouraged.
  • Multiple monitors: Allows management of documents on one monitor while participating in video communication on the other.
  • Document camera: A document camera is a real-time image capture device that functions as a high-resolution web camera, mounted on an arm. Document cameras can be used to show real-time images of such documents as in-process records, logbooks, or test data. These cameras can enhance the inspection and reduce the burden of additional copying and transmission of records.

  • Virtual view tools such as 360° cameras or virtual reality goggles may be used. Other companies may use a laptop or mobile device mounted on a movable trolley or gimbal for a stable picture.

  • In areas of the facility where the Wi-Fi signal may not be strong enough, consider whether it might be useful to install devices to amplify the signal.

Rationale

The hardware used in an inspection can significantly contribute to the efficiency and effectiveness of the inspection. The utility of a virtual facility tour is dependent upon the camera operator's responsiveness in providing a stable image of requested aspects of the manufacturing site.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Application — The Value of a Document Camera for a CDMO

The document camera is one of the most underrated yet highest-value pieces of equipment in a remote inspection. Consider the following scenario:

In a validation inspection, the HA asks to review the Batch Manufacturing Records of 3 PPQ batches, each record about 200 pages and entirely handwritten. If you rely on scanning:

  • 600 pages × an average of 30 seconds/page = about 5 hours of scanning time
  • Plus PDF organizing, naming and uploading: about 2 hours
  • Total: 7 hours of extra preparation work

Original Text

Problem Statement

What is the most suitable process for sharing documentation in a remote inspection? In a virtual inspection, what is the most suitable process for conducting interviews and providing virtual tours of the facility?

Recommendation — Documentation

The inspected site should prepare documents in digital format. Documents shared electronically should be:

  • Formatted to be searchable, to the extent possible
  • Provided in a clearly organized manner, consistent with the document request
  • Identified in a way that enables the inspector to understand each document's contents from its title or file name without having to open the document
  • Provided in a digital form that can be opened with widely-used software applications (e.g., .pdf or .csv format)

In general, the use of a document-sharing platform is recommended. The document-sharing platform should provide the appropriate level of security while also allowing auditors to easily locate, review, and annotate documents.

Documents may be shared by email using an email address specified for inspection communications. This should be avoided generally, as it is difficult to manage the large number of emails. File size may be limited if sharing via email.

Documents also might be shipped through traditional mail or courier services. However, this usually should be a last alternative, as it might require more time and paper documents are not easily searchable.

Remote Interviews and Virtual Tours

Livestream videos and videoconferences are the most effective method of conducting remote interviews and virtual tours of facilities. Examples of video communication platforms are GoToMeeting™, Microsoft Teams™, Cisco WebEx™, and Zoom™.

The inspected site should consider preparing a video giving a general overview of the facility, which could be made available during the opening meeting if requested by the inspector.

"Note that some materials developed by the inspected site for the inspection might be considered GMP records, which would be subject to additional protections. The purpose of the record defines whether or not it is a GMP record... most videos, photos, presentations, and interview records (if recorded) that are created specifically to support a remote inspection would not, in most cases, be GMP records. However, inspected sites should review the status of the documents with legal counsel in advance..."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept — Compliance Requirements for Searchable PDFs

Why is a "Searchable PDF" so important?

When reviewing a 200-page batch record, an inspector needs to quickly find a specific deviation number, parameter value or operator signature. With an image-only PDF the inspector has to flip through page by page, but with a searchable PDF a keyword entered with Ctrl+F is enough to locate it.

How to produce a searchable PDF?

  • Export directly from electronic systems (such as MES, QMS): inherently searchable
  • Scanning paper documents: use a scanner with OCR (optical character recognition)
  • Post-processing: convert using the OCR function in Adobe Acrobat

When a CDMO purchases scanning equipment, OCR capability should be listed as a mandatory specification.

Key Notes — Email Should Not Be Used for Bulk Document Sharing

Problems with sending inspection documents by email:

  • Attachment size limits (usually 10–25 MB), so large documents must be split
  • After many emails back and forth, version control becomes confused (which is the latest one?)
  • Inspection documents get mixed with everyday email in the inspector's inbox, making them hard to manage
  • There is no way to record whether the inspector has acknowledged receipt and can access the documents
  • It is hard to track which documents have been provided and which are still pending

The right approach: Set up a dedicated folder on the document-sharing platform, organized by the inspector's request number. Use email only to notify that "documents have been uploaded", not to transfer the documents themselves.

Key Notes — Are Inspection-Related Videos GMP Records?

This is a legal gray area that legal counsel needs to clarify before the inspection. PDA's position is:

  • Usually not GMP records: videos, photos and presentations produced for the purpose of the inspection
  • Possibly GMP records: if the video content documents an activity for which GMP regulations require records (for example, a live-streamed aseptic operation, if it is required to be retained as compliance evidence)

It is recommended to agree explicitly in the Pre-Inspection Agreement which image data must be retained, for how long, and in what format.

Topic D.3 — Planning for Effective Use of Technology | p15

Original Text

Problem Statement

What additional considerations might improve the use of technology in a virtual inspection?

Recommendation

In selecting technology for a virtual inspection, two points are important to keep in mind:

  • Technology Overkill: Technology is intended to help the successful execution of the inspection, not to showcase different options. Companies being inspected may range from large, well-resourced global companies to smaller organizations in remote locations with less-developed infrastructure. Balance should be maintained in technology options.
  • Technology Exploration: Exploring new technologies or options during the inspection is not recommended, as these options may require installation and training time or may unnecessarily delay the inspection.

Because many factors can impact the actual performance of the technological tools during a virtual inspection, all technology must be tested in advance. A half-hour real-time test several days before the inspection can be helpful to check for and resolve any issues, including any limitations on software use based on geographical region, elapsed time, and number of users.

In discussing the software and applications to be used, the two parties should be sure to identify any known or potential compatibility issues, such as antivirus software, that is known to inhibit the use of certain applications. The inspected site may recommend an internet browser to be used during the inspection.

If site personnel will be demonstrating site-specific software during the inspection, they should verify in advance that the software is accessible from the computer system that the personnel will use during the inspection. Software specific to the manufacturing location or laboratory, including software used for issuance of batch records, temperature and pressure monitoring, and other integrated manufacturing software systems, may not be accessible from all company computers.

Whether inspectors will be given direct access to data systems also should be discussed early. As covered more fully in Topic II.C.1 Inspector Access to Systems, allowing inspectors direct access to GxP systems is not recommended due to the need for training and the risk of creating errors within the systems.

Rationale

While it is important to select effective technological tools for a virtual inspection, it is equally important that site personnel and inspectors are able to use the tools, and that the parties have consistent expectations about how the technologies will be used.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes — Two Technology Failure Modes: Overkill vs. On-the-Spot Exploration

PDA points out the two most common technology mistakes in virtual inspections, both of which are major causes of inspection delays:

Technology Overkill: The company tries to showcase the most advanced technology platform, but the inspector is unfamiliar with it and the HA's IT policy may not allow installing new software. Result: the inspector spends 2 hours setting up an account instead of reviewing GMP documents.

Technology Exploration: In the middle of the inspection someone suddenly says "let's try this new feature" or "this other software would be clearer". Result: installation failures, incompatibility errors, and the inspection interrupted for 30–60 minutes.

Golden rule: All technology used on the day of the inspection should be tools that were fully tested before the inspection and confirmed by both sides. No surprises.

Practical Application — 30-Minute Pre-Inspection Technology Test Protocol

It is recommended to run the following tests (30–45 minutes) 3–5 days before the inspection begins:

  • Video platform test: Confirm that the HA inspectors can log in and that picture and sound quality meet requirements
  • Document platform test: Upload a test document and confirm that the inspector can access and download it
  • GMP software access test: On the computers to be used in the inspection, confirm that MES, LIMS, the environmental monitoring system and so on open normally
  • Network connection test: Test connection stability in the production areas, laboratories, warehouse and other locations on site
  • Backup phone line test: Place a test call to confirm that communication is still possible if the network goes down
  • Antivirus compatibility check: Confirm that the antivirus software does not block the video platform or document-sharing tools

After the tests are complete, prepare a "Technology Readiness Checklist" signed by both sides, to be attached to the pre-inspection agreement.

Analogy — You Don't Tune the Instruments During the Concert

A professional orchestra does not start tuning after the concert has begun. They are ready backstage and do one last check before going on stage.

The technology test for a remote inspection is the "tuning". The day of the inspection is the "performance", not the rehearsal. The inspector is not there to help you test your equipment.

Topic D.4 — Pre-Inspection Agreements | p16

Original Text

Problem Statement

What agreement should the parties reach before the inspection begins?

Recommendation

The parties should agree upon, and describe in writing, the equipment and technology to be used in the remote inspection, including the document-sharing platform and video communication platform. An agreement should be reached as far in advance of the start of the inspection as possible.

The parties also should define clear rules for the sharing, retention, and disposal of any electronic records as part of the inspection process to ensure appropriate handling of confidential and/or sensitive information and compliance with applicable laws.

The use of technology in a remote inspection is limited by the need to properly protect data and information. Cybersecurity issues should be considered carefully. Both parties should:

  • Confirm the applicability of confidentiality controls to prevent disclosure of confidential commercial, personal privacy, or trade secret information
  • Verify the security of the communication channels

Legal counsel should be consulted during the planning, as local law may require additional agreements from both sides. For instance, local law may prevent the recording of sound and images or may require authorization to capture or use images of individuals. Where applicable, the Data Protection Officer (DPO) of both organizations should assess the proposed procedures and identify, in advance, any confidential or sensitive information and its proper handling.

"The site should not record any portion of a remote inspection (whether by video, audio, or other recording) unless the inspected site has made a clear, formal request to do so, and the health authority has expressly granted permission. If the proposed video communication platform automatically records all sessions, all parties should be made aware of this fact during planning."

Rationale

Prior agreement to the technology to be used and the protections to be given will build confidence and help assure a successful inspection. Legal counsel for both parties should review the agreed-upon framework.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes — Audio and Video Recording: Prohibited Without Express Authorization

This is the topic with the highest compliance risk in remote inspections. Note that the language PDA uses is very strong: "unless... the health authority has expressly granted permission".

Why is recording prohibited?

  • The inspector's questions and discussions are law enforcement activities and are protected by confidentiality obligations
  • Recording without the HA's written consent may be regarded as obstructing enforcement, leading to harsher consequences
  • Some countries (for example Taiwan and Germany) have strict personal image privacy laws, and recording someone without their knowledge may be illegal

Special warning: The "automatic meeting recording" function of platforms such as Microsoft Teams and Zoom, if not switched off beforehand or disclosed to the inspector, may lead you to break the law unknowingly. Before the inspection, be sure to check the platform settings, and state the status of the recording function explicitly in the pre-inspection agreement.

Practical Application — Checklist of Core Elements of a Pre-Inspection Agreement

A complete remote Pre-Inspection Agreement should cover the following elements:

  • Name and version of the video communication platform used
  • Name of the document-sharing platform used and its access control rules
  • Inspector's download/print permissions (whether allowed, and to what extent)
  • Document retention period and post-inspection disposition
  • Confidentiality control provisions (which information is protected)
  • Cybersecurity requirements (encryption standard, access logs)
  • Audio and video recording rules (explicitly prohibited or explicitly authorized)
  • Handling of personal image privacy (confirmed by the Data Protection Officer)
  • Applicable governing law (especially for cross-border inspections)
  • Signed confirmation by each party's legal counsel

Analogy — Like the Pre-Construction Meeting for an Engineering Contract

Before a major construction project begins there is always a "Pre-Construction Meeting" where both sides confirm the construction specifications, safety rules and acceptance criteria. The minutes of this meeting are not a formality; they are the arbitration baseline for every later dispute.

The remote pre-inspection agreement serves the same function: it is not a bureaucratic procedure but a legal shield protecting both sides (the HA and the inspected site).

Problem Statement

What information and documents should the inspected site prepare in advance of a remote inspection?

Recommendation

Health authorities expect sites to be inspection-ready. As health authorities increasingly use remote tools for inspections, sites should maintain appropriate documentation to support a remote inspection on short notice.

  • Comprehensive inspection history, especially for the first remote inspection of the site and for-cause inspections

  • Information related to relevant investigations

Topic E.2 — Sharing of Inspection Reports | p18

Original Text

Problem Statement

Will a health authority share its inspection reports with other health authorities? Can an inspected site share inspection reports with inspectors from other authorities to facilitate the inspection?

Recommendation

Inspectors may leverage information and data that other health authorities have collected through on-site inspections. Inspected sites should anticipate that inspectors have reviewed other health authorities' inspection reports.

Confidentiality arrangements in place within the Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S), or between mutual recognition partners and other collaborating health authorities, allow health authorities to share and access GxP information on international organizations and supply chains. The PIC/S inspection reliance procedure is particularly important, as it sets out a framework for participating authorities to rely on the inspection information provided by the network.

Inspectors also may ask the inspected site to share inspection reports and GMP certificates from other health authorities. Some inspectors will expect the reports to be shared. Inspectors also may request evidence of appropriate post-inspection CAPA implementation.

Some health authorities, however, may require that they be consulted or informed before their inspection reports are shared. The site also should verify whether the relevant authorities have mutual recognition agreements (MRA) or if the document is subject to freedom of information legislation. If no MRA is in place and the document is not subject to freedom of information, or if the report includes a nondisclosure clause, personnel at the inspected site should consult with legal counsel and ask the issuing authority for approval to share the information.

Rationale

"Informed decisions on the GMP compliance of a manufacturing facility can be made, in certain circumstances, based on the outcome of work by another regulatory authority or authorities. Consequently, it is possible (outside the established framework of Mutual Recognition Agreements, or equivalent and where legal requirements allow) for Inspectorates to identify specific instances where an onsite inspection of a manufacturing facility in an overseas territory is not required because an acceptable level of GMP compliance has been confirmed and assured by another regulatory authority or authorities." — PIC/S Guidance Section 2.2

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept — The PIC/S Inspection Reliance Framework and MRAs

PIC/S (Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme) is a framework for international mutual recognition of inspection results. It currently has 50+ HA members (including EMA, FDA, TGA, MHLW and others).

MRA (Mutual Recognition Agreement): A bilateral agreement between two HAs that allows one party to adopt the other's inspection results directly, without repeating the inspection.

The practical significance for a CDMO is this: a good FDA inspection outcome may help you get more lenient treatment in an EMA inspection; conversely, an EMA 483-type observation may already have been seen on the FDA inspector's phone.

Key Notes — Check the Confidentiality Clauses Before Sharing Inspection Reports

This is a mistake many companies easily make in virtual inspections: the inspector asks you to provide another HA's inspection report and you send it straight over, but the last page of that report has a line of small print: "The content of this report may not be disclosed to third parties (Non-disclosure clause)".

The consequences may be:

  • Breach of the confidentiality arrangement with the issuing HA, affecting the future working relationship
  • If that HA has administrative jurisdiction, you may face fines or other sanctions

Correct process: After receiving a request to share a report, first confirm: (1) whether there is an MRA between the two HAs; (2) whether the report has a non-disclosure clause; (3) if in doubt, contact legal counsel and ask the issuing HA for authorization to share.

Analogy — The Rules for Sharing a Transcript

Your transcript (the inspection report) was issued to you by the school (the HA), but it does not automatically become your property, and you cannot share it with anyone you like. It is like an employer (another HA) asking to see your transcript when you apply for a job: you need to check the school's privacy policy first and, if necessary, ask the school to issue a consent letter.

Problem Statement

What are the timelines to respond to the pre-inspection notification and documentation request? Is it possible to send the requested documentation in multiple shipments?

Recommendation

Most authorities require inspected sites to provide documents for pre-inspection (in the virtual or hybrid context) or inspection (in the desktop context) within ten working days or two calendar weeks.   • Their format
  • Any translation requirements
  • The size of the site
  • The complexity of the operations
  • The inspection scope
  • The level of risk associated with the inspection

It may be necessary or helpful to share the information and documentation with the inspectorate in several different bundles to avoid delaying the review process. The quality and clarity of the documentation provided is critical to an efficient and effective inspection. Therefore, the inspector should allow the inspected site sufficient time to prepare. Key Notes — The Right Way to Handle Documents That Cannot Be Provided

This situation is more common than you might think. For example: the inspector asks to see a batch record from 5 years ago, but the company is migrating its systems, or the document is in off-site storage and has not been digitized.

The wrong approach: Stay silent and say something only once the document has been found, or provide an incomplete substitute document without explanation.

  • The target date by which it is expected to be available
  • Any alternative information available, stated together with the above

Proactive communication demonstrates integrity and does more to preserve trust with the HA than passively waiting for the problem to be discovered.

Topic A.1 - Use of Real-Time Interactions

Original Text

Problem Statement

What is the most efficient and effective way to use real-time interactions in a virtual inspection? Is it possible and recommended to include multiple people or subject matter experts (SME) in the live sessions?

Recommendation

As discussed in Topics I.D.2 Planning to Share Information and I.D.4 Pre-Inspection Agreements, for a virtual inspection, the inspector and inspected site should agree on a video communication platform. Prior to the inspection, all involved personnel should become familiar with the IT tools to be used.

Good meeting management is essential. It is preferable to establish one meeting link or connection for the entire day, which all attendees may use, rather than creating a separate meeting link for each sub-topic to be addressed during the day. Inspectors and inspected site personnel can easily leave and rejoin the ongoing discussions, although attendees should always be introduced upon joining. If attendance is not carefully managed, however, the inspected site could be overloaded with questions and requests, including potential duplication from those who participate only intermittently.

For each workstream, one or two members of the inspection team should ask all questions, and one person at the inspected site should coordinate the discussion as a moderator. Also refer to Topic II.B.1 Managing Multiple Workstreams.

Several people (such as SMEs) from the inspected company could join the live sessions to be ready to support the ongoing discussion. The inspected site should control the flow of people joining the live session by using a virtual waiting room, if possible. A person who is not directly interacting with the inspectors should be assigned to monitor the virtual waiting room.

The video communication platform can be used for written conversation and for sharing documents and data. A live chat channel with the inspectors (e.g., via Microsoft Teams® or Slack®) also may be used to facilitate communications if needed. It may be helpful, for instance, for the inspector to drop notes about requests, such as deviation numbers copied from provided spreadsheets, into this chat channel to ensure that the correct information is available to all. This channel should be agreed upon by the parties in advance.

The inspected site and inspectors should not communicate outside of the agreed-upon communication platform(s). Additional informal communications via text or mobile phone application, for instance, should not occur, as these would be difficult to track and document and may cause confusion.

As in on-site inspections, an internal chat-link for site personnel may be useful to facilitate internal information-sharing as well as to verify the timing of topics to be presented. The internal chat-link could be set up through an online messaging platform, text, or email.

In planning for virtual inspections, training personnel on virtual meeting discipline may be helpful. This training could include:

  • On-camera discipline: Training should address personal attire, background screen selection, and prevention of accidental entry of family members and colleagues during video communications. Personnel should keep their cameras on while in the virtual meeting room, notify other attendees when turning off the camera, face the camera properly, and understand expectations around eating and drinking on camera. Discuss and decide whether cameras may be turned off during document review periods.
  • Microphone discipline: Microphones should be muted when not in use and background noise avoided. Particular attention should be paid to feedback loops that result in unacceptable echoes. Often, this may result when a participant logs in from more than one device. If muting another person's microphone is necessary, inform the person before doing so.

The inspected site should log all questions asked by the inspectors, including questions asked through live conversation and in writing, as it would during an on-site inspection. The log allows for visibility, sharing, and tracking of the questions within the inspected site.

Rationale

To prevent disturbances and interruptions, virtual inspections require enhanced coordination as well as discipline in communication.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - One Meeting Link vs. Segmented Links

The logic of the One Full-Day Link strategy: during an inspection, the inspector may switch topics, wait for documents or step away after asking a question at any moment. If a separate meeting link is created for each sub-topic, staff repeatedly receive new invitations and leave and join meetings, which wastes time and increases the risk of confusion.

PDA PtC-Remote recommends creating one persistent link for the whole inspection day. All personnel may leave and rejoin freely, but attendees should always be introduced upon joining.

This matters especially for a CDMO: on any given day SMEs from QA, production, QC and regulatory affairs may need to support in turn, and a single link makes deployment more flexible.

Analogy - The Virtual Waiting Room Is Like a Hospital Registration Desk

Picture a hospital emergency waiting area: a nurse (the designated waiting-room monitor) controls who enters the consulting room (the virtual meeting room), so that only the person whose number has been called goes in and the room is not flooded with too many people at once.

The Virtual Waiting Room works the same way: on-site SMEs stand by in the waiting room, and a designated person decides when to admit them to the main inspection session. The inspector therefore always faces an organized group with a clear purpose, not a random stream of people coming and going.

Key Point - Virtual Meeting Discipline Is an Extension of Inspection Attitude

Many companies wrongly assume that "an online inspection is easier than an on-site one" and neglect virtual meeting discipline. PDA PtC-Remote explicitly identifies this as one of the common causes of a failed inspection. The two disciplines below must be trained in advance:

  • On-Camera Discipline: dress neatly (equivalent to the gowning or uniform requirements of an on-site inspection), keep the background clean and keep the camera on. Family members walking into frame or eating during the meeting are seen by the inspector as disrespect for the inspection.
  • Microphone Discipline: mute when not speaking and avoid feedback loops, which are usually caused by the same person being logged in on several devices at once. If someone else's microphone needs to be muted, tell them beforehand to avoid misunderstanding.

We recommend a full-team mock inspection one week before the inspection to become familiar with the tools and practice the discipline.

Topic B.1 - Managing Multiple Workstreams

Original Text

Problem Statement

What tools can an inspected site use to manage multiple workstreams in a virtual inspection?

Recommendation

Multiple workstreams may be required, for example, when different inspectors are reviewing different material in a single inspection. In this case, the inspected site should create a separate meeting link or virtual breakout room for each inspector or workstream and should assign a Remote Inspection Host for each inspector that needs an independent workstream.

The inspected site may use one full-day meeting link for full-team conversations including opening and wrap-up discussions, one full-day meeting link for Inspector A, and one full-day meeting link for Inspector B, with each to be used only as necessary. Alternatively, some video communication platforms offer virtual breakout rooms, which can be used in the same way. As with all other technologies, inspected site personnel should become fully familiar with these tools in advance of the start of the inspection.

When multiple workstreams exist, the Remote Inspection Host must coordinate to ensure that information is shared appropriately and to avoid duplication of requests. The inspected site should create a process that allows all requests for documents and other information to be visible to all members of the inspection team. The secured document-sharing platform might be used for this purpose. When dealing with multiple workstreams, all questions asked by inspectors must be logged, as discussed in Topic II.A.1 Use of Real-Time Interactions, and the question log must be accessible to all remote inspection hosts.

Rationale

Due to the higher complexity of the virtual inspection process, careful attention to and oversight of the different workstreams is essential. With time and experience, inspected site personnel and inspectors alike will become more familiar with the application of the different tools and processes, which will lead to a smoother inspection process.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy - Multiple Workstreams Are Like Several Hospital Wards Being Audited at Once

Imagine a hospital that receives three health inspection teams at the same time: one reviews internal medicine, one surgery and one the pharmacy. Without a director-level coordinator, all three teams would pull the same patient record from the nursing station simultaneously, causing confusion and duplicated information.

The Remote Inspection Host in a remote inspection is that director: coordinating the workstreams, ensuring that document requests are not duplicated and information is not missed, and keeping the agenda progress of the different inspectors balanced.

Key difference: in an on-site inspection, inspectors can see each other's activities; in a remote inspection each workstream runs on its own, so only a centralized question log gives full visibility.

Core Concept - Defining the Role of the Remote Inspection Host

The Remote Inspection Host is a specific role introduced by PDA PtC-Remote and is not identical to the "escort" or "guide" in an on-site inspection. Core responsibilities include:

  • Independently managing one workstream for the assigned inspector (including a separate video link or breakout room)
  • Updating and maintaining the shared question log in real time
  • Coordinating cross-workstream document visibility on the document-sharing platform
  • Reporting workstream progress to the Lead Remote Inspection Host

We recommend assigning one dedicated Remote Inspection Host to each inspector expected to work concurrently, and rehearsing the roles before the inspection.

Topic C.1 - Inspector Access to Systems

Original Text

Problem Statement

Should an inspector be allowed to access GMP/GDP operational systems?

Recommendation

If an inspector requests direct access to a GMP/GDP operational system, such as a laboratory information management system (LIMS) or building management system (BMS), the response should be the same as it would be during an on-site inspection. Inspectors should access systems only through trained site personnel, due to the risk that direct access by an external user could create errors in the system.

Expert site personnel should access the system, then allow the inspector to view the requested information through screen-sharing. The inspector can ask or direct the expert to obtain or display specific information and data from the system. The inspector also can direct the expert on what to do, what commands to execute, and what the inspector wants to see, provided that these activities do not create any errors in the system or create data that is not triggered by a process.

Not allowing the inspector direct access to a system will not represent an inspection refusal. Instead, it should reinforce to the inspector that proper training is needed before a person can be granted access to an electronic system.

Rationale

An inspection should not put a GMP/GDP system at risk at any time. Access to GMP/GDP operational systems requires dedicated training and supervision and should be justified. At the same time, it is essential to operate transparently and to permit the inspector to observe the system and the data it contains within the scope of the inspection. As in on-site inspections, inspectors can be provided full visibility by directing a company expert to display specific information and data from the system; therefore, such a practice cannot be considered as an inspection refusal.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Why Inspectors Are Not Allowed Direct Access to GMP Systems

This is a common misunderstanding: companies worry that refusing to let inspectors log in to systems directly will be seen as concealment. PDA PtC-Remote clarifies explicitly:

The reason for refusing direct access is the protection of data integrity, not concealment. If untrained external personnel operate GMP systems such as LIMS, BMS or ERP directly, they could:

  • Accidentally trigger unauthorized system events (untriggered data creation)
  • Modify or overwrite records that are in progress
  • Generate data entries that do not meet audit trail requirements
  • Violate the electronic record requirements of 21 CFR Part 11 or EU GMP Annex 11

Correct approach: trained expert site personnel log in to the system and share their screen so the inspector can see everything, and the inspector can direct the expert to navigate to any screen or data. The inspector's visibility is not restricted in any way.

Key Point - Prepare the Verbal Explanation: "Declining Direct Access ≠ Refusing the Inspection"

When an inspector asks for direct login during the inspection, on-site staff often become nervous and incoherent. We recommend preparing a standard script in advance, for example:

"We are happy to provide you with full access to all data in our [LIMS/BMS/ERP]. To protect data integrity and comply with our system access control procedures, we will have our trained expert log in and share the screen with you. You are welcome to direct our expert to navigate to any record, report, or function you wish to review. This is the same process we follow during on-site inspections."

This statement conveys three messages at once: (1) we are willing to be fully transparent; (2) restricting direct access is a GMP compliance requirement, not hostility; (3) the inspector's visibility is not limited.

Before the inspection, the Remote Inspection Host should rehearse this statement to avoid being caught off guard.

Practical Application - Standard Procedure for Screen-Share Proxy Operation

The following is the correct procedure for LIMS access:

  1. Preparation (before the inspection): designate a "proxy operator" for each system, usually a system administrator or senior user, who is familiar with system navigation and has access to all modules within the inspection scope
  2. Inspector makes a request: "Please show the environmental monitoring trend chart for batch 2024001"
  3. Host relays the request: notifies the QC system operator through internal chat
  4. Operator logs in and shares the screen: starts screen sharing, navigates to the requested location and confirms verbally, "Inspector, this is the environmental monitoring trend chart you requested, covering January 2023 to January 2024 for the Grade A filling room"
  5. Inspector gives follow-up instructions: may ask to drill down into any sub-page, export specific data or display the raw audit trail
  6. Record: all system access requests are entered in the question log, including time, inspector, request content and operator name

Topic D.1 - Responding to Requests

Original Text

Problem Statement

What are acceptable timelines for responding to requests from inspectors during the inspection? What requests should be brought to the attention of legal counsel?

Recommendation

A site's plan for responding to requests in a remote inspection should mirror the same way as working during an on-site inspection. Responses should be provided as soon as possible, consistent with the inspected site's operations. To facilitate efficient responses, SMEs and other experts in the company should be ready and available for the duration of the inspection. When it is necessary to retrieve archived documents, or when the team at the inspected site is working completely or partially remotely, the response time could be slightly longer. Communicate proactively and transparently with the inspector if some responses could take additional time to access, and agree on acceptable timing.

As discussed in Topic I.C.1 Remote Inspection Team Formation, the company must have sufficient personnel standing by to facilitate the handling of documents during the entire inspection, starting from the first request for documentation and records. Based on the types of requests received, personnel and resources may need to be shifted to ensure timely fulfillment of requests.

The inspected site may anticipate that legal counsel will need to review certain requests. The company should establish a strategy for these requests before the inspection begins, to help avoid delays during the inspection process. In a remote inspection, legal counsel might be consulted for:

  • Requests for video or photos of personnel operating in the inspected areas, even when it might be difficult or impossible to identify the participants
  • Requests by the inspector to record proceedings using photos, audio, video, or screenshots
  • Any request that the inspected company believes it must decline

When sharing documents during an inspection, consideration should be given to the size of the electronic files. It may be necessary to split documentation into multiple electronic "shipments" to ensure its delivery, depending on the method used for sharing.

Rationale

Timely responses to inspectors' requests are crucial to an efficient and effective inspection. However, challenges may arise in retrieving documentation and making documents available electronically during a remote inspection. The inspected site must communicate in a timely manner with the inspectors if the requested documentation cannot be provided by the specified due date, with justification and a target date for a complete response. Communication is especially relevant when the workforce at the inspected site is smaller than usual (e.g., during a pandemic) and when the request involves paper documents or documents requiring translation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point - Request Categories That Require Legal Involvement (Important Checklist)

PDA PtC-Remote specifically names three categories of requests for which a legal strategy should be prepared in advance for remote inspections:

  1. Video/Photos of Personnel: even if individuals cannot be identified, in many countries (particularly under the EU GDPR and Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act) filming staff still requires authorization. Confirm the company's position with Legal before the inspection.
  2. Inspector Recording: if the inspector asks to record the video call or take screenshots, the company has the right to ask about the purpose and to limit the conditions. Do not agree automatically without an agreement.
  3. Any Request Site Must Decline: for example, requests for competitors' customer information or confidential business documents outside the inspection scope. Rehearse the wording of refusals in advance so that staff do not panic on the spot and make unnecessary concessions.

Recommended action: hold a "legal scenario walkthrough" before the inspection so that the Remote Inspection Hosts are familiar with the standard response procedure for each type of request.

Core Concept - Principles for Setting Response Timeframes

PDA PtC-Remote states that responses should be provided "as soon as possible, consistent with the realities of site operations." This is not an absolute number but a dynamic commitment that must be actively managed through communication.

Factors affecting response time:

  • Whether the document is electronic or paper (paper needs scanning or photographing)
  • Whether the document needs translation (Traditional Chinese to English)
  • Whether the relevant SME is on site or working remotely
  • Whether the document is an archived document

Key principle: communicate proactively about expected delays, which is far more important than silence. Telling the inspector "this document will take 2 hours to retrieve because it needs to be scanned from paper" maintains inspection trust far better than explaining only after the deadline has passed.

Topic D.2 - Sharing Audit Reports

Original Text

Problem Statement

What should the inspected site do if asked by the inspector to share the audit reports of contract manufacturing organizations (CMOs) or suppliers?

Recommendation

As in on-site inspections, an inspector in a remote inspection can ask for any document that will provide information necessary to assess compliance. Inspectors from some health authorities may not have legal authority to demand, or may not ordinarily review, the inspected site's audit reports of CMOs or suppliers, so it is important to understand the inspector's perspective.

In a virtual inspection, extracts of an audit report, including the overall audit outcome and the front page of the report showing execution date and scope, can be displayed to the inspector through screen-sharing or use of a document camera. Some health authorities may request that the full report be displayed. In extraordinary cases, it might be necessary to provide an electronic copy of the document.

The inspected site may need to consider whether the quality agreement with the CMO or supplier includes language about the sharing of audit reports. This aspect should be considered in advance of the inspection to avoid creating delays in communicating the information to the inspector. Moving forward, the quality agreement should stipulate that such reports may be shared and should define the timeline for notifying the CMO or supplier that the document has been shared. It is a best practice to notify the CMO or supplier before or in parallel with sharing the complete report with an inspector.

Rationale

The request for an audit report in a remote inspection is equivalent to the request that can be made during an on-site inspection. These requests may be more prevalent in the remote setting, however, as inspectorates seek additional supporting information.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Application - Standard Procedure for Sharing CMO/Supplier Audit Reports

When an inspector asks to see a CMO audit report, we recommend following this staged process:

  1. Step 1: Confirm the scope of sharing
    First show the report cover page (date of execution, audit scope) and the summary of the overall audit outcome, presented through screen-sharing or a document camera
  2. Step 2: Assess the request for the full report
    If the inspector asks for the full report, pause first and confirm:
    • Does the Quality Agreement contain a sharing clause?
    • Is a confidentiality agreement (NDA) or non-disclosure clause involved?
    • Is confirmation from Legal needed?
  3. Step 3: Notify the CMO/supplier
    Notify them before or at the same time as sharing; this is the best practice specified by PDA PtC-Remote. A short email can be sent: "Our company is undergoing a remote inspection by [health authority], and the inspector has asked to review your company's audit report. We will share the [year] audit report in accordance with Clause X of the Quality Agreement."
  4. Step 4: Record the sharing
    In the question log, record which report was shared, which content, when it was shared, and the timestamp of the notification to the CMO/supplier

Key Point - Update the Sharing Clauses in Future Quality Agreements

PDA PtC-Remote explicitly recommends: all future Quality Agreements with CMOs/suppliers should include an audit report sharing clause.

Suggested clause elements:

  • Authorizing language permitting reports to be shared in the context of regulatory inspections
  • Time limit for notifying the other party before or at the time of sharing (for example, "notify within 24 hours after sharing")
  • A definition of what may be shared (summary vs. full report vs. cover page and conclusions only)
  • Procedure for handling exceptions (for example, reports involving critical findings)

If existing agreements lack this clause, a gap assessment should be performed before the inspection to identify which important CMO/supplier agreements need to be updated first.

Analogy - Sharing an Audit Report Is Like a Doctor Transferring Medical Records

When the attending physician (the inspector) asks to retrieve the treatment records (audit report) from another hospital (the CMO), the referring hospital (the inspected company) must first confirm that the patient (the CMO) knows and consents and that there is a referral authorization (the Quality Agreement sharing clause) before releasing the records.

Submitting the full report directly without notifying the CMO beforehand is like forwarding medical records without the patient's consent: even if the intention is to cooperate with the inspection, it may still breach the confidentiality agreement and lead to legal disputes with the CMO.

Topic D.3 - Sample Collection

Original Text

Problem Statement

During a remote inspection, can an inspector request samples from the inspected facility?

Recommendation

During a remote inspection, inspectors are unlikely to ask the inspected site to collect and send product samples from its facility or to collect and analyze an environmental sample (e.g., residue observed during a video tour) because inspectors normally expect to be involved in sample collection. In Europe, collection of samples is not standard.

If asked, the inspected site should clarify the conditions for collecting and sending the samples. The inspected site also should consider and mitigate any risks relating to the chain of custody. The site's inspection management team also should contact legal counsel before agreeing or declining to provide any samples.

Rationale

Though unlikely in a remote inspection, a request for samples might be made under special circumstances. The inspected site should clarify and diligently follow the sampling and shipment procedures to ensure that the samples remain representative. Import/export rules apply to this shipment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point - Sample Requests in Remote Inspections: Rare but Needing a Plan

PDA PtC-Remote states explicitly that it is highly unlikely for inspectors to request physical samples during a remote inspection, because:

  • Inspectors usually insist on being present in person during sampling to ensure sample representativeness and chain of custody
  • Sampling by inspectors is not standard in Europe in any case (not standard in Europe)
  • In a remote setting, the inspector cannot confirm the neutrality of the sampling (the concern of selective sampling cannot be excluded)

But "unlikely" does not mean "will not happen." We recommend that companies prepare a sample request contingency plan in advance, including:

  • Trigger conditions and contact process for engaging legal counsel
  • Chain of custody documentation format
  • A brief check of import/export rules (research completed in advance by a regulatory affairs specialist)
  • Standards for sample packaging, transport (cold chain or ambient) and tracking

Special note: even a decision to "decline to provide samples" must first be discussed with Legal, to avoid a direct refusal causing conflict.

Analogy - Chain of Custody Is Like the Custody Chain of Evidence in Court

In criminal proceedings, every transfer of physical evidence from the crime scene to the courtroom must be fully documented, and a break at any step can invalidate the evidence. The chain of custody for drug samples works on the same principle.

If the inspector asks for sampling but cannot be present in person, every step of sampling, packaging, transport and receipt must be documented and confirmed by both parties; otherwise the representativeness and freedom from contamination of the sample cannot be certified by a third party, and the analytical results lose their validity from a regulatory standpoint.

Core Concept - Impact of Import/Export Rules on Sample Shipment

PDA PtC-Remote reminds that sample shipment is subject to import/export rules. This is particularly important in cross-border remote inspections:

  • Biologics: may require a CITES permit or bilateral import/export authorization
  • Products containing controlled substances: require special permission from the narcotics control authority
  • Cold chain samples: confirm that the receiver in the destination country has suitable storage conditions and receiving capability
  • Some drug samples exported from Taiwan: may require an export permit from the Taiwan FDA

We recommend starting the import/export rule check immediately after receiving a sample request, to avoid unnecessary inspection delays caused by administrative obstacles.

Topic E.1 - Use of Daily or Intermediate Wrap-ups

Original Text

Problem Statement

Can I have a daily or intermediate inspection wrap-up meeting during a remote inspection?

Recommendation

As in on-site inspections, the inspected site should ask the inspectors for daily (or frequent) wrap-ups during virtual inspections to clarify any outstanding issues and to discuss the following day's content. Doing so gives the inspected site time to prepare necessary documents and employees. Alternatively, inspectors could provide these wrap-ups at the beginning of the following day.

Some inspectors do not normally provide wrap-ups, but a regular wrap-up should always be requested since it will facilitate the ongoing inspection and help avoid potential misunderstandings regarding requested information. Inspectors also should promptly communicate any shift in focus to a new topic.

In desktop inspections, regular exchange of information on the status of the documentation review and potential concerns is equally important for the efficiency of the inspection process.

Rationale

Risks related to miscommunication are higher during remote inspections than during on-site inspections due to the intrinsic limitations in communications. Regular wrap-ups held at a frequency congruent with the duration of the inspection can improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the overall process. These wrap-ups can allow the site to both identify and provide necessary clarifications and prepare documents and personnel for the next day's discussion. They also allow the inspector to ensure that the site clearly understands expectations and requests.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point - The Daily Wrap-up Is the Most Important Tool for Reducing Remote Communication Errors

PDA PtC-Remote states explicitly that the risk of miscommunication is higher in remote inspections than in on-site inspections (risks related to miscommunication are higher during remote inspections). This is because:

  • Inspectors cannot judge the level of understanding on site from body language, facial expressions or the atmosphere
  • Document requests travel through several digital channels and are easily missed or misunderstood
  • Cross-time-zone inspections can lengthen the confirmation loop
  • With several workstreams running in parallel, the inspector's focus may shift without the company being aware

Key action: even if the inspector does not propose it, the company should proactively request a wrap-up every day. Do not wait for the inspector to ask: proposing it proactively demonstrates inspection discipline and helps build a trusting relationship.

Analogy - The Daily Wrap-up Is Like a Daily Progress Check on a Remote Construction Project

Imagine the owner of a large construction site (the company) having a contractor (the inspector) carry out remote acceptance. If the owner does not confirm with the acceptance officer each day what was reviewed today, what issues were found and what will be reviewed tomorrow, then only when acceptance ends does the owner discover that items assumed to have passed still raised questions for the inspector, and by then it is too late to correct them.

The daily wrap-up is that daily progress call: it ensures both sides share the same understanding (alignment) of what was completed today, what remains open and tomorrow's plan.

Core Concept - The Obligation to Communicate Focus Shifts in Real Time

PDA PtC-Remote expects inspectors to promptly communicate any shift in focus. This is especially important in remote inspections:

In an on-site inspection, an inspector walking into another room is a visual signal of a focus shift; in a remote inspection, while the inspector switches between several document windows, company staff may be completely unaware and keep preparing documents for the old topic, wasting time and resources.

We recommend that the company proactively ask during the wrap-up meeting or in real-time chat: "Inspector, which topic are you most interested in at the moment? We want to make sure our resource planning meets your needs." This is not steering the inspection; it is a professional practice that improves efficiency.

Original Text

Topic A.1: Knowing an Inspection Has Ended

Problem Statement

How do we know the inspection has ended?

Recommendation

As mentioned in Topic I.C.2 Planning the Agenda, the inspector and inspected site should discuss the starting and ending dates for the inspection early in the process.

Remote inspections should end with a closing meeting via video communication platform, teleconference, or other communication method. If the inspectors do not clearly state that the inspection has ended, the inspected site should ask the inspectors for confirmation.

Rationale

For remote inspections, which intrinsically consist of iterative requests for information between personnel in different locations and for which the duration might not have been communicated in advance, it may not be obvious that the inspection is complete. Therefore, it is best to explicitly ask the inspectors.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Importance of a Formal Closing Meeting

The Closing Meeting is one of the most important milestones in a remote inspection. It marks the formal end of the inspection and starts the clock for report issuance and the CAPA response.

In a traditional on-site inspection, the moment the inspector leaves the site is usually an obvious signal that the inspection is over. In a remote inspection the whole process runs over electronic communication, and both sides may keep exchanging messages and documents, which makes the point at which the inspection "ends" quite blurred.

Three acceptable formats for a closing meeting:

  • Video communication platform
  • Teleconference
  • Another means of communication agreed by both parties

Key point: whichever format is used, both parties must clearly know that "the inspection has formally ended."

Analogy - Confirming the End of a Medical Visit

Imagine you go to the doctor for a routine health check. In a traditional face-to-face visit, when the doctor stands up, says "All right, that's it for today" and walks you to the door, you know the consultation is over.

But in a video consultation, the doctor may say, "I still need to see your other lab report, please send it to me," and then go silent for several days. You are not sure: is he waiting for my report, or is the consultation already complete?

A remote inspection is exactly the same. When you are unsure, ask proactively: "Has the inspection formally ended?" This is not rude; it is a sign of professional responsibility.

Key Point - The Special Ambiguity of Desktop Inspections

In a desktop inspection, the interaction is entirely based on the exchange of documents, with no video camera and no site tour. The inspector may make document review requests in batches, spread over several days or even weeks.

This kind of iterative requests for information makes it especially hard to tell where the end is. Confirming the end proactively prevents the site from assuming that "no new document request = the inspection is over" when the inspector may in fact have only paused to think.

Recommended practice: agree explicitly on the expected end date at the inspection planning stage (Topic I.C.2) and ask for written confirmation (such as an email) at the closing meeting.

  2. At the pre-inspection planning stage, how should the site define the "end conditions" explicitly in the agenda to avoid uncertainty about when the inspection ends?
Topic A.2 - Inspection Report and Findings and Response

Original Text

Topic A.2: Inspection Report and Findings and Response

Problem Statement

Will an inspection report be issued for a remote inspection? How will the inspection findings be communicated? Is the response process for a remote inspection different from that of an on-site inspection?

Recommendation

The inspector's task, regardless of the tools used, is to confirm compliance. Thus, the issuance of a report and communication of findings in a remote inspection should not differ from that of an on-site inspection. The common practice is for inspectors to finalize any observations identified during the remote inspection in a written inspection report, along with a request that the manufacturing site provide an answer and a plan for CAPAs, including a timeframe for response. This report is likely to describe, at a minimum:

  • The documentation that was reviewed and by whom
  • Whether physical aspects of the facility were assessed, and the methods used
  • Inspection outcome
  • Rationale for the decision

The inspection report also should indicate the processes that could not be assessed remotely and whether they will be assessed on-site to allow for completion of the inspection in a hybrid format. The report also may explain the need for an on-site inspection. This may be a standard practice for some health authorities following remote inspections. For other health authorities, a follow-up on-site inspection may be necessary only when the remote assessment is inconclusive in evaluating the site's compliance status.

The process to respond to remote inspection findings should be the same as in on-site inspections unless the health authority communicates a different procedure. During the closing meeting or equivalent, the inspectors should communicate the path forward for the inspection response process.

Rationale

While the remote inspection process is slightly different from an on-site inspection, the documentation and communication of the inspection outcome in most cases will remain the same, with the presentation of any inspectional findings, issuance of an inspection report, and expectations of responses as appropriate.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Minimum Required Content of the Inspection Report (Checklist)

PDA recommends that a remote inspection report contain at least the following four elements:

  • Record of documents reviewed: which documents were reviewed, and by which inspectors?
  • How the facility was physically assessed: was a site assessment performed? If so, which methods were used (such as a video tour or photographs)?
  • Inspection Outcome: what is the overall compliance conclusion?
  • Rationale for Decision: the basis and reasoning behind the inspector's judgment

In addition, the report should state specifically which processes could not be assessed remotely, and whether an on-site follow-up is needed to supplement the assessment.

Analogy - An Inspection Report Is Like a Doctor's Clinical Record

The medical record a doctor completes after a consultation documents: "which examinations were done today, what the results were, what the diagnosis is and what follow-up is recommended." Whether the visit is in person or by video, the requirements for format and completeness are the same.

The logic of an inspection report is exactly the same. Whether or not the inspector enters the site, the task is to "confirm compliance," and that confirmation must be presented in a formal written report, giving both sides a clear legal and quality basis.

If the consultation took place entirely online, the doctor should also state "which examinations were not completed because they could not be performed in person," to keep the record complete. The same applies to an inspection report.

Key Point - Items That "Cannot Be Assessed Remotely" Are the Trigger for a Follow-up On-site Inspection

This is one of the most important structural differences between remote and on-site inspections: the "items that could not be assessed remotely" explicitly listed in the report directly determine whether a follow-up on-site inspection (hybrid format or follow-up on-site inspection) is needed.

For the site, this means:

  • If certain processes are complex and high-risk and are hard to present adequately by video or documents, prepare alternative assessment options in advance (such as detailed video recordings or live video demonstrations)
  • If the regulatory authority by default requires an on-site follow-up after a remote inspection (the standard procedure of some HAs), the site should include this in its Post-inspection Action Plan

Practical conclusion: the purpose of pre-inspection preparation is to keep the list of "items that cannot be assessed remotely" as short as possible.

Practical Application - Best Practices for a CDMO's CAPA Response

The CAPA (Corrective Action and Preventive Action) response process for a remote inspection is exactly the same as for an on-site inspection, unless the regulatory authority advises a different procedure.

Recommendations for a CDMO:

  • Manage response timelines: at the closing meeting, proactively confirm the authority's response deadline (usually 15-30 days) and start the standard internal CAPA process
  • Respond by topic: for each finding in the report, write a root cause analysis, corrective action, preventive action and completion timeline
  • Tone of response: do not take a finding from a remote inspection lightly because "the inspector did not see it with their own eyes"; a finding in the report carries the same legal weight as one from an on-site inspection
  • Prepare early: for concerns you sense may be raised during the inspection, start drafting the CAPA immediately after the closing meeting without waiting for the formal report
Topic A.3 - Issuance of GMP/GDP Certificate

Original Text

Topic A.3: Issuance of GMP/GDP Certificate

Problem Statement

If the outcome of the remote inspection is positive, will a GMP/GDP certificate be issued?

Recommendation

If the outcome of the remote inspection is positive, a GMP or GDP certificate should be issued as part of the normal activity of the health authority, as per the applicable local and regional laws.

The certificate might specify that virtual tools were used to conduct the inspection, but this distinction may not be necessary as GMP/GDP certificates issued after an on-site inspection often do not include discussion of all tools used (e.g., site tour, document review prior to arrival on site).

Rationale

While the assessment process per se is slightly different in remote inspections than in on-site inspections, the decision to release a GMP/GDP certificate, and the validity of the certificate, is based on the evidence of compliance demonstrated during the inspection.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Validity of a GMP/GDP Certificate Is Not Reduced by the Inspection Format

This point is critical to a CDMO's commercial operations: a GMP/GDP certificate issued after a successful remote inspection is fully equivalent, in legal effect and validity period, to one issued after an on-site inspection.

Two key principles:

  • The certificate decision is based on evidence of compliance, not on the format of inspection
  • The certificate may carry a note that "virtual tools were used for the inspection," but this is only an explanatory remark and does not change its legal effect

Validity period: the same as a certificate issued after an on-site inspection (usually 2-3 years, depending on the regulatory authority).

Key Point - Explaining Certificate Validity to Management and Customers

In practice, a CDMO's customers (especially the Regulatory Affairs or Quality departments of big pharma) may doubt a "GMP/GDP certificate issued after a remote inspection" and worry whether other countries' regulators will accept it.

Recommended communication strategy:

  • Stress that "the certificate decision is based on evidence of compliance, not on the inspection tool"; this is PDA's official position and the consensus of the major HAs
  • If the certificate carries a "virtual tools used" remark, proactively explain that this is transparent disclosure, not a limitation on validity
  • For customers who need to file for marketing authorization in multiple markets, we recommend confirming in advance each market's regulatory position on accepting certificates issued after remote inspections

Message for CDMO management: compliance is the core asset; the inspection format used to demonstrate it is only a means and does not affect the final determination.

Analogy - The Format of an Examination Does Not Affect the Validity of a Degree

During COVID-19, many universities moved to online examinations. Students who completed exams through remote proctoring systems received degrees identical to those earned through traditional oral or in-person exams, and the diploma does not say "online examination."

The same applies to a GMP/GDP certificate. The validity of a degree comes from whether you truly have the corresponding competence, not from where the exam was held. Likewise, the validity of a GMP/GDP certificate comes from whether the plant meets GMP/GDP standards, not from whether the inspector physically entered the building.

Topic B.1 - Frequency of Reinspection

Original Text

Topic B.1: Frequency of Reinspection

Problem Statement

Is the frequency of reinspection impacted by the type of inspection (remote or on-site)?

Recommendation

The frequency of reinspection depends on multiple parameters, including criticality of the product, complexity of the process, compliance history of the facility, regulatory resources, geographic location of the facility, and format of the inspection (e.g., on-site, desktop, or virtual). This determination will be guided by the health authority's risk-based approach. The next inspection following a remote inspection may occur at a reduced time interval due to the inherent limitations of a remote inspection as compared to on-site inspections.

Rationale

Technology can help in performing a remote inspection that is adequate to allow the inspector to make an informed decision about the site's compliance status and/or compliance with the content in a regulatory submission. Even so, intrinsic limitations in a remote inspection may present challenges in accessing all the necessary information. Inspectorates are likely to take this into consideration when determining the frequency for the next inspection.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point - A Shortened Reinspection Interval Is the Hidden Cost of Remote Inspection

For a CDMO, this is the "hidden impact" of remote inspection that most needs to be explained to customers and management:

A remote inspection itself saves both sides travel time and cost; however, because of the intrinsic limitations of remote inspections, the regulatory authority may schedule an on-site inspection in the next inspection cycle, possibly earlier than the normal cycle.

Impact on CDMO customer management:

  • If a CDMO is scheduled for an earlier reinspection after a remote inspection, customers need to understand that this does not mean there is a problem at the site; it is the regulatory authority's standard practice based on the inspection format
  • More frequent inspections require the site to maintain a higher level of inspection-readiness, which means continuous investment of people and resources

Practical Application - How to Reduce Reinspection Frequency and Demonstrate Compliance Quality

A shortened reinspection interval fundamentally reflects "the regulatory authority's insufficient confidence in the information about the site." A CDMO can adopt the following strategies to build confidence and earn a longer reinspection interval:

  • Build a strong compliance history: a record with no Major Deficiencies or warning letters is the most important positive indicator in a regulator's risk assessment
  • Proactively provide documents beyond what is required: in a remote inspection, for facilities or processes the inspector cannot see in person, proactively provide high-quality video recordings, photographs or live demonstrations to reduce the "information gap"
  • Strengthen remote inspection presentation skills: a clear document management system and smooth video presentation directly affect the inspector's perception of site transparency
  • CAPA quality: CAPA responses to past inspection findings should be specific and convincing and supported by effectiveness-verification data, demonstrating the site's "capacity for self-correction"
  • Proactive communication: between inspections, proactively update the HA on major process changes and regularly submit change management records to maintain a transparent relationship

Core Concept - The Significance of Global Regulatory Convergence

This list of guidances shows an important trend: within just two years, 2020-2021, Australia (TGA), the UK (MHRA, two documents), the EU (EMA), the US (FDA, two documents) and the global pharmaceutical industry association (IFPMA) issued guidance on remote inspections almost simultaneously.

This "rapid coordination among multiple regulators" is called Regulatory Convergence and is quite rare in the history of pharmaceutical regulation. It shows that:

  • Remote inspection is not a special case but an inspection model recognized by the global regulatory system
  • Although the HAs differ in detail, the core framework is shared, which makes it easier for a CDMO to build a unified "remote inspection response system" that applies across multiple markets
  • PDA PtC Remote is positioned to offer integrated, cross-regulator recommendations from an industry perspective, built on the HA guidances

Key Point - Core Differences Between the Guidances

Although the overall direction of the HA guidances is consistent, some differences remain, and a CDMO should understand them market by market:

  • TGA (Australia): focuses on remote inspection expectations for overseas sites, which is especially relevant to a CDMO as an overseas supplier
  • MHRA (UK): issued two documents, one on GxP inspection adjustments during COVID and one on expectations for the post-pandemic "Return to On-site", providing a roadmap for normalizing inspections
  • EMA (EU): uses the term "Distant Assessments" rather than "virtual inspection," covering the three areas of GMP, GDP and PMF (Plasma Master File)
  • FDA: uses the term Remote Interactive Evaluations (RIE); its 2021 guidance is the most detailed and also covers BIMO (Bioresearch Monitoring) facilities
  • IFPMA: represents the industry perspective, complements PDA PtC and offers best-practice recommendations for sites

Analogy - The Convergence of Traffic Laws Across Countries

When self-driving vehicle technology first appeared, countries' traffic laws had not anticipated it. Once the technology matured, countries began drafting relevant rules at almost the same time; although the details differ, the core direction of "allowing self-driving vehicles on the road" is entirely consistent.

The regulatory development of remote inspection follows the same pattern. COVID-19 was the catalyst for a technological breakthrough, and the HAs built frameworks very quickly because they faced the same technological reality. For a CDMO supplying globally, understanding the "common language" among these guidances is the foundation for building a global inspection response capability.

Original Text

VIII. Appendix 2: 2021 PDA Research: Global Industry Responses to Remote Regulatory Inspections

In 2020, global health authorities increased their use of remote inspections of pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical manufacturers due to COVID-19 precautions. Manufacturers and health authorities alike worked rapidly to adapt on-site inspection practices to the virtual environment and to overcome related challenges.

PDA conducted this survey to better understand industry's response to these changes, as well as potential future needs. To gain these insights, PDA sought the input of global pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical employees who help develop company policies and procedures relating to regulatory inspections, interact with regulatory inspectors, and/or provide support during regulatory inspections. The survey was targeted at individuals who work for a company that manufactures finished products for human use, active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), or excipients.

Survey Methodology

The survey on Global Industry Approaches to Remote Regulatory Inspections was open for participation from November 9 through November 24, 2020.

A total of 59 respondents completed the survey. Survey respondents are experts involved in regulatory inspections within their current companies, whether from the regulatory affairs, manufacturing, quality, compliance, or other functional perspective. The survey results include a good balance between drug product manufacturers and API or excipient manufacturers, as well as responses from sites of varying sizes.

Respondents included industry professionals located in North America, Europe, Asia/Pacific, Africa, and the Middle East. Latin America was not represented among respondents.

The survey used skip logic to ask follow-up questions to selected subsets of respondents. For instance, Questions 8 through 13 were asked only of those who said in response to Question 7 that they had participated in or supported a remote regulatory inspection or assessment in 2020. As a result of this skip logic, the number of respondents varies depending on the question asked.

For numerous questions, respondents could select as many options as were applicable. These questions are denoted by the instruction to "Select all that apply." The percentages shown in the responses to these questions represent the percent of respondents rather than percent of total responses. The percentages for these questions will not total 100.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point: The Historical Context of the Survey Timing

The questionnaire was open for responses from 9–24 November 2020, only about 8 months after the COVID-19 outbreak formally began (the WHO declared a pandemic in March 2020). This means most respondents had very limited remote inspection experience, and many companies were still finding their way.

The survey therefore reflects the "prehistoric era of remote inspections," when the industry had not yet established complete operating norms and technical infrastructure. After 2021 industry readiness improved significantly, and the use of remote inspection tools (Teams, Zoom, SharePoint document rooms) became more mature. This time context must be taken into account when reading the survey results.

Key Point: The Impact of Latin America's Absence

The survey explicitly states that "Latin America was not represented." This is an important limitation when interpreting the results, because:

  • Anvisa (Brazil), one of the world's ten largest regulators, had a 0% response share in the questionnaire (Q9), and its remote inspection framework is relatively less mature
  • Many manufacturers of tropical disease drugs are located in this region, so remote inspection may mean something different to them
  • A CDMO operating in Latin America should separately assess the specific requirements of Anvisa and each country's authority when planning remote inspection preparation, and should not apply this survey's conclusions directly

Analysis of Respondent Composition

QA accounts for as much as 43.4%, making it the largest respondent group, which reasonably reflects the reality that inspection preparation is led by QA. It is worth noting that:

  • Manufacturing Operations (7.5%) is a relatively low share, yet the actual operational burden on manufacturing during a remote inspection (supporting virtual site tours, retrieving records in real time) is considerable
  • QC is only 1.9%, showing that the voice of the laboratory level is under-represented in this survey, even though the QC laboratory is often a key inspection area
  • More than half (51.9%) of the respondent sites have 250 or more employees, so the sample is skewed toward large sites; the experience of small CDMOs may differ

  5. Document demands increase rather than decrease (Q11): 26.7% said the authority requested more or more detailed information than in a physical inspection, and these additional requests mostly occurred before the inspection began (62.5%) and during the inspection (62.5%).

Main Challenges: Four Industry Pain Points

Combining the open-ended responses to Q30 with other questions, the main challenges identified by the industry fall into four categories:

  • Technology: IT security requirements incompatible with the platform the inspector uses (for example, the company has approved Teams/WebEx but the inspector asks for Zoom); insufficient video quality prevents effective assessment of site conditions; concerns that the technology could be falsified affect trust
  • Document Preparation: remote inspections require a large volume of electronic documents to be submitted before the inspection; follow-up requests can extend over weeks with no time limit
  • Communication: communication efficiency in video meetings is far lower than on site; explanations of observations are prone to ambiguity; inspectors cannot visually confirm operations on the spot
  • Frequency: because no travel time is needed, the threshold for authorities to conduct remote inspections is lower, and some respondents worry this may increase inspection frequency

Key Point: 54% of Companies Have Not Yet Updated SOPs for the FDA Statutory Requirement

Q31 shows that 46.3% of respondents were aware of the statutory provision 21 USC 374(a)(4), which authorizes the FDA to request records before or after an inspection, but had not yet revised the corresponding company procedures. Another 9.8% did not even know the provision existed.

For a CDMO operating under US FDA jurisdiction, this is an important compliance risk point. A "Remote Interactive Evaluation (RIE)" is not a formal inspection, but the FDA can request records from the manufacturer under this provision, and the outcome may affect the review of marketing applications (NDA/ANDA/BLA/DMF).

Practical Application: Post-Survey Learning - Action Checklist for a CDMO's First Remote Inspection

Based on the survey results, the following are the specific actions a CDMO should take when preparing for its first remote inspection:

Before the inspection:

  • Establish a "remote inspection SOP" covering the document distribution process, video platform selection, division of work among the backroom support team and communication rules with the inspector (including the recording policy)
  • Establish a secure document-sharing portal (Secure Portal): 71.4% of the industry chose this model, avoiding the transfer of sensitive documents by email
  • Designate a "Document Coordinator" dedicated to responding to document requests in real time so that inspectors do not have to wait
  • Confirm the security compliance of the video platform with the IT department, and test bandwidth and audio/video quality in advance
  • For FDA inspections: update the SOP to cover the record submission requirements of 21 USC 374(a)(4)

During the inspection:

  • Set up a "Backroom" command room: senior QA and department representatives provide real-time support to make sure document requests are answered within 30 minutes
  • Use bilingual summaries (where applicable): if the inspector's native language is not English, prepare language summaries of key documents in advance
  • Hold a Wrap-up meeting every day (recommended in Topic E.1 of Part II of this document) to confirm that all of the day's questions have been clarified

After the inspection:

  • If Written Observations are received, submit the CAPA within the specified deadline according to the written response procedure
  • Track subsequent "Additional Information Requests"; the survey shows such requests can extend over weeks, so an internal tracking mechanism is needed

Analogy: The Remote Inspection "Backroom" = The Catcher on a Baseball Mound

In a baseball game, when the pitcher (the representative hosting the inspector on site) faces the batter (the inspector), the catcher (the backroom document support) communicates in real time behind him. If the catcher is slow or poorly coordinated, the pitcher is put on the defensive.

The essence of a remote inspection is that it makes this backroom-frontroom collaboration even more critical. In an on-site inspection, the answer to an inspector's question can be found on a file shelf 10 steps away; in a remote inspection, the backroom must retrieve it from the system in real time, convert the format and upload it to the shared portal, and the efficiency of this whole process directly affects the inspector's trust in the manufacturer.

  • Appendix 1 (the list of 37 documents) is the standard reference basis for remote inspection document preparation. A CDMO should manage documents day to day using the three tiers of "always ready / ready within 2 weeks / retrieved from the archive," so that every document can be provided quickly and completely once the inspector asks.
  • CCS (Contamination Control Strategy, item 37) is explicitly included in the inspection document list, reflecting the trend of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022); a CDMO should make sure the CCS is a standalone, complete and logically clear document rather than spread across various SOPs.
  • The 2021 PDA survey (November 2020) was conducted early, when remote inspection was still an emerging practice, and represents the industry's first-hand reaction with insufficient preparation rather than best practice in a mature state.
  • The FDA is the authority that most actively uses remote inspection (51.6%), followed by the EMA (35.5%); a CDMO planning remote inspection preparation should use the requirements of these two as the baseline while also understanding the individual preferences of the MHRA, TGA and PMDA.
  • Remote inspections are often more time-consuming and demand more documents than physical inspections: 44.8% of respondents said they took longer than physical inspections and 26.7% said authorities requested more information; these two figures remind a CDMO not to underestimate the resource needs of a remote inspection.
  • The efficiency of the Backroom is the core variable in the success or failure of a remote inspection; establishing a clear backroom-frontroom division of work, a secure document-sharing platform (71.4% of the industry chose this route) and real-time communication mechanisms is a CDMO's most important infrastructure investment.
