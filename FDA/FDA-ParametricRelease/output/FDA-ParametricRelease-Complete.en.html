I. Introduction

Original Text

This guidance provides recommendations to applicants on information to include in support of parametric release for sterile products terminally sterilized by moist heat when submitting a new drug application (NDA), abbreviated new drug application (ANDA), new animal drug application (NADA), abbreviated new animal drug application (ANADA), biologics license application (BLA), or supplement or other postmarketing report.

Currently, FDA requires that sterile products meet certain sterility requirements before release to the market. In many cases, the requirements for batch release are fulfilled by conducting a sterility test on finished units drawn from the batch. Parametric release is defined as a sterility assurance release program where demonstrated control of the sterilization process enables a firm to use defined critical process controls, in lieu of the sterility test, to fulfill the intent of 21 CFR 211.165(a), and 211.167(a). Under this strategy, market release of terminally sterilized products can be based upon meeting the defined sterilization parameters and not on performing an approved sterility test. Meeting the requirements of the parametric release process can provide greater assurance that a batch meets the sterility requirement than can be achieved with a sterility test of finished units drawn from the batch.

"Parametric release is defined as a sterility assurance release program where demonstrated control of the sterilization process enables a firm to use defined critical process controls, in lieu of the sterility test."

This guidance does not provide information on procedures, studies, or data concerning efficacy and qualification/validation of moist heat sterilization processes. This guidance also does not provide information on sterility assurance validation programs. However, you may find information relating to such topics in the Agency's guidance for industry on Submission of Documentation for Sterilization Process Validation in Applications for Human and Veterinary Drug Products. Current Good Manufacturing Practices (CGMP) requirements for process validation are found at 21 CFR 211.100 and, for sterile products in particular, at 21 CFR 211.113(b). Adherence to CGMPs is required for all marketed products.

The principles in the guidance may also be applicable to products sterilized by other terminal sterilization processes, such as radiation sterilization, which may be suitable for parametric release. For these types of applications, we recommend the applicant discuss with the review division whether applying the guidance would be appropriate.

FDA's guidance documents, including this guidance, do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead, guidances describe the Agency's current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Parametric Release: Batch release based on meeting the critical process parameters (CPPs) of a validated sterilization process, in place of the traditional finished-product sterility test. The core logic: if you can demonstrate that the process was executed correctly, that is a stronger assurance of sterility than testing samples drawn from the finished product.

Terminal Sterilization: The drug product is sterilized in its final container, after filling and sealing are complete. The fundamental difference from aseptic filling is that terminal sterilization has a quantifiable kill step, whereas aseptic filling relies on maintaining a sterile environment across the entire process chain.

21 CFR 211.165(a) and 211.167(a): These two regulations require, respectively, that drug products meet quality standards before release and that sterile products undergo sterility testing. The regulatory basis for parametric release is that meeting the validated process parameters "fulfills the intent" of both regulations. Note that FDA uses the phrase "fulfill the intent", not "exempt from".

Analogy

Imagine you run a food factory and must make sure every batch of canned food is sterile. The traditional approach is to open a few random cans from each batch and check for bacteria (the sterility test). The problem is that you open only 20 cans out of a batch of 100,000. That is like flipping through 20 random books in a large library and then declaring that the whole library has no typos.

Parametric release works differently. You do not open the cans. Instead, you make sure the retort held at 121°C or above for at least 15 minutes, all temperature probes recorded acceptable results, and the chemical indicators changed color as expected. Because you have already scientifically validated that "all microorganisms are killed under these conditions", achieving the process parameters is itself the strongest evidence of sterility.

Key Notes

Limited scope: This guidance addresses only moist heat terminal sterilization. For other terminal sterilization methods, such as radiation sterilization, FDA recommends that applicants first discuss with the review division whether the principles of this guidance apply.

Validation details are not covered: This guidance does not cover the validation procedures, studies, or data for the sterilization process; these are addressed in a separate FDA guidance, "Submission of Documentation for Sterilization Process Validation". A CDMO building a parametric release system must refer to both guidances together.

Legal status: "Does not establish legally enforceable responsibilities". This is the standard caveat in every FDA guidance. In inspection practice, however, any deviation from the guidance recommendations needs a scientific justification and supporting documentation **(PDA TR30, Section 1.2)**.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: When an injectable product made for a client uses a terminal sterilization process, the CDMO can evaluate whether to apply for parametric release. Advantages include: (1) the sterility test takes 14 days, and parametric release can significantly shorten release time; (2) it removes the batch investigation risk caused by false-positive sterility test results; (3) it provides a higher level of sterility assurance than the sterility test. The prerequisite is that the CDMO has a mature sterilization process validation system and a robust process parameter monitoring system.

Practice Questions

  2. What do 21 CFR 211.165(a) and 211.167(a) each require? How does parametric release "fulfill the intent" of these requirements rather than "exempt" them?
  3. If your CDMO has both moist heat and radiation sterilization product lines, how would your strategy for applying for parametric release differ?
II. Background

Original Text

Sterility testing by cultivation of finished units drawn from the batch is limited in its ability to detect contamination because of the following: (1) the small number of samples required for testing, which restricts the ability to capture those microorganisms dispersed in a large volume, and (2) the limited ability of the prescribed culture media to stimulate growth of all potential microorganisms. Typically, these tests will detect only major errors in the manufacturing process that result in contamination of a large number of product units. However, data derived from in-process controls of a validated terminal sterilization process can provide more accurate information regarding product sterility because the probability of product bioburden surviving the sterilization process in any single unit of a product can be calculated to be less than one in a million.

"The probability of product bioburden surviving the sterilization process in any single unit of a product can be calculated to be less than one in a million."

Parametric release allows manufacturers to replace sterility testing of samples drawn from the finished product as a release criterion with acceptance criteria for the control of identified process parameters. These parameters, called critical parameters, are critical to a successful sterilization process and are based on an in-depth knowledge of the process, the product, the effects of the sterilization process on the product itself, and any microorganisms that become associated with the product during the manufacturing process. Parametric release of the batch is then based on documented evidence of the control of critical parameters, removing the need to test samples drawn from the finished product.

A sterilization load monitor, either in the form of a physical, chemical (ANSI 2008), or biological indicator, is included with each load to satisfy the requirement for a laboratory test. In addition, the sterilization load monitor is always considered a critical process parameter. A successful load monitor result, the meeting of the acceptance criteria of the critical parameters, and having a well validated sterility assurance program demonstrate that there is a state of control of the manufacturing process for the product. The load monitor(s) should be placed in appropriate positions to indicate that the load was exposed to a sterilization process which was measured and recorded for conformance with defined criteria for parametric release. This position(s) is determined based on the evaluation of development and qualification data. The location and number of monitors should be described and justified in the application. Alternative procedures for demonstrating that a load or part of a load was exposed to a sterilization process should be discussed with the review division(s) prior to submitting a plan for parametric release.

FDA conducts scientific evaluation of the parametric release program as part of a cooperative effort among our review staff, compliance staff, and field investigators.

FDA has accepted the practice of parametric release for drug products terminally sterilized by moist heat since 1985. Parametric release, described in the International Conference on Harmonisation (ICH) Q6A (ICH 2000), is endorsed by regulatory and/or pharmaceutical manufacturing groups in the US (PDA 1999, USP 2009), EU (PIC/S 2007, EMEA 2001), and Japan (Sasaki 2002).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Statistical limitations of the sterility test: FDA clearly identifies two fundamental weaknesses of the traditional sterility test. Suppose a batch contains 10,000 vials and 20 vials are sampled for sterility testing per USP <71>. Even with a 1% contamination rate (100 contaminated vials), the probability that all 20 samples pass is still about 82%. In other words, the traditional sterility test can detect only "gross contamination" and is almost powerless against "low-level contamination".

10⁻⁶ SAL (Sterility Assurance Level): "One in a million" means SAL = 10⁻⁶, i.e. the probability of a non-sterile unit is no more than one in a million per unit of product. This is the internationally accepted standard for terminally sterilized products **(PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 8.1)**.

Sterilization Load Monitor: This can be a physical indicator (such as a temperature recorder), a chemical indicator (such as a Class 5 integrating indicator), or a biological indicator (such as a spore strip containing Geobacillus stearothermophilus). FDA clearly states that the load monitor itself is "always considered a Critical Process Parameter".

Analogy

The sterility test is like "knocking on the doors of 20 random households in a city to confirm there are no burglars". If the city has 10,000 households and the 20 you knocked on happen to be fine, that does not mean the whole city is safe.

Parametric release is more like "installing cameras at every entrance and recording that the city gate stayed closed and locked all night". You do not need to check every household, because you have verified that the whole defense system works. The load monitor is the "sentry" stationed at the weakest spot in the city, confirming that the safety standard is met even under the worst-case condition.

Key Notes

Selection and placement of the load monitor: FDA recommends placing the load monitor at "appropriate positions", determined from development and qualification data. In practice, this means heat distribution and heat penetration studies must be completed first to identify the cold spot in the sterilizer, and the load monitor is then placed at the worst-case location **(PDA TR30, Section 4.3)**.

Classification of chemical indicators: FDA cites ANSI/AAMI/ISO 15882:2008 and recommends Class 5 integrating indicators because their response correlates closely with that of biological indicators. A Class 3 (specific test indicator) is acceptable only in "specific circumstances", for example when an overkill cycle is fully validated and the F₀ value far exceeds the minimum requirement.

International consensus: FDA has accepted parametric release since 1985. ICH Q6A, PDA TR30 (1999), USP <1222> (2009), PIC/S (2007), EMEA (2001), and Japan (2002) have all endorsed it. This is a mature practice with global regulatory consensus **(USP <1222>, Section 1)**.

Formula

Sterility Assurance Level (SAL) calculation concept:

SAL = 10⁻⁶ (standard requirement)

For the Overkill Approach:
  F₀ ≥ 12 minutes (typical target 15-20 minutes)
  Assume bioburden = 10⁶ CFU, D₁₂₁ = 1 min
  → probability of survival after sterilization = 10⁶ × 10⁻¹²  = 10⁻⁶ ✓

For the Bioburden-Based Approach:
  Calculate the required F₀ value from actual bioburden data
  and microbial heat resistance, to ensure SAL ≤ 10⁻⁶

Practical Application

CDMO decision on selecting the sterilization load monitor:

For a CDMO, the choice of load monitor directly affects the feasibility of parametric release:

  • Biological Indicator (BI): The most direct evidence of microbial kill, but it requires 48-72 hours of incubation before reading, which may offset the time advantage of parametric release
  • Chemical Indicator (CI): Results are readable immediately; a Class 5 integrating indicator is recommended. It is the most practical option for a multi-product CDMO and can enable "same-day release"
  • Physical Indicator (temperature probes): Provide continuous F₀ data records and are usually the primary monitoring method, used together with CI/BI

Most CDMOs adopt a combined approach of "physical monitoring (temperature probes + F₀ calculation) + Class 5 chemical indicator", balancing scientific rigor with release efficiency.

Section Summary

  • Definition of parametric release: Batch release based on meeting the critical parameters of a validated sterilization process, in place of the traditional sterility test, fulfilling the intent of 21 CFR 211.165(a) and 211.167(a)
  • Limitations of the sterility test: Limited by (1) the statistical weakness of small-sample testing and (2) the limited ability of the culture media to support growth of all microorganisms, so it can detect only large-scale contamination events
  • SAL 10⁻⁶: A validated terminal sterilization process can hold the probability of a non-sterile unit to one in a million or less, far better than the detection sensitivity the sterility test can achieve
  • The load monitor is a CPP: Whether physical, chemical, or biological, the load monitor is always considered a critical process parameter, and its position must be determined from heat distribution/heat penetration study data
  • Global consensus: FDA (1985), ICH Q6A (2000), PDA TR30 (1999), USP <1222> (2009), PIC/S (2007), and EMEA (2001) all support parametric release, a mature and internationally recognized practice

III. Content of Submissions for Parametric Release

Original Text

Section IV describes what submissions are required to obtain approval for parametric release. The approval of parametric release practices is based on an assessment of the applicant's proposed critical process parameters and how they are controlled. Demonstrated reliability of the production terminal sterilization cycle, microbiological control, and monitoring and control of production cycle parameters within established validated limits are part of this assessment. The terminal sterilization process for the product proposed for parametric release should be validated according to the Agency's guidance for industry on Submission of Documentation for Sterilization Process Validation in Applications for Human and Veterinary Drug Products.

FDA approval of the parametric release program will be based on how well the firm has addressed the risks to product sterility. A risk assessment statement consistent with the principles of ICH Q9 (ICH 2006) should be provided that describes the following:

  • Current strategies for control of the terminal sterilization program;
  • Risk that these strategies might fail to ensure sterility; and
  • How prior manufacturing experience and knowledge were incorporated into the risk assessment.
"FDA approval of the parametric release program will be based on how well the firm has addressed the risks to product sterility."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The core logic of FDA review: FDA is not reviewing whether you are perfect. It is reviewing whether you fully understand and control the sterility risks. The three review aspects map clearly onto the ICH Q9 risk management process **(ICH Q9(R1), Section 3.1)**:

  • Current control strategies → Risk Control
  • Risk of failure → Risk Evaluation
  • Prior experience → Risk Identification based on knowledge

Critical Process Parameters (CPPs): In the context of terminal sterilization, typical CPPs include sterilization temperature, sterilization time, F₀ value, chamber pressure, and loading pattern. The validated range of these parameters is the basis for the parametric release decision.

Analogy

Applying for parametric release is like applying to "graduate without exams". A university would never let anyone skip exams outright. You would have to submit a complete portfolio showing that you have achieved, by other means, learning outcomes equal to or better than what an exam could assess. FDA reviews the quality of that "portfolio": whether your control strategy is complete, whether you understand the possible failure modes, and whether your past experience supports the claim.

Key Notes

The validation prerequisite cannot be skipped: FDA clearly states that the sterilization process must first be validated in accordance with another guidance. Parametric release is therefore not a shortcut around validation. On the contrary, it demands stricter validation and process understanding than traditional release **(PDA TR30, Section 2.1)**.

ICH Q9 is the framework: FDA expects the risk assessment statement to follow ICH Q9 principles. This is not a suggestion but a substantive requirement. A CDMO should use FMEA or a similar tool to build a structured risk assessment document **(ICH Q9(R1), Annex I)**.

III.A Control Strategy for the Terminal Sterilization Program

Original Text

A control strategy is used to ensure that the acceptance criteria of the parametric release process and terminal sterilization cycle are met in order to ensure product sterility.

The control strategy should include the following:

  • The rationale for the methods implemented to monitor and control the terminal sterilization process used for the product release (the critical process parameters);
  • The rationale for the selection of critical process parameter(s);
  • A description of the acceptance criteria for parametric release;
  • A description of the drug product and container closure system (including secondary packaging, as applicable) that will be part of the parametric release program;
  • A description of the proposed production loading patterns and verification that they are within the validated limits for the terminal sterilization cycle, or a statement that they have not changed since last approved and validated (as applicable); and
  • A description of the microbiological monitoring plan for the product and components prior to terminal sterilization or a statement that the plan has not changed since last validated. Spore detection and heat resistance studies should be emphasized for bioburden based sterilization cycles.

If you are referencing information previously submitted to meet these recommendations, it should include the application number and submission date, and any other relevant citations to the Agency's records where the information can be found.

Footnotes:

14 See 21 CFR 314.50(g)(1) or 21 CFR 601.2.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Control Strategy: This is not just "listing which parameters you monitor". It is a complete chain of logic: why these parameters were chosen → how they are monitored → what standard counts as acceptable → which products and packaging are covered → whether the loading pattern is within the validated range → how the pre-sterilization bioburden is controlled.

The six elements, one by one:

  1. Rationale for methods: Do not just say "we use temperature probes". Explain why the number, placement, and accuracy of the probes are sufficient to represent the temperature state of the whole sterilizer chamber
  2. Rationale for CPP selection: Use scientific data to support why these parameters (and not others) are "critical"; this usually comes from process development and validation data
  3. Acceptance criteria: For example "F₀ ≥ 15 min for all probes", "Class 5 CI fully changed", "BI shows no growth"
  4. Product and container closure system: Different containers (glass vials vs. prefilled syringes vs. BFS containers) and secondary packaging affect heat penetration characteristics
  5. Loading patterns: How the sterilizer is loaded directly affects heat distribution. Any change that departs from the validated loading pattern may require revalidation
  6. Microbiological monitoring program: For a bioburden-based cycle, FDA places particular emphasis on spore detection and heat resistance studies

Analogy

A control strategy is like a "flood control plan". It is not enough to say "we have a levee" (monitoring method). You also need to explain why the levee is set at this height (CPP selection rationale), what water level counts as safe (acceptance criteria), the area the levee protects (product scope), whether the upstream reservoir storage pattern is within design limits (loading patterns), and the routine river water quality monitoring program (microbiological monitoring).

Key Notes

How Overkill vs. Bioburden-Based affects the control strategy:

Overkill Approach: Assumes the worst case (bioburden = 10⁶ CFU, D₁₂₁ = 1 min), typically with F₀ ≥ 12 min. The microbiological monitoring program can be relatively simple because the safety margin is large **(PDA TR30, Section 3.2)**.

Bioburden-Based Approach: The sterilization cycle is designed from actual bioburden data, and F₀ may be below 12 min (for example 8 min). In this case FDA particularly emphasizes "spore detection" and "heat resistance studies", because the safety margin is smaller and any change in bioburden directly affects SAL **(PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 8.20)**.

Special note for CDMOs: When products from multiple clients share the same sterilizer, the validated range of the loading pattern must cover all product combinations. If a new product has a different vial type or carton size, supplemental validation may be needed.

Formula

F₀ calculation (equivalent sterilization time):

F₀ = Σ Δt × 10^((T - 121.1) / z)

Where:
  Δt = each time interval (usually 1 min)
  T  = actual temperature during that interval (°C)
  z  = z value (usually 10°C, for G. stearothermophilus)
  121.1°C = reference temperature

Typical acceptance criteria for the Overkill Approach:
  F₀(min at cold spot) ≥ 12 min (minimum requirement)
  F₀(min at cold spot) ≥ 15 min (common practical target)

Bioburden-Based Approach:
  The required F₀ is determined by:
  - Actual bioburden count
  - D₁₂₁ value of the most resistant isolate
  - Target SAL = 10⁻⁶

III.B Risk Assessment, Process Understanding, and Prior Knowledge

Original Text

Successful parametric release systems are based on the reliability of the control strategy of the sterility assurance program. We recommend that your risk assessment focus on the risk of failure to achieve the minimum required probability of a non-sterile unit for each unit of every batch. The risk assessment should include the following:

  • Consistency of performance of the terminal sterilization cycle within the validated limits.
  • A discussion of risk to the sterility of the product relative to the following: (1) the production terminal sterilization cycle, (2) the production loading patterns, (3) the container closure system (including secondary packaging), and (4) any potential contamination risks from the environment (as appropriate). For an approved application, you should indicate any changes to the above items and provide an assessment of the risk to the sterility of the product associated with those changes. For example, although the established minimum sterilization time cannot be lowered, the maximum sterilization time can be increased if the appropriate stability data are provided to support the increase.
  • Experience with the proposed or similar product (and container closure system) and proposed or similar sterilization process, the overall risks to sterility, and the steps you have taken to assess and control these risks. For new products, prior knowledge from developmental and registration/exhibit batches may suffice.
  • A discussion of your overall prior knowledge and production and testing experience relevant to the drug product that will be subject to parametric release.
"For new products, prior knowledge from developmental and registration/exhibit batches may suffice."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Focus of the risk assessment: FDA clearly states that the risk assessment should focus on "the risk of not meeting the minimum non-sterile unit probability requirement", that is, the risk of failing to achieve SAL 10⁻⁶. This is not a general quality risk assessment but a dedicated assessment aimed squarely at "failure of sterility assurance".

Four sources of risk:

  1. Sterilization Cycle: Equipment failure, parameter drift, control system failure
  2. Loading Patterns: Loads outside the validated range, residual air creating cold spots
  3. Container Closure: Insufficient seal integrity leading to post-sterilization recontamination
  4. Environmental Contamination: Pre-sterilization bioburden levels exceeding the assumed level

Prior Knowledge: This is FDA's "entry path" for new products. Even without a large body of commercial production data, data from development batches and registration batches can be used to support a parametric release application.

Analogy

A risk assessment is like a health check before buying insurance. The insurer (FDA) will not underwrite you just because you say "I am healthy". It looks at: (1) the consistency of your checkup data (cycle consistency); (2) the specific health risks you face (the four sources of risk); (3) your family history and past health records (prior knowledge and manufacturing experience). "Prior knowledge" is like the pre-employment checkup for a new hire: even without long-term follow-up data, a recent comprehensive examination can provide a sufficient basis for judgment.

Key Notes

Change management and risk impact: FDA specifically notes that for an approved application, any "change" to the sterilization cycle, loading pattern, container closure system, or environment requires a risk assessment. This links directly to the change management requirements of ICH Q10 **(ICH Q10, Section 3.2.4)**.

One-way flexibility on sterilization time: FDA gives a specific example: "the minimum sterilization time cannot be reduced, but the maximum sterilization time can be increased (if supported by stability data)". This reflects the "decision hierarchy" of sterility assurance: any change that reduces sterility assurance requires a Prior Approval Supplement, whereas a change that increases the safety margin may be handled through a simpler pathway.

How a CDMO can demonstrate prior knowledge: A CDMO's advantage is its extensive experience with "similar products and similar processes". Even if a particular client's product is new, the CDMO can cite historical data from the same sterilizer, similar container systems, and similar loading patterns to build the prior knowledge base **(PDA TR30, Section 5.2)**.

Section Summary

  • The core of FDA review: Approval of parametric release depends on "how fully the applicant addresses sterility risk", not merely on listing the monitored parameters
  • Six elements of the control strategy: Rationale for CPP monitoring methods, CPP selection rationale, acceptance criteria, product and container description, loading pattern validation, and microbiological monitoring program (for bioburden-based cycles, emphasize spore detection and heat resistance studies)
  • The risk assessment focuses on SAL: Assess the risk of "failing to achieve SAL 10⁻⁶", covering four sources: sterilization cycle, loading pattern, container closure, and environmental contamination
  • Prior knowledge is the entry path: A new product can use development batch and registration batch data; a CDMO can cite historical experience with the same equipment and similar products to build prior knowledge
  • Change management directly affects parametric release: Any change that affects sterility assurance requires the risk to be reassessed and the control strategy updated, following ICH Q10 change management principles

  • Understand the different Filing Requirements: when to use an original application, a Prior Approval Supplement, or a Special Report/Annual Report

III.C Documentation for Parametric Release Process

Original Text

The following information specific to the proposed parametric release process should also be included in your submission:

  • A citation to a complete and detailed description of the current relevant terminal sterilization cycle.
  • Identification of the critical process parameters (process/cycle parameters and appropriate load monitors essential for product release) for the product(s) proposed for parametric release, including the minimum and maximum limits for these critical parameters. The critical process parameters should be within the limits that have been validated and approved for sterility assurance of the subject product(s).
  • Acknowledgement that adherence to the critical parameters of the parametric release program will substitute for the performance of a sterility test as the primary release criterion for the product and that sterility test results from the finished product will not be used to overrule any failure to meet the acceptance criteria of the parametric release program. In the event of failure, the specific sterilizer load will be rejected by the quality control unit and will not be released unless there is a provision for reprocessing.
  • Acknowledgement that regardless of the batch release technique used, any specimen tested according to the reference test method for sterility (e.g., compendium or FDA regulations) will meet the criteria for sterility (such as during testing for stability or postmarketing investigations).
  • A description of the sterilization load monitor that indicates the following: (1) the type of monitor being proposed, (2) how the load monitor will be used and analyzed, (3) what functions are being measured by the monitor, and (4) the rationale for the location of the monitor. Additionally, for indirect monitors, we recommend that you include a statement justifying the classification of the indirect indicator that you are using as defined by the American National Standard Institute (ANSI 2008). In certain circumstances a Class 3 indicator may be appropriate; however, a Class 5 indicator is recommended for most situations.
  • Documentation of the control system to verify exposure of the load to the sterilization process.
  • Revision of the certificates of analysis or batch release records for each product subject to parametric release to indicate that parametric release is now the method used to provide assurance of the requirement of sterility. We recommend that you provide a reference to show the link between batch release criteria and the commitments in the application.
"Sterility test results from the finished product will not be used to overrule any failure to meet the acceptance criteria of the parametric release program."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Logical structure of the seven documentation requirements:

  1. Reference to the sterilization cycle description: You do not need to repeat the entire validation report in the parametric release application, but you must provide a traceable reference link
  2. CPP identification and limits: Every CPP must have min/max limits, and they must be within the validated range
  3. Release principle statement: This is the most critical commitment: if the CPPs are met, the batch is released; if the CPPs are not met, the batch is rejected, and the sterility test result cannot "overrule" a CPP failure
  4. Sterility compliance statement: Even when parametric release is used, any sterility test performed by the compendial method must still pass (stability testing, post-marketing investigations, etc.)
  5. Load Monitor description: Four aspects: type, use and analysis, functions measured, and rationale for location
  6. Control system documentation: Verification that the load exposed to the sterilization process is under a control system (such as PLC logic and interlocks)
  7. CoA/batch record revisions: The release record must reflect the parametric release method and link to the commitments in the application

Analogy

The third requirement (a sterility test cannot overrule a CPP failure) is the cornerstone of the entire philosophy of parametric release. Imagine you have passed through a rigorous building safety inspection system, with all structural calculations, material tests, and construction monitoring in place. If one structural parameter fails at the end (for example, rebar spacing exceeds the limit), you cannot declare the building safe because "the building shows no cracks". "It looks fine" (a passing sterility test) cannot overrule "the structural parameter failed" (a CPP failure). Conversely, if all structural parameters pass, you do not need to drill holes in every wall to prove the inside is sound.

Key Notes

Practical meaning of the "cannot overrule" principle: If the F₀ value of a batch is below the acceptance criterion (for example, a probe records F₀ = 11.8 min against a criterion of ≥ 12 min), the batch still "cannot be released" even if you then run a sterility test on it and the result passes. The only way out is reprocessing (resterilization), provided you have an approved reprocessing procedure and can show that resterilization does not affect product quality **(PDA TR30, Section 6.3)**.

The four-aspect description of the Load Monitor: FDA expects very specific detail. For a Class 5 chemical indicator, for example, you need to state: (1) brand and model (type); (2) where it is placed in the sterilizer, when it is read after sterilization, and the reading criteria (use and analysis); (3) that it measures "the time-temperature integrated effect equivalent to a specific BI growth/no-growth result" (functions measured); (4) the scientific rationale for placing it at the identified cold spot (rationale for location).

Class 5 vs. Class 3 indicators: The response curve of a Class 5 (integrating indicator) correlates closely with that of a BI and is suitable for most situations. A Class 3 (specific test indicator) responds only under specific conditions; FDA allows its use "in some circumstances" but clearly states that Class 5 is the recommended standard choice **(USP <1222>, Section 4.3)**.

IV. Filing Requirements

Original Text

To request parametric release in an original application submission, the request should include information specific to parametric release along with sterilization validation information and product release criteria. For changes to an approved application, the request for parametric release should be submitted in a prior approval supplement under 21 CFR 314.70, 21 CFR 601.12 or 21 CFR 514.8(b)(2). The change to parametric release requires FDA approval before its implementation.

If the applicant has current experience using parametric release with a comparable sterilization cycle at the same manufacturing site, and the proposed product's manufacturing process fits into the same validation protocol for parametric release (e.g., container closure system, load patterns, cycle process parameters, and cycle acceptance criteria), then the applicant should meet the filing requirements with a special report for a human drug product, or an annual report for a veterinary drug product or a biologic product. If your product fits into one of these filing categories, contact the review division for your product to verify submission requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three filing pathways:

  1. Original Application: Parametric release is included when the NDA/ANDA/BLA is first submitted. The parametric release information is submitted together with the sterilization validation data and the release criteria.
  2. Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): The standard pathway for converting an approved product from traditional release to parametric release. It "may be implemented only after FDA approval", which is an explicit prior approval requirement.
  3. Special Report / Annual Report (simplified pathway): Applies only when there is existing parametric release experience at the same site with the same sterilization cycle, and the process conditions of the new product (container, loading, cycle parameters, acceptance criteria) fall entirely within the existing validated range.

Prior Approval Supplement: Under 21 CFR 314.70, a "major change in the manufacturing method that affects product quality" must be submitted as a PAS. Switching from "sterility test release" to "parametric release" clearly falls into this category. The applicant can begin using parametric release only after FDA reviews and approves it.

Analogy

These three pathways are like three scenarios for a change in building use: (1) a new building is planned as a restaurant from the start when the application is filed (original application); (2) an existing office building is to be converted into a restaurant, which requires a new occupancy permit (PAS, prior approval); (3) you already have a restaurant on a commercial street and the identical unit next door will also become a restaurant, so only a notification report is needed (Special Report, because the fire protection, ventilation, and sewer systems are all the same validated infrastructure).

Key Notes

The conditions for the simplified pathway are very strict: To use the Special Report/Annual Report pathway, all of the following must be met: (1) the same manufacturing site; (2) a comparable sterilization cycle; (3) the container closure system, loading pattern, cycle parameters, and acceptance criteria are all within the existing validation program. In practice, this means the same sterilizer model, the same type of vial, and the same loading configuration.

Recommended CDMO strategy: For a multi-product CDMO, the most effective strategy is to establish the parametric release foundation with the first product through a PAS (including validation of a broad range of loading patterns and containers), so that subsequent similar products can follow the simplified pathway. This requires building room for expansion into the initial validation program design **(PDA TR30, Section 7.1)**.

Always contact the review division first: In its last sentence, FDA clearly recommends: "contact the review division for your product to verify submission requirements". This is not boilerplate. Talking to FDA before deciding on the filing pathway avoids the risk of being asked to resubmit as a PAS after filing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Guide to the References

The following are the core references that CDMO practitioners should study in depth, ranked by practical importance:

1. PDA Technical Report No. 30 (1999): The most complete technical guidance document on parametric release. It covers the full methodology for choosing between the overkill and bioburden-based approaches, F₀ calculation, load monitor selection, and sterilization cycle development. This FDA guidance can be seen as the regulatory companion to PDA TR30 **(PDA TR30)**.

2. USP <1222> (2009): The pharmacopoeial definition and technical requirements for parametric release. It complements the FDA guidance: the FDA guidance tells you "how to apply to FDA", and USP <1222> tells you "technically how to do it" **(USP <1222>)**.

3. ICH Q9 (2006): The methodological framework for risk assessment. FDA clearly expects the risk assessment statement to follow ICH Q9 principles, so the use of tools such as FMEA and FTA is indispensable **(ICH Q9(R1))**.

4. PIC/S PI 005-3 (2007): The PIC/S guidance dedicated to parametric release; of particular importance are the latest requirements for terminal sterilization in Section 8 of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022 revision). A CDMO with clients in European markets must meet both FDA and PIC/S requirements **(PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 8.20–8.22)**.

5. ANSI/AAMI/ISO 15882:2008: The standard for selection, use, and result interpretation of chemical indicators. Understanding the Class 1–6 indicator classification system is the foundation for choosing a load monitor correctly.

6. CPG 460.800 (1987): The earliest FDA compliance policy guide accepting parametric release, and the historical precursor of this guidance. Knowing its content helps in understanding how FDA's oversight of parametric release has evolved.

Analogy

The relationship among these references is like the documents needed to build a house: the regulations (21 CFR) are the building code, which you must obey; the FDA guidance is the guide for submitting the building permit application to the city; PDA TR30 and USP <1222> are the architect's handbook and engineering specifications, telling you how to design and build; ICH Q9 is the structural safety assessment method; and PIC/S is the "mutual recognition of international building standards" you need if your house is also to be legally used in other countries.

Section Summary

  • Seven documentation requirements: Sterilization cycle reference, CPP identification and limits, release principle statement, sterility compliance statement, four-aspect description of the load monitor, control system documentation, and CoA/batch record revisions
  • The "cannot overrule" principle: A passing sterility test result cannot overrule a CPP failure. This is the philosophical cornerstone of parametric release, and violating it negates the scientific basis of parametric release itself
  • Four aspects of the Load Monitor description: Type, use and analysis, functions measured, and rationale for location; Class 5 is the recommended standard
