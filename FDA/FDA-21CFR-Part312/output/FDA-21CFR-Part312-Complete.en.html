  • Distinguish the IND from an NDA/BLA: an IND is a request, not an approval; it goes into effect automatically after 30 days unless FDA imposes a clinical hold

0.1 What Is an IND and Why Does It Exist?

Original Text

Statutory Basis: Section 505(i) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) prohibits the interstate shipment of any drug intended for investigational use in humans unless the sponsor has an effective Investigational New Drug Application on file with FDA. This is the foundational legal hook that makes Part 312 operative.

Purpose of the IND: The IND exists to give FDA the opportunity to review safety data and study protocols before clinical trials begin. Its twin purposes are:

  1. Ensuring adequate preclinical data exists to support the proposed human studies (safety threshold), and
  2. Ensuring adequate human subject protections are in place (ethical threshold).

How the IND becomes effective: An IND is not approved by FDA — it becomes effective automatically 30 calendar days after FDA receives a complete submission, unless FDA places the study on clinical hold within that window. A clinical hold is an order from FDA to delay or suspend a proposed or ongoing clinical investigation.

"A sponsor may begin a clinical investigation 30 days after FDA receives the IND, or on earlier notification by FDA that the investigation may begin." — 21 CFR §312.40(b)

Two principal IND types:

  • Commercial IND: Sponsor is a pharmaceutical or biotechnology company; the ultimate goal is submission of a New Drug Application (NDA) or Biologics License Application (BLA). Full IND with complete CMC, pharmacology, and clinical protocol sections required.
  • Research (Investigator) IND: Sponsor-investigator is typically an academic physician or institution; no commercial intent. FDA applies somewhat reduced expectations, but the IND must still meet Part 312 requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: IND vs NDA - A Request Is Not an Approval

An IND (Investigational New Drug application) is a notice stating "we intend to begin human trials"; it is not an FDA approval document. An IND goes into effect by "silent consent": if FDA does not issue a clinical hold within 30 days, the IND is considered effective.

An NDA / BLA (New Drug Application / Biologics License Application) is the true "marketing approval": FDA reviews the full safety and efficacy data from Phases 1-3 and then actively issues an approval letter.

Simple comparison: an IND is like applying to the city for a "construction start permit" (you wait 30 days to see whether you are told to stop before work begins); an NDA/BLA is the final "occupancy certificate" (issued only after the building passes inspection).

Analogy: An IND Is a "Building Start Permit"

Imagine you are building a hospital (developing a new drug):

  • The IND is the "construction application" you submit to the city planning office (FDA). You hand in the blueprints (CMC data), the site safety report (animal toxicology) and the construction plan (clinical protocol).
  • The city office has 30 days to review your plan. If it believes construction would endanger nearby residents (a subject safety concern), it issues a "stop-work order" (clinical hold).
  • If 30 days pass and nothing is said, you may start building (begin human trials).
  • The NDA/BLA is the final "certificate of completion": only after the whole hospital is built and has passed every inspection can it formally open (go on the market).

The key difference: starting work does not require the city office to "approve" you, only to "not object". Going to market requires a formal permit.

Common Misconception

Misconception: "An IND means FDA has approved human trials"

Correct understanding: An IND becoming effective means FDA has "not intervened", not that it has "actively approved". The difference is that if FDA finds a safety concern during review, it can impose a clinical hold within 30 days without having to issue any permission document.

This means that when a client tells a CDMO "the IND has been approved", the CDMO should verify whether the statement is accurate. The correct wording is "the IND is effective".

0.2 §312.1 - Scope: When Is an IND Required?

Original Text

§312.1(a) General scope: Part 312 applies to any clinical investigation of a drug that is subject to section 505(i) of the FD&C Act. An IND is required when a drug is:

  1. Not approved for marketing in the United States (i.e., not the subject of an effective NDA or BLA), AND
  2. Intended for use in a clinical investigation involving human subjects, AND
  3. Intended to be shipped in interstate commerce to conduct the investigation.

IND is required for:

  • Phase 1, 2, and 3 clinical trials for new chemical entities (NCEs) and new biological entities
  • Bioequivalence (BE) studies for certain products (see §312.2(b)(1) exemption below)
  • New indication studies for already-marketed drugs — if the drug crosses state lines in the context of an unapproved use, and the study is not covered by the marketed product labeling
  • New route of administration, new population, new dose range studies for approved drugs when these parameters go beyond the approved label

Key geographic trigger — "interstate commerce": The IND requirement is activated when investigational drug crosses state lines. Purely intrastate investigations with locally manufactured drug may theoretically be exempt, though this is increasingly rare in practice.

"A sponsor shall submit an IND to FDA if the sponsor intends to conduct a clinical investigation with an investigational new drug that is subject to §312.2." — §312.20(a)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: An IND Is Required Only When All Three Conditions Are Met

The requirement for an IND is triggered only when three conditions are met at the same time:

  • Condition 1: The drug is not yet approved for marketing in the US (or is used outside its approved scope)
  • Condition 2: It is used in a human clinical investigation
  • Condition 3: It is shipped across state lines

If any one condition is not met, an IND may not be required (but the other exemption criteria still need to be evaluated).

CDMO Practice: When Does a CDMO Encounter IND-Related Drugs?

  • Phase 1 clinical batch manufacture: The client provides an IND number and asks the CDMO to manufacture the first batch for human trials (the drug is shipped across state lines to clinical sites, so an IND is necessary)
  • Trials of a new indication for a marketed drug: The client holds an approved NDA but now wants to use the same drug for a new cancer indication, which is outside the approved scope, so a new IND is needed
  • Biosimilar development: Before filing a BLA, a biosimilar may also need an IND to run comparative clinical studies

Key Point: The "Interstate Commerce" Trigger

In practice, almost every multicenter clinical trial crosses state lines (the drug moves from the manufacturing site to the various clinical sites), so the "interstate" condition is almost always met.

A CDMO plant in California manufactures a drug and ships it to a clinical trial center in New York: interstate, so the IND applies. Even if the plant and the trial center are in the same state, FDA's jurisdiction covers all drugs that affect interstate commerce, so it is very difficult in practice to avoid the IND requirement entirely.

0.3 §312.2 - Exemptions from IND Requirement

Original Text

§312.2 lists specific categories of investigations that are exempt from the IND requirement. Understanding these exemptions is critical — both to avoid unnecessary IND filings and to avoid the error of not filing when one is required.

Exemption 1 — Lawful marketing exemption (§312.2(a)): A drug that is already lawfully marketed in the United States is not subject to IND requirements when used for clinical investigations that:

  • Are not intended to support approval of a new indication or significant labeling change,
  • Are not intended to support a significant change in the advertising for the product,
  • Do not involve a route of administration, dose, or use in a patient population that would significantly increase the risks associated with the product, and
  • Are conducted in compliance with the requirements for review by an Institutional Review Board (§56) and the requirements for informed consent (§50).

Exemption 2 — Bioavailability/bioequivalence (BA/BE) exemption (§312.2(b)(1)): A clinical investigation that is intended to show that a drug product is bioavailable or bioequivalent is exempt from the IND requirement provided all of the following conditions are met:

  • The drug product is not radioactively labeled,
  • The investigation is not intended to support approval of a new indication or significant labeling change,
  • The investigation is not intended to evaluate a new drug product,
  • The investigation involves a single dose of a marketed formulation, or the investigation involves the comparison of two marketed formulations, and
  • The drug product under investigation is otherwise lawfully marketed in the United States.

Exemption 3 — Foreign clinical investigations (§312.2(d)): Investigations conducted outside of the United States are not required to comply with the IND regulations, provided the data from those investigations will not be submitted to support a U.S. marketing application. If foreign data is intended to support a U.S. NDA or BLA, those studies must meet FDA standards (even if no U.S. IND was filed — the foreign data must still be conducted per ICH E6 GCP and the results must be interpretable by FDA).

Exemption 4 — Previously marketed drugs in certain Phase 1 contexts: FDA has historically applied reduced scrutiny for lawfully marketed drugs studied in Phase 1 settings where the dose, route, and patient population are within the approved label. However, this does not eliminate all reporting obligations — IRB oversight and informed consent still apply.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Exempt Does Not Mean Unregulated

Being exempt from the IND requirement does not mean a study is entirely unregulated. All exempt studies must still comply with:

  • IRB (Institutional Review Board) review obligations (21 CFR Part 56)
  • Informed consent requirements (21 CFR Part 50)
  • Applicable CGMP requirements (Parts 210/211)

An exemption only means "no IND document needs to be submitted to FDA"; it does not mean "all regulatory obligations are waived".

Analogy: The Logic of Exemption - A Drug with a "Credit History"

Think of the IND application as a "credit check": a brand-new drug (NCE) has no safety record, so FDA must review it from scratch, like a bank running a strict credit investigation on a new customer.

A lawfully marketed drug already has years of safety history. If you only use it for a standard BE study or routine clinical use, FDA allows you to "skip the credit check" because the risk profile is already well understood.

But once you use this "good credit" drug for a brand-new indication, a new dose or a high-risk population, the risk profile changes and you must file an IND.

Exercise: IND Required or Exempt?

Decide whether an IND is required in each of the following scenarios:

  1. A pharmaceutical company runs a BA study on an approved antihypertensive, comparing the original tablet with a new capsule formulation (same dose, same indication).
    → The §312.2(b)(1) BE exemption may apply
  2. A researcher at a US university studies an approved chemotherapy drug for a rare new cancer and ships samples across state lines.
    → New indication + interstate shipment = IND required
  3. A Phase 2 trial conducted in Europe whose data will support a US NDA.
    → A US IND is not necessarily required, but the data must meet FDA standards and ICH E6 GCP

Key Point: Practical Implications of the BA/BE Exemption

The BA/BE exemption (§312.2(b)(1)) is the exemption most commonly used in the generic drug industry. When a CDMO manufactures BE study batches for an ANDA (generic drug application), the client usually does not need an IND, but the CDMO must still:

  • Confirm that all exemption conditions are met (not radiolabeled, not a new indication, etc.)
  • Manufacture the BE study batches in a CGMP-compliant manner
  • Retain complete manufacturing and release records
0.4 IND Phases - Key Definitions & CMC Implications

Original Text

21 CFR §312.21 defines the three phases of clinical investigation. Phase definitions are critical for CDMOs because they directly determine the level of CMC (Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls) documentation and GMP compliance expected by FDA.

Phase 1 (§312.21(a)) — First-in-Human Safety Studies:

  • Typically 20–80 subjects, usually healthy volunteers (or patients in oncology)
  • Primary objective: safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD)
  • Secondary objectives: maximum tolerated dose, dose escalation parameters
  • CMC requirements: relatively lower — FDA recognizes manufacturing processes are still evolving; a preliminary process description, basic specifications, and analytical testing are sufficient
  • GMP standard: "Phase-appropriate GMP" — full commercial CGMP not required, but risk-based controls must be in place

Phase 2 (§312.21(b)) — Exploratory Efficacy and Dose Ranging:

  • Typically 100–300 patients with the target disease or condition
  • Primary objective: exploratory efficacy evidence, optimal dose range identification
  • CMC requirements: intermediate — process must be more defined, with validated analytical methods and tighter specifications; stability data program underway
  • GMP standard: increased GMP rigor; process consistency becomes critical as commercial-scale manufacturing planning begins

Phase 3 (§312.21(c)) — Confirmatory Efficacy and Safety:

  • Typically 300–3,000+ patients across multiple sites; large-scale, randomized controlled trials
  • Primary objective: confirmatory demonstration of efficacy and safety supporting marketing approval
  • CMC requirements: full commercial-scale GMP required; the CMC section of the IND must reflect the commercial manufacturing intent; all critical quality attributes (CQAs) must be defined; process validation (or process performance qualification) must be underway or completed
  • GMP standard: essentially equivalent to commercial CGMP (21 CFR Parts 210/211 or Part 600 for biologics)
"The CMC section should contain sufficient information about the drug substance and drug product to assure the proper identification, quality, purity, and strength of the investigational drug." — FDA IND Guidance (21 CFR §312.23(a)(7))

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Process Maturity in Phases 1/2/3 Is Like the "Three Stages of Building a House"

  • Phase 1 = Laying the foundation: You only need the foundation to be safe; the finishing details are not needed. The process can be imperfect, but there must be no safety concerns.
  • Phase 2 = Building the main structure: The walls must be straight and the doors and windows must meet specification; you begin to need the architect's drawings (process specification documents).
  • Phase 3 = Ready for move-in: Every detail must match the final approved building plan (the commercial process) exactly, because changes cannot be made casually afterwards; changes must be reported to the authority (manufacturing change supplement).

Key Point: Phase-Appropriate GMP

"Phase-appropriate GMP" is FDA's principle of flexibility, but the flexibility has limits:

  • Phase 1 does not require full process validation, but it does require risk-based quality control measures (such as sterility assurance for aseptic filling and raw material identity confirmation)
  • In a Phase 1 inspection, FDA focuses on whether "the drug is safe for use in humans", not on perfect CGMP compliance
  • A drug at any phase must have: correct labeling, sterility assurance (where applicable) and prevention of cross-contamination
0.5 Why Part 312 Matters for CDMOs

Original Text

Under 21 CFR Part 312, the sponsor is the entity that assumes responsibility for the IND — typically the pharmaceutical or biotechnology company. When a sponsor contracts out manufacturing to a CDMO, the sponsor retains IND ownership and responsibility, but the CDMO assumes direct operational obligations for anything it manufactures under that IND.

CDMO's key obligations under Part 312:

  1. Manufacture per IND specifications: The CDMO must manufacture the drug substance or drug product exactly as described in the IND CMC section. Any deviation from IND specifications is a regulatory event that could affect the validity of clinical data.
  2. Maintain records sufficient for FDA inspection: All batch records, raw material records, environmental monitoring records, and deviation records must be maintained and available for FDA review. FDA may inspect the CDMO during the IND phase even before commercial approval.
  3. Notify sponsor of deviations that might affect safety or efficacy: If the CDMO identifies an out-of-specification (OOS) result, a manufacturing deviation, or an equipment failure that could affect the quality of a clinical batch, the sponsor must be notified immediately. The sponsor is responsible for evaluating whether the deviation triggers a reporting obligation to FDA (e.g., IND safety report under §312.32).
  4. Support sponsor's IND amendment obligations: If the CDMO implements a manufacturing change (e.g., equipment change, site change, process modification), the sponsor must file a CMC amendment to the IND. The CDMO must proactively inform the sponsor of any such changes.
  5. Comply with phase-appropriate GMP: The level of GMP compliance expected by FDA scales with the clinical phase. CDMO must understand where its client's program is and apply the appropriate controls.

Failure scenarios for CDMOs:

  • Manufacturing deviation → clinical hold: A significant deviation (e.g., sterility failure, wrong excipient) can cause FDA to place the entire clinical program on clinical hold until the issue is resolved.
  • OOS result → batch rejection: An OOS result on a clinical batch means the batch cannot be used; if it was already administered to subjects, a safety evaluation is required.
  • Process change not filed → potential protocol violation: If the CDMO changes the manufacturing process without notifying the sponsor, and the sponsor fails to file an IND amendment, clinical data from those batches may be unacceptable to FDA.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Division of Responsibility Between Sponsor and CDMO

The legal responsibility structure under Part 312 is as follows:

  • Sponsor: holds the IND, is accountable to FDA and bears all reporting obligations (IND amendments, safety reports, etc.)
  • CDMO (contract manufacturer): manufactures according to the IND CMC specifications, is accountable to the Sponsor and maintains GMP compliance

Although a CDMO does not submit INDs to FDA directly, FDA can inspect the CDMO's plant and review its manufacturing records directly. The CDMO's compliance status directly affects the validity of the Sponsor's IND.

Analogy: the Sponsor is the "property developer" and the CDMO is the "building contractor". The developer answers to the owner (FDA), but if the contractor cuts corners, the project will still be ordered to stop.

CDMO Scenario: A Client Requests a Phase 1 Batch. How Should the CDMO Respond?

Scenario: A biotech company (the Sponsor) asks a CDMO to manufacture a small-molecule drug for its Phase 1 clinical trial. The client holds an effective IND (IND number XXXXX).

Items the CDMO should confirm immediately:

  1. Obtain a copy of the IND CMC section: confirm that the manufacturing method, specifications and analytical procedures are consistent with the IND description
  2. Quality Agreement: clearly define the deviation reporting timeframe (usually notification of the Sponsor within 24 hours), the obligation to give advance notice of process changes, and the mechanism for notification during FDA inspections
  3. Manufacturing record design: ensure the batch records are sufficient to support an FDA inspection and correspond to the process steps described in the IND
  4. Phase-appropriate GMP gap analysis: assess whether the existing GMP system is adequate to support Phase 1 requirements (usually it is, but key areas such as aseptic processing and raw material control need to be confirmed)
  5. Release procedure: confirm that the batch release criteria are consistent with the IND specifications; the CDMO is usually responsible for manufacturing release, while the Sponsor is responsible for IND batch disposition

Exercise: Judging Deviation Scenarios

A CDMO finds the following situations while manufacturing Phase 2 clinical batches. Decide the handling priority for each:

  1. In the aseptic filling environmental monitoring (EM), one contact plate exceeds the alert limit but is below the action limit.
    → Record it immediately per the deviation SOP; notify the Sponsor to assess whether it affects IND reporting
  2. For a batch that has already been released and shipped to the clinical site, it is later discovered that the expiry date on the label is wrong (3 months too short).
    → Highly urgent: a product recall may be needed, and the Sponsor must immediately assess its FDA reporting obligations
  3. The CDMO plans to replace the filling line equipment, changing from a Type A filler to a Type B filler, but has not yet updated the batch record template.
    → The equipment change must be notified to the Sponsor in advance; the Sponsor assesses whether an IND CMC amendment (prior approval supplement or annual report) must be submitted

Key Point: Impact of a Clinical Hold on the CDMO

An FDA clinical hold is the most severe action in IND oversight: FDA may order the suspension or delay of a planned or ongoing clinical trial.

The practical impact on a CDMO:

  • Batches in manufacture may be put on hold for release
  • Batches already manufactured may not be shipped to clinical sites
  • The CDMO may need to help the Sponsor prepare the clinical hold response requested by FDA (usually with a 30-day deadline)
  • The cost of delay to the whole clinical program can reach millions of dollars per week

This is why a CDMO's GMP compliance and rapid deviation reporting mechanism are critical to the overall timeline of the drug development program.

Section Summary

  • The nature of an IND: Established under FD&C Act §505(i), it is a "notification request" to begin human trials, not an "approval document"; it goes into effect automatically 30 days after submission unless FDA imposes a clinical hold
  • Three conditions that trigger an IND: the drug is not approved for marketing + it is used in human trials + it is shipped across state lines; an IND is needed only when all three are met
  • Key exemptions: clinical investigation of a lawfully marketed drug for the same indication and population (§312.2(a) lawful marketing exemption); qualifying BA/BE studies (§312.2(b)(1)); an IND exemption leaves IRB review and informed consent obligations unchanged
  • Phase progression: Phase 1 (20-80 subjects, safety, phase-appropriate GMP) → Phase 2 (100-300 subjects, exploratory efficacy) → Phase 3 (300-3000+ subjects, full commercial CGMP, process validation); CMC requirements step up with each phase
  • Core obligations of a CDMO: manufacture according to the IND CMC specifications → report deviations to the Sponsor promptly → give advance notice of process changes → maintain complete, inspection-ready manufacturing records → support the Sponsor in meeting all IND reporting obligations

1.1 §312.3 - Key Definitions

Regulatory Text & Definitions

§312.3 — Definitions and interpretations
The following definitions apply to this part:

Sponsor — A person who takes responsibility for and initiates a clinical investigation. The sponsor may be an individual or pharmaceutical company, governmental agency, academic institution, private organization, or other organization. The sponsor does not actually conduct the investigation unless the sponsor is also an investigator.

Investigator — An individual who actually conducts a clinical investigation (i.e., under whose immediate direction the drug is administered or dispensed to a subject). In the event an investigation is conducted by a team of individuals, the investigator is the responsible leader of the team.

Sponsor-Investigator — An individual who both initiates and conducts an investigation, and under whose immediate direction the investigational drug is administered or dispensed. The term does not include any person other than an individual. The requirements applicable to a sponsor-investigator under this part include both those applicable to an investigator and a sponsor.

Clinical investigation — Any experiment in which a drug is administered or dispensed to, or used involving, one or more human subjects. For the purposes of this part, the term "clinical investigation" means any such use of a drug except for the use of a marketed drug in the course of medical practice.

Phase of investigation — The FDA recognizes three phases (Phase 1, 2, 3) of clinical investigation of a drug in humans, as described in §312.21. Phases are defined by purpose, not by sequence; some drugs may proceed from Phase 1 directly to Phase 3.

IND safety report — An expedited report, submitted to FDA and all participating investigators, of an unexpected serious adverse drug reaction or findings from other studies that suggest a significant risk to human subjects.

Protocol — A document that describes the objectives, design, methodology, statistical considerations, and organization of a clinical trial. The protocol also typically describes the conditions under which the trial is to be performed and managed.

Reasonable basis to conclude — The evidentiary standard applied when determining whether a drug is exempt from IND requirements. Sponsors must have a reasonable basis (supported by published literature or prior experience) to conclude the drug meets applicable exemption criteria.

Commentary

Core Concept: Division of Responsibility Between Sponsor and Investigator

The Sponsor is the IND holder and bears ultimate regulatory responsibility to FDA, including: submitting and maintaining the IND, expedited reporting of serious adverse events (15-day reports), monitoring the overall safety of the clinical trial, and ensuring that all manufacturers (including CDMOs) comply with GMP.

The Investigator is the physician or scientist who actually conducts the study at the site. The Investigator is responsible for subject safety and adherence to the protocol and must report adverse events to the Sponsor, who compiles them and reports to FDA.

The key division of labor: the Sponsor holds the IND and answers to FDA; the Investigator conducts the study and answers for the subjects. The chain of responsibility between the two must be clear and documented throughout drug development.

The Special Position of the Sponsor-Investigator

This role is common among academic investigator-physicians, for example a university hospital professor who both submits the IND and personally conducts the clinical trial. This person must fulfill both Sponsor obligations (IND maintenance, safety reporting, CMC responsibility) and Investigator obligations (subject informed consent, protocol adherence).

Note: §312.3 explicitly states that a Sponsor-Investigator can only be an "individual"; a corporation or other legal entity cannot take this role.

Regulatory Significance of the Protocol

The protocol is more than a scientific document: it is a core component of the IND application (§312.23(a)(6)). FDA reviews the protocol to confirm that the study design is sound, the subject selection criteria are appropriate, the dose-escalation plan is safe and the endpoints are measurable. Any significant change to the protocol (protocol amendment) must be submitted to FDA in advance.

Timelines for IND Safety Reports

Under §312.32:

  • 15 calendar days: unexpected serious adverse reaction
  • 7 calendar days: unexpected life-threatening serious adverse reaction; it must first be reported orally or by telephone, followed by a written report within 15 days
  • Annual report: an IND must submit a progress report every year (§312.33)
1.2 IND Types - Commercial vs. Research

IND Type Classification

FDA recognizes several IND types, each serving a distinct regulatory and commercial purpose. The type of IND determines the level of CMC documentation expected, the long-term intent of the investigation, and the scope of FDA oversight.

Treatment IND criteria (§312.34): Drug must be intended to treat a serious or immediately life-threatening disease; no comparable or satisfactory alternative therapy exists; drug is under investigation in a controlled trial, or all clinical trials are complete.

Emergency Use IND (§312.310): Intended for immediate use in a specific patient where there is no time to submit a full IND. FDA may authorize use by telephone; sponsor must then submit a written IND within 15 days.

Commentary

Practical Logic for Choosing Among the Four IND Types

Commercial IND is the mainstream path for pharmaceutical companies: complete development documentation is built up from Phase 1, with the goal of obtaining NDA/BLA marketing approval. The IND is updated at each clinical phase, and CMC requirements rise step by step with the phase.

Research IND (non-commercial): commonly used by academic researchers, for example a PI at a university hospital testing a new indication for an existing drug, or conducting an exploratory mechanistic study. FDA's CMC requirements for this type of IND are relatively relaxed, but the safety requirements are exactly the same.

Treatment IND: for drugs that show a signal of efficacy but are not yet approved. For example, if a new drug for a rare cancer shows significant benefit in Phase 2, FDA may allow seriously ill patients with no other options to use it early while the NDA is under review.

Emergency Use IND: the fastest path; FDA can grant authorization by telephone. A typical scenario: a patient is infected with a rare pathogen, the only effective drug is still in development, and the treating physician calls FDA to request emergency authorization.

Analogy: Types of Driver's License

The four IND types are like different classes of driver's license:

  • Commercial IND = a commercial bus license: full training and strict testing, aimed at carrying passengers commercially (marketing and sale)
  • Research IND = an ordinary passenger car license: for own-use research, not commercialized, with lighter requirements
  • Treatment IND = a special permit: allows practice under specific conditions before the formal license is issued
  • Emergency Use IND = an emergency exemption: in an on-the-spot emergency, the police may temporarily authorize an unlicensed person to drive for a rescue, with the paperwork completed afterwards

Important: How CDMOs Relate to IND Types

Whichever IND type applies, an outsourced manufacturer (CDMO) has no direct regulatory obligations under the Sponsor's IND. The CDMO's responsibility is to manufacture the drug according to the Sponsor's specifications and GMP regulations. But the Sponsor is obliged to ensure its CDMO complies with GMP: if FDA finds GMP problems at the CDMO during an inspection, FDA can place a clinical hold on the IND.

1.3 §312.10 - Waivers

§312.10 — Waivers

(a) General. A sponsor may request FDA to waive any requirement of this part. A waiver request may be submitted either in an IND or in a separate document.

Grounds for waiver — sponsor must demonstrate one of the following:

  1. The alternative proposed satisfies the purpose of the requirement being waived, or
  2. The waiver is in the interest of the public health.

(b) Procedure. A waiver request is required to contain at minimum:

  • An identification of the specific requirement that is subject of the waiver request
  • A reference to the applicable part of the regulations
  • A statement of why the waiver is sought
  • Documentation or information supporting the waiver

FDA action: FDA may grant a waiver if the waiver request demonstrates adequate grounds. A waiver may be conditional and may require compensatory measures. FDA's grant of waiver must be documented in the IND file.

Practical examples of waiver requests:

  • Waiver of a specific stability time point when sufficient data from accelerated studies are available
  • Waiver of the annual IND report for a phase-completed study where a new IND submission would be filed
  • Waiver of specific CMC requirements when an orphan drug is manufactured in extremely small quantities

Commentary

Strategic Use of Waiver Requests

The §312.10 waiver mechanism is an important expression of FDA's regulatory flexibility. When a standard requirement is inapplicable or unreasonable in a particular situation, the Sponsor can request a waiver proactively, rather than having to justify itself after a violation.

Two statutory grounds for a waiver:

  • An alternative can achieve the same purpose: for example, if a requirement exists to ensure drug stability and the Sponsor can provide more complete accelerated stability data, FDA may accept this alternative and waive specific testing time-point requirements.
  • Consistent with the public health interest: for example, for an orphan drug for an extremely rare disease, requiring large-scale process validation is neither scientifically nor practically feasible, and FDA may grant a waiver on public health grounds.

Practical Points for Waiver Requests

Request proactively, not defend afterwards: a waiver must be requested and granted before the requirement is breached. FDA does not accept the logic of "I have already implemented an alternative that does not meet the requirement; please grant a waiver retroactively".

Document completeness: once a waiver is granted, the related documents must be kept in the IND file. During an FDA inspection or review, the investigator may ask to see the original waiver request and FDA's approval letter.

Conditional waivers: waivers FDA grants are often conditional, for example "the waiver of a certain stability time point is granted, but additional data must be provided before Phase 2 begins". The conditions must be fully implemented.

1.4 §§312.20–312.22 - Submitting an IND & Phase Definitions

§312.20 — Requirement for an IND

A sponsor shall submit an IND to FDA if the sponsor intends to conduct a clinical investigation with an investigational new drug. A sponsor need not submit a separate IND for each clinical investigation, as long as the investigations are described within the same IND.

Who may submit: Any person — including pharmaceutical companies, academic institutions, individual physicians, and government agencies — who intends to conduct a clinical investigation may submit an IND. The IND becomes effective 30 days after FDA receives it, unless FDA notifies the sponsor that the investigation is subject to a clinical hold.

§312.21 — Phases of an Investigation

(a) Phase 1. Phase 1 includes the initial introduction of an investigational new drug into humans. Phase 1 studies are typically closely monitored and may be conducted in patients or normal volunteer subjects. These studies are designed to determine the metabolism and pharmacologic actions of the drug in humans, the side effects associated with increasing doses, and, if possible, to gain early evidence on effectiveness. During Phase 1, sufficient information about the drug's pharmacokinetics and pharmacological effects should be obtained to permit the design of well-controlled, scientifically valid Phase 2 studies. The total number of subjects and patients included in Phase 1 studies varies with the drug, but is generally in the range of 20 to 80.

(b) Phase 2. Phase 2 includes the controlled clinical studies conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the drug for a particular indication or indications in patients with the disease or condition under study and to determine the common short-term side effects and risks associated with the drug. Phase 2 studies are typically well controlled, closely monitored, and conducted in a relatively small number of patients, usually involving no more than several hundred patients.

(c) Phase 3. Phase 3 studies are expanded controlled and uncontrolled trials. They are performed after preliminary evidence suggesting effectiveness of the drug has been obtained, and are intended to gather the additional information about effectiveness and safety that is needed to evaluate the overall benefit-risk relationship of the drug and to provide an adequate basis for physician labeling. Phase 3 studies usually include from several hundred to several thousand subjects.

§312.22 — General Principles of the IND Submission

FDA's primary objectives in reviewing an IND are, in all phases of the investigation, to assure the safety and rights of subjects, and, in Phase 2 and 3, to help assure that the quality of the scientific evaluation of drugs is adequate to permit an evaluation of the drug's effectiveness and safety. Therefore, although FDA will review the IND to determine whether the proposed studies represent an undue risk to subjects, FDA will not, except in extraordinary circumstances, impose requirements for specific kinds of investigations or clinical protocols beyond those necessary to assure the safety of the subjects.

Key principle: For Phase 1, the sponsor must provide evidence that the drug is reasonably safe for the proposed investigation. The amount of information necessary to make this determination depends on the novelty of the drug, the extent to which it has been studied previously, the known or suspected risks, and the developmental phase of the drug.

Commentary

The 30-Day "Silent Period" Mechanism of the IND

The IND effectiveness mechanism in §312.20 is "automatically effective 30 days after submission", unless FDA issues a clinical hold during that period. This means:

  • FDA does not need to approve the IND explicitly: silence means consent
  • If FDA has concerns, it must take action within the 30 days
  • In practice, FDA often communicates review comments by letter or telephone within the 30 days, and even when it does not impose a clinical hold, it will still raise requests

The mechanism is designed to prevent development delays caused by FDA review backlogs, while retaining a safety safeguard.

Analogy: The Three Clinical Phases Are Like Building a House

The logic of the three clinical phases is much like the three inspection stages of building a house:

  • Phase 1 = Foundation safety check: before the builder starts the walls, a structural engineer first confirms that the foundation can bear the load safely and has no risk of subsidence. The question at this stage is: "Can this foundation support the building safely?" In drug development terms: first confirm the drug's safety in humans and find the maximum tolerated dose before development continues.
  • Phase 2 = Structural review: once the foundation is confirmed, the structure is built, and the question is: "Is this design effective for the intended use of the building?" In drug development terms: verify the efficacy signal in patients with the disease and confirm that the dose range is reasonable.
  • Phase 3 = Review before the occupancy certificate is issued: once the house is fully built, the various authorities carry out a comprehensive inspection (fire, electrical, structural, environmental) to confirm it is safe to hand over for use. In drug development terms: large, multicenter trials confirm the overall benefit/risk and provide sufficient evidence for the NDA/BLA.

Phase 1 Flexibility: Patients vs. Healthy Subjects

§312.21(a) allows Phase 1 to be conducted in "patients or normal volunteer subjects". This choice is not arbitrary:

  • Oncology drugs: Phase 1 is usually conducted in cancer patients, because the toxicity is greater and it is ethically unacceptable to give them to healthy subjects
  • Anti-infectives: Phase 1 PK studies can be conducted in healthy subjects, with Phase 2 then conducted in patients
  • Biologics/gene therapies: these usually start in patients, because disease-specific mechanisms cannot be evaluated in normal individuals

The Core Philosophy of §312.22: Safety First, Without Over-Controlling the Design

FDA's position is that Phase 1 only requires "reasonably safe for proposed investigation". This is a risk-based minimum threshold; it does not require Phase 1 to reach commercial-grade scientific rigor.

This reflects the core logic of US regulation: do not obstruct innovation, but ensure basic safety. FDA will not require Phase 1 to be a perfectly designed randomized controlled trial, but it will ensure that subjects are not exposed to unreasonable risks.

1.5 Phase-Appropriate GMP for CDMOs

Phase-Appropriate CGMP Framework

FDA's 2008 guidance document "CGMP for Phase 1 Investigational Drugs" established the foundational framework for phase-appropriate manufacturing standards. This guidance clarified that full commercial CGMP compliance is not required for Phase 1, but certain minimum CGMP elements are always mandatory regardless of phase.

FDA 2008 Guidance — Key Points for Phase 1:

  • Full identity, strength, purity, and quality testing is required for Phase 1 drug substances and drug products
  • Written procedures (SOPs) are required; formal validation is not required for Phase 1
  • All personnel must be qualified; training records must be maintained
  • Equipment must be suitable and qualified for its intended use; full IQ/OQ/PQ is not required
  • Batch records with full traceability must be maintained
  • Sterility testing and relevant safety tests (e.g., endotoxin, particulates for injectables) are always required
  • Process validation is not required for Phase 1, but process development data must support GMP manufacture

Note on Phase 1 sterile injectables: Even with relaxed GMP requirements, sterile injectables require full sterility testing, endotoxin/pyrogen testing, container closure integrity testing, and particulate matter testing. These cannot be waived regardless of phase.

Commentary

The Core Contribution of the FDA 2008 Phase 1 CGMP Guidance

Before the 2008 guidance was issued, there was a great deal of uncertainty in the industry about Phase 1 GMP requirements: some believed Phase 1 needed almost no GMP, while others believed it had to fully meet commercial-grade GMP. FDA's 2008 guidance clarified the concept of "phase-appropriate CGMP":

  • The non-waivable baseline: testing of quality attributes (identity/strength/purity/quality) + safety testing (sterility, endotoxin) + batch record traceability
  • The parts that can be handled flexibly: process validation, formal equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ), the full set of stability studies
  • Core logic: the main risk in Phase 1 is "giving subjects an unsafe drug", not "insufficient manufacturing efficiency", so GMP focuses on safeguarding safety attributes.

Important: The Sponsor Holds the IND, No Matter How Many CDMOs Are Involved

No matter how many CDMOs or partner organizations an IND involves, the IND is always held by the Sponsor, and the Sponsor bears ultimate responsibility to FDA.

This means:

  • The Sponsor must audit and confirm the GMP status of every CDMO
  • If CDMO A handles the drug substance, CDMO B the drug product and CDMO C the aseptic filling, all three must meet the GMP requirements for their respective phase
  • FDA can inspect any one of the CDMOs, and if it finds serious GMP deficiencies it can place a clinical hold on the Sponsor's IND, even if the Sponsor itself has no problems
  • The Sponsor must record all manufacturing sites in the IND (§312.23(a)(7))

Application: A CDMO Receives an Order for a Phase 1 Sterile Injectable

Scenario: A CDMO receives an order from a biotech company to manufacture a sterile injectable (lyophilized vial) for a Phase 1 FIH (First-in-Human) clinical trial. The Sponsor has provided the full IND number and the manufacturing specifications.

Minimum GMP documentation required of the CDMO:

  • Batch manufacturing record (BMR): a complete batch record covering every manufacturing step, equipment ID, environmental monitoring data, operator signatures and deviation records
  • Quality Agreement: there must be a written quality agreement between the Sponsor and the CDMO that clearly defines the division of responsibilities
  • Release test reports: sterility, endotoxin (LAL), visible particulates (visual inspection), particulate matter (per USP <788>), potency/assay, appearance, reconstitution time, pH, moisture content (Karl Fischer)
  • Lyophilization cycle records: temperature/pressure records compared against the validated or qualified lyophilization profile
  • Container closure integrity (CCI): CCI test or validation data, according to the process design
  • Audit trail: CAPA records for all deviations; records of rejected batch handling

Not required at the Phase 1 stage: process validation (PPQ), full three-batch validation, and the full set of equipment IQ/OQ/PQ (equipment qualification documents are sufficient)

Section Summary

Key Regulatory Framework Points

  • §312.3 Definitions: Sponsor holds the IND and bears ultimate FDA responsibility; Investigator conducts the trial; Sponsor-Investigator combines both roles in a single individual (must be an individual, not a company).
  • IND Types: Four types — Commercial (NDA/BLA intent), Research/Non-commercial (academic, no NDA intent), Treatment (pre-approval access for seriously ill), Emergency Use (single-patient crisis). Each type carries different CMC depth expectations.
  • §312.10 Waivers: Any IND requirement may be waived by written request to FDA. Grounds: alternative satisfies requirement's purpose, or waiver is in public health interest. Must be pre-approved and documented in IND file.
  • §312.20 IND Submission: IND becomes effective 30 days after FDA receipt unless a Clinical Hold is issued. FDA does not "approve" an IND — silence is acquiescence.
  • §312.21 Phase Definitions: Phase 1 (20–80 subjects, safety/PK, patients or healthy volunteers); Phase 2 (hundreds, controlled, effectiveness + safety); Phase 3 (hundreds to thousands, expanded, NDA/BLA basis).
  • §312.22 General Principles: Phase 1 requires reasonable safety evidence; FDA will not impose unnecessary design requirements beyond subject safety protection.
  • Phase-Appropriate CGMP: FDA 2008 guidance establishes that full commercial CGMP is not required for Phase 1, but critical safety testing (sterility, endotoxin, identity, potency) and batch record traceability are always mandatory.

Key Takeaways

The Three Most Important Practical Points for CDMOs

  1. The IND belongs to the Sponsor, not the CDMO: the CDMO's responsibility is to manufacture to specification and comply with GMP, but regulatory ownership of the IND and responsibility to FDA always rest with the Sponsor. The Sponsor must list all manufacturing sites in the CMC section of the IND.
  2. Phase 1 does not waive safety testing: even under phase-appropriate CGMP, sterility, endotoxin and particulate testing of a sterile injectable cannot be omitted, whatever the clinical phase.
  3. GMP requirements escalate with the phase: the CDMO should specify the GMP requirements for each clinical phase in the contract and quality agreement, and ensure that process validation and scale-up work are started early for the Phase 2 → Phase 3 transition.

Common Inspection Observations (FDA Warning Letter Patterns)

  • The CDMO did not promptly report process deviations to the Sponsor, leaving gaps in the Sponsor's IND safety reports
  • Phase 1 batch records were incomplete, lacking environmental monitoring data or operator signatures
  • The Sponsor did not conduct regular GMP audits of the CDMO and was cited by FDA for "inadequate supplier oversight"
  • After scale-up from Phase 2 to Phase 3, the impact of the process on quality attributes was not re-evaluated and Phase 2 data were cited directly

2.1 Overall IND Format - §312.23(a)

The 10 Elements of a Complete IND Application

§312.23(a) specifies that an IND application shall contain the following elements, submitted as separate sections:

  1. (a)(1) Cover sheet — FDA Form 1571, identifying the sponsor, drug substance, and phase of investigation.
  2. (a)(2) Table of contents — Index of all submitted sections.
  3. (a)(3) Introductory statement and general investigational plan — Brief narrative description of the drug, development rationale, and the scope of the planned clinical investigation.
  4. (a)(4) Investigator's brochure (IB) — Summary of all prior human and animal data; updated as new information becomes available.
  5. (a)(5) Clinical protocol(s) — Detailed study design, dosing regimen, inclusion/exclusion criteria, safety monitoring plan.
  6. (a)(6) Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) — Element 7 in the CFR numbering; the primary CDMO deliverable. Full description of the drug substance and drug product manufacturing, specifications, controls, and stability.
  7. (a)(7) Pharmacology and toxicology information — Nonclinical pharmacodynamics, ADME, toxicology data supporting safe first-in-human exposure.
  8. (a)(8) Previous human experience — Summary of prior clinical experience, if any (foreign or domestic).
  9. (a)(9) Additional information — Special considerations: drug dependence potential, radioactive drugs, pediatric studies.
  10. (a)(10) Relevant information — Any other information that FDA requests or sponsor considers pertinent.
§312.23(a) — "An original IND application is required to contain the information set forth in paragraphs (a)(1) through (a)(10) of this section."

FDA reviewers in the Division of Pharmaceutical Review (or equivalent) focus primary scientific scrutiny on Element 6 (CMC) and Element 7 (Pharmacology/Tox) when evaluating whether a 30-day clinical hold should be issued.

Commentary

Core Concept: The CMC Section Is the CDMO's Main Arena

Of the 10 IND elements, the vast majority are the responsibility of the Sponsor's clinical, pharmacology and toxicology teams. The CMC section (§312.23(a)(7), the 6th content element) is the CDMO's core contribution to the IND and directly determines:

  • Whether FDA has concerns about manufacturing safety and issues a Clinical Hold (a 30-day stop order)
  • Whether investigational drug batches can meet release criteria for clinical use
  • Whether a manufacturing change requires advance notification to FDA

A CDMO usually provides its information in the form of a CMC Disclosure Package, which the Sponsor integrates into the IND application. The Sponsor bears ultimate responsibility to FDA for the accuracy and completeness of the CDMO's data, but the CDMO's actual manufacturing practices (documents, records, processes) face FDA inspection directly.

A Reminder on IND Numbering Confusion

The text of §312.23(a) labels CMC as the 7th sub-item (a)(7), but in the customary ordering of an IND application it is usually called the "6th element" (because the Cover Sheet and TOC are administrative formats and are sometimes not counted among the substantive scientific content). In an exam or review context, knowing the CFR citation §312.23(a)(7) is what matters most.

The 30-Day Clinical Hold and CMC

After receiving an IND application, FDA has a 30-day review period. If the data in the CMC section are insufficient, FDA may issue a Clinical Hold under §312.42, prohibiting the clinical trial from starting. Common CMC triggers include:

  • An unclear description of the sterility assurance method
  • Impurities above limits or lacking toxicological evaluation
  • Stability data insufficient to support the use period during the trial
  • Missing container compatibility data (especially for protein drugs)
2.2 Drug Substance CMC Requirements - §312.23(a)(7)(i)

§312.23(a)(7)(i) — Drug Substance Information

The CMC section for the drug substance (active pharmaceutical ingredient, API) must include the following seven elements:

1. Description
A description of the drug substance including its physical, chemical, or biological characteristics; structural formula where applicable; and the source of the substance. For biologics, describe the cell line, expression system, or fermentation organism.

2. Manufacturer
The name, address, and U.S. Drug Establishment Registration number (DEA registration, if applicable) of the drug substance manufacturer. If multiple manufacturers or suppliers are used, all must be listed.

3. Method of Preparation
A description of the synthesis, fermentation, extraction, isolation, or purification procedure with sufficient detail to assess the consistency and reproducibility of the process. Key intermediate steps, reagents, solvents, and catalysts should be identified. The level of detail scales with clinical phase.

4. Analytical Controls
A list of the tests performed, reference to analytical methods, and acceptance criteria for: identity, purity, potency (where applicable), and assay. Methods must be described or referenced to pharmacopeial methods. Residual solvents (ICH Q3C), elemental impurities (ICH Q3D), genotoxic impurities (ICH M7), and process-related impurities should be addressed.

5. Reference Standards
A description of the reference standard used in testing. For Phase 1, an in-house primary reference standard with characterization data is acceptable. By Phase 3, the reference standard should be fully characterized and traceable to a compendial or primary standard.

6. Container/Closure System
A description of the container/closure system used for bulk drug substance storage (e.g., HDPE drum, glass vial, stainless steel container). Compatibility of the container with the drug substance should be addressed, particularly for light-sensitive or moisture-sensitive materials.

7. Stability
Stability data sufficient to support the use of the drug substance during the planned clinical trial period. This should include accelerated stability data and real-time data to the extent available. An initial proposed shelf life or re-test date must be stated. The stability protocol should specify storage conditions, test intervals, and testing parameters.

Commentary

Core Concept: The Boundary Between Drug Substance and Drug Product

The Drug Substance (API) is the active ingredient of the drug itself, not yet made into the final dosage form. For example:

  • Small-molecule drugs: the chemical entity after synthesis and purification (bulk API)
  • Protein drugs: purified Bulk Drug Substance (BDS), stored in -80°C freezer bags
  • mRNA drugs: the mRNA solution after preparation (before LNP encapsulation)

The Drug Product is the final dosage form used clinically, after excipients are added and the product has been through processing (aseptic filling, lyophilization, etc.). The CMC submissions for the two fall under different subsections and correspond to different manufacturing sites and process control requirements.

Analogy: Drug Substance CMC Is Like an Ingredient Specification Sheet

Drug substance CMC is like the "ingredient acceptance specification" a restaurant requires of its ingredient suppliers: it must state the source (which farm/synthesis plant), the method of preparation (organic farming/synthetic route), the quality indicators (pesticide residues/impurity limits) and the storage method (cold chain/moisture-proof container). In Phase 1 the specification can be rough (the ingredient is still on trial); in Phase 3 every item must be backed by sufficient data, like a commercial supply contract.

Common FDA 483 Trigger: Impurity Control

Control of drug impurities is a high-risk area in CMC review. A CDMO should pay particular attention to:

  • Genotoxic impurities (ICH M7): a toxicity assessment using DEREK/Sarah calculations is needed; in Phase 1 the TTC (Threshold of Toxicological Concern) approach can be used to set limits
  • Residual solvents (ICH Q3C): all solvents used in the synthesis steps must be listed and compared against the Class 1/2/3 limits
  • Elemental impurities (ICH Q3D): a risk assessment report is needed, explaining which elements may be introduced (catalyst residues, equipment leaching)

Practical Advice on Reference Standards

In Phase 1, a CDMO usually uses one fully characterized API batch as the Working Reference Standard, and must provide identification (NMR, IR, MS), purity (HPLC), assay and appearance data. The standard should have a COA and be formally approved by QA. Before Phase 3, consider whether a Primary Reference Standard needs to be established and placed in the long-term stability program.

2.3 Drug Product CMC Requirements - §312.23(a)(7)(ii)

§312.23(a)(7)(ii) — Drug Product Information

The CMC section for the drug product (finished dosage form) must address the following elements:

Composition
A quantitative listing of all components in the formulation, including the drug substance and all excipients. Each excipient must be listed with its grade (e.g., USP, NF, Ph.Eur.), quantity per unit dose, and function (e.g., tonicity agent, pH adjuster, cryoprotectant, surfactant, preservative). The function of each excipient must be justified from a formulation science standpoint.

Manufacturer
Name and address of the drug product manufacturer (fill/finish site), and the packager/labeler if different. All contract manufacturing sites, including testing laboratories, should be identified.

Method of Manufacture
For sterile injectables, the manufacturing description must specify:

  • Sterilization method: terminal sterilization (preferred per FDA/EMA guidance) or aseptic processing; rationale for method selection
  • Process overview: compounding, filtration (0.22 µm membrane filter, pre-filter), filling, and lyophilization (if applicable)
  • Sterilizing filtration: filter validation (extractables, integrity test, bacterial challenge)
  • Critical process parameters (CPPs): bioburden at each step, hold times, temperature controls

Controls
Full release specifications with acceptance criteria for all tests performed on the finished drug product. For sterile injectables, this must include:

  • Appearance, pH, osmolality/tonicity
  • Assay (potency/concentration), related substances (degradation products)
  • Sterility (USP <71>)
  • Endotoxin (LAL test, USP <85>)
  • Particulate matter (USP <788> for large-volume parenterals; USP <789> for small-volume parenterals)
  • Container closure integrity (CCI)
  • Protein-specific: sub-visible particles (MFI or RMM), aggregation (SEC-HPLC), potency assay

Container/Closure System
Description of the primary packaging components (e.g., Type I glass vial, bromobutyl stopper, aluminum crimp seal) including supplier, grade, and compatibility data. Reference to USP <661> and USP <1660> for glass containers; stopper extractables data should be available by Phase 2–3.

Sterility-Specific Additional Requirements

  • Media fills: Results of most recent Aseptic Process Simulation (APS); 0 contaminated units expected per FDA guidance (≤3 per 10,000 units per EU Annex 1)
  • Environmental monitoring (EM): Classification of fill/finish area (ISO Class 5 critical zone); viable and non-viable EM results during clinical batch manufacture
  • Endotoxin (LAL): Method validation for product-specific inhibition/enhancement; limit calculation
  • Particulate matter: Light obscuration (HIAC) and microscopic methods per USP <788>/<789>
  • Container closure integrity (CCI): Description of method (vacuum decay, laser headspace, dye ingress) and acceptance criteria

Commentary

Core Concept: The Two-Tier Control Logic of Drug Product CMC

Drug product CMC covers two levels of control:

  • In-Process Controls (IPC): fill weight, pH confirmation and solution clarity, monitored in real time during manufacture to keep problem product from reaching final release
  • Release Specifications: the full set of tests performed before batch release, with results presented on the COA and the release signed off by the QP/QA

FDA requires both levels of control to be described in the CMC section. In Phase 1 the IPC can be simpler, but the tests related to sterility assurance (sterility test, endotoxin, particulates) must be performed in full from the start; there is no "phase-appropriate waiver" for them.

Regulatory Choice: Terminal Sterilization vs. Aseptic Processing

FDA's position (1987 Guidance + 2004 Aseptic Processing Guidance): terminal sterilization is preferred, because its sterility assurance level (SAL = 10⁻⁶) is higher than that of aseptic processing. Aseptic processing is used only if the drug cannot tolerate terminal sterilization (heat sensitive, radiation sensitive). The CMC section must state clearly which method is used and provide a justification.

FDA 483 High Risk: Media Fill Data

During an FDA inspection, Media Fill (simulated filling with growth medium) results are among the most frequently requested documents. The CMC section should include a summary of the most recent Media Fill (date, batch size, results, EM results). If the CDMO's Media Fill records are inconsistent with the facility described in the IND, this may trigger a Data Integrity citation or a Warning Letter.

The Difference Between USP <788> and <789>

Both are particulate tests, but they apply to different scopes:

  • USP <788> Particulate Matter in Injections: applies to large-volume injections (LVP, ≥100 mL) and non-intravenous injectable products; the limits are looser
  • USP <789> Particulate Matter in Ophthalmic Solutions: applies to ophthalmic injectables; the limits are stricter
  • Small-volume injections (SVP, <100 mL) are subject to the SVP limit criteria in USP <788>

Protein formulations additionally need protein aggregates evaluated under USP <787> (subvisible particles) or by MFI (micro-flow imaging).

2.4 Phase-Appropriate CMC Depth

Phase-Appropriate CMC Requirements

FDA's "Guidance for Industry: INDs for Phase 2 and Phase 3 Studies — Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Information" (2003) and related guidance documents establish the principle that CMC requirements should be commensurate with the phase of investigation and associated patient risk. The key regulatory basis is §312.23(a)(7)(iv):

§312.23(a)(7)(iv) — "Although in each phase of the investigation sufficient information is required to assure the proper identification, quality, purity, and strength of the investigational drug, the amount of information needed to make that assurance will vary with the phase of the investigation, the proposed duration of the investigation, the dosage form, and the amount of information otherwise available."

Commentary

Analogy: CMC Evolves from a Home Recipe to a Factory Formula

Phase 1 = a rough home recipe: you know the main ingredients and the general steps, and it is enough to make one dish (a safe drug). You do not need grams measured to the decimal point or an ISO-certified kitchen.

Phase 2 = a standardized small-restaurant recipe: the taste must be consistent every time, and you begin recording time/temperature/proportions. With more customers, quality control becomes important.

Phase 3 = an industrialized standard operating procedure (SOP): every step has validation data, and even if the cook changes, the product comes out exactly the same. This is the outpost before the NDA/BLA application: FDA will come to "inspect the kitchen" (PAI).

Key point: Phase-appropriate does not mean Phase 1 may be of poor quality; it means only that certain depths of documentation (validation reports, statistical design) can be matched to the phase. Sterility assurance and patient-safety-related tests must be performed in full from day one.

Formula: CMC Depth = f(Phase × Risk)

CMC Depth = f(Clinical Phase, Patient Population Risk, Dosing Duration, Dose Level)

Phase 1, Single-dose, Healthy volunteers, Low dose
  → Minimum viable CMC: outline synthesis + preliminary specs + accelerated stability

Phase 3, Chronic dosing, Paediatric patients, High dose
  → Full commercial CMC: validated process + full ICH specs + complete stability program

The higher the risk (chronic dosing, vulnerable populations, high doses), the closer the required depth of CMC documentation comes to the level of a commercial application. When assessing the adequacy of CMC, FDA reviewers also consider the risk design of the clinical protocol (the Phase × Risk matrix).

CDMO Practical Note: The Gray Area of Phase 1 GMP

FDA's 2008 guidance "CGMP for Phase 1 Investigational Drugs" states clearly that GMP requirements for Phase 1 may be "appropriately adjusted", but the following have no exceptions:

  • Complete batch production records
  • Complete release testing (including sterility, endotoxin and assay)
  • Written batch release authorization by QA
  • OOS (out-of-specification result) investigation procedures
  • An SOP documentation system

What can be simplified in Phase 1: the depth of facility validation, the extent of full validation of analytical methods, and the formality of the annual product review.

2.5 IND Amendments for CMC Changes

Types of IND Amendments

Once an IND is in effect, any changes to the CMC section must be submitted through the appropriate amendment mechanism. The classification of the amendment determines the required timing and whether FDA approval is needed before implementation.

Protocol Amendment — §312.30
Required when a change affects the safety of subjects or represents a significant alteration to the study design. For CMC, this applies when manufacturing changes could affect the drug's safety profile (e.g., change in sterilization method, formulation change affecting tolerability).

  • Timing: Submit before implementation; sponsor must wait 30 days for FDA review (or until FDA notifies sponsor that review is complete)
  • 30-day rule: Clinical investigation may not proceed until 30 days after FDA receipt, unless FDA notifies earlier

Information Amendment — §312.31
Used for CMC changes that do not affect the safety of subjects but represent significant new information about the drug substance or drug product. This is the most common amendment type for routine CMC updates.

  • Examples: Change in drug substance supplier (same API quality), specification tightening, new stability data, method changes
  • Timing: Submit within 30 days of obtaining the information; may implement before FDA review

Major Manufacturing Changes
The following changes require advance FDA review and must be submitted as either a Protocol or Information Amendment before implementation:

  • Change in manufacturing site (drug substance or drug product)
  • Significant change in manufacturing process (e.g., new synthesis route, new sterilization approach)
  • Change in specification that widens acceptance criteria
  • Change in container/closure system

Annual Report — §312.33
Minor CMC changes that do not individually warrant a separate amendment may be accumulated and reported in the annual IND report, submitted within 60 days of the anniversary date of the IND effective date. Includes:

  • Updated stability data (new time points)
  • Minor specification refinements based on accumulated batch data
  • New manufacturing lots manufactured under unchanged process
  • Reference standard updates

Commentary

Core Concept: A Decision Tree for Classifying Changes

For any CMC change, the CDMO and Sponsor should classify it using the following logic:

  1. Does the change affect patient safety? (directly safety-related) → Yes: Protocol Amendment (wait 30 days)
  2. Is the change significant new CMC information, but without a safety impact? → Yes: Information Amendment (submit within 30 days; may be implemented first)
  3. Is the change a minor improvement that does not affect quality or safety? → Yes: include in the Annual Report

When the classification is unclear, consult FDA (a Pre-IND meeting or Type B meeting) to avoid implementing a major change without FDA's concurrence.

Scenario: Reporting Obligations for a CDMO Site Change

Scenario: During the Sponsor's Phase 2 trial, it decides to move drug product filling from CDMO-A (US) to CDMO-B (Singapore).

What is needed?

  • A manufacturing site change is a significant change → submit as an Information Amendment (assuming the process is unchanged and there are no safety concerns)
  • If the process is also adjusted (for example, a change of sterilization method) → a Protocol Amendment may be needed
  • CDMO-B must provide: site information (name, address, FEI number), a facility description, a summary of recent Media Fill records, and EM data
  • CDMO-B must be registered under FDA Drug Establishment Registration

Risk: if manufacturing begins at CDMO-B without submitting an Amendment, the clinical batches are effectively produced at an unapproved site, which can trigger termination of the clinical trial under §312.70.

The Line Between the Annual Report and Immediate Submission

A common mistake among many CDMOs/Sponsors: treating an "Information Amendment that should be submitted immediately" as a minor change that "can wait for the annual report". The principle: any process adjustment that may affect batch-to-batch consistency should not wait until the annual report to be notified to FDA. The prudent approach is to set up an internal CMC Change Control mechanism that assesses the submission type of every change before it is implemented.

2.6 Sterile Injectables: Additional CMC Requirements

Additional CMC Requirements for Sterile Injectables

Sterile injectable drug products carry the highest patient safety risk of any dosage form, as there is no barrier between the drug and systemic circulation. FDA therefore imposes additional CMC data requirements beyond those for oral dosage forms.

Endotoxin (Bacterial) Limit
The endotoxin limit for parenteral drug products is established using the K/M formula:

  • K = Endotoxin threshold = 5 EU/kg/hr for intravenous administration (FDA/USP <85> default)
  • M = Maximum human dose (in mL or mg per kg per hour)
  • Endotoxin Limit (EL) = K ÷ M
  • For intrathecal products: K = 0.2 EU/kg/hr (more restrictive)

The LAL (Limulus Amebocyte Lysate) test must be validated for the specific product matrix to confirm absence of interference. Gel-clot, turbidimetric, and chromogenic methods are all acceptable.

Container Closure Integrity (CCI)
The CMC section must describe the container closure integrity testing method and acceptance criteria. Deterministic methods (vacuum decay, laser headspace analysis, high voltage leak detection) are preferred over probabilistic methods (dye ingress) for risk-based CCI assurance. The CCI program must cover: initial CCI validation, incoming container inspection, and post-process CCI verification.

Aseptic Processing
If aseptic processing is used, the CMC section should include:

  • Media fill (APS) summary results: number of units filled, number contaminated, lot numbers and dates
  • Environmental monitoring (EM) classification: ISO Class 5 for open product exposure; ISO Class 7 background; ISO Class 8 support areas
  • Personnel gowning qualification summary

Extractables and Leachables (E&L)
For Phase 1: preliminary extractables data from container/closure and single-use systems is acceptable; a formal E&L risk assessment should document rationale for not yet having full leachables data.
For Phase 2–3: leachables data from actual drug product stored under stability conditions must be generated. Toxicological risk assessment (per PQRI guidance) must support any detected leachables above the Safety Concern Threshold (SCT = 0.15 µg/day).

Commentary

Formula: Endotoxin Limit Calculation K/M

Endotoxin Limit (EL) = K ÷ M

K = threshold constant (intravenous = 5 EU/kg/hr)
M = maximum dose (maximum dose per kg body weight per hour, in mL/kg/hr or mg/kg/hr)

Worked example:
  Product: protein injection, IV, maximum dose 10 mL/hr, body weight 70 kg
  M = 10 mL ÷ 70 kg ÷ 1 hr ≈ 0.143 mL/kg/hr
  EL = 5 EU/kg/hr ÷ 0.143 mL/kg/hr ≈ 35 EU/mL

If the labeled concentration of the product is 1 mg/mL, the specification is ≤35 EU/mg (or ≤35 EU/mL)

Note: for intrathecal administration K = 0.2 EU/kg/hr, so the limit is stricter

The CDMO must ensure that the LAL test has been subjected to interference testing (inhibition/enhancement test) for the drug matrix, and that the LAL test records for every batch are completely filed.

Compliance Requirements for Aseptic Process Simulation (Media Fill)

Both the FDA 2004 Aseptic Processing Guidance and EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) require:

  • A Media Fill every 6 months for each aseptic filling line
  • Each simulation batch size should represent a normal commercial batch size, with a minimum of 5,000 units (for assessing a low contamination rate)
  • Acceptance criterion: the target is 0 contaminated units; a contamination rate above 0.1% is regarded as an automatic failure and requires a full investigation
  • A Media Fill failure is a high-risk event and may trigger an FDA Warning Letter

In the IND CMC, summary information (not the full report) of at least one recent Media Fill must be provided, including date, scale and results.

E&L: The Minimum Compliance Threshold for Phase 1

Many small biotech companies submit incomplete E&L data in a Phase 1 IND. FDA usually accepts the following as the Phase 1 minimum:

  • A summary of the supplier's Extractables study report for the container/closure (usually provided by the container supplier)
  • A written risk assessment: explaining why the Leachables data are not yet complete at this stage and when completion is planned
  • Confirmation that the materials used meet relevant biocompatibility standards such as USP <661> / USP <87> / ISO 10993

If Leachables data still cannot be provided before Phase 3, FDA may ask for supplementary data at the Pre-NDA meeting, which becomes a bottleneck for the NDA submission.

2.7 FDA Right to Inspect Manufacturing Sites

FDA Inspection Authority for IND Manufacturing Sites

Under §312.68 and the general authority of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act §704), FDA has the right to inspect all facilities involved in the manufacture, processing, packing, and testing of investigational drugs listed in an IND application.

Which sites are subject to inspection?
Any manufacturing site listed in the IND CMC section is potentially subject to FDA inspection, including:

  • Drug substance synthesis or fermentation facilities
  • Drug product fill/finish facilities (CDMOs)
  • Contract testing laboratories (analytical testing, stability)
  • Packaging and labeling sites

CDMO Obligations During an IND

  • Record maintenance: All batch production records, deviation reports, OOS investigations, change control records, and validation documentation must be maintained and accessible for FDA review
  • Allow inspection: Refusal to permit an FDA inspection constitutes grounds for clinical hold (§312.44) or disqualification of the facility
  • Report manufacturing deviations: Critical deviations (particularly those affecting sterility or potency of released clinical batches) must be communicated to the sponsor promptly; the sponsor is obligated to assess whether an IND amendment or safety report is required

Pre-Approval Inspection (PAI)
For Phase 3 sites, FDA may conduct a PAI concurrent with NDA/BLA review. CDMOs used during Phase 3 clinical trials are high priority targets for PAI. A PAI failure can result in a Complete Response Letter (CRL) even if the clinical data are compelling.

Commentary

The Legal Position of a CDMO in the IND System for Inspections

A CDMO is usually not the IND holder (Sponsor), but once it is named in the CMC section it falls under FDA jurisdiction. The specific legal bases are:

  • FD&C Act §704: FDA has the authority to enter and inspect any establishment where drugs are manufactured, processed, packed or held
  • §312.68: FDA may require the Sponsor to provide, or to permit FDA direct access to, IND-related records
  • 21 CFR Part 211 (CGMP): a CDMO must comply with CGMP, whether or not its client (the Sponsor) holds an IND

In practice, FDA inspection findings (Form FDA 483 Observations) and Warning Letters are issued directly to the CDMO, not to the Sponsor. The CDMO bears the legal consequences of its own compliance problems.

Although such a difference seems minor, during an FDA inspection it is a Data Integrity red flag. The CDMO should establish a mechanism to ensure that the manufacturing description in the IND is updated in step with process changes, and that the Sponsor submits the corresponding Amendment.

3.1 §312.30 — Protocol Amendments

Original Text & Regulatory Requirements

§ 312.30 Protocol amendments.

A sponsor shall submit a protocol amendment describing any change in a Phase 1 protocol that significantly affects the safety of subjects. A sponsor shall submit a protocol amendment describing any change in a Phase 2 or 3 protocol that significantly affects the safety of subjects, the scope of the investigation, or the scientific quality of the study. Examples of changes requiring a protocol amendment include:

  • Any increase in drug dosage or duration of exposure of individual research subjects beyond that in the current protocol
  • Any significant increase in the number of research subjects
  • The addition of a new test article
  • The addition of a new clinical investigator or research site
  • A significant change in the study design (e.g., adding or dropping control groups)

New Protocol Amendments

A sponsor who adds a new protocol after the IND goes into effect shall submit the new protocol to FDA as a protocol amendment. The sponsor may begin the clinical investigation on the date the amendment is submitted to FDA unless the sponsor has received a clinical hold or is conducting a Phase 1 investigation, in which case FDA's 30-day review period applies before enrollment begins.

Three Categories of Protocol Amendments

  1. New Protocol Amendment: Adding an entirely new clinical study (e.g., adding Phase 2 study to an existing Phase 1 IND). The new protocol must be submitted before any subjects are enrolled in the new study.
  2. Protocol Change Amendment: Modifying an existing protocol — changing dose, duration of exposure, patient population, endpoints, or study design elements that affect subject safety or scientific quality.
  3. New Investigator Amendment: Adding a new investigator or clinical site to the study. Must include a signed Form FDA 1572 (Statement of Investigator) and curriculum vitae.

When FDA Prior Approval (30-Day Wait) is Required:

  • Any change that represents a significant increase in risk to subjects
  • Adding a new indication to the IND
  • Phase 1 protocols: sponsor must wait 30 days after submitting the protocol amendment before initiating the study, unless FDA notifies that study may proceed earlier

Notify Within 30 Days (Information Amendment):

  • Minor changes that do not significantly affect subject safety, scope, or scientific quality
  • Administrative changes (e.g., sponsor contact information update)
  • Changes already reflected in other amendments submitted concurrently
"A sponsor shall submit a protocol amendment describing any change in a Phase 2 or 3 protocol that significantly affects the safety of subjects, the scope of the investigation, or the scientific quality of the study."
— 21 CFR §312.30(b)

Tutorial Commentary

Three Types of Protocol Amendments

The core question under §312.30 is: "Will this change affect subject safety, the scope of the investigation, or scientific quality?" If so, a protocol amendment must be submitted.

In practice, three questions give a quick screen:

  1. Is it an entirely new study? → New protocol amendment (with the full protocol text + FDA Form 1572)
  2. Is it a change to dose, timing, population or design in an existing protocol? → Change in protocol amendment (with the revised protocol and a description of the change)
  3. Is it only the addition of a new investigator or site? → New investigator amendment (with FDA Form 1572 + CV)

All three fall under "Protocol Amendments"; what differs is the content and the attachments required.

Rule Differences between Phase 1 and Phase 2/3

§312.30 sets different start rules for different study phases:

  • Phase 1: After a new protocol is submitted, the sponsor must wait 30 days (FDA's implied approval period) unless FDA notifies the sponsor earlier that the study may proceed. This is because Phase 1 is the first study in humans, where safety uncertainty is highest.
  • Phase 2/3: The study may begin on the same day the amendment is submitted, unless a clinical hold notice is received or FDA requests that the sponsor wait.

Practical warning: many sponsors wrongly assume that every protocol amendment requires a 30-day wait. In fact, the 30-day waiting period applies only to new Phase 1 protocols and to changes that significantly increase risk.

New Investigator Amendment in CDMO Context

When a multicenter clinical trial adds a study site, the sponsor must submit a new investigator amendment before subjects are enrolled. Each new investigator needs:

  • A signed FDA Form 1572 (Statement of Investigator, the investigator's undertaking)
  • The investigator's CV (to confirm qualifications and relevant experience)
  • Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval documents

CDMO role reminder: a contract manufacturer is not an investigator and does not sign Form 1572. However, a CDMO that manufactures clinical trial material must ensure that its facility information is covered in the CMC section of the IND, and must inform the sponsor of any significant change to the facility or methods.

Quick Classification

Decide which type of protocol amendment applies to each situation below, and whether FDA's clearance must be awaited:

  1. Under a Phase 1 IND, the sponsor wants to add a Phase 2 dose-confirmation study (new indication, same drug)
  2. In an ongoing Phase 3 study, the assessment time for the primary endpoint is extended from 6 months to 9 months (reason: the treatment effect was observed to appear more slowly)
  3. A Phase 2 study adds a clinical site in Australia, led by an investigator with extensive specialty experience

Reference answers are below in the right-hand column

Answer: (1) New protocol amendment; wait 30 days (Phase 1 rule). (2) Change in protocol amendment; may be implemented immediately (Phase 3), but supporting documentation must be submitted. (3) New investigator amendment; no waiting required, but it must be submitted before enrollment.

3.2 §312.31 — Information Amendments

Original Text & Regulatory Requirements

§ 312.31 Information amendments.

A sponsor shall report to FDA, as a matter of routine communication, information relevant to the investigation that is not a protocol amendment, IND safety report, or annual report. Such information shall be submitted in the form of an information amendment whenever:

  • The sponsor acquires new nonclinical data that could affect the safety of subjects
  • The sponsor acquires new information regarding the chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) section
  • There is a change in the responsible medical officer or sponsor contact
  • There are significant changes in the sponsor's organizational structure

Timing: Information amendments shall be submitted within 30 days of the sponsor's acquiring the relevant information. If the information is directly related to an ongoing study's safety, submission shall be made as soon as practicable.

CMC Changes Requiring Information Amendments:

  • Change in manufacturing site for drug substance or drug product
  • Adoption of a new analytical method that materially changes release specifications
  • Change in synthesis route for the API (could affect impurity profile)
  • Change in container/closure system
  • Change in sterilization method or cycle parameters (for sterile products)
  • New stability data indicating shortened shelf life or significant degradation
  • Out-of-specification results for clinical batches already administered to subjects

What is NOT an Information Amendment:

  • Protocol changes (those go as Protocol Amendments under §312.30)
  • Safety events meeting SUSAR criteria (those go as IND Safety Reports under §312.32)
  • Annual summary information (that goes in Annual Report under §312.33)
"A sponsor shall report to FDA, as a matter of routine communication, information relevant to the investigation that is not a protocol amendment, IND safety report, or annual report."
— 21 CFR §312.31(a)

Tutorial Commentary

The Role of the Information Amendment: "The Catch-All for Other Important Information"

§312.31 can be understood as the "general intake slot" of the IND system: any important, newly obtained information that does not fall under a protocol amendment, a safety report or an annual report is submitted to FDA through an information amendment.

In practice, information amendments arise most often in two situations:

  • CMC updates: changes of manufacturer, method or facility; any manufacturing or analytical change that may affect the quality of the clinical trial material requires an information amendment
  • New nonclinical data: new findings from animal studies (toxicity, pharmacology) that may affect the safety assessment of the clinical program

CMC Information Amendments — The Regulatory Obligation Most Directly Relevant to CDMOs

For a contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO), §312.31 is one of the most directly relevant provisions, because CMC changes frequently originate on the manufacturing side:

  • Manufacturing site change: for example, production moves from Site A to an affiliate or a contract facility B → the sponsor must submit an information amendment to update the CMC section of the IND
  • Analytical method change: a new HPLC method replaces the old one for potency testing → an information amendment is required, with a summary of the new method's validation
  • Sterilization process change: changes to sterile filtration conditions or terminal sterilization cycle parameters → an information amendment with validation data is required

Core principle: the CDMO is not the IND holder and cannot submit an information amendment directly. However, the CDMO is obliged to notify the sponsor promptly of any manufacturing or testing change that affects the quality of the clinical material, so that the sponsor can submit the information amendment within the 30-day timeframe.

Common FDA 483: CMC Changes Not Submitted as Information Amendments

When reviewing IND documents and internal records, FDA investigators specifically check for the following problems:

  • Manufacturing records show that the batch production site has changed, but the IND information in FDA's system still shows the original site → a typical 483 observation
  • Batches made after an analytical method change have been used in clinical trials, but the sponsor has not reported the method change to FDA → a serious information reporting deficiency
  • New stability data show a shortened shelf life, but the IND still states the original expiry period → expired investigational product may continue to be used in the study

Consequence: such lapses may lead FDA to question the data integrity of the entire clinical program, and may even trigger a clinical hold.

Analogy: An Information Amendment Is Like a "Change Notice" on a Building Permit

Imagine an architect who has obtained a building permit for a building (analogous to IND clearance). After construction starts, any significant change in materials or methods must be notified to the permitting authority (analogous to FDA). An information amendment is like such a "construction change notice": it does not alter the overall design blueprint (so it is not a protocol amendment), and it is not a safety incident (so it is not a safety report), but it is still an important change that the authority needs to know about. Making changes without notification could result in the whole permit being revoked (analogous to an IND clinical hold).

3.3 §312.32 — IND Safety Reports (Key Section)

Original Text & Regulatory Requirements

§ 312.32 IND safety reports.

(a) Definitions.

  • Adverse event (AE): Any untoward medical occurrence associated with the use of a drug in humans, whether or not considered drug related.
  • Serious adverse event (SAE): Any adverse event that results in death, is life-threatening, requires inpatient hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, results in persistent or significant disability or incapacity, is a congenital anomaly/birth defect, or is an important medical event that may not be immediately life-threatening but may jeopardize the patient and may require medical or surgical intervention to prevent one of the listed outcomes.
  • Unexpected adverse drug reaction: An adverse drug reaction, the nature or severity of which is not consistent with the applicable product information (e.g., Investigator's Brochure for an unapproved product or, for an approved product, the package insert).
  • Suspected adverse drug reaction (SADR): Any adverse event for which there is a reasonable possibility that the drug caused the adverse event.

SUSAR = Serious + Unexpected + Suspected Adverse Reaction

Reporting Obligations:

(b)(1) 7-Day Expedited Report: A sponsor shall notify FDA and all participating investigators in a written IND safety report of any suspected adverse drug reaction that is both serious and unexpected and that is fatal or life-threatening. The 7-day clock begins on the day the sponsor first receives information indicating that the SUSAR is fatal or life-threatening.

(b)(2) 15-Day Expedited Report: A sponsor shall notify FDA and all participating investigators in a written IND safety report of any adverse experience associated with the use of the drug that is both serious and unexpected and that is not fatal or life-threatening. The report shall be submitted within 15 calendar days after the sponsor's initial receipt of information that a reportable adverse experience has occurred.

(b)(3) Follow-up Reports: A sponsor shall also notify FDA of any findings from animal or in vitro studies that suggest a significant risk for human subjects including reports of mutagenicity, teratogenicity, or carcinogenicity. Such findings shall be reported within 15 calendar days after the sponsor receives information suggesting significant risk.

(b)(4) Annual Report Safety Summary: A sponsor shall submit a safety update in the annual report (§312.33) describing the sponsor's approach to monitoring for serious and unexpected adverse experiences and a summary of such experiences occurring during the study.

Content of Safety Reports:

  • A narrative description of the event including relevant medical history
  • Description of adverse event: onset, duration, severity, outcome
  • Drug information: dose, route of administration, start/stop dates, batch/lot number
  • Assessment of the relationship between the drug and the adverse event (causality assessment)
  • Relevant concomitant medications and medical conditions
  • Proposed actions to be taken: dose modification, protocol amendment, or clinical hold request

Tutorial Commentary

SUSAR Definition Broken Down: All Three Criteria Must Be Met

A SUSAR (Serious Unexpected Suspected Adverse Reaction) must meet all three of the following criteria at the same time:

  1. Serious: death, life-threatening event, hospitalization, persistent disability, congenital anomaly, or an important medical event (any one is sufficient)
  2. Unexpected: the nature or severity of the event is not listed as a known adverse reaction in the Investigator's Brochure, or is listed but the severity actually observed is greater than the known description
  3. Suspected (causal relationship): there is a reasonable possibility that the investigational product caused the event, rather than it being entirely attributable to the subject's underlying disease or other factors

An expedited safety report is required only when all three criteria are met. If the event is not serious (not an SAE), is expected, or a causal relationship can be ruled out, it is not a SUSAR and only needs to be summarized in the annual report.

Detailed Criteria for Judging "Unexpected"

"Unexpected" is the element most likely to cause disputes. FDA's position is:

  • Reference document: the adverse reaction information in the Investigator's Brochure (IB). If the drug is already marketed, the approved labeling (Package Insert / Prescribing Information) applies instead.
  • If the IB describes an adverse reaction as "mild headache" and a subject experiences "severe headache requiring hospitalization", the event is still regarded as "unexpected" even though "headache" is listed in the IB, because the severity exceeds what the IB describes.
  • When the Investigator's Brochure is updated and new safety information is added, a reaction that was previously "unexpected" becomes "expected", and similar events in future no longer require expedited reporting. This is one of the key reasons sponsors update the IB.

Analogy: Safety Reporting Is Like Mandatory Occurrence Reporting in Aviation

The aviation industry has a mandatory incident reporting system: regardless of whether the pilot or airline is "at fault", certain safety events (such as mechanical failures, bird strikes and runway incursions) must be reported to the regulator within a set time. The purpose of the system is systematic safety improvement, not assigning blame.

The logic of IND safety reporting is exactly the same: FDA is not trying to hold the sponsor accountable, but needs to receive safety signals promptly so that, where necessary, it can take regulatory action to protect subjects (such as issuing a clinical hold). A sponsor therefore cannot delay or omit a report because it "believes the event is unrelated to the drug"; if the SUSAR definition is met, the event must be reported, and the report should state the sponsor's causality assessment.

Fatal FDA 483: Sponsor Failed to Submit a 7-Day Report

§312.32 is one of the provisions most frequently cited in FDA clinical investigation inspections. Typical 483 observations include:

  • "Failure to submit 7-day IND safety report for a fatal SUSAR"
    Scenario: the sponsor received a report of a subject's death, judged it to be an "expected reaction" and did not submit a 7-day report. At inspection, FDA found that the nature of the death actually exceeded the IB description and should have been "unexpected", constituting a reportable SUSAR.
  • "Failure to submit IND safety report within 15 calendar days"
    Scenario: after receiving a serious adverse event report, the sponsor spent time on internal evaluation, and the report was submitted after the 15-day deadline.
  • "Safety reports lack required causality assessment or lot number information"
    Scenario: a safety report was submitted but omitted the lot number, or the causality assessment was a single sentence with no scientific rationale.

Severity of consequences: a missing 7-day report is among the violations FDA treats most severely and can directly trigger a clinical hold, affecting the entire development program.

The CDMO's Role and Boundaries in Safety Reporting

This is the boundary question that CDMO personnel most need to understand:

The CDMO's core obligation: provide all manufacturing-related information (lot numbers, formulation, process, OOS investigation results) to support the sponsor's safety assessment. If a CDMO conceals or delays reporting a manufacturing problem, the sponsor may miss its statutory reporting deadline, which is a serious compliance risk.

3.4 §312.33 — Annual Reports

Original Text & Regulatory Requirements

§ 312.33 Annual reports.

A sponsor shall within 60 days of the anniversary date that the IND went into effect, submit a brief report of the progress of the investigation that includes:

(a) Individual study information: For each clinical study in the IND, a brief summary of:

  • Title of study and study number
  • Protocol phase (Phase 1, 2, or 3)
  • Study objective and patient population studied
  • Total number of subjects planned and enrolled
  • Most frequent and most serious adverse experiences by body system
  • Summary of all IND safety reports submitted during the year
  • Current status of each study: ongoing, suspended, completed, or discontinued

(b) Summary of current status of the IND including the following information:

  • Summary of the overall status: list of studies, number of subjects enrolled, current study phase
  • Description of the general investigational plan for the coming year
  • A summary of the protocol changes made during the year and the reason for each change

(c) A brief summary of significant foreign marketing developments:

  • If the drug has been marketed in a foreign country, provide a brief summary of significant foreign marketing developments, including foreign regulatory authority actions, foreign adverse events, and differences in labeling from US labeling

(d) Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) updates:

  • Any changes in the manufacturing or testing information for the drug substance or drug product that were implemented during the past year
  • Current specifications for the drug substance and drug product
  • Stability updates: summary of any new stability data generated during the year

(e) Microbiological information (for antimicrobial drugs):

  • Summary of microbiological data, including in vitro susceptibility data and data related to development of bacterial resistance

Deadline: Annual reports are due within 60 calendar days of the anniversary of the IND effective date. There is no grace period. If the IND became effective on April 14, the annual report is due by June 13 of each subsequent year.

Tutorial Commentary

The Six Required Elements of the Annual Report

The framework of the §312.33 annual report can be remembered as "six modules":

  1. Individual study information: status of each clinical trial, number enrolled, and summary of adverse events
  2. Overall IND status and future plans: progress over the past year + the study plan for the coming year
  3. Summary of protocol amendments: all protocol amendments submitted during the year and the reasons for them
  4. Foreign marketing developments: if the drug is marketed in other countries, report foreign regulatory developments
  5. CMC updates: any change in manufacturing or test methods during the year, plus the latest specifications and stability data
  6. Microbiology data (anti-infective drugs): in vitro susceptibility data and information on the development of resistance

The annual report is not an expedited safety report: it is a year-end review document and cannot replace the obligation to make 7-day or 15-day reports in real time.

Annual Reports and the IND Effective Date: Calculating the Clock

The §312.33 deadline is 60 days after the anniversary of the IND effective date. The IND effective date is not the date of FDA approval; it is the 31st day after FDA receives the IND application (if no clinical hold is issued):

  • IND received: for example 2026-04-14
  • IND effective date: 2026-05-14 (if no clinical hold)
  • First annual report due: 2027-07-13 (1 year after the IND effective date + 60 days)

The sponsor must track the IND effective date in an internal system and set up reminders to avoid missing the deadline. FDA's annual report tracking system actively checks submission compliance.

Annual CMC Update: Collaboration Obligations with the CDMO

The CMC update section of the annual report consolidates the results of the CDMO's communications with the sponsor throughout the year. It needs to include:

  • A summary of all information amendments (§312.31) submitted during the year
  • Any changes to production sites, processes and test methods
  • Any significant deviations in batch manufacturing records and the investigation results
  • The latest stability data for the drug product or drug substance, including any out-of-trend or OOS results

If the CDMO did not notify the sponsor of important manufacturing information during the year, the sponsor may only discover the information gap shortly before the annual report deadline, and will then either submit an incomplete annual report or hurriedly file a large number of supplementary information amendments. Both are compliance risks.

Common Annual Report Lapses

  • Omitting terminated studies: even if a study has stopped recruiting, if subjects are still in the follow-up period, the related adverse events must still be listed in the annual report
  • Incomplete safety event summary: listing only the SUSARs reported on an expedited basis during the year, and omitting trends in non-SUSAR adverse events that are still important
  • CMC update omitting manufacturing site changes: even if an information amendment was already submitted under §312.31, the annual report must still summarize it; this cannot be used as a reason to omit the annual CMC summary
  • Vague description of future plans: FDA uses the annual report to assess whether the clinical development plan is reasonable; wording such as "as circumstances require" may prompt an FDA information request

Reporting Mechanism Comparison

Tutorial Commentary

A Memory Framework for the Comparison Table

The core differences among the three reporting mechanisms can be remembered in one sentence each:

  • 7-day: "Someone is dying or has died → report immediately"
  • 15-day: "A serious new finding, but not immediately fatal → report within two weeks"
  • Annual report: "The full ledger of all safety information for the year → hand it in within 60 days after the anniversary"

Another point to remember: both the 7-day and 15-day reports must be sent to FDA and all investigators, whereas the annual report goes to FDA only. This is because one purpose of expedited safety reports is to let frontline investigators know about potential safety risks immediately, so as to protect subjects currently being enrolled.

Gray Areas in the Three SUSAR Criteria

Situations that most often cause disputes in practice:

  • The boundary between "serious" and "non-serious": determining an "important medical event" is subjective. For example, severe hypoglycemia requiring intravenous glucose but no hospitalization is usually judged "serious", because it could become life-threatening without timely intervention.
  • When "unexpected" is updated: when the sponsor decides to update the IB to reflect a newly recognized adverse reaction, the updated IB turns a previously "unexpected" reaction into an "expected" one. But the IB update itself must also be notified to FDA (through a protocol amendment or an information amendment).
  • Disagreement on "suspected" causality: the investigator records "Unrelated", but after medical review the sponsor concludes that a causal relationship cannot be ruled out → the sponsor should treat the event as "suspected", submit the report, and explain in it the difference between the two assessments. FDA's position is: when in doubt, report.

SUSAR Quick Checker

Did the event result in:
  □ Death?
  □ Life-threatening condition?
  □ Hospitalization (or prolonged hospitalization)?
  □ Persistent or significant disability?
  □ Congenital anomaly?
  □ Important medical event (requiring medical intervention to prevent the above outcomes)?
→ If any box is checked = SERIOUS ✓

In the Investigator's Brochure (IB):
  □ This type of event has never been listed?
  □ Or it is listed, but the actual severity exceeds the IB description?
→ If any box is checked = UNEXPECTED ✓

Is there a reasonable possibility that the investigational product caused it?
  □ Temporal relationship (occurred within a reasonable time after dosing)?
  □ Biological plausibility (explainable by the pharmacological mechanism)?
  □ Improvement after stopping the drug (dechallenge positive)?
→ If a reasonable possibility exists = SUSPECTED ✓

All three ✓ → SUSAR → proceed to the timeline decision:
  Fatal/Life-threatening? → 7-DAY REPORT
  Other serious unexpected? → 15-DAY REPORT
3.6 Practice & Application

Practice: Classify the Report Type

Exercise 1: Classify the Report Type

Classify each situation below: is it a 7-day report, a 15-day report, or only to be mentioned in the annual report?

  1. In a Phase 2 study, a subject was hospitalized on day 3 after injection of the investigational product with acute liver failure and required urgent evaluation for liver transplant. The investigator assessed the event as "probably related". Hepatotoxicity is listed in the IB only as "mild, reversible ALT elevation", and acute liver failure is never mentioned.
  2. In a Phase 1 trial, a subject developed Grade 2 nausea (requiring drug treatment but not hospitalization). The IB clearly lists nausea as a common adverse reaction (Grade 1-3).
  3. New animal toxicology data show liver adenomas in high-dose rats, while the human dose in the current Phase 2 trial is 1/5 of the carcinogenic dose in animals.
  4. In a Phase 3 study, a 73-year-old female subject died in week 8 of treatment; the preliminary cause of death was a heart attack (MI), and she had a known history of hypertension and diabetes. The investigator considered the event "unlikely related" to the drug. The IB contains no cardiovascular events.

Exercise 1: Answer Analysis

  1. 15-day report (not 7-day, because "hospitalization" is serious, but "evaluation for transplant" does not yet confirm "life-threatening"; if the condition worsens to life-threatening, it escalates to a 7-day report). SERIOUS ✓ (hospitalization) + UNEXPECTED ✓ (acute liver failure exceeds the IB) + SUSPECTED ✓ (probably related) = SUSAR → 15-day report
  2. Annual report. Grade 2 nausea: not an SAE (no hospitalization, not life-threatening). SERIOUS ✗ → not a SUSAR; no expedited report is needed.
  3. 15-day report (the animal carcinogenicity finding = "significant risk for human subjects"; submit a 15-day report under §312.32(b)(3))
  4. 15-day report (arguable, but the conservative position applies). Death = SERIOUS ✓. Myocardial infarction not listed in the IB = UNEXPECTED ✓. "Unlikely related" ≠ "impossible": FDA's position is that as long as a causal relationship cannot be completely ruled out, the event is still SUSPECTED ✓. When in doubt, report.

Application: CDMO Endotoxin OOS Scenario

CDMO Application Scenario: Endotoxin OOS — The Full Process from Manufacturing Deviation to IND Safety Report

Background:

A CDMO is manufacturing an intravenous product for a Phase 2 clinical trial for a biotech company (the sponsor). During routine stability testing, the QC department finds that for 3 batches already released and shipped to clinical sites (lots CT-2026-011, 012, 013), the endotoxin level exceeds the specification upper limit by 2 times (OOS result). These 3 batches have been administered to a total of 47 subjects, and about 12 subjects are still on treatment.

Questions:

  1. After the CDMO QC department finds the OOS result, what actions should it take immediately?
  2. What specific information should the CDMO provide to the sponsor?
  3. After receiving the notification, what evaluations should the sponsor perform to decide whether an IND safety report is required?
  4. If a safety report is required, does the 7-day or 15-day timeline apply? What elements should the report include?
  5. Besides the safety report, does the sponsor need to submit any other type of amendment?

Full Analysis of the CDMO Scenario

Step 1 — CDMO immediate actions (within 24 hours of OOS confirmation):

  • Start the OOS investigation procedure and confirm whether the result is a "true OOS" (excluding laboratory error)
  • Notify the sponsor immediately (by phone + written confirmation), stating: which batches, the OOS values, and the shipping/recall status of the batches already administered
  • Quarantine all affected batches (if unused stock remains)
  • Notify QA in parallel, start the deviation investigation, and preserve all raw records

Step 2 — Information the CDMO provides to the sponsor:

  • Lot numbers, manufacturing dates, shipping dates and shipping destinations of the OOS batches
  • The specific OOS test results (test date, test method, upper and lower specification limits, measured value)
  • Preliminary root cause assessment (process, raw materials, test method, equipment issues)
  • Number of subjects dosed (if known)
  • Summary of the relevant batch manufacturing records (Batch Record)
  • Any similar historical deviation records (trend analysis)

Step 3 — Sponsor safety assessment (deciding whether an IND safety report is triggered):

  • Clinical risk assessment of endotoxin at 2 times the limit: in an intravenous product, endotoxin can directly cause a pyrogenic reaction and, in severe cases, septic shock
  • Check whether any of the 47 subjects have reports of unexplained fever, chills or hypotension, especially adverse events within 1-4 hours after dosing
  • Assess "unexpected": is endotoxin-related systemic inflammatory response listed in the IB? Typically, endotoxin reactions caused by manufacturing contamination are not described

Step 4 — Determining the report type:

  • If the investigation finds any subject with a febrile reaction or a more serious event (hospitalization, hypotension) related in time to dosing: → 15-day IND safety report (SAE + Unexpected + Suspected)
  • If the investigation finds a subject with a life-threatening severe septic reaction: → 7-day IND safety report
  • Even if there are no symptoms at present: → the sponsor should at least submit an information amendment (§312.31) reporting the CMC OOS issue, and evaluate whether the enrollment/dosing plan needs to be changed (a protocol amendment may be required)

Step 5 — Other required submissions:

  • §312.31 information amendment: OOS manufacturing finding → CMC information amendment (within 30 days)
  • §312.30 protocol amendment: if it is decided to stop using the affected batches and modify the study procedures, a protocol amendment must be submitted
  • Consider proactively requesting a discussion with FDA (Pre-Meeting Request) to assess whether a clinical hold is needed
  • Notify all investigators (proactively contact each site to confirm the condition of the subjects)

Key lesson: the CDMO's responsibilities lie in Step 1 and Step 2. The sponsor's responsibilities begin at Step 3. The speed and completeness of communication between the two directly determine whether the sponsor can complete the safety assessment and submit the report within the statutory deadline.

Section Summary

Highest Compliance Risk:

The 7-day report under §312.32 is the provision with the most serious consequences in the entire IND sponsor obligation framework. Failing to report a fatal or life-threatening SUSAR within 7 days can directly lead to a clinical hold, halting all ongoing studies and devastating the drug development program. For a CDMO, the most direct responsibility is to ensure that adverse manufacturing events are notified to the sponsor "immediately and completely", and notification must never be delayed on the grounds of "waiting for the OOS investigation to finish".

4.1 §312.50–312.53 — Sponsor Obligations to Investigators

Regulatory Text & Analysis

§312.50 — General Sponsor Responsibilities

Sponsors are responsible for selecting qualified investigators and providing each investigator with the information needed to conduct an investigation properly, for ensuring proper monitoring of the investigation(s), for ensuring that the investigation(s) is conducted in accordance with the general investigational plan and protocols contained in the IND, for maintaining an effective IND with respect to the investigations, and for ensuring that FDA and all participating investigators are promptly informed of significant new adverse effects or risks with respect to the drug.

§312.51 — Transfer of Obligations to a Contract Research Organization (CRO)

A sponsor may transfer responsibility for any or all of the sponsor's obligations under this part to a contract research organization. Any such transfer shall be described in writing. If not all obligations are transferred, the writing is required to describe each obligation being assumed by the CRO. A CRO that assumes any obligation of a sponsor shall comply with the specific regulations applicable to that obligation and shall be subject to the same regulatory action as a sponsor for failure to comply with any obligation assumed under this part. The sponsor retains ultimate responsibility for all transferred obligations that the CRO fails to fulfill.

§312.52 — Use of Contract Research Organizations

If a sponsor uses a CRO, the sponsor shall transfer in writing each obligation being assumed by the CRO. The FDA may inspect the CRO to determine whether it is in compliance with the regulations applicable to the obligations assumed and whether it is performing the obligations that were transferred to it.

"A contract research organization that assumes any obligation of a sponsor shall comply with the specific regulations applicable to that obligation and shall be subject to the same regulatory action as a sponsor for failure to comply."
— 21 CFR §312.52(b)

§312.53 — Selecting Investigators and Monitors

Before permitting an investigator to begin participation in an investigation, the sponsor shall obtain the following:

  • A signed Form FDA 1572 — Statement of Investigator — from each investigator
  • A curriculum vitae or other statement of qualifications of the investigator showing the education, training, and experience that qualifies the investigator as an expert in the clinical investigation of the drug for the use under investigation
  • A protocol describing the clinical study to be conducted by the investigator
  • Financial disclosure information if required under 21 CFR Part 54

The sponsor must also select a monitor who is qualified by training and experience to monitor the progress of the investigation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Five Pillars of Sponsor Obligations

§312.50 defines five core sponsor obligations to investigators, which can be remembered by their initials: SIPME

  • S — Select: select qualified investigators
  • I — Inform: provide the Investigator's Brochure (IB) and all necessary information
  • P — Protocol compliance: ensure that the study is conducted in accordance with the IND protocol
  • M — Monitor: continuously monitor the progress of the study
  • E — Emergent safety: immediately notify FDA and all investigators of significant new safety concerns

These five obligations cannot be "delegated away" to escape liability: even when they are transferred to a CRO, the sponsor retains ultimate responsibility.

Analogy: The General Contractor and Subcontractor Model

The sponsor is like a general contractor that has taken on a "clinical trial building project" commissioned by FDA (the owner). The CRO, the investigators and the CDMO are all subcontractors.

The key legal logic:

  • The general contractor may outsource the rebar work to a subcontractor (transfer of obligations under §312.52)
  • But if the rebar is defective, the owner (FDA) holds the general contractor to account, not the subcontractor
  • FDA also has the right to inspect the subcontractor's site directly (CRO inspection rights)
  • If a subcontractor fails to comply, it is subject to the same regulatory action as the general contractor (equivalent regulatory standard for CROs)

This logic directly explains why sponsors must choose their CROs and CDMOs carefully, and put adequate controls in the quality agreement.

Transfer of Obligations to a CRO Must Be in Writing: Verbal Agreements Are Invalid

The "in writing" requirement of §312.51 is not a mere formality: a verbal agreement transferring obligations to a CRO is invalid. If FDA cannot find a written transfer agreement at inspection, the sponsor remains responsible for all obligations the CRO performed.

Practical reminder: the CRO contract must include an "obligation transfer matrix" that explicitly lists which Part 312 obligations the CRO assumes. A general clause such as "the CRO agrees to provide services" does not satisfy the written requirement of §312.51.

4.2 Form FDA 1572 — Statement of Investigator

Form FDA 1572 — Required Elements

Form FDA 1572 is the formal commitment document signed by each clinical investigator before participating in an IND study. It is not merely administrative paperwork — it is a legal agreement between the investigator and the federal government. Providing false information on Form FDA 1572 may constitute a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. §1001.

Required information fields on FDA 1572:

  • Name and address of the investigator
  • Name and address of the research facility where the investigation will be conducted
  • Names of sub-investigators who will be assisting the investigator in the study
  • Name and address of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) responsible for reviewing and approving the study
  • Names of other IRBs that will review the study at each additional site
  • Reference to the current protocol and investigator's brochure

Investigator commitments upon signing FDA 1572:

  1. Conduct the study in accordance with the signed protocol and any amendments
  2. Personally conduct or supervise the described investigation
  3. Inform patients or subjects that the drugs are being used for investigational purposes
  4. Ensure requirements relating to obtaining informed consent (21 CFR Part 50) are met
  5. Report to the sponsor adverse experiences (AEs) that occur in the course of the investigation
  6. Read and understand the information in the Investigator's Brochure (IB), including potential risks and side effects of the drug
  7. Ensure that all associates, colleagues, and employees assisting in the investigation are informed about their obligations in meeting the above commitments
  8. Maintain adequate and accurate records per §312.62 and make them available for inspection
  9. Ensure that the IRB complies with 21 CFR Part 56 requirements
  10. Comply with all other requirements regarding obligations of clinical investigators under 21 CFR Part 312

Record retention obligation from FDA 1572 / §312.62:

An investigator shall retain records required to be maintained per §312.62 for a period of 2 years following the date a marketing application is approved for the drug for the indication for which it is being investigated; or, if no application is to be filed or if the application is not approved for such indication, until 2 years after the investigation is discontinued and FDA is notified.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Legal Status of FDA 1572

Form FDA 1572 is not an ordinary form; it has the force of federal law. Key points:

  • Federal statement: signing it is a formal statement to the federal government; knowingly providing false information may be prosecuted under federal criminal law, 18 U.S.C. §1001
  • IRB designation: the IRB named on Form 1572 is the body FDA regards as responsible for reviewing the study; changing the IRB requires that the sponsor be informed and FDA be notified
  • Sub-investigator obligations are linked: the principal investigator (PI) is responsible for the conduct of all sub-investigators listed on Form 1572
  • Obligation to read the IB: by signing Form 1572 the investigator commits to having read and understood the IB; if an investigator is later found not to have understood the known risks of the drug, Form 1572 becomes an important legal basis

Drug Disposition Record Requirements: Tracked by Lot and by Subject

Drug disposition records must track (§312.62(a)):

For each lot:
  ├── Quantity received (Received from sponsor)
  ├── Date received
  ├── Lot number
  └── Details of use / return / destruction:
        ├── Which subject it was dispensed to (Subject ID)
        ├── Dose per administration
        ├── Administration dates
        ├── Unused quantity
        ├── Quantity returned to sponsor
        └── Destruction record (Destroyed: method, date, witness)

Every record must be traceable through:
  Original lot number → subject → administration date → outcome (adverse event / efficacy)

Why this matters so much: if a serious adverse event (SAE) occurs in a clinical trial, FDA and the sponsor must be able to trace which lot of drug was given to which subject. Incomplete records are among the most common Warning Letter deficiencies in clinical trials.

The Dual-Trigger Mechanism for Record Retention

The retention period runs for 2 years from the later of two events:

  • Event A: the NDA/BLA approval date for the drug
  • Event B: the date the study is terminated and FDA is notified

Practical difficulty: an investigator cannot know "when the NDA is approved". The sponsor is therefore obliged to notify investigators of the NDA approval date, so that they can count the 2-year retention period from that date. Quality agreements often include a clause that "the sponsor shall notify the CDMO within 30 days of NDA approval".

4.3 §312.60–312.62 — Investigator Responsibilities

Investigator Obligations: Conduct, Control & Records

§312.60 — General Responsibilities of Investigators

An investigator is responsible for ensuring that an investigation is conducted according to the signed investigator statement, the investigational plan, and applicable regulations; for protecting the rights, safety, and welfare of subjects under the investigator's care; and for the control of drugs under investigation. An investigator shall, under 21 CFR Part 50, obtain the informed consent of each human subject to whom the drug is administered, except as provided in §50.23 or §50.24. Additional specific responsibilities of clinical investigators are set forth in Parts 50 and 56.

§312.61 — Control of the Investigational Drug

An investigator shall administer the drug only to subjects under the investigator's personal supervision or under the supervision of a sub-investigator responsible to the investigator. The investigator shall not supply the investigational drug to any person not authorized under this part to receive it.

Critical implications of §312.61:

  • The investigational drug cannot be shared between study sites unless properly authorized
  • The investigator cannot give the drug to a physician not listed as sub-investigator on Form FDA 1572
  • The drug cannot be used for "compassionate use" or "expanded access" outside the IND framework without separate authorization
  • Drug accountability records must be maintained to demonstrate compliance with this section

§312.62 — Investigator Recordkeeping and Record Retention

(a) Disposition of drug: An investigator is required to maintain adequate records of the disposition of the drug, including dates, quantity, and use by subjects. If the investigation is terminated, suspended, discontinued, or completed, the investigator shall return the unused supplies of the drug to the sponsor, or otherwise provide for disposition of the unused supplies of the drug in accordance with the sponsor's instructions.

(b) Case histories: An investigator is required to prepare and maintain adequate and accurate case histories that record all observations and other data pertinent to the investigation on each individual administered the investigational drug or employed as a control in the investigation. Case histories include the case report forms and supporting data including, for example, signed and dated consent forms and medical records including, for example, progress notes of the physician, the individual's hospital chart(s), and the nurses' notes. The case history for each individual shall document that informed consent was obtained prior to participation in the study.

(c) Record retention: An investigator shall retain records required to be maintained under this part for a period of 2 years following the date a marketing application is approved for the drug for the indication for which it is being investigated; or, if no application is to be filed or if the application is not approved for such indication, until 2 years after the investigation is discontinued and FDA is notified.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Division of Responsibilities among Investigator, Sponsor and CDMO

Under Part 312 Subpart D, the responsibilities of the three roles are divided as follows:

  • Sponsor: holds the IND, bears ultimate responsibility for the entire trial, and supplies the drug to investigators
  • Investigator: conducts the trial at the clinical site, manages subjects, and maintains drug control records
  • CDMO: manufactures the investigational drug, maintains production batch records, and provides CMC data for the sponsor's IND

Each of the three roles carries its own FDA inspection risk: FDA may inspect the sponsor, the investigator and the CDMO separately, and deficiencies found may become grounds for a clinical hold.

Analogy: Drug Control Is Like Controlled Substance Logistics

The drug control requirements of §312.61 closely resemble the logic of controlled substance logistics:

  • Every step is recorded: just as morphine must be logged at every step from the pharmacy to the ward, investigational drug must be accounted for at every step from the CDMO to the sponsor to the investigator to the subject
  • No transfer: an investigator may not give the drug to other physicians to use, just as a pharmacy may not pass controlled substances to a neighboring clinic
  • Remaining quantities must be returned or destroyed: after the trial, unused drug must be returned or destroyed as the sponsor directs, and proof must be retained

This analogy helps explain why records must be precise down to the granularity of "lot number × subject × each administration".

Case History vs. CRF — Both Are Required by §312.62(b)

§312.62(b) makes clear that "case histories" comprise two levels of documents:

  • Source documents: physician progress notes, hospital chart, nurses' notes; these are the "original data"
  • Case Report Form (CRF): a standardized data form abstracted from the source records

Data integrity focus: at FDA inspection, if CRF data are inconsistent with the source medical records, this is a major GCP violation (data integrity violation). The two must be verifiable against each other (source data verification, SDV).

4.4 GMP Requirements for Investigational Drugs — The CDMO Perspective

CGMP for Investigational Drugs: Phase-Dependent Standards

§312.60 states that investigators are responsible for ensuring investigational drugs comply with "applicable current good manufacturing practice." This cross-reference triggers the full GMP framework — but the applicable standard varies by phase.

FDA Guidance: CGMP for Phase 1 Investigational Drugs (2008)

FDA published guidance clarifying minimum CGMP for Phase 1 investigational drugs. Key minimum requirements:

  • Written procedures for all critical manufacturing operations — batch record templates, SOPs for weighing, mixing, filling, sterilization, and labeling
  • Identity, strength, purity, and quality testing — confirmation that the drug manufactured is the drug intended, at the correct potency, without unacceptable impurities
  • Equipment maintenance and calibration records — traceable to measurements, dated, reviewed
  • Batch records with full traceability — who did what, when, with what equipment, using which materials, at what process parameters
  • Complete investigation of failures — out-of-specification (OOS) results, deviations, unexpected observations must be formally investigated and documented
  • Personnel training records — evidence that all personnel performing critical operations have been trained on applicable procedures

Phase 2 and Phase 3 — Full 21 CFR Parts 210/211 Compliance Required

For Phase 2 and Phase 3 clinical trials, the full requirements of 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211 apply. This means all provisions governing facilities, equipment qualification, component testing, in-process controls, laboratory controls, production records, and batch release apply to the clinical trial material. CDMOs manufacturing Phase 2/3 material must comply with the same GMP standards as commercial drug manufacturing.

Special Case: Sterile Phase 1 Injectables

Even for Phase 1, sterile injectable products require full aseptic processing controls. The rationale is straightforward: a non-sterile injection can cause immediate, serious, life-threatening infection in a human subject. Phase 1 injectables must therefore demonstrate:

  • Aseptic processing controls documented and validated (or justified for Phase 1 where formal validation may not yet be complete)
  • Media fills demonstrating aseptic technique capability
  • Environmental monitoring program for cleanroom and RABS/isolator
  • Sterility testing per appropriate pharmacopoeial method
  • Container-closure integrity testing
  • Personnel aseptic technique qualification

FDA's 2004 Aseptic Processing Guidance and the PICS Annex 1 (2022) both apply to sterile Phase 1 investigational products manufactured in commercial sterile manufacturing facilities.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is Phase 1 GMP "Minimal but Not Absent"?

The core logic of FDA's 2008 guidance: the main purpose of a Phase 1 trial is to confirm safety and pharmacokinetics, not efficacy. This means:

  • The number of subjects is small (usually 10–30), but each is a high-risk volunteer
  • Drug quality directly affects the interpretation of safety signals in a Phase 1 trial: if the drug is microbiologically contaminated or insufficiently pure, a safety signal may be a false positive (caused by the contaminant) or may mask the true toxicity of the drug itself
  • Therefore "minimal GMP" does not mean "no GMP": it means the minimum necessary controls to ensure drug quality

Common misconception: small batch = no need for strict GMP. The correct view: small batch + first-in-human use = quality control of every batch matters more, because there is no large-batch data to smooth out statistical error.

The GMP Floor for Phase 1 Sterile Injectables Is Non-Negotiable

Whether in Phase 1 or Phase 2/3, the consequences of non-sterility in a sterile injectable are immediate and severe:

  • Intravenous injection of a bacterially contaminated drug can cause sepsis within hours
  • Historical case: the 2012 New England Compounding Center (NECC) incident in the United States, in which contaminated epidural steroid injections caused 64 deaths and more than 750 cases of fungal meningitis and related infections
  • The tragic irony for a Phase 1 safety trial: if inadequate GMP leads to infection of subjects, the entire clinical program and its safety database will be called into question

CDMO practice: clients often ask for reduced sterility control standards on the grounds that "it is only Phase 1, so the specifications can be looser"; this is a wrong and dangerous request. A CDMO should insist that aseptic process standards are not compromised by clinical phase.

4.5 CDMO Obligations Under Part 312 (Indirect)

CDMO's Position in the Part 312 Framework

CDMOs are not directly named as regulated entities in 21 CFR Part 312 — the IND holder (sponsor) is the primary regulated party. However, CDMOs are operationally bound by Part 312 through multiple indirect mechanisms that create real regulatory exposure:

1. Quality Agreements Referencing Part 312 Obligations

Under FDA's 2013 guidance "Contract Manufacturing Arrangements for Drugs: Quality Agreements," CDMOs and their sponsor clients must establish written quality agreements that allocate specific GMP and regulatory obligations. Best-practice quality agreements for clinical-stage products will reference §312.62 (record retention), §312.65 (inspections), and §312.50 (sponsor obligations that flow to CDMO manufacturing operations). If a quality agreement is absent or inadequate, FDA may hold the sponsor responsible for CDMO failures — which in turn drives sponsor commercial pressure on the CDMO.

2. FDA Inspection Rights at Manufacturing Sites

21 CFR §312.65 grants FDA the authority to inspect any facility where the investigation drug is manufactured or stored. Sponsors are required under §312.65 to ensure that FDA inspectors have access to all manufacturing records, equipment, and personnel. FDA does not need advance notice to inspect a CDMO manufacturing clinical investigational drugs. CDMOs must maintain manufacturing batch records, equipment maintenance records, personnel training records, and environmental monitoring data in a form that can be immediately presented to FDA inspectors.

3. IND CMC Section — CDMO Must Be Listed

The Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) section of an IND must identify all manufacturing sites involved in the production of the investigational drug. This means:

  • CDMO name, address, and registration number must appear in the IND CMC section
  • CDMO manufacturing processes, equipment, and controls must be described in the CMC section
  • Any change to the CDMO or manufacturing process may require an IND amendment
  • If the CDMO has not been inspected by FDA and FDA determines an inspection is necessary, FDA may place the IND on clinical hold pending inspection

4. CDMO Manufacturing Records Support Sponsor's IND

The sponsor's annual IND report (§312.33) must include a summary of manufacturing changes and a description of the current manufacturing process. CDMOs must provide updated CMC data to the sponsor for each annual report. If CDMO records are incomplete or manufacturing processes have changed without IND notification, the sponsor's annual report may be inaccurate — an IND violation for which the sponsor bears responsibility.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The CDMO's "Indirect but Real" Part 312 Obligations

Part 312 does not name CDMOs directly, but there are four mechanisms that bind a CDMO in the clinical trial supply chain:

  1. Quality Agreement: the sponsor passes Part 312 obligations down to the CDMO by contract
  2. Inspection Rights: FDA may inspect the facility directly, without going through the sponsor
  3. CMC Disclosure: the CDMO is named in the IND, and any inconsistency in the information is a violation
  4. Record Support: CDMO records are the underlying data for the sponsor's IND reports

Commercial impact: if a CDMO has a major GMP deficiency, it affects not only its own FDA inspection outcome but can also trigger a clinical hold on the sponsor's IND, affecting every clinical program that uses drug made by that CDMO.

Analogy: The CDMO Is the IND's "Unnamed but Visible" Partner

Imagine the IND as the host contract for a wedding, with the sponsor as the couple (the IND holder) and the CDMO as the banquet venue (the manufacturing facility):

  • The names on the contract are the couple's, but the success of the wedding depends heavily on the quality of the venue
  • If the venue has a food safety problem (GMP violation), the whole wedding (clinical program) may grind to a halt
  • The health department (FDA) can inspect the venue directly, without notifying the couple in advance
  • The venue's name must be publicly disclosed in the wedding filing documents (IND CMC)

Conclusion: although the CDMO is not a "party to the marriage", when something goes wrong, everyone shares the consequences.

Manufacturing Deviation Notification Flow — The CDMO's Obligations

When a manufacturing deviation occurs at a CDMO, the notification obligations under the Part 312 framework are as follows:

  1. CDMO internal investigation: deviation occurs → quarantine the batch → start the deviation investigation (usually within 24–72 hours)
  2. Notify the sponsor: notify the sponsor within the time limit set in the quality agreement (usually within 24 hours for a major deviation)
  3. Sponsor evaluation: the sponsor evaluates whether the deviation requires an IND amendment or a safety report
  4. IND amendment decision: if the deviation affects drug safety or CMC data, the sponsor must submit an IND amendment
  5. Regulatory reporting: if the deviation may affect subject safety, the sponsor submits a safety report to FDA under §312.32

Important: the CDMO notifies the sponsor, and the sponsor decides whether to notify FDA; the CDMO does not report directly to FDA (unless asked when FDA inspects directly). This one-way flow is central to the Part 312 responsibility structure.

4.6 Clinical Hold (§312.42) — The CDMO Business Risk

Clinical Hold: Authority, Grounds & Resolution

§312.42 gives FDA the authority to issue a clinical hold — an order to the sponsor to delay a proposed clinical investigation or to suspend an ongoing investigation. A clinical hold is one of FDA's most powerful enforcement tools because it immediately halts human studies.

Grounds for a Clinical Hold

FDA may place an IND on clinical hold if it finds that:

  • Unreasonable risk: Human subjects would be exposed to an unreasonable and significant risk of illness or injury based on available data
  • Unqualified investigator: The clinical investigator named in the IND is not qualified by reason of his/her scientific training and experience to conduct the investigation described in the IND
  • False or misleading materials: The IND contains an untrue statement of a material fact or omits material information required by Part 312
  • Insufficient CMC information: The IND does not contain sufficient information required under §312.23 to assess the risks to subjects of the proposed studies
  • Manufacturing deficiencies: If FDA discovers that conditions in the manufacturing process, testing, or controls create a basis for concluding the drug cannot be made with appropriate purity, quality, strength, or identity

Scope of Clinical Hold

A clinical hold may be placed on all studies in the IND, or on specific studies within the IND. FDA may suspend all clinical investigations of a drug if manufacturing deficiencies affect the product across all studies.

Clinical Hold Resolution

To have a clinical hold removed, the sponsor must:

  1. Receive a written communication from FDA specifying the basis for the clinical hold
  2. Prepare a complete response addressing all issues identified by FDA
  3. Submit the complete response to FDA

FDA must respond within 30 calendar days of receipt of the complete response. If FDA does not respond within 30 days and the complete response adequately addresses the deficiencies, the sponsor may resume the investigation (for Phase 2/3) after notifying FDA. FDA's response may be a "complete response letter" lifting the hold, or a request for additional information.

Manufacturing-Based Clinical Hold Scenario

FDA inspects a sterile drug manufacturing facility (CDMO) and observes the following during an FDA Form 483 inspection of an aseptic processing line:

  • Three consecutive media fills have failed (growth observed in simulation units)
  • Environmental monitoring shows persistent Gram-positive organisms at Grade A fill zone
  • Aseptic gown qualification program shows expired qualifications for two filling operators
  • Investigation of a prior EM excursion was inadequately documented with no root cause identified

Based on these findings, FDA may conclude that drugs manufactured at this site cannot be assessed as safe for human administration and may issue a clinical hold on all IND studies using drug product manufactured at this CDMO site — across all sponsor clients' programs.

Integrated Scenario: Media Fill Failure at a CDMO for Phase 2 Sterile Injectables

Scenario: You are the QA Director of a CDMO. Your site manufactures Phase 2 sterile mAb injectables for three clients:

  • Client A: oncology mAb, Phase 2 trial under way (50 subjects), about to move into Phase 3
  • Client B: autoimmune disease mAb, Phase 2 just starting (10 subjects), with a trial plan running 18 months
  • Client C: rare disease mAb, Phase 2, no dosing yet, next batch due to be supplied in 3 weeks

Triggering event: the media fill on your filling line has failed for the second consecutive time. The first failure occurred 3 months ago; the root cause investigation attributed it to "a personnel gowning technique problem", but the investigation documents show that the root cause was incomplete, and the subsequent CAPA consisted only of "refresher training", with no substantive change.

Immediate actions (first 72 hours):

  1. Open a deviation report and immediately quarantine all commercial batches produced concurrently during the media fill period (confirm whether there are any)
  2. Under the quality agreement, notify the sponsor representatives of all three clients within 24 hours
  3. Suspend all ongoing filling operations until the root cause investigation is complete
  4. Start enhanced environmental monitoring to obtain baseline data

Decision matrix after notifying the sponsors:

  • Client A: the batches already dosed to current subjects were not affected by this media fill failure (the filling dates preceded the failure). The sponsor evaluates whether an IND amendment or safety report is required
  • Client B: same as above, but the sponsor must evaluate the supply plan for future batches
  • Client C: the next batch is put on hold; the CDMO must not manufacture until the media fill problem is resolved

Preparing for FDA interaction:

  1. Prepare a complete investigation report: linkage analysis of the two media fill failures, root cause (re-evaluated), and CAPA (specific measures, not just training)
  2. If FDA announces an on-site inspection (surveillance inspection or directed inspection): all batch records, EM trends and media fill records must be immediately available
  3. If FDA issues a clinical hold: the sponsors of all three clients must prepare IND clinical hold responses, and the CDMO must provide complete technical supporting documents

Business lesson: the inadequate CAPA after the first media fill failure is the root of this crisis. CAPA effectiveness checks must genuinely verify that the problem has been resolved, not close the CAPA as a formality.

Chapter Summary — Section 4

Key Regulatory Framework Summary

§312.50–312.53: The Sponsor–Investigator–CRO Triad

The sponsor holds ultimate responsibility for all aspects of the IND investigation. Obligations may be transferred in writing to CROs, but the sponsor remains liable for CRO failures. Form FDA 1572 is the binding commitment document that ties each investigator to the regulatory framework — it functions as a signed federal statement with potential criminal liability for false information.

§312.60–312.62: Investigator Operational Obligations

Investigators must control the investigational drug strictly — no unauthorized transfer or use outside the IND framework. Drug disposition records must track every unit from lot receipt to subject administration to return or disposal, with traceability to specific lot numbers, subjects, and dates. Case histories (source documents and CRFs) must be maintained with source data verifiability. All records must be retained for 2 years after marketing approval or 2 years after investigation discontinuation plus FDA notification — whichever is later.

GMP Standards: Phase-Dependent, Safety-Absolute

Phase 1 requires minimum CGMP (written procedures, identity/strength/purity testing, traceable batch records, failure investigation, training records). Phase 2/3 requires full 21 CFR Parts 210/211. Sterile injectables require full aseptic processing controls regardless of phase — there is no reduced standard for sterility because the safety consequences of a non-sterile injection are immediate and potentially fatal.

CDMO Obligations: Indirect but Consequential

CDMOs are bound through four mechanisms: quality agreements, FDA inspection rights, IND CMC disclosure requirements, and manufacturing record support for the sponsor's IND. The responsibility matrix shows CDMOs own batch records, GMP execution, validation, and environmental monitoring — while sponsors own IND filing, safety reporting decisions, investigator management, and CMC section compilation.

Clinical Hold: The Highest-Stakes Outcome

FDA may issue a clinical hold based on manufacturing deficiencies at a CDMO. A manufacturing-based clinical hold can suspend all clinical programs using product from that CDMO — across all sponsor clients. The 30-day FDA response requirement applies to the sponsor's complete response, not to the underlying CDMO investigation, which may take months. CDMOs must understand that their GMP compliance directly protects their clients' clinical programs and their own commercial viability.
