  • Understand where the HF guidance sits under the dual drug and device requirements
  • Recognize the legal status of an FDA draft guidance: non-binding recommendations, but a reflection of FDA's current thinking
  • From a CDMO perspective, understand why HF validation logic matters even when you do not own the device design

I. Introduction and Scope

This document provides guidance to industry and FDA Staff on the underlying principles of human factors (HF) studies during the development of combination products as defined under 21 CFR Part 3. This guidance describes Agency recommendations regarding HF information in a combination product investigational or marketing application and clarifies the different types of HF studies; the recommended timing and sequencing of HF studies; and how HF studies are part of the process to maximize the likelihood that the combination product user interface is safe and effective for use by the intended users, uses, and environments. In addition, the guidance describes how HF studies relate to other clinical studies. The guidance also provides process considerations for HF information in investigational or marketing applications to promote development and timely review of safe and effective combination products.

Commentary — Introduction, Paragraph 1

What Are Human Factors (HF)?

HF is the science of how people interact with products. Its core goal is that a product can still be used correctly and safely under real-world conditions, including stress, poor lighting and users with limited knowledge.

For combination products, HF is not a matter of design aesthetics; it is a regulatory-required safety validation process.

21 CFR Part 3 — Source of the Combination Product Definition

Title 21, Part 3 of the US Code of Federal Regulations sets out the definition and classification of combination products. This guidance builds on it to make clear which products HF studies apply to.

Analogy: The User Interface Is Like a Car Dashboard

Even a high-performance car can have accidents if the dashboard is confusing and the gear shift is not intuitively placed. Likewise, even if the drug itself is effective, a poorly designed autoinjector may prevent patients from successfully administering the dose.

User interface = everything the user touches when operating the combination product: appearance, labeling, operating steps and feedback mechanisms.

Scope — Application Types

This guidance focuses on HF issues related to combination products that are comprised of a drug or biological product and a device (also referred to in this guidance as medical device) for review in an investigational or marketing application submitted to the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), the Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH), or the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER). The application types include an investigational device exemption application (IDE), an investigational new drug application (IND), biologics license application (BLA), new drug application (NDA), or premarket approval application (PMA). However, the principles and recommendations may be applicable to combination products reviewed under other types of applications (e.g., premarket notification (510(k)) or abbreviated new drug application (ANDA)) as appropriate.

Commentary — Scope of Application

FDA's Three Centers × Combination Products

Combination products are assigned to a lead review center according to their primary mode of action (PMOA):

  • CDER: drug-led (e.g., prefilled syringes, autoinjectors)
  • CBER: biologic-led (e.g., biologic delivery devices)
  • CDRH: device-led (e.g., drug-coated stents)

Regardless of which center is assigned, the HF requirements apply equally.

Note: 510(k) and ANDA May Also Apply

FDA states explicitly that the principles in this guidance "may apply" to 510(k) (device) and ANDA (generic drug) combination products. If a design change could affect HF, consult OCP proactively.

Related Guidances and Legal Status of This Document

Related information is available in the Agency Guidance Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Optimize Medical Device Design and the Agency Draft Guidance Safety Considerations for Product Design to Minimize Medication Errors. Additionally, this guidance supplements other existing guidance documents developed by CBER, CDRH, CDER, and the Office of Combination Products (OCP) that describe other aspects of product development.

FDA's guidance documents, including this guidance, do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead, guidances describe the FDA's current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word "should" in FDA's guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required.

Commentary — Related Guidance and Legal Status

The Role of the Office of Combination Products (OCP)

OCP is the FDA office that coordinates the review of combination products. It is responsible for:

  • Determining which center should lead the review of a combination product
  • Coordinating communication between CDRH (device HF expertise) and CDER/CBER (drug regulatory lead)
  • Accepting sponsor inquiries on the applicability of HF studies

Important: Draft Guidance ≠ Legal Requirement

This document is a draft guidance issued in February 2016. It represents FDA's thinking at that time, but:

  • It is not a binding regulation (not legally binding)
  • "Should" = recommendation, not "must" or "shall"
  • It may be revised after public comments are considered

Even so, regulators refer to this framework during review, and a sponsor that deviates without sound justification still faces review risk.

Analogy: Guidance Is Like "FAA Flight Advice"

A pilot is not strictly required to follow every FAA Advisory Circular, but after an accident it is hard to justify not having followed the advice. FDA guidance works in a similar way: it is not mandatory, but any deviation needs sound justification.

Two Key Reference Guidances

  • HF device guidance (2016): Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Optimize Medical Device Design — the general HF methodology foundation
  • Drug safety-in-design guidance (draft): Safety Considerations for Product Design to Minimize Medication Errors — prevention of use errors on the drug side

This combination product HF guidance builds on these two documents and integrates both the device and drug perspectives.

II. Background

What Are Combination Products?

Combination products, as described in 21 CFR Part 3, are comprised of any combination of a drug and a device; a device and a biological product; a biological product and a drug; or a drug, a device, and a biological product. The constituent parts of a combination product retain their regulatory status (as a drug, device, or biological product) after they are combined. Accordingly, a combination product remains subject to the regulatory requirements associated with its constituent parts.

Generally, HF studies are conducted to evaluate the user interface of a product. FDA often receives requests to clarify how HF concepts apply to the development of a combination product when one of the constituent parts is a device. Inquiries include:

  • What types of HF studies might need to be conducted for the combination product?
  • When is the appropriate time to perform HF Validation studies?
  • What is the role of HF studies as compared to other types of clinical studies?
  • Are additional HF studies necessary when the design of the combination product changes?

Other general inquiries relate to regulatory considerations such as when a HF study is subject to review and approval by an institutional review board (IRB), and how HF studies are considered in User Fee determinations.

Commentary — Background: Definition of Combination Products

The Four Combination Forms of Combination Products

  • Drug + device (e.g., prefilled syringes, inhalers)
  • Device + biological product (e.g., cell therapy delivery devices)
  • Biological product + drug (e.g., mixed formulations)
  • Drug + device + biological product (three-way combination)

The most common is the first type: autoinjectors, PFS (prefilled syringes), insulin pens and inhalers.

Key Principle: After Combination, Each Constituent Part Retains Its Regulatory Status

The drug solution in an autoinjector is still a "drug", and the injector body is still a "medical device". A combination product is therefore governed by both drug regulations (CGMP, 21 CFR Part 211) and device regulations (QSR, 21 CFR Part 820).

This is why HF studies are particularly complex for combination products: two sets of requirements must be met at the same time.

HF Requirements for the Device Constituent Part

For medical devices, the use of human factors and usability engineering (e.g., applying the knowledge of human behavior, abilities, and limitations to the design of a medical device) plays a key role in maximizing the likelihood that the device will be safe and effective for use by the intended users, for the intended uses, and for the intended use environments. Under the medical device design control requirements described in 21 CFR 820.30, design validation must include a risk analysis where appropriate. As part of the risk analysis, device manufacturers should identify and analyze potential use-related hazards, including lessons learned from reported errors with similar products, and as appropriate, incorporate and validate design features that mitigate or eliminate these hazards. This assessment informs the device design development to eliminate or minimize use errors that could cause harm or compromise medical treatment.

Commentary — HF Regulatory Requirements for the Device Constituent Part

21 CFR 820.30: Device Design Control Requirements

Section 820.30 of FDA's device Quality System Regulation (QSR) requires that "design validation" include appropriate risk analysis. Human factors studies are an important part of that risk analysis process.

Core logic of design controls:
Identify use-related hazards → analyze risk → improve the design → validate that the design is effective

Definition of Use-Related Hazards

These are hazards caused not by a defect in the device itself but by "improper operation by the user", for example:

  • The autoinjector needle cap is hard to remove → the patient uses excessive force and is injured
  • The dose display window is unclear → the patient misreads the dose
  • The activation button is not intuitively placed → the injection is interrupted midway
  • Poorly designed waste disposal → risk of needlestick injury

Analogy: HF Is a "Stress Test of the Use Scenario"

Factory quality control tests drug stability at 40°C/75%RH; HF tests whether the device can still be operated correctly "under real use conditions", with a patient's trembling hands, dim lighting and anxiety.

Both verify performance under "extreme conditions"; only the source of stress differs.

HF Requirements for the Drug Constituent Part

For a drug product, goals for reducing use-related hazards are reflected in the process and data that support selection of the drug formulation, assurance of product quality, drug risk management activities, and in pharmaceutical quality system principles. Drug development should take into account the user interface and factors that can reduce the risk for medication errors; i.e., features to enhance patient safety. Such features include product appearance, identification markings (such as imprint codes on solid oral dosage forms), container closure, packaging configurations, labeling (including labels on containers and cartons), and nomenclature. The Prescription Drug User Fee Act IV (PDUFA IV) provides that one of the development goals is to ensure drug safety by prospectively designing a drug that minimizes the risk for errors made by intended end users.

Commentary — HF Expectations for the Drug Constituent Part

Use Error Prevention Mechanisms on the Drug Side

For drugs, HF is not just the device interface but the design of the entire use chain:

  • Formulation selection: viscosity affects ease of injection
  • Container closure system: needle cap material and removal force
  • Label design: font size, contrast, warnings for easily confused drug names
  • Packaging configuration: multi-dose vs. single-dose, to avoid overdose risk
  • Nomenclature: avoiding LASA (look-alike, sound-alike) drug name confusion

Links to Related ICH Guidelines

This section cites three ICH guidelines:

  • ICH Q8(R2): Pharmaceutical Development — basis for formulation design quality
  • ICH Q9: Quality Risk Management — framework for use risk assessment
  • ICH Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System — organizational structure supporting HF activities

Policy Significance of PDUFA IV

PDUFA IV listed "proactive design to reduce user error" as one of the goals of drug development. This means HF is not only a device-industry issue: FDA expects drug companies to build use safety into product design from the start, rather than dealing with recalls after launch.

CDMO Practice Perspective: The Container Closure System Affects HF

As a CDMO filling combination products, the following process parameters directly affect HF validation results:

  • Fill volume accuracy: affects the readability of the dose display window
  • Silicone coating level: affects plunger glide force and therefore the force needed to inject
  • Needle cap removal force: the intersection of container closure integrity testing and HF validation
  • Extractables / leachables (E&L): if they alter formulation viscosity, they may in turn affect ease of injection

Even if a CDMO does not own the device design, a process change can trigger the customer's need for HF revalidation.

Combination Products: Both Sets of Requirements Apply

For a combination product that includes drug and device constituent parts, both the device design control requirements and drug development expectations apply to the entire combination product. Therefore, when evaluating a combination product, the design of the product user interface should be assessed in HF studies if needed to ensure that use-related hazards associated with the product are eliminated or mitigated to reduce patient adverse events and medication errors attributable to use-related errors. This document focuses on human factors considerations for combination products to promote consistency in their design, development and review.

Commentary — Integrating the Dual Requirements

Core Principle: Device Requirements + Drug Requirements = Both Apply at the Same Time

A combination product is subject to both:

  • 21 CFR 820.30 (device design controls) — requires HF as part of design validation
  • PDUFA IV / ICH Q8-Q10 (drug development framework) — requires proactive use-safety design

These two sets of requirements do not replace each other; they are layered on the same combination product. This is why the guidance exists: to help sponsors understand how to satisfy both within a single HF program.

Hierarchy of HF Study Objectives

  • Eliminate use-related hazards — the ideal, solved at the root through design
  • Mitigate use-related hazards — when elimination is not possible, reduce severity or occurrence
  • Communicate residual risk — through labeling, instructions for use and training materials

The purpose of an HF validation study is to confirm that the design has achieved the goal of "eliminating or effectively mitigating" use-related hazards.

Analogy: HF Validation Is Like "Nonclinical Safety Studies + User Scenarios"

A drug needs toxicology studies to confirm its safe dose range; a combination product also needs HF validation to confirm that, "in the hands of the intended users, it can achieve its therapeutic purpose in real use scenarios without causing additional harm".

Both are essential components of a marketing application, and neither can be omitted.

CDMO Strategy Recommendation: Understand the Customer's HF Plan

When taking on combination product filling work, a CDMO should proactively ask the customer:

  • Is HF validation complete, or still in progress?
  • Do our process specifications (e.g., plunger force, fill volume tolerance) affect the HF validation already completed?
  • If the process changes, does the customer need to repeat the HF study? (This may trigger a supplement.)
  • Does the customer need us to provide HF-related test data on the container closure system?

Understanding these questions early avoids later commercial risk and regulatory delay.

Practice Questions

  3. As a CDMO, your customer asks you to confirm whether a change in silicone coating level will affect HF validation. Explain how silicone level relates to HF and how you would respond to the customer.
  4. What role does OCP (Office of Combination Products) play in HF review of combination products? When should you proactively contact OCP?

III.A — Glossary and Concepts

Original Text

For purposes of this document, the following definitions and concepts apply to HF studies, the final finished combination product, and the major clinical study. For additional information on these terms see the sections that follow the glossary. For related definitions see Agency guidance Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Optimize Medical Device Design.

1. Human Factors Study (or HF Study)

A study conducted with representative users to assess the adequacy of the combination product user interface design to eliminate or mitigate potential use-related hazards. Typically, HF studies are part of an iterative design process that is driven by the complexity of the combination product and the nature of the safety considerations. The HF study evaluates:

  1. The ability of the user to perform critical tasks, and
  2. The ability of the user to understand the information in the packaging and labeling — such as product labels or instructions for use — that inform the user's actions and that are critical to the safe and effective use of the combination product (e.g., product preparation, administration, maintenance and disposal, or what actions to take if an adverse reaction occurs).

Both types of evaluations may be part of the HF Formative and HF Validation studies described below.

a. HF Formative Study

A study conducted on a combination product prototype user interface at one or more stages during the iterative product development process to assess user interaction with the product and identify potential use errors. HF Formative studies are iterative and inform the need for user interface changes (e.g., product design or labeling changes) and inform the content of the HF Validation study.

b. HF Validation Study

A study conducted to demonstrate that the final finished combination product user interface can be used by intended users without serious use errors or problems, for the product's intended uses and under the expected use conditions. The study should demonstrate that use-related hazards for the final finished combination product have been eliminated or that the mitigation for residual risks is acceptable; i.e., the benefit of product use outweigh the residual risk of the product. The study participants are representative of the intended users and the study conditions are representative of expected use conditions.

2. Final Finished Combination Product

The final finished combination product is the product intended for market and submitted in the marketing application. This term applies to the combined final device, drug, and/or biological product configuration including all product user interfaces (e.g., proposed packaging, labels and labeling, including training programs).

3. Major Clinical Study (or Major Clinical Trial)

As opposed to a HF study, a major clinical study is a larger scale clinical study that occurs during a later phase of combination product development. Major clinical studies provide the primary support for the safety and effectiveness of a product for a proposed indication (e.g., adequate and well-controlled studies). In addition to adequate and well-controlled studies, other types of later phase larger scale clinical studies may also be considered major clinical studies; e.g., a long-term extension study.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Is a "Human Factors Study"?

The essence of an HF study is to recruit representative users to operate the product and observe whether they can complete the critical steps safely and whether they can understand the instructions for use.

It evaluates two things:

  • Physical operating ability: can the user correctly perform the critical tasks?
  • Information comprehension: can the user understand the instructions on the label and in the instructions for use?

This is not a clinical trial and does not test drug efficacy. It tests whether the design of the interface between the person and the device is safe and usable.

Analogy: An HF Study Is Like a Pilot's Simulator Exam

Before obtaining a license, a pilot must handle various emergency situations in a simulator. The examiner watches every action: were any steps omitted? Was anything done in the wrong order?

An HF study is similar: real users (patients, nurses, caregivers) operate the combination product in a near-realistic setting, while researchers observe and record use errors without giving guidance. This is a true "stress test of the human-device interface".

Important: The Regulatory Meaning of "Final Finished Combination Product"

This term has a precise regulatory meaning: it is the version you intend to market and submit for review, including the device, drug/biological product, packaging, labeling, instructions for use and even the training program.

HF validation must be performed with this final version. You cannot use a pre-modification prototype, devices from a different lot, or a different version of the instructions for use. This has a major impact on CDMOs (see the application notes below).

Practice Questions

  1. A company wants to fine-tune the font size of the instructions for use after the HF validation study. How would FDA view this change? Does the validation need to be repeated?
  2. Can the results of an HF formative study be used directly to support a marketing application?

Be Clear on the Difference Between the Three Terms

Formative is a "design optimization tool"; Validation is a "regulatory threshold"; Major Clinical is an "efficacy threshold". The three have different purposes and must not be confused.

III.B — Evaluation of Use-Related Risk

Original Text

Consistent with a risk-based design and development paradigm, the foundation for HF study designs, testing and evaluation should be a use-related risk analysis of a combination product. A use-related risk analysis is a crucial step to help identify use-related hazards associated with the combination product, as well as to characterize high-risk hazards so they can be mitigated or eliminated through improved product interface design.

The use-related risk analysis will help identify critical tasks that should be evaluated in a HF study, inform the priority of testing the tasks in a HF study, and determine if there are specific use scenarios to include in testing. A variety of methods can be used to develop and analyze use-related hazards. Two methods frequently used are Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) and Fault Tree Analysis (FTA).

The use-related risk analysis should take into account: all the intended uses, users, and use environments; therapeutic or diagnostic procedures associated with the use of the combination product; similar products used within the environments; and any associated medical factors that may affect the safe use of the combination product. In addition, if previous models of the same or similar combination products exist, the risk analysis should incorporate information on known use-related problems with those products. This information can be obtained from the applicant's own experience as well as from public sources such as literature, adverse event reports, and product safety communications.

III.B.1 — Critical Tasks

The use-related risk analysis should identify critical tasks. Critical tasks are user tasks that, if performed incorrectly or not performed at all, would or could cause harm to the patient or user, where harm is defined to include compromised medical care. Thus, categorizing a task as critical is dependent on the unique considerations for each combination product.

The Agency expects the risk analysis for the combination product to include: an identification of all the critical tasks required for using the combination product, the consequences for failing to perform each critical task correctly, and the strategies that have been applied in the design of the user interface to eliminate or reduce risks to acceptable levels. Such an assessment should include considerations of the indication, the users, the environment and other conditions that might influence the importance of a particular task.

Some examples of critical tasks include:

  • The patient being able to successfully self-administer a drug at the prescribed dose identified in the labeling. Failure could harm the patient due to mis-dosing, under-dosing, overdosing, or inability to deliver a dose.
  • The user being able to safely dispose of a used syringe. Failure could result in needle sticks.
  • The patient being able to appropriately navigate the user interface for a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) delivery system. Failure could result in missed doses, inappropriate repeat doses, or overdoses.
  • The user being able to understand instructions for inserting a capsule into an inhaler to release the drug, and being able to insert the capsule. Failure could result in the patient swallowing the capsule instead of inhaling the contents, lack of treatment effect, or medication-related adverse events.
  • The user being able to distinguish a product from others of similar appearance. Failure could result in delivery of the wrong drug.
  • The user being able to complete a series of critical tasks required to prepare and administer a reconstituted drug from a combination product kit containing a prefilled diluent syringe, drug vial, empty syringe, needle, transfer device and infusion pump — including preparing the drug under sterile conditions, connecting the system, and introducing the reconstituted drug solution into an infusion pump. Failure could result in medication errors and/or use-related infection.

Appendix A identifies task failures that may occur with general categories of combination products such as injectors and inhalers. The information can be used to guide the applicant when conducting a risk analysis.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Fundamental Difference Between "Use-Related Risk" and "Inherent Risk"

Inherent risk: arises from the chemical or biological properties of the product itself. Example: a biologic is immunogenic and may cause an allergic reaction.

Use-related risk: arises from the way people interact with the device interface. Examples: a patient forgets to remove the needle cap → the dose cannot be delivered; a patient believes one press was not enough and injects again → overdose.

Use-related risk analysis looks at "human behavior", not "drug toxicity". It is a kind of FMEA for the human-device interface.

Analogy: Applying FMEA Thinking to "People"

In a process FMEA we ask: "If this step goes wrong, what are the consequences? How likely is it? Can it be detected?"

Use-related risk analysis does exactly the same thing, except that "process steps" are replaced by "user operating steps". For example:

  • Step: remove the needle cap
  • Failure mode: forgotten / incompletely removed
  • Consequence: dose not delivered (under-dose / no dose)
  • Design control: add a clear visual / tactile cue, or build in a mistake-proofing (poka-yoke) mechanism

Core Concept: What Is a "Critical Task"?

The definition is straightforward: if a task is performed incorrectly, or not performed at all, and this could cause harm (including compromised medical care), it is a critical task.

"Harm" is defined broadly and includes:

  • Direct physical injury (needlestick, infection, overdose, ineffective dose)
  • Compromised medical care (disease worsening because of an insufficient dose)
  • Administration errors (wrong patient / wrong drug)

Every critical task must be evaluated in the HF study.

Regulatory Focus: The Risk Analysis Must Cover Existing Problem Reports

FDA explicitly requires that, if similar products are on the market, the risk analysis must incorporate:

  • Adverse event reports (FDA MAUDE database)
  • Product safety communications
  • Use errors documented in the literature
  • The applicant's own user feedback data

This means you cannot start the risk analysis from zero; you must first study what has gone wrong before.

Practice Questions

  1. A patient uses an autoinjector to treat rheumatoid arthritis, with one subcutaneous injection per week. Which use scenarios are most likely to cause a critical task to fail? How does the risk matrix differ between home use and clinic use?
  2. A lyophilized powder combination product that requires reconstitution has which additional critical tasks compared with a PFS? What are their risk levels?
III.B.2 — Intended Users and Use Environments

Original Text

Prior to performing a risk analysis, it is important to identify all intended users and use environments for the combination product. Intended users may be categorized into distinct user groups by their different characteristics (e.g., use responsibilities, tasks performed, age ranges, skills, or experience levels).

For combination products, distinct user groups generally are health care professionals (HCPs) and lay users (non-health care professionals). Within these two groups there are likely further subgroups based on different tasks, roles, abilities and education.

Subgroups of the HCP user population can include those with significantly different roles (e.g., nurses, pharmacists, physicians, emergency medical technicians, home health care providers). Also, within the HCP user population there may be individuals that have experience with the use of similar products and individuals that do not (e.g., injector-experienced vs naïve) or that do or do not have experience with similar appearing products with different instructions for use or different hazards.

Lay users (non-health care professionals) are those who use the product for self-administration (the patient) or those who administer the product to others as a caregiver (e.g., a family member, sports coach). Within this population, experience of individual users with similar products or products under development may vary widely. For example, when considering a drug-autoinjector combination product, some lay users may be naïve to the use of any autoinjector or may be naïve to certain types of autoinjectors. Also, lay users may have experience with a different product that might influence their interaction.

Environments of use can have diverse characteristics that affect the users' interactions with the product. Combination products may be used in various professional health care / clinical settings that include emergency departments, intensive care units, inpatient bedsides, procedure suites, outpatient clinics, mobile units, and stocking and storage locations. Likewise, they can also be used in non-clinical settings including homes, schools, offices, and various modes of transportation (e.g., ambulances, airplanes).

These environments may vary with respect to temperature, lighting and noise levels, ambient activity levels, number of people in the vicinity, and the availability of associated/accessory medication or devices. Also, a combination product that is intended for home use may be confused with other family member or pet medications stored in the same location. Such environmental conditions may lead to use errors. These environmental factors should be considered in the use-related risk analysis, and included within the design of the HF study as appropriate when they present a use hazard.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Users Are Not Only Healthcare Professionals

This is the point most often underestimated in HF studies. The real users of combination products are often:

  • The patients themselves (self-injecting biologics at home: adalimumab, etanercept, insulin)
  • Family members / caregivers (a parent giving a child an EpiPen, a spouse assisting with an injection)
  • Non-professional caregivers (a sports coach using an EpiPen, a school nurse using an emergency device)

These people have no medical training, may be completely unfamiliar with the device, and may operate it under stress or in an emergency, which is entirely different from a trained nurse in a hospital.

Analogy: Selecting Users for an HF Study Is Like Enrolling Subjects in a Clinical Trial

Just as a clinical trial cannot use only healthy young people to represent all patients, an HF study cannot simply recruit conveniently available university or nursing students to represent "all users".

You need to find truly representative groups: arthritis patients (poor grip strength), elderly patients (declining vision and fine motor skills), first-time users (high cognitive load), and users of other products (who may carry over incorrect habits).

The Reality of Use Environments: How Bad Can the Home Be?

FDA explicitly lists the environmental challenges of home use:

  • Poor lighting: bathrooms and bedrooms may be dimly lit
  • Noise and distraction: TV sound, a crying child
  • Temperature variation: improperly stored drugs (which need to warm up after being taken out of the refrigerator)
  • Risk of mix-ups: family members' or pets' medications stored in the same place
  • Anxiety and stress: high-pressure operation in an anaphylaxis emergency

HF studies must simulate these real conditions. A success rate obtained in a perfect laboratory environment does not represent the real world.

Practice Questions

  1. A new adalimumab autoinjector is about to be launched, and the Humira Pen is already on the market. Should patients with experience of such "similar products" be treated as a separate group in the HF study? Why?
  2. EpiPen is an emergency anaphylaxis treatment device, and the user may be a parent in a state of panic. How should the use environment simulation for the HF study be designed? Can it be fully reproduced in a laboratory?
III.B.3 — The Role and Limits of Training

Original Text

Training is often proposed as a way to mitigate or control risks. However, before determining if training is appropriate for the combination product, first it is important to eliminate risks that are inherent to the product design. If there are residual risks, the next step is to determine if training is needed.

For example, for a new product that is developed as similar to or an alternative to a currently marketed product with use techniques that are well understood by the users, then training may not be necessary. Such an example might be a prefilled syringe with a staked needle for use by a health care professional. On the other hand, if there are residual risks for which training may be appropriate, the next step is to consider whether there is an opportunity for training, and if so, whether there is an expectation that training will routinely and consistently occur, before the first use of the combination product. In cases where training would be appropriate but is not expected to routinely or consistently occur, the HF study should evaluate the user interface in the absence of training.

For combination products when training is expected or needed to control or mitigate residual use-related hazards, it is important to determine what the training is likely to encompass and how it will be performed, who is responsible for conducting the training, and how to ensure consistency in the training method.

Consider, for example, a combination product being developed for a hospital-based surgical procedure. A risk analysis might determine that HCP training is required prior to the first use of the product to minimize the risk of errors related to assembling all the combination product constituent parts, preparing the treatment area and the surgical device constituent part before beginning the procedure, administering the drug constituent part(s), monitoring patient responses after using the product, and/or managing interactions across multiple users during the procedure. Due to the nature of the product and its use environment, all users would be expected to receive training before using the product. The HF study would evaluate the adequacy of the training in minimizing these potential risks.

In addition, when considering training to mitigate residual risks, it is important to consider how frequently the training will occur, as well as the length of time between the training session(s) and product use. For some combination products, training and first product use is separated by days, weeks, or months. Retention of information from the first, and possibly the only, training a user receives can decrease over time (i.e., training decay). For example, for a combination product designed for once a week self-injection, post-training information retention one week later can be anticipated to be lower than it would one hour later. If the risk analysis shows that training decay is a source of use-related error, then the HF study design should evaluate the effect of training decay. The HF Validation study should simulate the effect training decay may have on the users.

Tutorial Commentary

FDA's Core Position: Training Cannot Replace Design

This is one of FDA's most important policy positions in the HF field:

Priority of risk controls (similar to the hierarchy of controls in industrial safety):

  1. Eliminate by design: make the problem impossible by design (mistake-proofing)
  2. Mitigate via design: let the design itself reduce the severity or likelihood of the error
  3. Training / labeling: only when design cannot fully solve the problem may training be used as a supplementary mitigation measure

What FDA does not accept: "We will use training to teach users how to avoid this design problem." If the design makes errors easy, training is not an effective mitigation.

Analogy: The Philosophical Difference Between Design and Training

Imagine an electrical outlet designed so that a child can easily insert a finger. The manufacturer says: "We will train children not to do that." This is clearly not enough; the right approach is to change the design (add safety shutters or change the shape of the sockets).

Likewise, if the needle cap of an autoinjector is not intuitive enough, patients may easily fail to remove it when nervous, and the manufacturer cannot rely on the instructions alone. The design itself must make the removal action clear and hard to get wrong.

Core Concept: "Training Decay" Is a Regulatory Consideration

This concept is seldom mentioned in QA practice in Taiwan, but FDA explicitly requires it to be taken into account:

Scenario: a patient receives autoinjector training → a week later, injects alone at home for the first time. Within that week the memory of the training decays, especially for tasks with many steps or that are rarely performed.

HF study response: in the design of the HF validation study, deliberately place a time interval (for example, several hours to several days) between training and simulated use, to simulate the effect of training decay in the real world.

This also affects the design of training materials provided by a CDMO: if the customer's training materials are poorly designed, the CDMO has a responsibility to raise the issue.

CDMO Practice Scenario

A customer commissions a CDMO to fill a biosimilar autoinjector and asks the CDMO to support the production of a user training video.

CDMO QA should confirm with the customer:

  • Is the training video classified as part of the "user interface"? (If so, the validation study must cover it.)
  • Is training expected before every use, or only once at first use?
  • Does the patient population include groups that cannot reliably carry out a training program (e.g., elderly patients with cognitive impairment)?
  • If the training video is modified later (even slightly), does the HF validation need to be re-evaluated?
III.C — Human Factors Formative Studies

Original Text

HF Formative studies are designed to evaluate early combination product prototypes, taking into consideration the identified use-related hazards. HF Formative study results guide prototype design changes to eliminate or mitigate use-related hazards identified during the product development process. The use of iterative HF Formative studies optimizes the design of the combination product user interface for safety, and minimizes the risk of first discovering use problems during late stages of development (e.g., during an HF Validation study, during a major clinical study, or after finalizing commercial plans).

Iterative HF Formative studies and related design modifications are performed until the user interface design appears to be sufficiently optimized for safety and ready for HF Validation testing. Iterative modifications to the user interface may include changes to the physical design attributes, changes to the packaging and labeling (including instructions for use) and changes to a training program.

The results of HF Formative studies should inform the design of the final finished combination product. None of the individual subjects in the HF Formative studies should participate in the HF Validation studies to avoid the potential for bias.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: A Formative Study Is a "Design Tool", Not a "Regulatory Document"

Many people misunderstand the purpose of a formative study. Its functions are to:

  • Identify use problems early in product design
  • Verify that design modifications effectively solve the problems
  • Build an understanding of user behavior in order to design a better validation study

Formative study data are not used to pass regulatory review; they are a tool for internal design iteration. However, FDA may ask to review these data during review, to assess whether the manufacturer exercised due diligence in optimizing the design.

Analogy: A Formative Study Is a "Design Draft"; Validation Is the "Final Exam Paper"

Like an architect who draws countless sketches, builds scale models and has clients try them out when designing a building, all of that is the formative stage. The design drawings finally handed over are the formal documents to be approved.

Similarly, a manufacturer may run 3 to 5 rounds of formative studies (5 to 15 users per round), finding problems, modifying the design and retesting in each round. Only once the design is mature is the validation done (usually 15 to 30 users, depending on the user group).

Important Rule: Formative and Validation Participants Must Not Overlap

FDA explicitly requires that participants in a formative study must not take part again in the validation study.

The reason is simple: people who took part in a formative study have a "learning effect". They know some details of the device design and may perform better than real users in validation, causing bias.

The implication for CDMOs: if the customer used certain participant groups during development, these people must be tracked and excluded from later validation recruitment. This is a strict requirement of study design.

Practice Questions

  1. A formative study finds that 25% of patients cannot correctly remove the needle cap within 10 seconds. What action should the design team take? Can changing the instructions for use alone solve the problem?
  2. How long should the formative study report be retained? If FDA requests to review it during NDA review, what must the manufacturer provide?
III.D — Human Factors Validation Studies

Original Text

HF Validation studies demonstrate that the final finished combination product user interface would maximize the likelihood that the product will be safely and effectively used by intended users, for the intended uses in the intended use environments. There are two types of HF Validation studies: HF Simulated-Use and HF Actual-Use Validation. For most combination products, FDA expects that a HF Simulated-Use Validation study will be sufficient to assess the adequacy of the user interface.

III.D.1 — HF Simulated-Use Validation Studies

The HF Simulated-Use Validation study focuses on confirming that the design of the final finished combination product (i.e., after iterative prototype design changes) user interface adequately mitigates or eliminates the identified use-related risks. Simulation methods for these studies vary and may include the use of a manikin, injection pads, placebo, and other elements intended to simulate the patient, the procedure, or the environment of use.

The conditions of the HF Simulated-Use Validation study should be sufficiently realistic so that the results represent relevant aspects of actual use of the product once introduced into the market. Tasks to be performed in the HF Simulated-Use Validation study should include those critical tasks identified in a use-related risk analysis that may be associated with user interface problems. The study design should provide for the identification of any unanticipated hazards or unexpected use behaviors that were not previously identified.

III.D.2 — HF Actual-Use Validation Studies

As noted above, FDA expects that for most combination products, a HF Simulated-Use Validation study will be sufficient to assess the adequacy of the user interface design. However, there are rare circumstances when it is difficult to simulate the conditions of use, physical characteristics of the product, or environment of use. Thus, a HF Actual-Use Validation study may be needed to confirm the adequacy of the user interface design.

HF Actual-Use Validation studies either (1) use the final finished combination product (including the drug, not a placebo) in a simulated use setting or (2) use the final finished combination product in a real (not simulated) environment of use.

  • A HF Actual-Use Validation study of the combination product that includes the actual drug in a simulated use setting may be necessary when the drug can affect the user's ability to perform a critical task. For example, for a drug that causes coughing on inhalation which could result in incomplete dosing, inhaler designs to minimize the risk of not completing an inhalation could not be evaluated without use of the actual drug.
  • The other type of HF Actual-Use Validation study is in a real environment of use. For example, based on the hazard analysis and results of an HF Simulated-Use Validation, it may be appropriate to evaluate in a real environment use-related risks associated with a complex combination product intended for use in crisis/emergency settings or with a combination product that has a complex operating procedure. In these instances, the user's tasks could be influenced by the presence of noise, rapidly changing circumstances, distractions, etc.

FDA recommends that applicants for combination products discuss with FDA the availability of simulation techniques and whether HF Simulated-Use Validation and HF Actual-Use Validation studies are needed to evaluate the user interface.

Regardless of the type of HF Validation study, if use errors or problems (e.g., failures, "close calls," use difficulties, and/or new findings) are identified in an HF Validation study, these should be evaluated to (1) identify the root cause(s), (2) determine the potential for harm (including the clinical significance of such errors or problems and the potential for compromised medical treatment), and (3) determine whether additional measures to eliminate or mitigate hazards are necessary.

If the HF Validation study shows that additional measures are necessary to address the risk of failures that are deemed clinically significant, then the HF Validation study will be considered failed. Changes to the user interface may be needed to eliminate or mitigate hazards and a new HF Validation study should be performed to evaluate the changes.

Also, if the product design changes or the user population changes, then the completed HF Validation study may or may not be applicable to the design change. A use-related risk analysis should be completed and, dependent upon the findings of the risk analysis, a new HF Validation study may be advisable. If the product design remains unchanged but the applicant seeks to add a new user population, then as applicable, a new use-related risk analysis and new HF Validation study should be performed.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Validation Study Is a Regulatory Milestone

The HF validation study is the final regulatory threshold of the whole HF process. It must meet four key conditions:

  • Representative users: the recruited participants must truly represent the intended user population (including the most challenging subgroups)
  • Representative use conditions: simulate the real use environment (home, clinic, emergency)
  • Production-equivalent device: the device used must be equivalent to the final marketed version (or very close to it)
  • No coaching: participants must not be prompted or corrected during the study; natural behavior is observed

If a validation study fails, it cannot be passed with a simple "remedial measure". The design must be modified and the entire validation then repeated.

Consequences of Validation Failure: It Must Be Redone, Not Just Handled with CAPA

If the validation study finds clinically significant use errors, FDA's position is:

  1. The validation study is judged to have "failed"
  2. The user interface (design, labeling, instructions for use) must be modified
  3. A new validation study must be performed

This means that finding problems at the validation stage is very costly: delays and multiplied costs. This is why iteration in formative studies is so important: the earlier a problem is found, the easier it is to fix.

Validity of Validation After a Design Change

FDA explicitly states that if the product design or the user population changes, the existing validation study may no longer be valid. The following situations require re-evaluation:

  • Product design change (even one that looks minor) → the use-related risk analysis must be redone, and then a decision made on whether a new validation is needed
  • Adding a new user population (e.g., extending from HCPs to lay users) → a new risk analysis and validation are needed

For a CDMO: if the CDMO changes the fill volume, plunger force or needle protection mechanism, even if the customer thinks it is "about the same", the completed HF validation may be invalidated. Responsibilities must be clearly defined in the contract.

CDMO Practice Scenario: Responsibility for the Production-Equivalent Device

The customer needs to use a "production-equivalent device" in the HF validation study. As the filling manufacturer of the combination product, the CDMO may be asked to supply these devices.

The CDMO must ensure that the devices supplied conform to:

  • Fill volume: exactly consistent with the final marketed specification
  • Fill accuracy: within the specification range, with no noticeable deviation
  • Plunger force (glide force): directly affects whether the user can press smoothly to inject
  • Needle protection mechanism: the activation force and design of the safety needle shield
  • Appearance and feel: the same as the final marketed product (test units of a different color or material must not be used)

If the CDMO later changes any of the above parameters because of process optimization (for example, a formulation adjustment changes viscosity → plunger force changes), the customer must be notified immediately and the impact on the validity of the HF validation assessed.

Practice Questions

  1. A simulated-use validation study was conducted with 25 participants, of whom 3 failed to complete the critical task "confirm that dose delivery is complete". Root cause analysis showed that the viewing window on the device was too small for elderly patients to see clearly. What should the next step be?
  2. A pharmaceutical company completed a validation study for the HCP population and now wants lay users to be able to use the same device to self-inject at home. Is this validation study still valid? What additional steps are needed?
III.E — Human Factors Knowledge Task Studies

Original Text

In situations when the understanding of the information provided in a combination product's labels or labeling is a critical task to using a product safely and effectively, a study to assess the user's understanding of such information (Knowledge Task study) is appropriate. Knowledge Task studies may occur as part of the HF formative, or HF validation process.

However, in comparison to other types of HF studies in which critical task performance is assessed by observing user interaction with the product, Knowledge Task studies focus on the understanding and interpretation of important information in the user interface that will be applied to make use-related decisions. The users' understanding of the labeling is evaluated by questioning test participants and assessing whether the information has been understood.

Knowledge Task studies may focus on particular aspects of labeling. For example, a Knowledge Task study could evaluate:

  • HCP's understanding of their roles and responsibilities when introducing a combination product as part of a new procedure, or associated with complex medical/surgical procedures that involve many different HCPs;
  • The user's ability to select the appropriate task from a lengthy set of instructions that include different options;
  • The user's understanding of how to identify defective or expired product;
  • The user's awareness and understanding of the combination product's pertinent safety information provided in the instructions for use;
  • The user's ability to recognize clinical signs, identified in the instructions for use, that would prompt medical attention — e.g., shortness of breath, allergic reaction, weakness, signs of disease progression; or
  • The user's understanding of the diagrams provided in the labeling.

Certain types of Knowledge Task studies are also used in the development of non-prescription products. Generally, these are quantitative studies that evaluate whether results are statistically significant.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: A Knowledge Task Study Tests "Understanding", Not "Operation"

This is an easily overlooked component of the HF study system. An ordinary HF study tests "whether the user can do it correctly", by observing behavior.

A knowledge task study tests "whether the user understands", assessing cognition through questionnaires and interviews.

Typical knowledge task questions:

  • "After using this autoinjector, how long should you wait before removing the device?"
  • "If you notice redness or swelling of the skin after the injection, what should you do?"
  • "Please find the instructions on storage temperature in the instructions for use."
  • "What does the step shown in this pictogram mean?"

Analogy: Practical Exam vs. Written Exam

A driver's license test has two parts: the road test (practical operation) and the written test (knowledge and understanding).

HF studies are similar:

  • Simulated-use study = the road test (observing whether you can drive correctly)
  • Knowledge task study = the written test (testing whether you understand traffic rules and can recognize hazardous situations)

The two can be conducted separately or integrated into the same HF study.

When Is a Knowledge Task Study Especially Needed?

A knowledge task study becomes much more important in the following situations:

  • Complex multi-step instructions for use: for example, a lyophilized powder combination kit requiring reconstitution, with many steps that are easy to confuse
  • Conditional instructions: "If symptom X appears, perform step Y; otherwise perform step Z"
  • Understanding of safety warnings: users need to be able to recognize signs, such as allergic reactions and injection site infection, that require medical attention
  • Procedures in which several HCPs share the work: for example, in an operating room where the pharmacist, nurse and surgeon are each responsible for different steps
  • Understanding of pictograms: especially in multilingual markets, where cultural differences in how pictograms are understood can lead to errors

CDMO Practice Scenario: The HF Impact of Label Design

A CDMO is usually responsible for (or assists with) the label and outer packaging design of combination products. The following label design problems may be found in a knowledge task study:

  • The step pictograms in the instructions for use are not clear enough (elderly patients cannot read the numbering in dim light)
  • Patients' understanding of the storage condition statement ("Store refrigerated at 2-8°C; allow to warm up for 30 minutes before use") is insufficient
  • Patients overlook the post-administration precaution ("Do not receive vaccinations within 24 hours after injection")
  • The position of the lot number / expiry date marking confuses patients (especially on multi-dose injection pens)

If the customer finds label problems in the knowledge task study, the CDMO may need to help modify the label design and reprint, which affects production schedule and cost. The CDMO should therefore give HF-focused review comments at the first-draft stage of label design.

Integrated Practice Questions

  1. An HF study program is under way for a biologic PFS for rheumatoid arthritis. Design a complete HF program framework with three stages (formative, knowledge task and simulated-use validation), describing the purpose, sample size, user populations and success criteria of each stage.
  2. The HF validation study of an autoinjector shows that the pass rate for all critical tasks exceeds 90%, but 2 patients failed the knowledge task on "recognizing when medical attention is needed". How would FDA evaluate this result?
  3. As a CDMO QA manager, the customer tells you that they plan to use the first batch of samples made at your plant in the HF validation study, and that this batch used a different syringe body supplier (another supplier's glass syringes) from the final commercial batches. What should you advise the customer to do?

IV.A — Considerations for Submission of Combination Product Human Factors Study Data

IV.A Original Text — When HF Data Must Be Submitted

For the following two groups of combination products, generally human factors data should be submitted: (1) products for use outside the health care environment or by laypersons (e.g., home-use products, products for self-administration by patients or lay-caregivers) and (2) combination products having a device constituent part for which human factors data should be submitted.

For combination products that do not fall within these two categories, a use-related risk analysis for the combination product should be completed and the use-related risks reviewed to assess the need for HF studies. If the use-related risk analysis identifies the need for HF studies, then a HF Validation study should be conducted and the results submitted for review.

For example, a syringe is not on the list of high priority devices for human factors review, and the following illustrates certain considerations for when a HF Validation study may be needed for a prefilled syringe.

  • Prefilled syringe with staked needle & needle guard — for HCPs in acute care:
    • If the design is commonly used and well understood (absent other use-related risk concerns), FDA would not expect a HF Validation study. The applicant should submit its risk analysis and justification and seek Agency comment.
    • If the design is unique/novel, there are use experience concerns with similar products, or other factors increase use-related hazard — a HF Validation study should be conducted.
  • Same syringe — for patients with neuromuscular disorder or visual impairment:
    • Because unique user characteristics may affect safe use, an HF Validation study should be conducted to demonstrate the design adequately mitigates risks for intended use in these patients and environments.
  • Same syringe with unique color application to prevent medication errors:
    • Even if indication, users, and environment remain unchanged, a HF Validation study may be necessary to ensure HCPs can readily distinguish the new syringe from similar products containing different drugs. Such a study might focus on knowledge-based tasks.
  • Same syringe used with various tubing, connectors, pumps in a high-risk procedural setting:
    • A HF Validation study is likely necessary to assess the entire system, including detailed assessments of instructions, diagrams, training, or other aspects that might become part of a postmarket safety program.

Other scenarios and alternative approaches are possible. FDA encourages applicants to contact the Agency to discuss specific product proposals.

Commentary — When HF Data Must Be Submitted

Core Concept: Two Types of Combination Products That Must Submit HF Data

FDA expects HF data to be submitted for the following two types of products:

  • Type 1: Products used outside a healthcare facility or by lay users (home use, patient/caregiver self-injection)
  • Type 2: Combination products with a device constituent part for which HF data submission is required

Products that fall into neither category still need to complete a Use-Related Risk Analysis (URRA) to assess whether an HF study is needed.

Key Reminder: The Default Position Is to Do the Risk Analysis

FDA's logic is not that certain product types are exempt from HF, but rather: perform the use-related risk analysis first, and let its results determine whether an HF Validation Study is needed. If the analysis shows a study is not needed, the analysis and rationale must be submitted for FDA review; if FDA disagrees, the study must still be performed.

This means that not doing HF must be actively justified; it is not a passive exemption.

The CDMO Perspective

When a CDMO supplies prefilled syringes (autoinjector, wearable injector) to pharmaceutical clients, if the client's product is a home self-injection type, an HF Validation requirement is almost certainly triggered.

A CDMO should remind clients as early as the commercial discussion stage: device design (barrel dimensions, needle shield removal force, plunger resistance) all affect HF study results, and any manufacturing specification change should be communicated to the client early so that it does not disrupt their HF plan.

IV.B — Considerations for Design Changes After HF Validation

IV.B Original Text — Design Changes After HF Validation

FDA recognizes that combination product design changes may occur premarket or postmarket after HF Validation studies have been completed. For example, during premarket development the results of a clinical trial may reveal design flaws that were not detected in HF Formative or HF Validation studies. Similarly, during postmarket development an applicant may plan a design change to respond to use-related safety reports, complaints/problems, to address a manufacturer-initiated postmarket corrective and preventative action plan, or to meet the needs of an expanded indication or user population.

Some modifications to a product's internal design or to some of its external features may not need validation in a HF study (e.g., a change in a material that does not affect user interface). However, design changes made after HF Validation that relate to identified critical tasks or may result in new use-related errors or hazards that could lead to harm should have new HF Validation study assessments.

When making design changes, the applicant should conduct an updated use-related risk assessment of the new design. FDA encourages applicants to consider such things as:

  • Does the design change alter the user interface in any way (e.g., audible, tactile, color recognition, user instructions, etc.)?
  • Does the design change alter an existing critical task or add a new critical task?
  • Does the design change alter the expected users or their knowledge base?

To facilitate discussion with FDA, the applicant should provide a proposal about what, if any, additional HF testing is needed. The proposal should include: a detailed description of why the change is being made, a description of what specifically is changing, a use-related risk analysis of the new design, and where appropriate a proposal for evaluating potential risk mitigations of the new design and the effects of the change.

FDA encourages applicants to expeditiously identify the change plans and to discuss with the Agency the types of HF and other clinical or non-clinical studies that may be applicable before the applicant's approval of the design change.

Commentary — Design Changes After HF Validation

Core Concept: HF Triggers for Design Changes

Not every design change requires repeating the HF Validation. FDA's decision framework is:

  • No effect on the user interface (e.g., a change of internal material) → generally no re-validation needed
  • Affects Critical Tasks or may create new use errors/hazards → a new HF Validation must be performed

The three key questions: Does the user interface change? Are critical tasks affected? Do the intended users or their knowledge base change?

Design Change Assessment Flow

Design change plan
    ↓

    ↓

  Q1: Does the user interface change?
  Q2: Are critical tasks affected?
  Q3: Do user characteristics change?

Key Reminder: Post-Market Design Changes Carry Higher Risk

If a design change is made after marketing because of a CAPA, complaints, or an indication expansion, then in addition to a new HF Validation it may also trigger:

  • A supplement to the NDA/BLA with the drug regulatory authority
  • A 510(k) or PMA supplement with the device center
  • Clinical data bridging studies

A design change costs far more after marketing than before, which is why FDA repeatedly stresses: discuss with the Agency early.

The CDMO's Most Important Hidden Regulatory Risk

When a CDMO changes a component for supply chain or process reasons (for example: a syringe barrel from a different supplier, a plunger rod with different friction, or a needle shield with a different removal force), the client (MAH/Sponsor) may need to perform a Bridging HF Study or repeat the validation.

Practical recommendations:

  • State in the quality agreement that changes affecting device usability require advance notice to the client (at least 6 months is recommended)
  • Add an "HF impact assessment" field to the change impact assessment
  • Component changes after commercial launch are especially sensitive, because the client may already have completed HF Validation and any change could invalidate it
IV.C — Review of Human Factors Information in Combination Product Investigational Applications

IV.C Original Text — HF in Investigational Applications (IND/IDE)

The combination product's specific use-related risk analysis generally informs the Agency's expectations for whether HF information on a combination product should be submitted in an investigational application. The risk analysis itself should be submitted in the investigational application for the combination product.

If the applicant determines from the risk analysis that a HF study is not needed, the applicant should provide the use-related risk analysis along with the justification for this conclusion. If the use-related risk analysis indicates that a HF study is necessary, FDA encourages applicants to submit the following HF information for feedback before commencing the HF Validation study:

  • Use-related risk analysis and any updated risk analysis of design changes;
  • A summary of HF Formative study results and analysis;
  • A summary of changes made to the product user interface after the HF Formative studies, including how the results from the HF Formative studies were used to update the user interface and use-related risk analysis;
  • The draft HF Validation study protocol; and
  • Intend-to-market labels and labeling (including instructions for use if any are proposed) that will be tested in the HF Validation study.

When this information is submitted to the investigational application, FDA will review the information — including the use-related risk analysis and the draft HF Validation study protocol — and intends to provide comments or recommendations to increase the likelihood of an acceptable HF study design that will adequately test for potential use failures.

Also, during Agency review of draft HF Validation study protocols that include product labeling (e.g., instructions for use), FDA intends to provide preliminary comments on the user interface labels and labeling being tested. However, final labeling is determined after review of the entire marketing application that includes information beyond that in the HF Validation study.

Commentary — HF Information in an IND

Core Concept: The Role of HF Information in an IND

Submitting HF information in an IND (Investigational New Drug application) serves as a front-end risk control: it lets FDA review the plan and give comments before the applicant carries out a costly HF Validation Study, reducing the risk of validation failure.

The core IND submission package:

  • Use-related risk analysis (URRA)
  • Summary of formative study results
  • Summary of user interface changes made after the formative studies
  • Draft protocol for the HF Validation Study
  • Proposed labeling and instructions for use (IFU)

Analogy: Draft Review Is Like a Building Permit Application Before Construction

Think of the HF Validation Study as "constructing a building": before breaking ground, you hand the blueprints (the study protocol) to the authority (FDA) for review to confirm that the design meets regulatory requirements, so you avoid being told to tear it down and rebuild when half finished.

FDA's early review is non-binding, but it is still highly valuable: running the validation after obtaining FDA's written comments carries far less risk than running it blindly and then being asked to repeat it.

Key Reminder: The Final Labeling Is Not the Same as the HF Validation Labeling

When reviewing the HF Validation Study protocol, FDA may offer preliminary comments on the draft labeling, but the final labeling is not determined until the entire marketing application (NDA/BLA/PMA) has been reviewed.

This means that even after the HF study is complete, FDA may still ask for labeling changes. If the changes affect the performance of critical tasks, the applicant may need to conduct an additional HF Validation Study.

IV.D — Review of HF Studies and Certain Labeling in Marketing Applications

IV.D Original Text — HF in Marketing Applications

As applicable, FDA will review HF Validation study results submitted in the marketing application to assess whether the data confirm validation of the user interface and certain aspects of the proposed labels and labeling (e.g., instructions for use).

FDA cautions applicants leveraging a master file for HF data, that in some instances the master file data may suffice for one constituent part alone, but not for the combination product as a whole (e.g., device with a specific drug/biological product). The applicant should determine whether sufficient information would be available in the master file or whether the applicant should conduct and submit additional HF studies for the combination product as a whole.

During FDA review of labeling in a marketing application, FDA may determine that the final user interface labeling should differ from the HF Validated labeling. This may occur, for example, based on:

  • Results of the major clinical trial;
  • Other safety data or medication error data;
  • New nomenclature considerations; and
  • Labeling content and format requirements.

The labeling assessment also considers current postmarket experience with the same or similar products, which might indicate that modification of the instructions for use is appropriate to mitigate a risk. After review of the marketing application, depending on the potential impact of resulting labeling differences on performance of critical tasks, an additional HF Validation study may be needed to ensure that the changes minimize the use-related risks without creating additional hazards.

Commentary — HF Review in Marketing Applications

Core Concept: Scope of HF Review in a Marketing Application

When reviewing an NDA/BLA/PMA, FDA focuses on the following in the HF Validation Study results:

  • Whether the user interface passed validation (including IFU, labeling, and training materials)
  • Whether all critical tasks passed in the validation study
  • Whether any failed task has an adequate explanation or mitigation

The Master File Trap

Some device manufacturers (such as syringe suppliers) submit a Device Master File (DMF) to FDA so that drug companies can reference their HF data. However, FDA explicitly warns:

DMF data may cover only the device itself, not the combination product as a whole (the device combined with a specific drug or biologic).

A drug company cannot rely solely on the HF data in a DMF; it must still assess whether additional HF studies are needed for the complete combination product.

Labeling Changes May Trigger Additional HF Validation

This is the most easily overlooked risk in marketing application review: after reviewing the complete application, FDA may ask for changes to the IFU or labeling so that it differs from the version tested in the HF Validation Study.

If the changes affect the performance of critical tasks, the applicant needs to conduct an additional HF Validation Study, which may delay the approval timeline.

Best practice: communicate fully with FDA on the draft labeling at the IND stage to reduce surprises during review.

  1. A CDMO client's autoinjector has completed its HF Validation Study. During BLA review, FDA asks for the order of the IFU steps to be changed to match the labeling format of other similar products. Does this trigger a need for additional HF validation? How would you assess it?
  2. A device manufacturer's DMF contains HF data for healthcare professional use, but your client's product is for home self-injection by patients. Are these DMF data sufficient? Why?
V — Relationship of Human Factors and Major Clinical Studies of the Combination Product

V Original Text — HF and Major Clinical Studies

As explained in preceding sections, HF studies of a combination product are conducted as part of the product design controls process. An appropriate HF development program will maximize the likelihood that the combination product user interface is safe and effective for use by the intended users, uses and use environments. However, the HF Validation study is not sufficient to establish the safety and effectiveness of the combination product for the proposed indication. Specifically, data from the major clinical study(ies) establish the combination product's safety and effectiveness for the proposed indication and the complete labeling summarizes the essential scientific information needed for the safe and effective use of the product.

Therefore, ideally, before conducting the major clinical study(ies), the HF Validation study should be conducted on the final finished combination product, including the user interface (e.g., instructions for use, training materials, and any other user labeling, if applicable). The HF Validated product would then be ready for further evaluation in the major clinical study(ies) that will be submitted in the marketing application.

Noting that in some cases it may be appropriate to conduct your human factors studies in parallel to your major clinical studies or after your clinical studies to address modifications to your product.

FDA recognizes that in some circumstances the data to support safety and efficacy of the combination product may be adequate without the inclusion of the final finished combination in a major clinical trial. For certain products, the sequencing of the HF study prior to the clinical study may be less critical to inform our understanding of the product's safety and efficacy, allowing for greater flexibility in the timing of the human factors validation study relative to major clinical studies.

In other cases, a sponsor may encounter a need to change the combination product design in the course of the development program, even after clinical studies have been completed. The type and extent of data to support such changes depend on the nature of change, development stage, and other contextual factors, and FDA would consider the totality of the data provided to support the approvability of the combination product in any such circumstances.

However, for certain combination products, we might expect or encourage you to use the final finished combination product in your major clinical studies. In such cases, we recommend that you conduct the HF-Validation study on the final finished combination product prior to the major clinical studies.

And, in all cases, we encourage you to discuss your combination product development plans with the Agency as appropriate and consider such discussion as a component of your development meeting, including the pre-IND, IDE and EOP2 meetings.

Commentary — The Relationship Between the HF Validation Study and Clinical Studies

Core Concept: HF Validation ≠ Proof of Safety and Efficacy

Many people mistakenly believe that passing HF Validation means the product is "safe and effective." FDA clearly distinguishes the two in this section:

  • HF Validation Study: confirms that the user interface design allows intended users to use the product safely and correctly → addresses "can it be used correctly?"
  • Major Clinical Study (Phase 3): confirms the product's safety and efficacy for a specific indication → addresses "does it have a therapeutic effect?"

Neither can be omitted; they address different dimensions of the question.

Ideal Development Timeline

Phase 1 / Phase 2
  → Formative studies
  → Iterative user interface design
  → Submit URRA + draft HF Validation Protocol to the IND
  → Obtain FDA feedback
        ↓
HF Validation Study (using the final finished product)
  → All critical tasks pass
  → User interface confirmed
        ↓
Phase 3 major clinical study
  → Use the HF-validated "final finished combination product"
  → Collect drug safety and efficacy data at the same time
        ↓
Marketing application (NDA/BLA/PMA)
  → Submit Phase 3 results + complete HF Validation report
        ↓
Marketing approval

Analogy: Test the Driving Interface Before Driving on the Road

Imagine the development of a car with driver-assistance features:

HF Validation = confirming that the steering wheel, dashboard, and warning sound design let the driver operate the car correctly

Phase 3 clinical = testing on real roads whether the vehicle drives safely and meets performance targets

FDA's ideal scenario is to first confirm that the driving interface design is sound, and then put the car on the road for large-scale testing, rather than discovering interface defects only after hitting the road, having to redesign, and then repeating the large-scale testing.

Boundary Conditions of Flexibility

FDA acknowledges that running HF Validation "in parallel" or "after the clinical study" is acceptable in some circumstances, but with conditions:

  • If FDA requires the clinical study to use the "final finished product," the HF Validation must be completed before Phase 3
  • If the combination product design is changed after the clinical study ends, additional bridging data are needed to support it
  • Clinical data collected with one device design cannot directly support approval of a different device design

Key risk: Inconsistency between the clinical data and the design may prevent the application from being approved.

Three Important Meeting Opportunities

FDA recommends discussing the combination product development plan (including the HF strategy) with the Agency at the following development milestones:

  • Pre-IND Meeting: before submitting the IND, confirm the structure of the HF plan, whether HF Validation is needed, and the timing relative to clinical studies
  • IDE Meeting (Investigational Device Exemption): if the device in the combination product requires an IDE, align HF and clinical expectations at this meeting
  • EOP2 Meeting (End of Phase 2): before Phase 3 starts, confirm that the HF Validation results meet requirements and that Phase 3 uses the final finished product

CDMO Perspective: Clinical Lock and Manufacturing Freeze

From the CDMO perspective, "Phase 3 uses the final finished product" means that the device specifications of the combination product must be frozen before entering Phase 3 (Manufacturing Freeze).

Practical challenges:

  • Phase 3 materials usually need to start production at the end of Phase 2; if the device design is still being adjusted at that point, the CDMO cannot freeze the production specifications
  • During the technology transfer stage, the CDMO should confirm with the client whether Phase 3 materials use the "HF Validation version" or an "engineering version"
  • If the CDMO changes a component supplier during Phase 3, it must assess whether the client needs an HF bridging study and build this into the change timeline

Important Note on the Draft Guidance

This document is a 2016 Draft guidance and Contains Nonbinding Recommendations. FDA's final regulatory framework for HF of combination products continues to evolve.

Additional reference: "Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Medical Devices" (FDA, 2016). This is the formal final guidance, provides the most complete HF framework, and should be read together with this document.

The OCP (Office of Combination Products) plays a coordinating role in HF review of combination products, coordinating device HF issues between CDER/CBER (the drug centers) and CDRH (the device center).

Section IV & V — Key Takeaways

Summary: Process Pathway for HF in Combination Products

Key Takeaways

Core Decision Logic

  • Home use/self-administered products → HF Validation is almost certainly required
  • Unique device design, special users, complex systems → HF Validation required
  • Design changes affecting the user interface or critical tasks → HF re-validation required
  • Master File data → cannot directly replace HF studies on the combination product as a whole

CDMO Key Action Checklist

  • Add a "notification clause for component changes affecting device usability" to the quality agreement
  • At technology transfer, confirm the client's HF Validation status and the device version used
  • Before supplying Phase 3 materials, confirm they are the HF-validated final specification version
  • When changing component suppliers after launch, proactively assess whether the client needs an HF bridging study
  • Understand OCP's coordinating role in combination product review so that client regulatory inquiries are routed correctly

One Last Analogy: The FDA HF Process Is Like "Design Review + Construction Acceptance"

Submitting the draft HF protocol at the IND stage = design review of the blueprints before construction (Design Review)

HF Validation Study = acceptance after construction is complete (Commissioning)

Phase 3 clinical = actual occupancy testing in the accepted building

HF review of the marketing application = the authority's final approval of the occupancy permit (Certificate of Occupancy)

If any step goes wrong, the whole building (the combination product) cannot be approved for the market.
