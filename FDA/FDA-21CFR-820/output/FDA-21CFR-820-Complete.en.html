0.1 What Is QMSR — The 2024 Final Rule & Its Purpose

Original Text

Background — FDA Quality System Regulation Amendments (Final Rule, Feb 2, 2024):

"FDA is amending the device current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements of the Quality System Regulation (QSR), which are codified in 21 CFR part 820. The agency is revising part 820 to incorporate by reference the international consensus standard ISO 13485:2016, Medical devices – Quality management systems – Requirements for regulatory purposes. FDA also is making other revisions to part 820 to align with the incorporated standard. This final rule renames part 820 the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR)."
— 89 FR 7496, Feb 2, 2024 (Docket No. FDA-2019-N-1947)

Historical Background — QSR (Quality System Regulation, 1996):

The original QSR took effect on June 1, 1997. It was FDA's first comprehensive quality system standard for medical device manufacturers and was modeled on ISO 9001 (1987 version). Over the following decades, ISO 13485 emerged as the global benchmark — adopted by the EU (IVDR/MDR), Canada (CMDCAS), Japan (JPAL), and most competent authorities worldwide. This created a dual-compliance burden: U.S. manufacturers had to maintain both an FDA QSR system AND an ISO 13485 QMS simultaneously.

21 CFR § 820.1 — Scope (QMSR, effective Feb 2, 2026):

"(a) The requirements in this part govern the methods used in, and the facilities and controls used for, the design, manufacture, packaging, labeling, storage, installation, and servicing of all finished devices intended for human use. The requirements in this part are intended to ensure that finished devices will be safe and effective and otherwise in compliance with the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (the act). This part establishes basic requirements applicable to manufacturers of finished devices. If a manufacturer engages in only some operations subject to the requirements in this part, and not in others, that manufacturer need only comply with those requirements applicable to the operations in which it is engaged."
— 21 CFR § 820.1(a) (paraphrased from QMSR final rule; verify against current eCFR)

"(b) FDA does not intend this part to apply to manufacturers of components or parts of finished devices, but such manufacturers are encouraged to use appropriate provisions of this regulation as guidance."

Why FDA Changed the Rule — Stated Rationale:

  • Global harmonization: Align U.S. requirements with international standard already adopted by most competent authorities.
  • Reduce duplicative burden: Manufacturers certified to ISO 13485 will have substantially overlapping QMS documentation; QMSR eliminates the need to maintain two parallel systems.
  • Leverage modern quality concepts: ISO 13485:2016 incorporates risk-based thinking, lifecycle management, and software-specific provisions absent from the 1996 QSR.
  • FDA enforcement alignment: FDA can inspect against ISO 13485 clauses, providing a common framework recognized by inspectors globally (MDSAP).
"By incorporating ISO 13485 by reference, FDA is able to leverage the work of a consensus standards organization and provide requirements that are consistent with an internationally recognized QMS standard. This will reduce the compliance burden for manufacturers that maintain a quality management system consistent with ISO 13485."
— FDA preamble, 89 FR 7496 (Feb 2, 2024)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Are the QSR and the QMSR?

QSR (Quality System Regulation): the old regulation that took effect in 1996, containing FDA's own medical device manufacturing requirements. It listed 15 subparts in detail (Subparts A–O), covering everything from design controls to statistical techniques, and each section was a stand-alone federal regulation.

QMSR (Quality Management System Regulation): the 2024 final rule renamed and restructured Part 820. The key change is that §820.7 "incorporates by reference" the international standard ISO 13485:2016. In other words, the full text of ISO 13485 has the same legal force as the text of 21 CFR Part 820.

The most important analogy: the old QSR was like a cookbook FDA wrote itself. The new QMSR says, "We now cite ISO 13485, the international cookbook, directly, but for a few dishes we have extra rules."

Everyday Analogy: International vs. Local Driver's License

Imagine you are a manufacturer selling medical devices in 30 countries. In the past you needed both an "ISO 13485 license" (for the EU, Canada and Japan) and an "FDA QSR license" (for the US). The exams for the two licenses were 80% identical, but the questions were formatted differently, so you had to prepare two completely separate sets of documents.

The QMSR reform is like saying: "The US now accepts the ISO 13485 international license as the foundation, and you only need to add a few US-specific items (such as UDI and MDR reporting)." One system, far less duplicated work.

The Legal Significance of Incorporation by Reference

"Incorporation by reference" is a technical mechanism of US administrative law that allows a federal agency to treat the content of an external document (such as an ISO standard) as if it were written into the CFR text.

Practical implications:

  • ISO 13485:2016 is not "reference material" or "industry best practice"; it is part of 21 CFR 820 and is legally mandatory
  • FDA investigators can cite ISO 13485 clauses directly when issuing FDA 483 observations
  • Both ISO 13485:2016 and Clause 3 (terms and definitions) of ISO 9000:2015 are incorporated by reference
  • ANSI (American National Standards Institute) provides a free read-only version of ISO 13485:2016, in line with the incorporation by reference requirements of 1 CFR Part 51

Important Warning: Transition Obligations for Existing Manufacturers

The final rule was published on February 2, 2024, but becomes effective on February 2, 2026, giving a full two-year transition period.

Obligations during the transition period:

  • February 2, 2024 to February 1, 2026: manufacturers must still comply with the old QSR (you cannot "drop" QSR compliance early)
  • However, if you establish a QMSR-compatible system early, FDA does not object to early adoption
  • From February 2, 2026: the old QSR text is formally retired and the QMSR is fully in effect

For a CDMO, this means that if a client conducts a quality audit in 2025, the site may be asked to demonstrate both compliance with the old QSR and QMSR readiness.

Tutorial Commentary

Analysis of the Structural Change: From 15 Subparts to 2 Subparts

The old QSR was like a building with 15 rooms, each room (subpart) containing rules written by FDA itself. The QMSR rebuilt the building to keep only 2 rooms (Subpart A and Subpart B), but posted a notice at the entrance: "For the content of the other rooms, please read the book ISO 13485:2016."

Subpart A (General Provisions) contains:

  • §820.1 Scope
  • §820.3 Definitions
  • §820.7 Incorporation by Reference

Subpart B (Supplementary Provisions) contains the requirements that FDA considered ISO 13485 insufficient on and that needed to be added:

  • §820.10 Requirements for a Quality Management System (QMS Requirements)
  • §820.35 Control of Records: removes the exemption for audit records
  • §820.45 Device Labeling and Packaging Controls: mandates physical label inspection

Key Warning: ISO 13485 Certification Does Not Mean Exemption from FDA Inspection

This is the most common misunderstanding about the QMSR and must be communicated clearly in inspection preparation:

Even if your site holds a valid ISO 13485:2016 certificate, FDA still has the authority to inspect under 21 CFR Part 820, and the inspection results can lead to an FDA 483 and a Warning Letter.

The only exception is MDSAP (Medical Device Single Audit Program). Sites participating in MDSAP can substitute an MDSAP audit report for a routine FDA inspection, but this is a separate program; the exemption is not provided by ISO 13485 certification itself.

In the preamble to the final rule, FDA states explicitly that ISO 13485 certification is a useful quality indicator but does not constitute a legal basis for compliance.

§820.45 Label Inspection: Why Is Automation Not Enough?

This FDA-specific requirement deserves particular attention: ISO 13485 only requires label inspection, not "physical" inspection. FDA added to the QMSR that label inspection must include physical inspection of a representative sample by personnel. Even if you have a fully automated vision system, a manual verification step is still required.

Imagine the plant's labeling machine has a vision system that confirms the UDI barcode. The QMSR requires that, before release, QC personnel must also pick up a few units and visually confirm that the label is correct. The vision system and the manual inspection are additive, not substitutes for each other.

0.3 Who Must Comply — Scope of Applicability & Transition Timeline

Original Text

21 CFR § 820.1 — Who Is Covered:

"The requirements in this part apply to the design, manufacture, packaging, labeling, storage, installation, and servicing of all finished devices intended for human use. Manufacturers engaged in only some of these operations need only comply with those requirements applicable to those operations."
— 21 CFR § 820.1(a) (QMSR)

Entities Required to Comply with QMSR:

  • Finished device manufacturers — any entity that designs, manufactures, fabricates, assembles, or processes a finished medical device (Class I, II, or III) intended for commercial distribution in the United States
  • Specification developers — entities that develop device specifications but contract out manufacturing
  • Contract manufacturers (CMOs/CDMOs) — manufacturers who produce finished devices or device constituent parts under contract, including assemblers, packagers, repackagers, re-labelers, and relabelers
  • Contract sterilizers — entities performing sterilization as a service on finished devices
  • Remanufacturers — entities that substantially change the finished device from its original performance
  • Initial distributors of foreign manufacturers — U.S. importers who are the initial receivers of foreign-manufactured devices

Entities NOT Required to Comply (but encouraged):

  • Component or parts manufacturers — companies that supply components (e.g., rubber stoppers, glass vials, needle assemblies as raw components) but do not manufacture finished devices. FDA does not require these entities to comply with Part 820, though it encourages use of QMSR as guidance.
  • Blood and blood products — regulated under 21 CFR Parts 606 and 610, not Part 820
  • Human tissue (HCT/Ps) regulated as HCT/Ps — subject to Part 1271 unless regulated as a device

Tutorial Commentary

Who Must Comply with the QMSR? A Practical Decision Framework

There are only two core questions for deciding whether the QMSR applies:

  1. Do you manufacture a "finished device"?
    • Yes → you must comply with the QMSR
    • No (you only supply parts/components) → not mandatory, but compliance is recommended
  2. Is your device intended to be marketed in the US?
    • Yes → the QMSR applies even if the site is outside the US
    • No (exported solely to other markets) → 21 CFR Part 820 does not apply (but other countries' regulations may apply)

The Special Status of Contract Manufacturers (CDMO/CMO)

This is one of the core concepts that a sterile injectables CDMO most needs to master.

The CDMO's dual obligations: if your CDMO accepts contract production of a "finished device" (for example, a fully filled, sealed, sterile pre-filled syringe), you are a "manufacturer" within the meaning of the QMSR, regardless of who the client is or whose brand it is.

QMSR scope of work applicable to a CDMO (depending on the contract scope):

  • Filling, sealing and aseptic processing → production and process controls (ISO 13485 §7.5)
  • Labeling and packaging operations → §820.45 physical label inspection
  • Final release → §820.35 record requirements
  • Client-designed devices → may involve design transfer documentation (MDF)

Analogy: Regulatory Responsibility of a Contract Manufacturer

Imagine a well-known brand A contracts factory B to produce pre-filled syringes (filled with drug product). Brand A designs the device, holds the 510(k) and is responsible for the marketing authorization.

But factory B is still a "manufacturer" under the QMSR and must establish and maintain a quality system that complies with ISO 13485:2016. If FDA inspects factory B and finds inadequate process controls, factory B can receive an FDA 483 or even a Warning Letter, even though every product carries A's brand name.

A contract cannot "transfer" a manufacturer's QMSR obligations.

Recommended Transition Strategy (Practical Perspective)

An effective QMSR transition plan should include the following steps (we recommend completing them before February 2, 2026):

  1. Gap analysis: compare the ISO 13485:2016 clauses against the current procedures under the old QSR, and identify gaps in documentation and practice
  2. Terminology update: DHF → MDF, DMR → MDF, DHR → relevant production records; update all SOPs, forms and training materials
  3. Implementation of new requirements：
    • Establish a Quality Manual (if you do not yet have one)
    • Update the §820.45 physical label inspection procedure
    • §820.35 removal of the audit record exemption: confirm that management review and internal audit records are within the scope FDA can access
  4. Training: all personnel should understand the architectural changes in the QMSR, especially QMS managers and regulatory affairs staff
  5. Updated declarations: update the statements on ISO 13485:2016 and 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR in supplier questionnaires and client Quality Agreements

0.4 Combination Products & CDMO Relevance — Cross-Regulatory Obligations

Original Text

Combination Products — Regulatory Framework:

"A combination product is a product comprised of two or more regulated components, i.e., drug/device, biologic/device, drug/biologic, or drug/device/biologic, that are physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or mixed and produced as a single entity."
— 21 CFR § 3.2(e)(1)

21 CFR Part 4 — Regulation of Combination Products:

FDA's Office of Combination Products (OCP) administers 21 CFR Part 4. When a combination product contains both a drug (or biologic) constituent part and a device constituent part, both sets of CGMP regulations may apply. The QMSR final rule made conforming edits to 21 CFR Part 4 to replace QSR references with QMSR references.

"For a combination product that includes a device constituent part and a drug or biological product constituent part, the manufacturer shall comply with the current good manufacturing practice requirements applicable to the constituent part that has the primary mode of action for the combination product, as well as the current good manufacturing practice requirements for the other constituent parts, as provided in this part."
— 21 CFR § 4.4(a) (paraphrased)

Dual CGMP Compliance for Drug-Device Combinations:

"In case of any conflict between the requirements of this part and the requirements of ISO 13485, the requirements of this part, as interpreted consistent with the FD&C Act and other applicable FDA regulations, control."
— 21 CFR § 820.1(b) (paraphrased from QMSR; verify against eCFR)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Dual CGMP Obligations for Combination Products

This is the regulatory logic a sterile injectables CDMO most needs to understand deeply, because many modern biologic products (monoclonal antibodies, insulin, vaccines and so on) are marketed as pre-filled syringes or autoinjectors.

Key determination: Primary Mode of Action (PMOA)

FDA uses the PMOA to decide which CGMP system is the "primary" one:

  • If the therapeutic effect comes mainly from the drug component (such as insulin) → 21 CFR Parts 210/211 are the primary regulations
  • If the therapeutic effect comes mainly from the device design (such as the mechanical expansion of a drug-eluting stent) → 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR is the primary regulation

But whichever is "primary", the other regulation still applies. This is the "dual CGMP obligation."

CDMO Floor Alert: Trigger Points for Dual Obligations in Pre-Filled Syringes

A common misunderstanding among sterile injectables CDMOs: "I only fill the drug solution; the syringe barrel is supplied by the client, so I do not need to worry about Part 820."

This is wrong. The following situations all trigger QMSR obligations:

  • After the CDMO performs fill-finish, the final product is a "filled pre-filled syringe", which is the device constituent part of a combination product as defined in 21 CFR § 3.2(e)
  • If the CDMO is the sole manufacturer of the combination product (that is, it makes both the drug and the device constituent part) → it must comply with both 21 CFR 211 and Part 820 QMSR
  • Even if the client is responsible for the 510(k) or PMA, the CDMO is still a "manufacturer" under Part 820

Practical compliance recommendation: in the Quality Agreement, define clearly which party is responsible for which part of the QMSR requirements (especially responsibility for maintaining the MDF).

CDMO Application Scenario

Scenario: a Taiwanese sterile injectables CDMO is contracted to fill a monoclonal antibody drug into a Uniject™ prefillable autoinjection system, which is handed to the client for labeling and marketing in the US. The client holds the BLA (Biologics License Application) and the CDMO holds the DMF (Drug Master File).

The CDMO's regulatory obligations matrix:

  • 21 CFR Parts 210/211: filling, aseptic processing, environmental monitoring and final release. This is the primary CGMP (the drug is the PMOA)
  • 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR: quality management of the device constituent part (the autoinjection system), covering the purchasing controls (Clause 7.4) and production and service provision (Clause 7.5) required by ISO 13485
  • 21 CFR Part 4: confirm how the dual CGMP framework applies and clarify the division of responsibilities with the client
  • 21 CFR Part 830: confirm whether the UDI requirements apply and how they are presented on the label
  • ISO 13485 §7.4: purchasing control documentation for the Uniject™ system supplier

Inspection preparation focus: if an FDA inspection focuses on the device constituent part, investigators will review the design transfer documentation (MDF-related information obtained from the supplier), incoming acceptance records for the device constituent part, §820.45 physical label inspection records, and the explicit agreement on QMSR responsibilities in the Quality Agreement.

The FD&C Act Takes Precedence over ISO 13485: What Happens in a Conflict?

The FD&C Act precedence clause in §820.1(b) is very important; it describes the legal status of ISO 13485 within the QMSR framework:

Although ISO 13485 is "incorporated by reference" into 21 CFR Part 820, it is not the source of US law; the FD&C Act and FDA regulations are. When a requirement in ISO 13485 conflicts with a specific FDA regulation, the FDA requirement prevails.

Example: ISO 13485's label inspection requirement is less strict than FDA §820.45. In that case the §820.45 requirement prevails: even if you fully comply with ISO 13485, failing to perform physical label inspection is still an FDA violation.

This principle also applies to the following: ISO 13485 uses "safety and performance", whereas FDA interprets this as "safety and effectiveness", the latter being the standard language of US law.

Section Summary

  • The essence of QSR → QMSR: the FDA 2024 final rule (89 FR 7496) renamed 21 CFR Part 820 as the QMSR. The core change is that §820.7 incorporates by reference ISO 13485:2016 and Clause 3 of ISO 9000:2015, giving these two international standards the same legal force as CFR text. The effective date is February 2, 2026.
  • A much leaner architecture: of the old QSR's 15 subparts (Subparts A–O), Subparts C–O are all marked "Reserved", and the substantive requirements moved to ISO 13485. The QMSR keeps Subpart A (General Provisions: §§820.1, 820.3, 820.7) and Subpart B (Supplementary Provisions: §§820.10, 820.35, 820.45).
  • FDA-specific additional requirements cannot be ignored: three FDA-specific requirements go beyond the basic requirements of ISO 13485. §820.35 removes the exemption for management review and audit records (FDA can now review these records); §820.45 requires physical inspection of representative samples of labels (an automated system cannot fully replace it); and §820.10 requires that "applicable regulatory requirements" include all relevant FDA regulations.
  • Who must comply: manufacturers of finished devices (Class I/II/III), specification developers, contract manufacturers (CDMO/CMO), contract sterilizers, remanufacturers and the initial US importers of foreign manufacturers must all comply with the QMSR. Component-only manufacturers are not required to, but compliance is recommended.
  • Dual CGMP obligations for combination products: combination products containing a device constituent part (such as drug-filled pre-filled syringes and autoinjectors) must comply with both 21 CFR Parts 210/211 (drug CGMP) and 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR (for the device constituent part); how they apply is determined by the 21 CFR Part 4 framework.
  • Key CDMO points on site: a sterile injectables CDMO that performs fill-finish of a combination product is a device manufacturer within the meaning of the QMSR. The Quality Agreement must define the division of QMSR responsibilities, especially responsibility for maintaining the MDF (Medical Device File), the application of ISO 13485 purchasing controls (Clause 7.4) to suppliers of the device constituent part, and the execution records for §820.45 physical label inspection.
  • ISO 13485 certification does not mean exemption from FDA inspection: holding ISO 13485:2016 certification only shows conformity to an international standard; it does not remove FDA's authority to inspect under 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR. The only thing that can replace a routine FDA inspection is an audit result from the MDSAP program. The FD&C Act and FDA regulations take precedence over ISO 13485 in any conflict.

1.1 §820.1 — Scope

Original Text

§820.1(a) — General

(1) Current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements are set forth in this quality system regulation. The requirements in this part govern the methods used in, and the facilities and controls used for, the design, manufacture, packaging, labeling, storage, installation, and servicing of all finished devices intended for human use. The requirements in this part are intended to ensure that finished devices will be safe and effective and otherwise in compliance with the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (the act). This part establishes basic requirements applicable to manufacturers of finished medical devices.

(2) If a manufacturer engages in only some operations subject to the requirements in this part, and not in others, that manufacturer need only comply with those requirements applicable to the operations in which it is engaged.

(3) With respect to class I devices, design controls apply only to those devices listed in §820.30(a)(2). This regulation does not apply to manufacturers of components or parts of finished devices, but such manufacturers are encouraged to use appropriate provisions of this regulation as guidance. Manufacturers of human blood and blood components are not subject to this part, but are subject to part 606 of this chapter.

§820.1(b) — Responsibility of manufacturers
Each manufacturer shall be responsible for establishing and maintaining an adequate quality system with the organizational structure, responsibilities, procedures, processes, and adequate resources for assuring that devices will be safe and effective and otherwise in compliance with this part.

§820.1(c) — Applicability
The quality system regulation applies to any finished device manufacturer who imports, exports, repackages, or relabels a device. A manufacturer of a finished medical device must comply with this part regardless of where the device is manufactured.

§820.1(d) — Foreign manufacturers
If a manufacturer who offers devices for import into the United States refuses to permit or allow the completion of a Food and Drug Administration inspection of the foreign facility for the purpose of determining compliance with this part, it shall appear for purposes of section 801(a) of the act, that the methods used in, and the facilities and controls used for, the design, manufacture, packaging, labeling, storage, installation, or servicing of any devices produced at such facility that are offered for import into the United States do not conform to the requirements of section 520(f) of the act and this part and that the devices manufactured at that facility are adulterated.

§820.1(e) — Exemptions or variances
Any person who wishes to petition for an exemption or variance from any device quality system requirement is subject to the requirements of section 520(f)(2) of the act. Petitions for an exemption or variance shall be submitted according to the procedures set forth in §10.30 of this chapter, the FDA's administrative procedures. Guidance documents on the exemption procedures are available from CDRH.

Tutorial Commentary

Four Key Points on the Scope of §820.1

1. Who is covered: manufacturers of "Finished Devices"
Part 820 has jurisdiction over manufacturers of "finished devices". Component manufacturers are not subject to mandatory application, although FDA encourages them to use Part 820 as guidance. This is where many people go wrong: if you only make O-rings or electronic chips, Part 820 is not mandatory for you.

2. Geographic scope: it applies if the device is sold in the US
§820.1(c) states explicitly that wherever the device is manufactured, the manufacturer must comply with Part 820 if the device is marketed in the US. This means medical device manufacturers in Taiwan, China and Europe fall under FDA's QMSR jurisdiction as long as they export to the US.

3. Class I design control exemption
Class I devices are generally exempt from design controls (§820.30), with exceptions: §820.30(a)(2) lists specific Class I devices that still require design controls (such as certain sterilizers and pacemaker testers). Class II and Class III devices all require full design controls.

4. The "any manufacturing activity" principle
§820.1(a)(2) provides that if a manufacturer takes part in only some manufacturing activities, it only needs to comply with the requirements for the relevant activities. For example, if a company only does final packaging and no manufacturing, it only needs to comply with the Part 820 provisions on packaging and labeling.

Common Pitfall: Who Counts as a "Manufacturer"?

Part 820's definition of "Manufacturer" is surprisingly broad, and the following roles may all be included:

  • Specification Developer: even if it does not produce anything itself, a company that designs the device specifications and outsources production is a "Manufacturer" under Part 820
  • Remanufacturer: an entity that significantly modifies or refurbishes an original device in a way that changes its performance specifications
  • Contract Sterilizer: a supplier that is only responsible for sterilization may also be subject to Part 820
  • Relabeler: an entity that relabels a device made by another party under its own brand

See the definition in §820.3(o) for details.

QMSR Update (2024 Final Rule)

Under the QMSR final rule, §820.1 was revised to bring ISO 13485:2016 into the US regulatory framework through "incorporation by reference". Practical implications:

  • Manufacturers with ISO 13485:2016 certification can demonstrate Part 820 compliance more easily
  • FDA investigators will evaluate against the ISO 13485 requirements
  • The scope logic of §820.1 is essentially unchanged, but the definition architecture has moved to ISO 13485

Tutorial Commentary

Regulatory Implications of the "Complaint" Definition

The complaint definition in §820.3(b) has three key elements:

  1. Written, electronic or oral: oral complaints count too! A telephone complaint must be recorded in writing
  2. Alleging "deficiencies": a user's subjective dissatisfaction is not necessarily a complaint; it must relate to a problem with the device itself
  3. "After released for distribution": this is the time threshold. A nonconformance during manufacturing is not a complaint; it falls under CAPA management

Pitfall: if someone calls to say "the instructions for use are unclear", is that a Complaint? Yes, if the deficiency in the instructions could affect the safe use of the device.

The Breadth of the "Component" Definition

The Component definition in §820.3(d) includes software and firmware, which is different from the concept of an "ingredient" in drug GMP. A medical software app that is part of a medical device is a "component" and must be subject to design controls.

QMSR note: ISO 13485 uses "product" rather than "component", but the concept covers a similar scope.

"Establish" = Define + Document + Implement

The definition of "Establish" in §820.3(l) is very important: whenever the regulation requires you to "establish" a procedure, you must do all of the following:

  • Define: specify clearly what is to be done
  • Document: write it down (paper or electronic)
  • Implement: actually carry it out

Having only an SOP without implementing it means the procedure is not "Established". During an FDA inspection, showing documents alone is not enough; you must demonstrate actual execution.

Analogy: DMR vs DHR vs DHF — A Restaurant Analogy

Use a restaurant to understand the difference between the three records:

  • DMR (Device Master Record) = the recipe book: specifies how the dish "should" be made, including ingredient specifications, cooking procedures and quality standards. It is a permanent specification document.
  • DHR (Device History Record) = the daily record of dishes served: records how this batch of dishes was "actually" made today, including which ingredients were used (lot numbers), who made it, when, and the QC results. There is one for every batch.
  • DHF (Design History File) = the dish development file: records how the dish "was designed", from the initial concept, trial records and improved versions to the final design. It is usually archived once development is complete.

Important Pitfall: "Manufacturer" Includes Contract Sterilizers and Relabelers

The definition of "Manufacturer" in §820.3(o) is one of the most important pitfalls in Part 820. The following roles are all explicitly listed as a "Manufacturer":

  • Contract Sterilizer: accepts contracts only for EO/radiation/steam sterilization and takes no part in other manufacturing steps, yet is still subject to Part 820

Significance under the QMSR: under the QMSR framework, the integration of risk management (ISO 14971) goes deeper. Manufacturers must demonstrate risk-based decision-making in design controls, CAPA, supplier management and other areas.

Quality, §820.3(s): centered on "fitness for use", including safety and performance. This is the goal definition of "quality".

Quality System, §820.3(v): the sum of "organizational structure + responsibilities + procedures + processes + resources", and the means of achieving quality.

The QMSR renames the "Quality System" as the "Quality Management System (QMS)" to align with ISO 13485 terminology, but the conceptual architecture is the same.

1.4 DHF / DHR / DMR — Comparing the Three Key Documents

  • (b) Production process specifications including the appropriate equipment specifications, production methods, production procedures, and production environment specifications;
  • (c) Quality assurance procedures and specifications including acceptance criteria and the quality assurance equipment to be used;
  • (d) Packaging and labeling specifications, including methods and processes used; and
  • (e) Installation, maintenance, and servicing procedures and methods.

  • (a) The dates of manufacture;
  • (b) The quantity manufactured;
  • (c) The quantity released for distribution;
  • (d) The acceptance records which demonstrate the device is manufactured in accordance with the DMR;

Tutorial Commentary

The Three Most Easily Confused Relationships

DHF vs DMR：

The DHF is the "past tense": a record of the design decision history, including all rejected options, design review meeting minutes and intermediate versions. The DMR is the "present tense": the currently effective manufacturing specification, a controlled document that requires version control and approval.

The DHF answers "why was it designed this way"; the DMR answers "how should it be made".

DMR vs DHR：

The DMR is the "requirement"; the DHR is the "execution record". The DHR must be able to prove that "actual manufacturing conformed to the DMR". During an FDA inspection, a DHR that cites one DMR version while actual manufacturing used a different version is a serious deficiency.

DHF vs DHR：

The DHF is created during the design phase and, in principle, stops growing once the design is frozen (it is updated only for design changes). A DHR is created anew for each production run and keeps accumulating.

Common FDA 483 Deficiencies for the DHF/DMR/DHR

  • DHF deficiencies: incomplete design verification records; design reviews without the participation of an independent person; design inputs that are not measurable (vague requirements)
  • DMR deficiencies: specifications not updated to reflect the actual process; cross-referenced documents that cannot be traced; missing software specifications
  • DHR deficiencies: incomplete batch records; not all acceptance/rejection decisions recorded; batches that do not correspond to a DMR version

When Is It a "Device"? The Decision Logic for Part 820 Applicability

Whether a product falls under Part 820 depends on the following decision chain:

Step 1: Is it a "Medical Device" (as defined in FD&C Act §201(h))? If yes, continue.

Step 2: Is it a "Finished Device" (§820.3(m))? If yes, Part 820 applies in full.

Step 3 (combination products): If it contains a drug or biological product component, confirm the PMOA:

  • PMOA = Device → Part 820 applies primarily, with Part 211 applying as a supplement
  • PMOA = Drug/Biologic → Part 211 applies primarily, with certain provisions of Part 820 applying as a supplement

Common examples of combination products: pre-filled syringes (Device + Drug), drug-eluting stents (Device + Drug), insulin autoinjector pens (Device + Drug) and CAR-T cell therapy (Biologic + Device).

CDMO Special Note: Dual Compliance Obligations for Combination Products

For a contract manufacturer (CDMO), if the client's product is a combination product, you need to confirm in particular:

  • Has the client obtained a PMOA determination from OCP? You should ask the client to provide the RFD result
  • If the PMOA is Drug, does your facility comply with both Part 211 and the relevant Part 820 provisions (design controls, CAPA, complaints)?
  • Part 820 + Part 211 dual inspection: FDA may send investigators from both CDRH and CDER to inspect jointly
  • Enhanced batch record requirements: you must meet the information requirements of both the DHR (device perspective) and the Batch Record (drug perspective)

Practical Application: Part 820 Obligations of a CDMO Performing Contract Fill

Scenario: a CDMO in Taiwan provides drug coating and final packaging services for a client's drug-eluting stent (DES). The client has confirmed that the PMOA of the DES is Device (the metal stent provides the primary therapeutic function).

Minimum Part 820 obligations of this CDMO:

  • §820.1(a)(2) partial activity principle: it only needs to comply with the Part 820 provisions relevant to the activities it takes part in (coating, packaging)
  • DMR management: it must hold, or have access to, the sections of the DMR relevant to its work (coating specifications, packaging specifications)
  • DHR creation: it must create a DHR for every coating/packaging run, recording: operation date, batch size, operators, drug lot numbers used, coating thickness measurement records and package integrity test records
  • Process controls (§820.70): the coating process must have a written SOP and process validation that meets the DMR specifications
  • Equipment controls (§820.72): all coating and measurement equipment needs a calibration program
  • Supplier management (§820.50): suppliers of drug coating materials must be on the approved supplier list and have a quality agreement
  • Complaint management (§820.198): if the CDMO receives any complaint about its coating work, it must handle it under the Part 820 complaint handling procedure

Part 211 requirements this CDMO also needs (because the product contains a drug component):

  • Component testing and release of the coating drug (§211.84)
  • Aseptic process controls (if the coating process takes place in an aseptic environment)

Practical recommendation: CDMOs of this type usually establish an "integrated QMS" that complies with both ISO 13485 (device) and ISO 9001 + GMP (drug), and define the respective obligations of the client and the CDMO explicitly in the quality agreement.

Tutorial Commentary

Changes to the CAPA Architecture under the QMSR / ISO 13485

Under the QMSR framework (aligned with ISO 13485), there is an important conceptual shift in how CAPA is handled:

  • ISO 13485 §8.5 manages corrective action and preventive action separately (unlike some QMSs that merge the two)
  • ISO 13485 places more emphasis on a "risk-proportionate response": a low-risk CA can follow a simplified procedure, while a high-risk one requires a formal CAPA

Subsection 1 — §820.4: QMS Requirements (Regulatory Text)

§820.4 — Quality Management System Requirements

Text effective February 2, 2026 (21 CFR Part 820 QMSR Final Rule)

Each manufacturer subject to this part must establish, implement, and maintain a quality management system that meets the requirements of ISO 13485:2016, Medical devices — Quality management systems — Requirements for regulatory purposes, incorporated by reference in §820.1(b), as supplemented by the requirements in Appendix A of this part.

What "Incorporated by Reference" Means Legally

Under the Administrative Procedure Act (5 U.S.C. 552(a)) and 1 CFR Part 51, when FDA incorporates a document by reference into the Code of Federal Regulations, that document carries the full legal force of federal regulation. Compliance with ISO 13485:2016 is therefore not voluntary guidance — it is a federal legal requirement for all manufacturers subject to 21 CFR Part 820.

The Office of the Federal Register publishes availability notices when standards are incorporated. Manufacturers must use the specific version cited in §820.1(b) — ISO 13485:2016 — regardless of whether newer versions of the standard are released.

Scope of Application

§820.4 applies to manufacturers of finished devices intended for commercial distribution in the United States. The term "manufacturer" is defined in §820.3 and includes any person who designs, manufactures, fabricates, assembles, or processes a finished device. Contract manufacturers (CDMOs), specification developers, and repackagers are all potentially subject depending on their role.

Structure: ISO 13485 + Appendix A

The QMSR operates as a two-layer framework:

  • Layer 1 — ISO 13485:2016: The baseline QMS standard providing all general requirements for documentation, management responsibility, resources, product realization, and measurement/analysis/improvement.
  • Layer 2 — Appendix A: FDA-specific supplemental requirements that either modify ISO 13485 provisions or add requirements that have no ISO 13485 equivalent. Where Appendix A and ISO 13485 differ, Appendix A controls.

Commentary — §820.4 Core Provisions

The Core Architecture of the QMSR in One Sentence

The logic of §820.4 is very simple: "You must establish a QMS that conforms to ISO 13485:2016 and also meet the FDA-specific requirements in Appendix A." This is the backbone of the entire 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR.

The former QSR (Quality System Regulation, 2002 edition) listed detailed requirements directly. The new QMSR (final rule issued 2022, effective February 2, 2026) instead uses "incorporation by reference" to bring ISO 13485 into the federal regulations as a whole.

What Is "Incorporation by Reference"?

Put simply: FDA has "printed" the ISO 13485:2016 document into the US federal regulations. Legally, it has exactly the same effect as listing the requirements clause by clause directly in the CFR.

Practical implications:

  • A company that does not follow ISO 13485:2016 violates the federal regulation 21 CFR Part 820 and may face an FDA Warning Letter, an import ban, or seizure of devices
  • Court decisions, FDA 483 observations, or Warning Letters can all cite specific clauses of ISO 13485 as the basis for a violation
  • The specific version referenced (ISO 13485:2016) must be used, even if ISO later publishes an updated version

Analogy: Standard Highway Rules plus State-Specific Additions

Imagine ISO 13485:2016 as the federal highway traffic code, which applies to everyone. Appendix A is California's (= FDA's) additional rules for vehicles driving within California (such as special emission standards). Both sets of rules must be followed, and where the two conflict, the California rules take precedence.

For a CDMO, you serve both "all customers on the highway" (ISO 13485 customers) and "customers entering California" (products marketed in the US). The latter must comply with both sets of rules.

Important: Which Version of ISO 13485 Applies?

You must use ISO 13485:2016 (3rd edition, published March 1, 2016). If ISO publishes ISO 13485:2026 or a later version in the future, the legally required version for FDA remains the 2016 edition until FDA amends the version cited in §820.1(b). This "version freeze" feature deserves particular attention in the early period of QMSR implementation.

Subsection 2 — ISO 13485:2016 Sections 4, 5, and 6

Section 4 — Quality Management System

4.1 General Requirements: The organization shall establish, document, implement, and maintain a QMS and maintain its effectiveness in accordance with the requirements of the standard. The organization shall document its QMS in a manner that ensures the ability of devices and services to meet requirements and to facilitate improvement of the QMS.

4.2 Documentation Requirements:

  • 4.2.1 General: QMS documentation shall include a documented quality policy and quality objectives; a quality manual; documented procedures required by the standard; documents needed to ensure the effective planning, operation, and control of processes; records required by the standard; and any other documentation specified by applicable regulatory requirements.
  • 4.2.2 Quality Manual: Must include the scope of the QMS with justification for any exclusions; documented procedures or references to them; a description of the interaction between QMS processes; and a description of the structure of the documentation used in the QMS.
  • 4.2.3 Medical Device File (Technical File/DHF): Each device or family of devices must have a dedicated medical device file that includes documents providing a description of the device, its intended use, labeling, and specifications; procedures and records relevant to the device including design and development, production, testing, and post-market activities.
  • 4.2.4 Control of Documents: Procedures for approving documents for adequacy, reviewing/updating and re-approving, identifying changes and revision status, ensuring current versions at point of use, ensuring readability, identifying external documents, and preventing unintended use of obsolete documents.
  • 4.2.5 Control of Records: Records established to provide evidence of conformity and effective operation of the QMS shall be controlled. Retention period must be at least equivalent to the lifetime of the device as defined by the manufacturer, but not less than 2 years from the date of product release by the manufacturer, or as specified by applicable regulatory requirements.

Section 5 — Management Responsibility

5.1 Management Commitment: Top management shall provide evidence of its commitment to development and implementation of the QMS and maintaining its effectiveness by communicating regulatory requirements, establishing quality policy and objectives, conducting management reviews, and ensuring availability of resources.

5.3 Quality Policy: Top management shall ensure the quality policy is appropriate to the organization's purpose; includes a commitment to comply with requirements and to maintain the effectiveness of the QMS; provides a framework for quality objectives; is communicated and understood at appropriate levels; and is reviewed for continuing suitability.

5.4 Planning: 5.4.1 Quality objectives must be established at relevant functions and levels, be measurable, consistent with the quality policy, and include those needed to meet product requirements. 5.4.2 Quality management system planning must maintain the integrity of the QMS when changes are planned and implemented.

5.5 Responsibility, Authority, and Communication: Top management shall define and communicate responsibilities and authorities. A management representative must be appointed with authority and responsibility for ensuring QMS processes are established and maintained, reporting on QMS performance, and promoting awareness of applicable regulatory requirements and QMS requirements throughout the organization.

5.6 Management Review: Top management shall review the QMS at planned intervals to ensure its continuing suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness. Records of management reviews shall be maintained.

Management review inputs must include: feedback, complaint handling, reporting to regulatory authorities, audit results, monitoring and measurement of processes and products, corrective and preventive actions, follow-up actions from previous reviews, changes that could affect the QMS, and recommendations for improvement.

Management review outputs must include decisions and actions related to: improvement needed to maintain the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the QMS; improvement of product related to customer requirements; and resource needs.

Section 6 — Resource Management

6.1 Provision of Resources: The organization shall determine and provide the resources needed to implement and maintain the QMS, maintain its effectiveness, and meet applicable regulatory requirements and customer requirements.

6.2 Human Resources: Personnel performing work affecting product quality shall be competent based on appropriate education, training, skills, and experience. The organization shall determine the necessary competence, provide training or take other actions to achieve necessary competence, evaluate the effectiveness of actions taken, ensure personnel are aware of the relevance and importance of their activities, and maintain appropriate records of education, training, skills, and experience.

6.3 Infrastructure: The organization shall determine, provide, and maintain infrastructure needed to achieve product conformity. Infrastructure includes buildings, workspace, and associated utilities; process equipment (hardware and software); and supporting services such as information systems.

6.4 Work Environment and Contamination Control: The organization shall determine and manage the work environment needed to achieve conformity to product requirements. Requirements include documented requirements for health, cleanliness, clothing of personnel if contact with product or work environment could adversely affect product quality. Arrangements for controlling contaminated or potentially contaminated products shall be documented to prevent contamination of other products, the work environment, or personnel.

Commentary — ISO 13485 §4–6 in Detail

Where ISO 13485 §4–6 Sit in the QMS Architecture

ISO 13485:2016 as a whole has 8 clauses, structured as follows:

  • §1–3: Scope, normative references, terms and definitions (not requirements)
  • §4: QMS foundation (focus of this section: document and record control)
  • §5: Management responsibility (focus of this section: top management commitment, quality policy, management review)
  • §6: Resource management (focus of this section: personnel, facilities, work environment)
  • §7: Product realization (design and development, purchasing, production)
  • §8: Measurement, analysis and improvement (CAPA, internal audit)

§4–6 form the "infrastructure" of the QMS, while §7–8 are the "execution layer" operating on top of that foundation.

The Four Tiers of the §4.2 Documentation System

A complete ISO 13485 documentation system is usually divided into four tiers:

  • Tier 1: Quality Manual (QM) — describes the whole QMS and is usually made available externally
  • Tier 2: Procedures (SOP/QP) — describe cross-functional processes: "what to do and who does it"
  • Tier 3: Work instructions (WI/SWI) — describe operating details: "how to do it"
  • Tier 4: Forms and records — written evidence of the results of execution

Documents (Tiers 1–3) and records (Tier 4) have different control requirements: documents need version control, approval, and issuance control; records need retention period management.

Analogy: Management Review Is Like a Board's Quarterly Report with a Mandatory Agenda

The management review in ISO 13485 §5.6 is not a meeting that the boss convenes whenever they like and fills with whatever they want to discuss. It is more like the quarterly report to the board of a listed company:

  • Mandatory agenda (inputs): complaints, nonconformities, CAPA status, audit results, regulatory feedback, process performance indicators. All of these must be reported and none can be skipped
  • Mandatory outcomes (outputs): resource decisions, improvement actions, updated quality objectives. These must be turned into concrete decisions and recorded
  • Regulatory significance: during an inspection, FDA investigators will directly ask to see the management review records to confirm that top management is genuinely involved in the QMS

In FDA's eyes, a management review without complete input and output records is the same as one that was "never held."

The Four Steps of Personnel Competence Management under §6.2

ISO 13485 §6.2 requires a clear competence management cycle:

  1. Determine (determine needs): identify the positions that affect quality and list the competence required
  2. Provide (provide training): design a training plan or take other actions
  3. Evaluate (evaluate effectiveness): confirm that training has actually improved competence (not just that attendance sheets were signed)
  4. Maintain records: education, training records, skills, and experience

Common CDMO deficiency: having only training completion records and no effectiveness evaluation (such as test scores or assessment records), which leads to FDA 483 observations.

§6.4 Work Environment and Contamination Control: Special Attention for Sterile Manufacturing

For a sterile medical device CDMO, the work environment requirements of §6.4 are directly tied to facility design:

  • There must be written environmental specifications (temperature, humidity, particulates, microbial limits)
  • If contact between personnel and product could affect quality, there must be written requirements for clothing and health status
  • Procedures for segregating contaminated or potentially contaminated product must be documented to prevent cross-contamination

These requirements overlap with the facility requirements of the 2004 FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance; QMSR §820.4 + ISO 13485 §6.4 are their regulatory basis.

Subsection 3 — Document Control & Record Retention Requirements

ISO 13485 §4.2.4 — Control of Documents

A documented procedure shall be established to define the controls needed for:

  • Approving documents for adequacy prior to issue
  • Reviewing and updating as necessary and re-approving documents
  • Ensuring that changes and the current revision status of documents are identified
  • Ensuring that relevant versions of applicable documents are available at points of use
  • Ensuring that documents remain legible and readily identifiable
  • Ensuring that documents of external origin are identified and their distribution controlled
  • Preventing the unintended use of obsolete documents, and applying suitable identification to them if they are retained for any purpose

The organization shall prevent the deterioration or loss of documents. Changes to documents shall be reviewed and approved either by the original approving function or another designated function that has access to pertinent background information upon which to base its decisions.

ISO 13485 §4.2.5 — Control of Records

Records shall be established and maintained to provide evidence of conformity to requirements and of the effective operation of the QMS. Records shall remain legible, readily identifiable, and retrievable. A documented procedure shall be established to define the controls needed for:

  • Identification of records
  • Storage and protection of records
  • Retrieval of records
  • Retention time of records
  • Disposition of records

The organization shall retain records for at least the period equivalent to the lifetime of the medical device as defined by the organization, but not less than 2 years from the date of product release by the manufacturer, or as otherwise specified by applicable regulatory requirements.

FDA Retention Requirement (Appendix A / QMSR)

FDA's record retention requirement for device history records (DHRs), device master records (DMRs), and quality system records is more specific than ISO 13485's baseline. Under the QMSR, records must be retained for a period equivalent to the design and expected life of the device, but in no case less than 2 years from the date of release of the device for commercial distribution by the manufacturer.

The practical calculation: identify the expected lifetime of the device (e.g., a reusable surgical instrument with 10-year expected service life), add 2 years, and that is the minimum retention period. For many implantable devices with long service lives (cardiac pacemakers, orthopedic implants), this calculation can extend retention requirements to 15–25+ years.

Commentary — Document Control and Record Retention

Seven Elements of Document Control (Required in the §4.2.4 Document Control Procedure)

A document control procedure that meets ISO 13485 §4.2.4 must cover the following seven control points:

  1. Approval: documents must be reviewed and signed off by an authorized person before issue
  2. Review & Update: review periodically and, where necessary, revise and re-approve
  3. Revision Status: clearly indicate the version number, effective date, and a summary of changes
  4. Currency at Point of Use: only the latest version is available at the point of use
  5. Legibility: documents must be clear and identifiable, and must not be blurred
  6. External Documents: customer specifications, regulatory requirements, standards, etc. must be identified and their distribution controlled
  7. Obsolete Documents: old versions must be segregated or clearly marked "obsolete" to prevent unintended use

Formula for Calculating the Record Retention Period

FDA QMSR requirement:

Retention period = MAX(expected device lifetime + 2 years, date of final release + 2 years)

Practical examples:
• Single-use syringe (expected lifetime = 1 use, reasonable shelf life ~3 years)
  → retain at least 2 years from the release date
• Reusable surgical instrument (expected lifetime 10 years)
  → retain 12 years from the release date (10 + 2)
• Heart valve implant (expected lifetime 20 years)
  → retain 22 years from the release date (20 + 2)

ISO 13485 §4.2.5 baseline: the product lifetime defined by the manufacturer, but at least 2 years (from the date of product release)

Conclusion: FDA requirements are usually equal to or stricter than ISO 13485. Follow the FDA requirement.

Distribution Control in Practice

Distribution control of SOPs presents different challenges in paper and electronic systems:

  • Electronic QMS (eQMS): the system automatically ensures that each point of use can access only the current version, and obsolete versions are locked automatically, which makes compliance easier
  • Paper distribution: a distribution list must be maintained to track which persons/departments hold which version; when a version is updated, the old copies must be retrieved or their destruction confirmed
  • External documents (customer specifications/standards): a mechanism is needed to monitor whether customer specifications have been updated, so that the latest version is always used

Common FDA 483 Observations: Document Control

  • Work instructions that are not the current version are used on the production floor (most common)
  • Authority for document review and approval is not clearly defined
  • Electronic record systems lack a complete audit trail (relevant to 21 CFR Part 11)
  • Obsolete and current documents are stored in the same location without effective segregation
  • External documents (such as customer drawings) are not included in the control system

Subsection 4 — FDA Appendix A: FDA-Specific Additions and Modifications

Appendix A to 21 CFR Part 820 contains FDA-specific requirements that supplement ISO 13485:2016. These fall into two categories: (1) requirements that modify or clarify how ISO 13485 provisions apply in the US context, and (2) requirements with no ISO 13485 equivalent that reflect longstanding FDA regulatory priorities.

Design Controls — Mandatory Under FDA, Optional Under ISO 13485

ISO 13485:2016 §7.3 permits manufacturers to exclude design and development requirements if the design and development of medical devices is performed by an outside party or if the design is well established and documented. This exclusion is permissible under the international standard for manufacturers that only produce to customer-owned designs.

FDA's Appendix A removes this option for manufacturers subject to §820.4. Design controls are mandatory for all manufacturers of Class II and Class III devices, and for Class I devices with design specifications. A specification developer or US agent who holds the device master record cannot claim ISO 13485 §7.3 exclusion under QMSR. This is one of the most significant FDA additions.

Complaint Handling — Cross-Reference to §820.198

While ISO 13485 §8.2.2 addresses feedback and §8.5.1 addresses complaint handling in general terms, FDA Appendix A requires compliance with the specific requirements of §820.198, which mandates:

  • A formally designated unit responsible for receiving, reviewing, and evaluating complaints
  • Written procedures for complaint handling
  • Evaluation of every complaint to determine whether a MDR report is required under 21 CFR Part 803
  • Complaint records to be kept at the manufacturing establishment
  • Specific content requirements for complaint records including the device name, lot/control number, date received, name/address of complainant, nature of complaint, and investigation results

MDR (Medical Device Reporting) Cross-References

Appendix A establishes explicit cross-references between QMSR quality system activities and the MDR reporting obligations under 21 CFR Part 803. The QMS must include procedures that identify when events associated with marketed devices constitute reportable events, and that ensure timely reporting within the applicable MDR timeframes (30-day for non-malfunction serious injury/death; 5-day for MDR-reportable events requiring immediate action).

Language Requirements

For devices distributed in the United States, records, labeling, and submissions required under QMSR must be in the English language or have English translations available. This supplements ISO 13485, which does not specify a language requirement. A company whose operational language is not English must ensure English-language versions of device history records, complaint files, and quality records are accessible to FDA investigators during inspections.

Correction and Removal Reporting — Cross-Reference to 21 CFR Part 806

Appendix A establishes that CAPA activities under ISO 13485 §8.5.2 and §8.5.3 must interface with field correction and removal reporting obligations under 21 CFR Part 806. Where a CAPA involves a device already distributed, the QMS must trigger evaluation of whether a §806 report is required.

Commentary — Appendix A FDA-Specific Requirements

Important Warning: The Five Gaps ISO 13485-Certified Companies Most Often Miss

If your company already holds ISO 13485:2016 certification but has not yet assessed QMSR compliance, the following are the most common compliance gaps:

  1. Design controls (§7.3) excluded as not applicable: at the time of ISO 13485 certification, design and development may have been excluded, but FDA does not accept this exclusion. If the company enters the US market, it must establish design control procedures.
  2. Detailed complaint-handling requirements: the requirements of ISO 13485 §8.2.2 are less strict than FDA §820.198, and the company may lack a procedure for evaluating "whether each complaint requires an MDR submission."
  3. MDR trigger mechanism: QMS procedures may have no automatic link from ISO 13485 nonconformity handling (§8.5) to the trigger point for evaluating 21 CFR Part 803 MDR reporting.
  4. Availability of English-language records: if the QMS records of a company in a non-English-speaking country exist only in the local language, FDA will be unable to review them during an inspection.
  5. Recall/corrective action link to Part 806: the ISO 13485 §8.5 CAPA procedure may not include a step to assess whether a Part 806 correction and removal report must be submitted to FDA.

Why Are Design Controls So Important for a CDMO?

The business model of many CDMOs (contract development and manufacturing organizations) is "the customer owns the design, the CDMO handles manufacturing." Under a pure ISO 13485 framework, such a CDMO may choose to exclude the §7.3 design and development requirements.

But under the QMSR framework:

  • If the CDMO holds the Device Master Record (DMR) for a product of a US customer, the CDMO may be considered a party that must maintain design control records, even though the customer "owns" the design
  • If the CDMO makes any change to, or interpretation of, the design specifications during manufacturing, the design control requirements are triggered
  • Recommendation: clearly allocate design control responsibilities with the customer in the Quality Agreement

§820.198 Complaint Files: Minimum Record Content

Each complaint record must contain at least:

  • Device name
  • Date the complaint was received
  • Any device identification (UDI, lot number, serial number)
  • Name and address of the complainant (if available)
  • Nature of the complaint
  • Date of investigation and the investigator
  • Investigation results and corrective actions taken
  • Whether an MDR was submitted (if not, record the reason)

A complaint record system without the above fields will draw an FDA 483 observation even if it complies with ISO 13485.

Analogy: Appendix A Is "FDA's Additional Safeguard Clauses"

If ISO 13485:2016 is a standard contract, Appendix A is the "special additional clauses" that FDA requires for operating in the US market. These clauses reflect the regulatory priorities FDA has built up over many years under the QSR: design controls (the core of PMA review), complaint tracking (the source of MDR triggers), and recall efficiency (rapid notification under Part 806).

From FDA's perspective, these are not an "extra burden" but the minimum baseline for protecting US patient safety.

Subsection 5 — Comparison: ISO 13485 §5/§6 vs. Old QSR §820.20

The original QSR (1996/2002 Quality System Regulation) had a self-contained §820.20 addressing management responsibility. The QMSR replaces those provisions by incorporating ISO 13485 §5 and §6. The table below maps old §820.20 provisions to their QMSR equivalents.

What Stayed the Same

The fundamental obligations of management responsibility did not change materially: top management must be engaged, there must be a management representative, resources must be adequate, management reviews must occur, and quality policies and objectives must be established. The QMSR adopted these existing requirements wholesale via ISO 13485.

What Changed — Key Additions

  • More prescriptive management review: ISO 13485 §5.6 specifies exactly what inputs must be reviewed and what outputs must be produced — more rigorous than old §820.20(c)
  • Competence evaluation: Old §820.20 required "adequate" personnel; ISO 13485 §6.2 requires formal competence determination, training, effectiveness evaluation, and records
  • Work environment: Old QSR addressed this in §820.70(c); ISO 13485 §6.4 consolidates it under resource management
  • Medical Device File: New concept under ISO 13485 §4.2.3 — each device or device family must have a dedicated file aggregating all relevant QMS documents and records

Commentary — Comparison of the Old and New Regulations

Why Understand the Old-versus-New QSR Comparison?

Many device companies that have operated in the US market for years have designed their QMS procedures around the structure of the former QSR (1996/2002). A compliant transition to the QMSR requires you to:

  1. Identify which ISO 13485 clause each legacy QSR procedure maps to
  2. Find the new ISO 13485 requirements (such as §4.2.3 medical device file and §6.4 work environment)
  3. Identify the new FDA Appendix A requirements (such as design controls that cannot be excluded)
  4. Update QMS procedures, train personnel, and update internal audit checklists

The Biggest Substantive Change: Mandatory Inputs/Outputs of Management Review

Former QSR §820.20(c) required "conducting management reviews at defined intervals and maintaining records," but did not specify what the review must cover.

ISO 13485 §5.6 raises the requirements considerably:

Mandatory inputs (at a minimum, must cover):

  • Customer feedback (including complaints)
  • Reporting to regulatory authorities
  • Audit results (including internal audits)
  • Process monitoring and measurement results
  • Product monitoring results
  • CAPA status
  • Follow-up actions from previous management reviews
  • Changes that could affect the QMS
  • Recommendations for improvement

Mandatory outputs (decisions/actions must be produced):

  • Improvements needed to maintain the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the QMS
  • Improvement of product related to customer requirements
  • Resource needs

ISO 13485 §4.2.3 Medical Device File: Explanation of a New Requirement

This is a new requirement with no direct equivalent in the former QSR. The "medical device file" is not a single document but an index or folder structure that compiles all QMS documents and records related to a specific device or device family:

  • Device description, intended use, and labeling
  • Design specifications and test records (DHF elements)
  • References to production procedures and test procedures
  • Installation and servicing procedures (if applicable)
  • Post-market surveillance requirements and records

For a CDMO: the medical device file owned by the customer is not necessarily handed over to the CDMO in full. The Quality Agreement must specify which documents are maintained by whom.

Application Scenario — CDMO ISO 13485 to QMSR Gap Analysis

Scenario: Contract Sterile Device CDMO — ISO 13485 Certified, Entering US Market

Background: SterileMed Manufacturing (hypothetical) is a contract manufacturer based in Singapore. It holds ISO 13485:2016 certification from a Notified Body. Its client base includes EU and Japanese OEMs. It has been approached by a US Class II device company to manufacture their sterile single-use laparoscopic instruments under a Supply Agreement. This will be the CDMO's first US-distributed product.

QMSR Compliance Gap Analysis — Actions Required:

1. Design Controls Assessment

SterileMed's ISO 13485 certification excludes §7.3 because all device designs are customer-supplied. Under QMSR Appendix A, this exclusion is not permitted. Action required: determine the scope of design responsibility. If SterileMed only manufactures to customer-locked specifications without modification, it may argue it is not performing design and development under the QMSR definition. However, if it specifies any manufacturing process parameters that affect design outputs, it must establish design controls. Recommend: negotiate a Design Control Matrix with the US client defining where each party's responsibility begins and ends; document this in the Quality Agreement.

2. Complaint Handling Upgrade to §820.198

SterileMed's existing complaint procedure meets ISO 13485 §8.2.2 but lacks: (a) a formal unit designated for US complaint handling; (b) a documented MDR evaluation step for each US complaint; (c) specific record content fields required by §820.198. Action required: revise complaint procedure to add MDR evaluation logic tree, define responsible US-designated unit (can be the client company under Quality Agreement), align complaint record format with §820.198 requirements.

3. English Language Records

SterileMed's quality records are maintained in English and Mandarin Chinese. For FDA inspection purposes, English versions must be the primary accessible versions. Action required: confirm all Device History Records, CAPA records, complaint files, and management review records are available in English; ensure FDA investigators have access during US inspections.

4. MDR Cross-Reference in QMS Procedures

No existing procedure connects ISO 13485 §8.5 CAPA activities to US MDR reporting timelines. Action required: add a procedure step (or new procedure) that routes US complaint-related CAPAs through a 21 CFR Part 803 evaluation; establish 30-day and 5-day MDR clocks in the QMS.

5. Record Retention Adjustment

SterileMed currently retains records for device lifetime + 2 years per ISO 13485. For the US product (single-use instruments, 3-year shelf life), FDA formula yields: 3 years (device lifetime) + 2 years = minimum 5 years from release. Current retention policy likely already meets this. Action required: verify retention calculation for each product type; if any product has a device lifetime exceeding current ISO-driven retention periods, update retention schedule.

Commentary — CDMO Compliance Gap Analysis Framework

Five-Step Framework for Transitioning from ISO 13485 to the QMSR

For any manufacturer that already holds ISO 13485 certification and wants to enter the US market, we recommend using the following framework for gap analysis:

  1. Step 1: Design control review
    Confirm whether the scope of your ISO 13485 certification includes §7.3. If it is excluded, assess whether FDA still requires it (depending on your actual role in the US). Clearly allocate design control responsibility with your US customer in the Quality Agreement.
  2. Step 2: Complaint procedure upgrade
    Compare against the specific requirements of §820.198, update the complaint procedure to include an MDR evaluation step, and confirm that the record format contains all required fields.
  3. Step 3: Establish the MDR trigger mechanism
    Build a clear pathway in the QMS: "nonconformity/complaint → MDR reporting evaluation → submit on time," and set up a timing mechanism (30 days/5 days).
  4. Step 4: Confirm availability of English-language records
    Confirm that all records that FDA may request during an inspection (DHR, CAPA, complaints, management review) can be provided in English.
  5. Step 5: Re-check record retention periods
    Recalculate the minimum retention period for each product type using the FDA formula, and update the record control procedure.

The Quality Agreement Is a Key Compliance Tool for a CDMO

In the relationship between a CDMO and an OEM, the Quality Agreement is the core document for allocating §820.4 compliance responsibilities. It should clearly specify:

  • Who holds the Device Master Record (DMR)
  • Who is responsible for design controls
  • Who receives and evaluates US market complaints
  • Who is responsible for MDR submissions
  • Who is responsible for Part 806 correction and removal reports
  • Each party's obligation to cooperate during a US FDA inspection

Failing to define these responsibilities in the Quality Agreement is one of the biggest risks a CDMO faces during an FDA inspection.

FDA Inspection Preparation: Document Checklist Related to §820.4

  • Management review procedure and the most recent review records
  • Organization chart and description of responsibilities

Section Summary — Key Takeaways

Core Points — §820.4 and ISO 13485 Integration

  • §820.4 is brief but comprehensive: Manufacturers must meet ISO 13485:2016 (incorporated by reference with legal force) plus FDA Appendix A supplements. The entire QMSR regulatory framework flows from this single provision.
  • ISO 13485 §4 (Documentation): Four-tier document hierarchy; controlled documents require version management, approval, and distribution control; records require retention for device lifetime + 2 years minimum under FDA rules.
  • ISO 13485 §5 (Management): Top management accountability is formalized — quality policy, objectives, management representative, and especially management review with prescribed inputs and outputs. This is substantially more prescriptive than the old QSR.
  • ISO 13485 §6 (Resources): Competence-based training with effectiveness evaluation; infrastructure maintenance; work environment and contamination control documentation.
  • Appendix A FDA additions: Design controls cannot be excluded; complaint handling must meet §820.198 detail level including MDR evaluation; MDR and Part 806 cross-references must be built into QMS procedures; English language records must be available.
  • Document vs. Record distinction: Documents guide future action (mutable, version-controlled); records evidence past action (immutable, retention-controlled). Treating records as documents or vice versa is a common 483 finding.

Key Takeaways

Core Points of §820.4 + ISO 13485 Integration

  • §820.4 is the starting point for everything: once a company is deemed a "manufacturer" under 21 CFR Part 820, it must fully comply with ISO 13485:2016 (legally enforceable) plus the additional FDA requirements in Appendix A.
  • ISO 13485 §4 (documentation system): understand the fundamental difference between controlled documents and records. Documents may be changed but must go through change control; records must not be altered and are managed according to their retention period. FDA record retention requirement: product lifetime + 2 years (or at least 2 years after commercial release, whichever is longer).
  • ISO 13485 §5 (management responsibility): the management review must cover the required inputs and produce records of the required decisions. Missing any one mandatory input constitutes a nonconformity.
  • ISO 13485 §6 (resources): personnel competence management needs the complete four-step cycle (determine needs → provide training → evaluate effectiveness → maintain records); work environment requirements must be documented.
  • The five Appendix A gaps: design controls cannot be excluded, detailed §820.198 complaint requirements, the MDR trigger procedure, the Part 806 link, and availability of English-language records.
  • Special note for CDMOs: clearly allocating design control responsibility through the Quality Agreement is the most important compliance safeguard for a CDMO under the QMSR framework.

Tutorial Commentary

Why Does FDA Mandate Design Controls While ISO 13485 Allows an Exemption?

When ISO 13485:2016 §7.3 governs design control, it allows a manufacturer, with justifiable reasons, to exclude the design control requirements, provided the rationale for the exclusion is documented in the quality manual and shown not to affect quality. A common exclusion scenario: OEM manufacturers that build strictly to customer specifications (customer-specified design).

FDA's position is completely different: anyone who manufactures a Class II or Class III device (and certain Class I devices) must implement design controls, with no room for exemption. This is exactly why FDA Appendix A exists: it explicitly lists the optional clauses of ISO 13485 that are mandatory under the US regulatory framework.

Practical significance: A company that holds both ISO 13485 certification and an FDA 510(k) clearance cannot rely on the ISO 13485 exclusion clause alone to avoid an FDA inspection of design controls.

Analogy: Building a House

Design controls are like the architectural design process; an everyday analogy helps in understanding each stage:

  • User Needs: The homeowner says, "I want a house for four people with two bathrooms, on a budget of NT$5 million." This is a need, not yet a specification.
  • Design Inputs: The architect translates the needs into technical specifications: "floor area ≥ 35 ping, seismic coefficient 0.3g, building materials compliant with fire codes." These are measurable requirements.
  • Design Outputs: Complete architectural drawings, structural calculations, and construction specifications. These are the basis for construction.
  • Design Verification: The structural engineer reviews the drawings: "Do the beam and column dimensions on the drawings meet the seismic specification?" Verification confirms that the outputs meet the inputs (drawings vs. specifications).
  • Design Validation: A live-in test after completion: "When four people actually move in, does the house meet the original needs of use?" Confirmation under actual conditions of use.
  • Design Transfer: The drawings and construction specifications are handed over to the construction crew (the manufacturing department) to ensure the house is built as drawn.

Key Pitfall: Verification ≠ Validation (A Frequent FDA 483 Observation)

This is one of the most common design control observations in FDA 483s:

  • Design Verification: Confirms "Did we build it right?" by testing whether the outputs meet the input specifications. Example: measuring whether the catheter outer diameter meets the drawing specification of 3.5±0.1mm.
  • Design Validation: Confirms "Did we build the right thing?" by testing whether the device meets user needs under actual or simulated conditions of use. Example: having nurses actually perform catheter insertion to verify clinical usability.

The most common mistake: In Validation, only repeating the Verification tests (purely technical specification testing), without using initial production units and without testing under simulated actual use conditions. FDA §820.30(g) explicitly requires the use of "initial production units or equivalents."

Additional pitfall: Design Validation ≠ Process Validation. The former confirms that the device design meets user needs, while the latter confirms that the manufacturing process can consistently produce product that meets specifications. Both must be performed.

What the DHF Must Contain

The Design History File is the "complete record" of design controls and must contain or reference:

  • Design and Development Plan
  • Design Input documents
  • Design Output documents (including drawings and specifications)
  • All Design Review records (including dates, participants, and conclusions)
  • Verification records (including test methods, results, and approval)
  • Validation records (including conditions of use, lots of test samples, and statistical analysis)
  • Design Changes with impact assessment
  • Risk Management File (ISO 14971)
  • Design Transfer confirmation

Note: The DHF does not need to contain every document itself, only to "contain or reference" them. However, the referenced documents must be available for inspection at any time.

Tutorial Commentary

The Three-Tier Structure of Purchasing Controls

Purchasing controls consist of three interlinked requirements, none of which can be omitted:

  1. §7.4.1 Supplier evaluation and qualification: Establish an Approved Supplier List (ASL). Evaluation methods may be questionnaires, audits, test samples, or review of certification documents (e.g., an ISO 13485 certificate). The evaluation criteria must be documented.
  2. §7.4.2 Purchasing information: Each purchase order (PO) or supply agreement must contain enough technical requirements for the supplier to know "what you need." Merely writing "per contract" is not sufficient; it must be traceable to specific specifications.
  3. §7.4.3 Verification of purchased product: Upon receipt, the product must be verified to meet the purchasing requirements. The extent of verification depends on the risk to device quality: the higher the risk, the stricter the verification.

Critical Supplier: Contract Sterilizer

For sterile medical device manufacturers, the contract sterilizer is one of the most critical suppliers. The special requirements are as follows:

  • The manufacturer's responsibility cannot be transferred: Even if sterilization is outsourced, the device manufacturer remains fully responsible for the sterilization result. FDA treats the contract sterilizer as part of the "manufacturer" (see the definition in §820.3(o)).
  • Sterilization validation records: The manufacturer must keep the sterilization process records for each lot (sterilization temperature, pressure, time, biological indicator results, etc.) in the DHR. These records are usually provided by the contract sterilizer, and the manufacturer must incorporate them into its own record system.
  • Periodic supplier audits: For critical suppliers (especially those with a direct impact on terminal sterilization), periodic on-site audits are best practice and a focus of FDA investigators.
  • Change control: If the contract sterilizer changes its sterilization equipment, process parameters, or facility, the manufacturer must be notified, and process revalidation may be required.

Frequent FDA 483 Observations: Inadequate Supplier Control

The following are common FDA 483 observations related to purchasing controls:

  • The Approved Supplier List (ASL) does not include all critical suppliers, or the list is not reviewed and updated periodically
  • Purchasing documents (POs) lack specific technical specifications and only refer to "general quality requirements"
  • Incoming inspection records are incomplete or do not follow the approved procedure
  • No periodic audits of the contract sterilizer; reliance only on certificates provided by the supplier
  • A supplier re-evaluation procedure exists but is not executed at the established frequency

Tutorial Commentary

The Seven Elements of Controlled Production Conditions

§7.5.1 requires production under "controlled conditions." Each of these seven elements has corresponding documentation requirements:

  • (a) Product specification documents: The drawings and specifications in the Device Master Record (DMR)
  • (b) Work instructions (WI): Detailed written procedures for each manufacturing step
  • (c) Facilities and environment: Cleanroom classification, temperature and humidity control, HEPA filtration system
  • (d) Monitoring activities: Environmental monitoring (EM) program, in-process quality control
  • (e) Release procedures: Lot release acceptance criteria
  • (f) Labeling and packaging: Labeling control procedures (to prevent lot mix-ups and mislabeling)
  • (g) Personnel qualification: Training records, confirmation of qualification

Process Validation Logic: IQ → OQ → PQ

§7.5.6 requires validation of "processes whose results cannot be verified by subsequent monitoring or measurement." The most typical example is the sterilization process. The standard three stages of validation:

Key Pitfall: Design Validation ≠ Process Validation

These two "validation" concepts must be strictly distinguished; they are the concepts manufacturers most often confuse in FDA inspections:

  • Design Validation (§7.3.7): Confirms that the device design (the product itself) meets user needs. It is a one-time activity, performed during product development, with results filed in the DHF.
  • Process Validation (§7.5.6): Confirms that the manufacturing process can consistently and reliably produce product that meets specifications. It requires repeated demonstration (usually 3 lots) and revalidation when the process undergoes significant change, with results filed in the DMR/DHR.

Examples of FDA 483 observations: "The sterilization process validation report shows that only one validation lot was executed, which does not meet the established criterion of three consecutive successful lots" and "The acceptance criteria in the process validation report were set after the validation was executed (retrospective acceptance)."

Application Scenario: CDMO Prefilled Injectable Combination Product

Scenario: A contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO) takes on fill-finish services for a prefilled syringe (PFS). The PFS is a combination product: the syringe is a Class II medical device and the drug is a biologic. The device manufacturer holds a 510(k) clearance, and the drug is held under a BLA by a separate Sponsor. The CDMO is responsible only for aseptic filling and final packaging.

Question: Which design control requirements apply to the CDMO?

  • Design controls (§7.3 / §820.30): The CDMO does not bear device design control responsibility, because the CDMO is not the Specification Developer of the device design. The DHF for the device design is held by the device manufacturer (or the Sponsor). However, the CDMO is obliged to understand and implement the specifications of the design outputs (fill volume, seal integrity, etc.).
  • Process validation (§7.5.6): The CDMO must perform process validation of the aseptic filling process. Under 21 CFR Part 820 (device side) + 21 CFR Part 211 (drug side), both sets of requirements apply. The validation protocol must be approved by the Sponsor in advance.
  • Purchasing controls (§7.4): The CDMO has purchasing control obligations for the critical consumables it uses, such as single-use assemblies and filters.
  • Sterilization assurance: If terminal sterilization is performed by the CDMO (for example, terminal radiation sterilization), the CDMO may be regarded as a manufacturer under FDA §820.3(o) and must meet the full sterilization control requirements. For aseptic processing, there must be aseptic process simulation (media fill) validation records, consistent with PDA TR-22 and the FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance.
  • Dual compliance pathway: The drug component must comply with 21 CFR Part 211, and the device component with 21 CFR Part 820. FDA determines the lead center (CDER or CDRH) for a combination product based on its primary mode of action (PMOA), but both sets of GMPs must be satisfied.

Conclusion: The CDMO's main obligations in this scenario center on process validation (§7.5.6), purchasing controls (§7.4), production controls (§7.5.1), and traceability of sterilization records (§7.5.5), rather than device design controls. However, the CDMO must fully understand the device design output specifications and ensure that its process does not compromise the device characteristics established by the design.

Special Requirements for Sterile Medical Devices

For sterile medical devices, in addition to general production controls, §7.5.5 adds the following special obligations:

  • Traceability of lot sterilization records: The parameter records of each sterilization lot must be traceable back to the corresponding device production lot (DHR). This is a key item FDA inspectors verify.
  • SAL documentation: The basis for achieving the Sterility Assurance Level (SAL ≤ 10⁻⁶) must be documented, including bioburden data and sterilization cycle validation.
  • Sterile label: Labeling such as "STERILE" or "STERILE EO" on the device must be supported by corresponding sterilization validation documents. If the packaging is damaged during handling or storage, the sterile state is compromised, and there must be a clear reprocessing or disposal procedure.

FDA cross-reference from Part 820: Labeling of medical devices for US distribution shall comply with the UDI requirements of 21 CFR Part 830 and 21 CFR §801.20.   • Label each device with a UDI in both human-readable interpretation (HRI) and automatic identification and data capture (AIDC) technology (barcode or RFID);
  • Submit device identifier (DI) and production identifier (PI) information to FDA's Global UDI Database (GUDID);
  • Ensure the UDI appears on the device label and, where required, directly on the device itself (Direct Part Marking, DPM);

A UDI consists of two components:

  • Device Identifier (DI): A mandatory, fixed portion of a UDI that identifies the labeler and the specific version or model of a device.
  • Production Identifier (PI): A conditional, variable portion of a UDI that identifies one or more of the following when included on the label: lot or batch number, serial number, software identification, manufacturing date, expiration date.
"Each manufacturer of a device intended to be implanted shall establish and maintain procedures for identifying with a control number each unit, lot, or batch of finished devices."
— 21 CFR §820.65

Tutorial Commentary

Identification vs. Traceability: Key Differences

These two concepts are often used interchangeably, but there is a clear division between them at the regulatory level:

  • Identification (§7.5.3): Ensures that at every stage of production the product carries a clear identity: "what product this is and what production status it is in (accepted/quarantine/segregated)." This is real-time control to prevent lot mix-ups and misuse.
  • Traceability (§7.5.4): Establishes a record system so that any finished device can be traced backward to the raw material lots, production equipment, and operators used, and tracked forward to the customers who received that lot of devices. This is the basis for after-the-fact investigation and recall management.

The highest standard for implantable devices: §820.65 and §7.5.4 impose the strictest traceability requirements on implantable devices: every single unit (unit-level) must have a control number and be traceable to all components and suppliers.

The Link Between UDI and Recall Management

The ultimate purpose of the UDI system is to improve device safety, and its core use case is the Targeted Recall:

Before UDI was widespread, if a lot of devices had a safety problem, the manufacturer might have to recall every lot of the same model, even if only a few specific lots were affected. With the UDI PI (lot number/serial number), the affected lots can be precisely identified and recalled alone, greatly reducing the scope and cost of the recall and also lessening the disruption to patients.

Link to hospital asset management: FDA also expects hospitals and other healthcare facilities to record the UDI of implantable devices in patient records, forming a two-way "device-patient" tracking chain. The manufacturer's traceability system must be able to support this.

The UDI System: Three Core Obligations

The UDI system is a major reform FDA has pushed in recent years, and it places three core obligations on device manufacturers:

  1. Labeling: The device label must include the UDI, in both a human-readable (HRI) form and a machine-readable (AIDC: barcode or RFID) form. The barcode format is usually GS1 DataMatrix (2D barcode) or GS1-128 (linear barcode).
  2. Database submission (GUDID): The manufacturer must submit the device's basic information (DI information, device description, whether it contains latex, whether it is sterile, etc.) to the FDA GUDID (Global UDI Database). This is a publicly searchable database.
  3. Record keeping: The DHR must include the UDI information (DI + PI) for each lot. This integrates the identification and traceability system with UDI, forming complete lifecycle tracking.

3.1 Design Controls

  • The FDA QMSR mandates design controls for Class II/III devices; the ISO 13485 exclusion clause is not valid under the FDA framework
  • Waterfall model: User Needs → Design Inputs → Design Outputs → Verification → Validation → Transfer → DMR
  • Verification (confirming outputs meet inputs) ≠ Validation (confirming the device meets user needs); both are mandatory requirements
  • The DHF must cover the complete design history, containing or referencing all design control records

3.2 Purchasing Controls

  • Three-tier structure: supplier evaluation (ASL) → purchasing documents (PO/technical specifications) → verification of purchased product
  • The contract sterilizer is a "manufacturer" as defined by FDA, and sterilization validation records must enter the DHR
  • Supplier changes must go through the purchasing change control process and may trigger revalidation

3.3 Production & Process Controls

  • Process validation (IQ → OQ → PQ) applies to all "special processes," especially sterilization and aseptic filling
  • Design Validation ≠ Process Validation: the former is filed in the DHF and the latter in the DMR/DHR, each with a different purpose and timing
  • Drug-device combination products: Part 820 (device) + Part 211 (drug) both apply, and dual compliance is indispensable
  • Sterile devices: each lot's sterilization records must be traceable to the product lot, and the "STERILE" label must be supported by validation documents

3.4 Identification & Traceability

  • Identification (§7.5.3) = real-time status marking; traceability (§7.5.4) = after-the-fact record tracing
  • UDI (21 CFR Part 830) is a major FDA addition to ISO 13485: DI (fixed) + PI (variable)
  • Implantable devices must achieve unit-level traceability, all the way to the UDI record in the patient's medical record

FDA Inspection Focus Points

In FDA inspections related to design controls and product realization, the most common 483 observations include:

  1. Design Validation did not use initial production units but instead used Engineering Samples
  2. Confusion between design verification and validation: only technical specification tests were performed, with no confirmation under simulated use conditions
  3. Incomplete DHF: missing design review records, design change records, or risk management documents
  4. The Approved Supplier List (ASL) is out of date or does not cover all critical suppliers
  5. Acceptance criteria in process validation reports were set "retrospectively" (the criteria were decided only after the validation data were available)
  6. UDI was not submitted to GUDID, or the DHR does not record UDI lot information

4.1 Nonconforming Product — Control of Nonconforming Product (ISO 13485 §8.3)

Original Text

Regulatory Basis: ISO 13485:2016 §8.3 / 21 CFR Part 820 QMSR incorporates ISO 13485 by reference. The organization shall ensure that product which does not conform to product requirements is identified and controlled to prevent its unintended use or delivery.

Core Requirements:

  • Identification: Nonconforming product must be clearly identified — labeling, tags, physical segregation, system flags in electronic batch records.
  • Segregation: Physical or system-based quarantine from conforming product. Separate holding areas with controlled access. The purpose is unambiguous — prevent mix-up, prevent inadvertent release.
  • Documentation: Written records describing the nonconformity, the product involved, quantities, dates discovered, and the disposition decision with rationale.

Disposition Decisions (§8.3.3):

  • Rework: Processing to bring product into conformance with specification. Requires written rework procedure, re-testing per the original acceptance criteria, and documentation that rework was performed. Rework must be evaluated for adverse effect on product.
  • Scrap / Destruction: Final disposition for product that cannot be reworked or accepted. Destruction must be documented — quantity, method, personnel, date.
  • Use-as-Is (Deviation/Concession): Acceptance of nonconforming product under a documented concession. Requires risk-based justification demonstrating the nonconformity does not affect safety, effectiveness, or essential performance. Customer or regulatory notification may be required. Cannot be used for safety-critical nonconformities.
  • Return to Supplier: For incoming material nonconformities — documented return with supplier notification and quarantine until resolution.

Re-verification After Rework (§8.3.4): Product that has been reworked shall be re-inspected or re-tested against original acceptance criteria. The re-inspection results shall be documented in the DHR (Device History Record).

Advisory Notice Considerations: If a nonconformity is discovered in distributed (released) product, the organization must evaluate:

  • Does it constitute a malfunction that could cause or contribute to serious injury if it recurs? → MDR evaluation required (21 CFR Part 803)
  • Does the nonconformity affect a significant number of devices? → Field Correction / Recall consideration (21 CFR Part 806)
  • Is an Advisory Notice to users required (ISO 13485 §8.3.4)?

Linkage to CAPA: A single nonconformity event may be handled as a deviation. However, if trend analysis reveals a pattern (same nonconformity type recurring), this must trigger a CAPA to address the systemic root cause. ISO 13485 §8.3 → §8.5.2 linkage is mandatory and must be documented.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Four Disposition Paths for Nonconforming Product

Rework: The most common choice, but with conditions. There must be a documented rework procedure, and after rework the product must be retested against the original acceptance criteria. Rework must not lower the testing requirements. The DHR must show the complete chain: original test failure, rework record, retest passed.

Scrap: The simplest option but the most costly. It applies when the product cannot be reworked or when the cost of rework is unreasonable. The scrap record must include the quantity and the destruction method; it is not enough to write "discarded".

Use-as-Is / Concession: The highest-risk option. A documented risk assessment is needed to explain why the nonconformity does not affect safety and effectiveness. For safety-critical nonconformities (such as a sterility failure or a labeling error), use-as-is concession must never be used.

Return to Supplier: Used for incoming nonconformities. It must be combined with a supplier CAPA request; otherwise the problem will keep recurring.

Analogy

Nonconforming product control is like quality control of ingredients in a restaurant:

  • Identification/segregation = the chef finds an expired ingredient, immediately attaches a "Do Not Use" label and puts it in a separate refrigerator
  • Rework = a vegetable has a blemished part; cut off the bad part and the rest can still be used, but it must be inspected again
  • Scrap = the whole batch of ingredients has spoiled and is all thrown away
  • Use-as-is concession = the vegetable looks poor but is safe to eat, so it is made into soup (the reason for the decision must be recorded)
  • Return = the ingredients delivered by the supplier do not meet the specification, so the whole batch is sent back

The difference: for a medical device, a "use-as-is concession" requires a documented risk assessment, whereas in a restaurant the head chef's judgment is enough. The regulation requires auditable records, not personal judgment.

FDA 483 Inspection Trap

★ Missing rework procedure: One of the most common observations. The company performed rework, but there is no documented rework procedure in the SOPs and no evaluation of the potential impact of rework on product quality. FDA expects the rework procedure to be pre-approved, not drawn up after the fact.

★ Use-as-is concession without a risk assessment: The use-as-is decision rests only on verbal confirmation or an email, with no formal documented risk assessment and approval.

★ Nonconforming product not physically segregated from conforming product: The two are mixed in the same warehouse area and separated only by an ERP status flag. If the flag is misread, there is a risk of release.

★ Nonconformity in released product not evaluated for MDR: A customer complaint indicates that a batch had a process nonconformity, but the company only handled the return and did not evaluate whether an MDR needs to be submitted.

Practice Scenarios

  1. After filling, a batch of syringe plungers was found to be out of specification dimensionally (+0.1 mm). The inspector granted a use-as-is concession without completing any documented evaluation. Which QMSR requirements does this violate?
  2. At final inspection, 5% of the sampled catheters from the original manufacturer had surface scratches, and the decision was to rework and repolish the whole batch. The retest after rework consisted only of a visual inspection, with no repeat of the mechanical strength test. What is wrong with this?
  3. The same nonconformity pattern (a dimensional deviation in one component) occurred 8 times in the past 6 months, and each time it was dispositioned as a use-as-is concession and closed individually. Does this meet QMSR requirements?
4.2 CAPA — Corrective and Preventive Action (ISO 13485 §8.5.2 / §8.5.3)

Original Text

CAPA is consistently the most-cited area in FDA 483 observations and Warning Letters for medical device manufacturers. The QMSR (and underlying ISO 13485) requires a documented, closed-loop CAPA system.

Corrective Action (CA) — §8.5.2 — Reactive:
Purpose: Eliminate the cause of an existing nonconformity to prevent recurrence.

Required Steps for Each CA:

  1. Detection & Documentation: Record the nonconformity. Source may be: internal audit finding, customer complaint, nonconforming product, process monitoring data, MDR/regulatory report.
  2. Severity/Impact Assessment: Does this affect distributed product? Is patient safety at risk? Does it require MDR or Field Correction?
  3. Root Cause Analysis (RCA): Must demonstrate systematic investigation, not just surface-level description. Tools include:
    • 5-Why Analysis: Iteratively ask "why" until the systemic root cause is identified (not just the immediate cause)
    • Fishbone/Ishikawa Diagram: Categorize potential causes across 6M framework: Man, Machine, Material, Method, Measurement, Mother Nature (Environment)
    • Fault Tree Analysis (FTA): Top-down deductive analysis of failure modes — required for complex systemic failures or safety-critical issues
    • Is/Is Not Analysis: Systematically eliminate potential causes by comparing where the problem occurs vs. where it does not
  4. Corrective Action Plan: Specific actions addressing the root cause(s). Must include: responsible person, target completion date, resource requirements.
  5. Implementation: Documented execution of each action item. Include evidence: updated SOP version, training records, equipment calibration records, process change validation data.
  6. Effectiveness Check (Verification of Effectiveness — VoE): After implementation, collect objective evidence that the root cause has been eliminated and the nonconformity has not recurred. Specify:
    • Metric to be measured (e.g., reject rate, complaint rate)
    • Time period for data collection
    • Acceptance criteria (e.g., zero recurrence in 90 days)
    • Person responsible for verification

Preventive Action (PA) — §8.5.3 — Proactive:
Purpose: Eliminate the cause of a potential nonconformity to prevent its occurrence.

Sources of PA Inputs (required to systematically analyze):

  • Statistical trend analysis of process data, inspection data, reject rates
  • Internal audit findings (negative trends, near-misses)
  • Management review outputs (resource gaps, quality objectives not met)
  • Customer feedback and satisfaction data
  • Risk management outputs — identified risks not yet manifested
  • Post-market surveillance data
  • Regulatory intelligence (competitor recalls, FDA safety communications)

Documentation Requirements:

  • CAPA record must capture all steps above with dates and responsible parties
  • CAPA records must be included as input to Management Review (§5.6)
  • CAPA effectiveness data must be retained as quality records
  • CAPA system metrics (open CAPAs, overdue CAPAs, effectiveness failure rate) reported to management

Tutorial Commentary

A Practical Guide to Root Cause Analysis Tools

5-Why example: Problem: a labeling error appears on the product packaging

  • Why 1: The label was printed incorrectly → Why 2: The label template was not updated in time → Why 3: The design change was not communicated to the packaging department → Why 4: The notification procedure for design changes covered only the manufacturing department → Why 5: Root cause: the design change control procedure (DCO) does not cover the notification obligations to all affected departments

Note: the "5" in 5-Why is only a guideline. Sometimes the root cause is found after 3 whys, sometimes it takes 7. The key is to keep asking until you reach a systemic root cause, rather than stopping at the level of individual error.

The 6M structure of the fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram:

  • Man (people): inadequate training, staff rotation, communication errors
  • Machine (equipment): equipment failure, overdue calibration, inadequate maintenance
  • Material: raw material variability, change of supplier
  • Method: unclear SOPs, missing steps, flawed process design
  • Measurement: test method error, instrument drift
  • Environment: temperature and humidity, cleanroom contamination, vibration

FTA (Fault Tree Analysis): Starting from the top-level failure event and working downward, it identifies all possible combinations of failure paths. It is suited to systematic safety failure analysis of complex products. FDA expects FTA to be considered in the CAPA for high-risk devices.

Analogy: CAPA vs Deviation

A deviation is like "a single cold": you take medicine (immediate correction), the symptoms go away, and you do not need to change your lifestyle systematically. Every deviation is recorded, handled and closed.

CAPA is like "systematically rebuilding the immune system": if you catch a cold 10 times a year, medicine alone is not enough. You must find the root cause (lack of sleep? low immunity? the work environment?), then make systematic changes (adjust your routine, improve nutrition, improve your environment), and follow up for six months to confirm the improvement (verification of effectiveness).

The key difference: a deviation is immediate control of a single event; CAPA is the fundamental repair of a systemic defect. The question FDA investigators ask most often is: "Does your CAPA system escalate recurring deviations to CAPA?"

High-Frequency FDA 483 / Warning Letter Observations

★ CAPAs not closed (most common, #1): Large numbers of CAPAs have been open for months or even years, with no closure date and no effectiveness verification. FDA investigators will usually ask to see "a status report of all CAPAs over the past 12 months".

★ Missing effectiveness verification (most common, #2): When the CAPA is closed, the effectiveness verification field says "N/A" or "Implementation completed" and no data were collected. This directly negates the whole purpose of the CAPA.

★ Root cause stops at the surface: The RCA only states "operator did not follow the SOP" and does not go on to ask "why did the operator not follow the SOP?" The cause may be a flawed SOP design, insufficient training or an excessive workload.

★ Deviations not escalated to CAPA: The system shows that the same type of deviation occurred 12 times in the past 6 months, each closed individually, and never triggered a CAPA. This indicates that the trend analysis system has failed.

★ Preventive action (PA) completely missing: The company has only CA and no PA. In the management review, the PA input is blank. FDA considers the lack of PA to mean that the QMS is reactive rather than proactive.

Worked Example: CAPA Effectiveness Verification

CAPA trigger: package seal failure rate = 3.2% (specification: ≤0.5%)
Root cause: heat sealer temperature drift (calibration record found to be 6 months overdue)

Setting the effectiveness verification criteria:
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
VoE metric: seal failure rate
VoE window: production data for 90 consecutive days after the action is implemented
VoE acceptance criterion: seal failure rate ≤ 0.5% (≥3 batches, n ≥ 300 per batch)
VoE owner: QE Engineer (name), target closure date: [date + 90 days]
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Actions: recalibrate the heat sealer + weekly preventive calibration plan + SOP revision
Implementation date: [date]

VoE results (after 90 days):
Batch A: failure rate 0.2% (pass)
Batch B: failure rate 0.3% (pass)
Batch C: failure rate 0.1% (pass)
→ VoE passed, CAPA closed, date: [date]

Note: if any batch exceeds the limit during the VoE collection period, the VoE has failed, and the CAPA must be reopened and a deeper root cause analysis performed.

Practice Questions

  1. Your company received 15 CAPAs in the past 3 months. Of these, 8 are more than 30 days overdue and 3 are more than 90 days overdue. An FDA investigator asks to see the CAPA status report. How do you explain and respond?
  2. The root cause analysis conclusion of a CAPA is "operator training was insufficient", and the corrective action is "retrain the operators and update the training records". How would the effectiveness of this CAPA be verified? Design a specific VoE plan.
  3. What sources should trigger preventive action (PA)? What types of data should the PA input section contain in your company's monthly management review?

The Six Core Components of a CAPA System

What "closed-loop" means: The most important feature of CAPA is that it is "closed-loop": every CAPA must have clear closure criteria (VoE passed). The problem FDA investigators find most often is that a CAPA was opened but never closed, or was closed without effectiveness verification data.

4.3 Complaint Files — §820.198 (FDA-Specific, Not in ISO 13485)

Original Text

Statutory basis: §820.198 is a FDA QMSR-specific requirement with no direct equivalent in ISO 13485:2016. ISO 13485 has §8.2.1 (Feedback) but §820.198 is far more prescriptive and has MDR cross-links.

Definition of "Complaint" (§820.198(a)):

"Any written, electronic, or oral communication that alleges deficiencies related to the identity, quality, durability, reliability, safety, effectiveness, or performance of a device after it is released for distribution."

Key elements of the definition:

  • Any channel: Written letter, email, phone call, social media, distributor report, service technician report — all qualify
  • Post-distribution only: Pre-release failures are NCR/CAPA events, not complaints under §820.198
  • Alleged deficiency: The complaint does not need to be valid or verified — the allegation itself triggers the process
  • Not a complaint: General inquiries, requests for pricing, user errors without device deficiency claim (though document all contacts regardless)

Required Written Procedure (§820.198(a)):

  • Procedure for receiving, reviewing, and evaluating complaints
  • Uniform and timely manner
  • Complaints assigned to a formally designated unit (may be part of CAPA team or separate)
  • Oral complaints reduced to writing upon receipt

Complaint Investigation Requirements (§820.198(c)):

Every complaint shall be reviewed and evaluated to determine if an investigation is necessary. Investigation is REQUIRED unless the complaint meets both exclusion criteria:

  1. The same complaint (same cause, similar device) has been previously investigated, AND
  2. The investigation was performed by a formally designated unit, AND
  3. A corrective action was taken

Otherwise, investigation is mandatory. If no investigation is conducted, the file must document the reason why and the name of the individual responsible for that decision.

MDR Decision — 30-Day Timeline (§820.198(d) + 21 CFR Part 803):

For each complaint involving possible failure of a device, labeling, or packaging to meet any of its specifications, the organization must determine:

  • Does the complaint involve a death or serious injury caused by the device? → MDR 30-day report to FDA
  • Does the complaint involve a device malfunction that would likely cause or contribute to a death or serious injury if it were to recur? → MDR 30-day report
  • Is there an MDR-reportable event under 21 CFR Part 803 (manufacturer) or Part 803.50 (importer)?
"§803.50(a): Device manufacturers must submit MDR reports within 30 calendar days after becoming aware of information that reasonably suggests that one of their marketed devices: (1) May have caused or contributed to a death or serious injury; or (2) Has malfunctioned and this malfunction would be likely to cause or contribute to death or serious injury if it were to recur."

Records Required in Each Complaint File (§820.198(e)):

  • Device name, lot/serial number, UDI (if required)
  • Complainant name, address, phone
  • Nature of complaint, date received
  • Investigation results: findings, conclusions
  • Corrective action taken (if any)
  • Reply to complainant (if any)
  • MDR determination: whether reportable, date determination made, and if reported, the MDR report number

Complaint Records Accessibility: Complaint files must be accessible at the manufacturing establishment and must be available during FDA inspection. For manufacturers with multiple facilities, all complaint files for devices manufactured at a given facility must be located at that facility.

Tutorial Commentary

FDA 483 Inspection Trap: The Fatal Break Between Complaint and MDR

★ The most dangerous 483 observation: complaint not escalated to MDR. This is not just a procedural issue; it can lead to a Warning Letter or even a criminal investigation. A common scenario: a customer service representative takes a call from a hospital saying "the device failed during surgery and the procedure had to be aborted". Customer service logs it as a "product complaint" and passes it to QA, and QA decides that "an aborted surgery is not a serious injury" and does not submit an MDR. FDA eventually finds that a forced surgical abort falls within the scope of "serious injury", and an MDR should have been submitted within 30 days.

★ Oral complaints not documented: A salesperson receives a complaint by phone and records "customer dissatisfied" in the CRM system, but does not create a formal written record or route it to QA as the complaint definition requires. If these "hidden complaints" are dug out of the CRM system or email during an FDA inspection, they become a major 483 observation.

★ No documentation of the MDR decision: The MDR field in the complaint file is blank, or says only "Not Reportable" without recording who made the decision, the date of the decision and the basis for it. §820.198(d) requires the MDR decision to be documented, and the rationale must be recorded even when the decision is not to report.

★ Misunderstanding the start of the 30-day clock: The clock starts on "the date the company becomes aware of information that may constitute an MDR event", not on "the date the investigation is completed" or "the date causation is confirmed". As soon as there is a "reasonable suggestion", the 30 days begin to run.

Calculating the MDR 30-Day Deadline

MDR 30-day clock rules (21 CFR §803.20):
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Clock start (Day 0):
  The manufacturer/importer "becomes aware of" information
  that reasonably suggests that an MDR reportable event
  has occurred

  Note: "becomes aware" ≠ "confirms". The clock starts once the
  information reaches any employee of the company
  (including sales, technical and customer service staff)

Example calculation:
  Day 0: Customer service receives the complaint call (record the time)
  Day 1-5: Complaint documented in writing and passed to QA
  Day 5: QA preliminary assessment; death/serious injury may be involved
  Day 6-25: Investigation (device retrieval, technical evaluation, clinical data collection)
  Day 30 (deadline): MedWatch 3500A must be submitted to FDA

If the full investigation cannot be completed within 30 days:
  Submit an Initial Report: submit the known information before Day 30
  Submit a Supplemental Report: add to it once the investigation is complete
  → A late report is better than none, but not reporting is unlawful
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────

MDR decision tree:
Complaint reaches QA
  │
  ├─ Does it involve a distributed device? → No → Not an MDR; document the conclusion
  │                     ↓ Yes
  ├─ May the device have caused a death? → Yes → 30-day MDR + 5-day emergency report (if death is confirmed)
  │
  ├─ May the device have caused a serious injury? → Yes → 30-day MDR
  │  ("Serious injury" = life-threatening, results in permanent impairment, requires medical intervention,
  │   hospitalization or prolonged hospitalization, congenital anomaly or defect)
  │
  ├─ Did the device malfunction, and would a recurrence be likely to cause the above outcomes?
  │  → Yes → 30-day MDR (malfunction-type MDR)

Practice: Is This a Complaint? Does This Need an MDR?

Scenario 1: A hospital biomedical engineer calls to say that your company's infusion pump alarmed and stopped infusing automatically during the night shift. The patient was not injured, and the pump reset itself and resumed normal operation.

4.4 Internal Audit (ISO 13485 §8.2.4)

Original Text

The organization shall conduct internal audits at planned intervals to provide information on whether the QMS:

  1. Conforms to the planned arrangements and the requirements of ISO 13485 / QMSR
  2. Conforms to the QMS requirements established by the organization
  3. Is effectively implemented and maintained

Audit Program Requirements:

  • Planned and systematic: Audit schedule covering all processes, all departments, and all QMS elements over a defined cycle (typically annual or risk-based frequency)
  • Documented: Audit plan, audit scope, criteria, methods, responsibilities, and records
  • Risk-based frequency: High-risk areas (sterilization, clean room operations, CAPA, complaints) audited more frequently
  • Comprehensive coverage: All requirements of the standard must be audited at least once per audit cycle

Auditor Independence Requirement:

  • Auditors shall not audit their own work — independence is non-negotiable
  • Auditors must be qualified: trained in audit techniques and the processes they are auditing
  • For small organizations: may use external auditors or cross-functional auditors (auditor from Department A audits Department B, and vice versa)
  • Qualifications of lead auditors must be documented

Audit Report Requirements:

  • Findings documented with objective evidence (specific records examined, observations made, personnel interviewed)
  • Nonconformities classified: Major (system-level failure) vs Minor (isolated occurrence)
  • Audit report submitted to management responsible for the audited area
  • Corrective actions: Auditee management must respond with corrective action plan and timeline
  • Verification: Follow-up audit or verification of corrective action effectiveness

Management Review Input: Internal audit results must be presented at management review as a required input per §5.6.2. Metrics include: number of findings, major/minor classification, closure rates, recurring findings.

Tutorial Commentary

Design Principles for the Internal Audit Program

Risk-based audit frequency: Not every process needs to be audited once a year. High-risk processes (aseptic filling, sterilization validation, the CAPA system, complaint handling) should be audited twice a year or even more often, while low-risk administrative processes (document control, training management) may need only one audit a year. The rationale for the risk-based tiering of audit frequency should be documented in the audit program.

Practical solutions for auditor independence: For small organizations (under 50 people), full departmental independence is hard to achieve. Common compliant solutions:

  • Establish a cross-audit matrix: QA audits production, the production supervisor audits the warehouse, and the warehouse supervisor audits the QC laboratory
  • Engage a contract auditor to perform at least one full system audit each year
  • Use the findings of FDA/Notified Body audits as a supplement

Analogy

An internal audit is like a company's "annual health checkup": a regular, systematic assessment of the health of the QMS. The difference lies in how it is done:

  • A good audit = like proactively going for a health checkup, finding early problems and preventing them
  • A poor audit = like a last-minute "catch-up audit" done only 2 weeks before an FDA inspection: a box-ticking exercise that finds no real problems

FDA investigators will ask to see the internal audit records and pay particular attention to whether the audit findings are genuine (whether any problems were actually found) or whether the audit reports always show "zero findings" (which suggests that the auditors are either not competent or have been told not to find problems).

Key FDA Inspection Focus Areas for Internal Audits

★ Audit program does not cover all QMS elements: The company has an audit program, but in the past 2 years it has never audited complaint handling or the CAPA system. FDA investigators may conclude on this basis that the QMS internal audit is incomplete.

★ No auditor qualification records: The auditor has only a one-line description saying "completed audit training", but no auditor qualification assessment records (audit technique courses, assessment results, OJT records).

★ Audit findings not linked to CAPA: The audit report contains a major nonconformity, but no CAPA was opened; there is only an "improvement plan" email. ISO 13485 §8.2.4 explicitly requires that audit nonconformities initiate CAPA.

★ Corrective actions for audit findings not verified: The corrective action for a nonconformity from the previous audit is recorded as "completed", but there is no follow-up verification record confirming the effectiveness of the corrective action.

4.5 Records Requirements (ISO 13485 §4.2.4 + QMSR)

Original Text

Record Retention Periods:

  • ISO 13485 §4.2.4: Records shall be retained for at least the lifetime of the device as defined by the organization, but not less than the minimum period specified by applicable regulatory requirements, and not less than 2 years from the date of product release by the organization.
  • FDA QMSR (21 CFR §820.180): All quality records shall be retained for a period of time equivalent to the design and expected life of the device, but in no case less than 2 years from the date of manufacture of the device.
  • Practical requirement: The more conservative retention period applies. For an implantable device with a 10-year expected life: retain records for 10+ years from manufacture date (not release date).

Types of Records:

  • Device History Record (DHR): Demonstrates the device was manufactured in accordance with DMR (Device Master Record). Contains: lot/batch numbers, quantity manufactured, quantity released, dates, acceptance activities, labeling, UDI assignment, distribution records.
  • Quality Records: CAPA records, complaint files, audit reports, management review minutes, calibration records, personnel training records, supplier qualification records, NCR/deviation records.
  • Device Master Record (DMR): The specification file — drawings, formulation, component specs, manufacturing procedures, labeling specifications, QC procedures. (This is a controlled document, not a quality record per se, but must be maintained.)

Record Control Requirements (§820.180):

  • Legibility: Records must be legible and remain legible throughout the retention period
  • Identification: Records must be uniquely identified (date, author, device/lot reference)
  • Storage & Protection: Secured against deterioration, damage, loss; environmental controls appropriate to the record medium
  • Retrieval: Records must be retrievable within a reasonable time for inspection purposes
  • Retention: Formal retention schedule defined in a Records Management SOP
  • Disposition: Approval required before destruction; destruction must be documented

Electronic Records — 21 CFR Part 11: Electronic records used in lieu of paper records must comply with 21 CFR Part 11:

  • Audit trail: captures who, what, when for all record entries and changes
  • Electronic signatures: legally binding, include name, date/time, and meaning of signature
  • System validation: the electronic record system must be validated (IQ/OQ/PQ)
  • Record backup and disaster recovery plan
  • Authority checks: users may only create, modify, or delete records within their system role

Key Compliance Points for 21 CFR Part 11 Electronic Records

The audit trail is central: The electronic system must automatically capture every creation, modification and deletion of a record, and log: the operator ID, the date/time of the operation (not alterable), the original and changed values, and the reason for the change (usually mandatory). The audit trail must not be disabled or deleted by the operators themselves.

System validation requirements: Electronic systems used for GMP records, such as ERP, MES, LIMS and eQMS, all require validation. The validation scope includes installation qualification (IQ), operational qualification (OQ) and performance qualification (PQ). After a system upgrade, the affected functions need to be revalidated.

Legal effect of electronic signatures: Under Part 11, an electronic signature has the same legal effect as a handwritten signature, but it must meet these conditions: it contains the signer's full name, the date and time, and the "meaning" of the signature (for example: reviewed, approved, executed).

FDA Data Integrity Observation Warnings

★ Audit trail disabled: During an FDA inspection it was found that the audit trail function of the LIMS had been disabled through an administrator account, making it impossible to trace the modification history of records for the past 6 months. This is a major data integrity failure and directly triggers a Warning Letter.

★ Shared login accounts: Several analysts use the same LIMS account, so the specific operator cannot be identified. 21 CFR Part 11 requires every electronic signature to be uniquely linked to a specific individual.

★ Record retention system not validated: The company stores GMP records on an unvalidated Google Drive or Dropbox, with no access control and no audit trail, which does not comply with Part 11.

Record Retention Calculation: Practical Scenarios

The calculation of record retention periods is often misunderstood. The key principle: use the longer of "device design/expected life" and "2 years after the date of manufacture".

Note: for implanted devices or life-sustaining use, FDA recommends retaining records until the last known patient using the device has died. In practice, many companies adopt a maximum retention policy of "25 years after the device is withdrawn from the market".

Original Text

Regulatory Basis: 21 CFR Part 4 — Regulation of Combination Products. When a product consists of a drug component and a device component (e.g., drug-eluting stent, autoinjector prefilled syringe, inhaler with formulation), both regulatory frameworks apply.

Complaint Handling — Dual Obligation:

  • QMSR (§820.198): Governs device component — malfunction, performance, durability deficiencies
  • 21 CFR §211.198: Governs drug component — drug complaint files for finished pharmaceutical products under CGMP for Drugs (cGMP 21 CFR Part 211)
  • A complaint about a prefilled syringe system must simultaneously be evaluated under BOTH frameworks: Is it a device malfunction? Is it a drug quality defect?

Post-Market Safety Reporting — MDR vs ADE:

Decision Matrix — Which Report for Combination Product?

  • If device component malfunctions (e.g., autoinjector failed to deliver dose): MDR under Part 803 (device PMOA)
  • If drug component caused adverse reaction (e.g., unexpected drug interaction): ADE report under drug regulations
  • If both components contributed: Both MDR and ADE reporting may be required
  • PMOA (Primary Mode of Action) of the combination product determines which center (CDER vs CDRH) has primary jurisdiction — but does not eliminate the other center's requirements

Cross-Reference 21 CFR Part 4: The combination product manufacturer must document their post-market reporting decision rationale for each event. FDA's Office of Combination Products (OCP) has jurisdiction over disputes regarding which reporting pathway applies.

Tutorial Commentary

The Dual-Track Framework for Combination Product Complaint Handling

The most common challenge: A combination product (such as a prefilled syringe) receives a complaint saying "the patient had a severe allergic reaction after the injection". QA must evaluate both of the following at the same time:

  • Device side (QMSR §820.198): Is the syringe leaking? Has the plunger failed? Has the auto-injector mechanism malfunctioned? → If so, an MDR (Part 803) may be triggered
  • Drug side (§211.198): Is there a problem with the drug formulation, impurities or degradation products? → If so, an ADE report (§314.81) may be triggered
  • Both are possible: A syringe malfunction exposes the drug to air (device defect), while degradation products cause the allergy (drug defect) → an MDR and an ADE may both need to be submitted

A combination product manufacturer must define this dual-track evaluation process explicitly in the complaint SOP, and have a designated person (or a cross-functional committee) responsible for the final MDR vs ADE decision.

Analogy

Dual-track reporting for a combination product is like a warranty claim on a "hybrid car":

  • If the electric motor fails (device defect) → go to the device warranty department
  • If there is a fuel quality problem with the gasoline engine (the drug analogy) → go to the fuel quality department
  • If the failure results from an interaction between the electronic control system and the fuel system → both departments must handle it

The difference: the medical device regulatory framework explicitly requires that the MDR/ADE decision be documented for every event, and "uncertainty" cannot be used as a reason not to report. If in doubt, the conservative approach is to report.

Common Compliance Pitfalls for Combination Products

★ PMOA misjudgment: The company considers the PMOA of a prefilled syringe to be "drug", so it set up only an ADE reporting process and overlooked the requirements of §820.198 and Part 803. FDA holds that the device function of the syringe system (the delivery mechanism) must not be ignored, even when the PMOA is the drug.

★ Only one complaint file system: The QMS of the combination product company has only a device complaint file procedure (§820.198) and no drug complaint file procedure (§211.198). The gap was found during an FDA CGMP for Drugs inspection.

★ MDR and ADE functions unaware of each other: Device QA submitted an MDR but did not notify the drug regulatory affairs department; the drug regulatory affairs department submitted an ADE without knowing that an MDR had already been submitted on the device side. The two reports described the same event inconsistently, which drew FDA attention.

The Essential Differences Among the Three Frameworks

Core difference between FDA QMSR and ICH Q10: The QMSR CAPA emphasizes "the integrity of the closed-loop system" and "the statutory link from complaint to MDR"; the ICH Q10 CAPA places more emphasis on "the rigor of scientific investigation" and "the use of trend data in the APR (Annual Product Review)". The two frameworks are essentially the same in their VoE requirements, but the QMSR requirements for complaint handling (§820.198) are far more complex than those of drug GMP.

ISO 13485 as the foundation of QMSR: FDA QMSR incorporates its CAPA requirements by reference to ISO 13485. This means that a manufacturer certified to ISO 13485 already meets most of the QMSR CAPA requirements, but must still meet the additional requirements of §820.198 (complaint files) and 21 CFR Part 803 (MDR).

The Hierarchy of the Three Frameworks: Analogy

Think of the three frameworks as driving licence requirements of three different levels of strictness:

  • ISO 13485 = the international driving licence standard: a globally accepted baseline that defines the minimum requirements for a CAPA system
  • ICH Q10 = the "highway driving test" of the pharmaceutical industry: more emphasis on scientific methods and data, addressing the complexity of pharmaceutical processes
  • FDA QMSR = the "special vehicle licence" for medical devices under US federal regulation: on top of ISO 13485, it adds mandatory MDR reporting obligations and complaint file requirements, and it has the force of law

A combination product manufacturer needs both the "drug highway licence (ICH Q10)" and the "device special vehicle licence (QMSR)". Both must be met, and neither can substitute for the other.

Application Scenario — CDMO Scenario: Complete Decision Tree for a Particulate Matter Complaint

Application Scenario: CDMO Scenario

Background: Your company is a CDMO that fills and assembles prefilled syringes (PFS) for a medical device manufacturer. The customer (the NDA holder) calls your customer service department to report that they received 3 PFS returned by a hospital from a distributed batch, and visual inspection found fine particles (particulate matter) in the drug solution. The entire batch has been distributed, and some units have already been used on patients.

Complete QMSR decision tree: six key decision points:

Decision point 1: Is this a "complaint"? (review against the §820.198 definition)

  • Type of communication: customer phone call (oral communication) → must be documented in writing immediately
  • Type of claim: quality defect (quality deficiency in distributed device/product)
  • Distribution status: distributed ✓
  • Determination: Yes, this is a formal complaint under §820.198. The customer service representative must complete the written record during or immediately after the call and route it to complaint handling (QA).

Decision point 2: Does it need to be investigated?

  • Has a corrective action already been taken on an identical past complaint? Assume not → an investigation is required
  • Scope of investigation: retrieve the returned samples, pull the batch filling record, review filling process data, IPC particle count records and filter integrity test records
  • In parallel: the customer (the NDA holder) should be notified immediately, as they may also have their own complaint file obligations under §820.198

Decision point 3: Is this a nonconforming product (NCR) event?

  • Investigation finding: particles ≥10 µm were confirmed in the 3 returned PFS (exceeding the USP <788> specification)
  • The same batch has been released and distributed: → distributed nonconforming product
  • NCR opened: batch number, description of the nonconformity, preliminary impact assessment
  • Root cause investigation started (ahead of CAPA): review of filling environment monitoring, condition of the filling needles, incoming water and API particle data

Decision point 4: Is an MDR required? (the 30-day clock has started)

  • Clock start (Day 0): the day customer service received the call
  • MDR evaluation criteria:
    • May the device/product malfunction cause a death? Not confirmed
    • May the device/product malfunction cause a serious injury? → Intravenous injection of a drug containing particles can cause emboli, vascular occlusion and organ damage → potential serious injury
    • If the malfunction recurred, could it cause a serious injury? → Yes
  • Determination: an MDR report must be evaluated. If the investigation confirms a device malfunction (a problem with the filling system), an MDR (MedWatch 3500A) must be submitted to FDA by Day 30.
  • Note: if a patient has received the injection and had an adverse reaction → the ADE reporting obligation may already have been triggered (if it is a combination product, or if the NDA holder has the obligation)

Decision point 5: Is a field correction or recall needed?

  • Is PFS from the same batch still in distribution? → Assess the feasibility of a recall (21 CFR Part 806)
  • Could the problem affect other batches? → Extend the scope of the investigation upstream/downstream
  • FDA notification obligation: if a recall is decided → FDA must be notified within 10 days (or, depending on the circumstances, the hospitals may be recalled directly)

Decision point 6: Is CAPA triggered?

  • If the root cause is confirmed (for example: a filling needle was not replaced as scheduled due to wear, causing metal particles to shed) → CAPA is mandatory
  • CAPA scope: revise the SOP for filling needle replacement frequency, update the preventive maintenance plan, strengthen in-line particle monitoring
  • VoE design: particle count data from the next 3 consecutive batches conform to USP <788>

Decision Tree Commentary

Division of Responsibility Between the CDMO and the NDA Holder

What makes this scenario special is that the CDMO is the manufacturer (subject to QMSR), while the NDA holder is the drug marketing authorization holder (subject to drug GMP). The division of responsibility between the two must be defined in advance in the Quality Agreement:

  • CDMO obligations: maintain the §820.198 complaint file, open the NCR, carry out the technical investigation, MDR evaluation (device side), CAPA
  • NDA holder obligations: drug complaint file (§211.198), ADE reporting, recall decision (21 CFR Part 806), primary responsibility for communication with FDA
  • Shared obligations: timely information sharing (time limits specified in the quality agreement), coordination of the dual-track MDR vs ADE evaluation

Practical recommendation: The quality agreement should specify "the time limit for notifying the CDMO of a complaint (for example, within 24 hours)" and "milestones for the CDMO to report investigation progress to the NDA holder". Without a clear agreement, both parties may receive 483 observations during an FDA inspection.

Top 3 Compliance Risk Points in This Scenario

★ Risk 1: The 30-day MDR clock is ignored. The CDMO assumes that "MDR is the NDA holder's obligation" and has no MDR evaluation process of its own. But if the CDMO is the device manufacturer, it also has MDR evaluation obligations under §820.198 and Part 803.

★ Risk 2: The root cause analysis is too superficial. The RCA for the post-filling particle problem stops at "sporadic contamination of the filling environment" and does not trace back to a missing maintenance plan or inadequate engineering controls. Even if the VoE passes, the problem may recur in the next batch.

★ Risk 3: The CAPA does not cover evaluation of other batches. The CAPA corrects only the batch with the problem and does not review whether "other batches manufactured while the root cause existed" are also affected. During an FDA inspection, the question will be: "Did your CAPA assess the scope of impact? Does it need to be extended to other batches?"

Section Summary

Key Regulatory Takeaways

  • §820.198 is FDA-unique: The complaint file requirement under §820.198 has no equivalent in ISO 13485 §8.2.1. It establishes a specific, enforceable framework that connects complaint handling directly to MDR obligations under 21 CFR Part 803.
  • CAPA closed-loop is non-negotiable: The effectiveness verification (VoE) is the single most-cited missing element in CAPA 483 observations. Opening a CAPA without a defined VoE criterion is equivalent to not having a CAPA system at all.
  • 30-day MDR clock starts at awareness, not confirmation: The threshold is "reasonably suggests" — objective certainty of causation is not required. Late MDRs and missed MDRs are among the most serious compliance failures.
  • Nonconforming product in distributed devices triggers MDR evaluation: Every recall, field safety corrective action, and MDR process ultimately starts with a properly managed NCR that includes a distributed product impact assessment.
  • Records are the audit trail of compliance: Without properly retained, legible, and retrievable records, regulatory defenses collapse. The 2-year minimum is a floor, not a target — actual retention periods must reflect device expected life.
  • Combination products require parallel QMSR + drug GMP compliance: A single adverse event may simultaneously trigger MDR under Part 803 and ADE under Part 314.81 — QA systems must be built to handle both.

Commentary Summary

Six Core Takeaways

  1. §820.198 is unique to QMSR: The complaint file system goes far beyond the feedback requirement of ISO 13485 and links directly to the mandatory MDR reporting obligation. This is the difference most easily overlooked by non-US manufacturers with ISO 13485 certification when entering the US market.
  2. The "soul" of CAPA is VoE: Verification of effectiveness (VoE) is the final link in the CAPA closed loop. A CAPA without VoE is a CAPA that has not been completed. This is the CAPA deficiency cited most frequently in FDA Warning Letters.
  3. The MDR 30-day clock starts from "awareness": Not from "completion of the investigation" or "confirmation of causation". Once any employee receives the relevant information, the company is considered "aware", and it must have a clear complaint escalation process to ensure that QA is informed immediately.
  4. Nonconforming product + distributed = mandatory MDR evaluation: Every nonconformity found in distributed product must go through the full MDR evaluation process, with the decision documented, even if the final determination is that no report is required.
  5. Record retention period ≥ device design life and ≥ 2 years: Record retention for implants and long-term-use devices often exceeds 10 years, and the specific retention period for each type of device must be clearly defined in the SOP.
  6. Dual-track obligations for combination products: QMSR (§820.198) + drug GMP (§211.198), MDR (Part 803) + ADE (§314.81). The quality agreement must allocate each party's responsibilities in advance.

An Overall Analogy for the CAPA System

A complete QMSR quality assurance system can be compared to the human immune system:

  • Nonconforming product control = innate immunity: quickly identifying and isolating external threats (defective product) and preventing them from "infecting" (reaching the market)
  • CAPA system = adaptive immunity: recognizing the pattern of the pathogen (the root cause), building long-lasting protection (systemic correction), and remembering the antibodies (effectiveness verification data) to prevent recurrence
  • Complaint handling §820.198 = the external inflammation monitor: feedback from patients and hospitals is the "pain signal" and must be passed systematically to the immune center (QA), rather than being suppressed by customer service with a "painkiller"
  • MDR reporting = the emergency distress signal (SOS): when a threat exceeds what the individual can handle, it must be escalated to a higher-level authority (FDA) to coordinate the response
