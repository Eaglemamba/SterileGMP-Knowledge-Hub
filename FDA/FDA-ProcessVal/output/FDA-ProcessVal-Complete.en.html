Introduction — What This Guidance Represents

Introduction - Position and Nature of This Guidance

FDA Guidance Nature

This guidance represents the Food and Drug Administration's (FDA's) current thinking on this topic. It does not create or confer any rights for or on any person and does not operate to bind FDA or the public. You can use an alternative approach if the approach satisfies the requirements of the applicable statutes and regulations.

This document represents FDA's current thinking on this topic. It is not legally binding, but it reflects FDA's expectations. An alternative approach may be used if it satisfies the requirements of the applicable regulations.

Original Text (FDA, January 2011)

This guidance outlines the general principles and approaches that FDA considers appropriate elements of process validation for the manufacture of human and animal drug and biological products, including active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs or drug substances), collectively referred to in this guidance as drugs or products. This guidance incorporates principles and approaches that all manufacturers can use to validate manufacturing processes.

This guidance aligns process validation activities with a product lifecycle concept and with existing FDA guidance, including the FDA/International Conference on Harmonisation (ICH) guidances for industry, Q8(R2) Pharmaceutical Development, Q9 Quality Risk Management, and Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System. Although this guidance does not repeat the concepts and principles explained in those guidances, FDA encourages the use of modern pharmaceutical development concepts, quality risk management, and quality systems at all stages of the manufacturing process lifecycle.

The lifecycle concept links product and process development, qualification of the commercial manufacturing process, and maintenance of the process in a state of control during routine commercial production. This guidance supports process improvement and innovation through sound science.

Guidance Scope — What Is Covered

  • Human drugs
  • Veterinary drugs
  • Biological and biotechnology products
  • Finished products and active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs or drug substances)
  • The drug constituent of a combination (drug and medical device) product

What Is NOT Covered

  • Type A medicated articles and medicated feed
  • Medical devices (separate guidance applies)
  • Dietary supplements
  • Human tissues intended for transplantation under section 361 of the PHS Act
  • Automated process control systems (computer hardware/software validation — addressed separately)
  • Regulatory submission content requirements (refer to appropriate Center guidance)
Key Statement: "FDA encourages the use of modern pharmaceutical development concepts, quality risk management, and quality systems at all stages of the manufacturing process lifecycle."

Commentary  |  Core Positioning of This Guidance

What does "current thinking" mean?

Legally, FDA guidance documents are "nonbinding recommendations", but in regulatory practice FDA investigators use them as the yardstick. Put simply: you may choose not to follow it, but you must have a sound scientific justification. For a CDMO, this kind of guidance is effectively mandatory.

Why align with ICH Q8/Q9/Q10?

Before 2011, FDA and ICH thinking on validation was not aligned. The significance of the 2011 guidance is that it "embeds" process validation in ICH's quality system framework:

  • ICH Q8(R2): Pharmaceutical Development - design space, PAT
  • ICH Q9: Quality Risk Management - identify, assess and control risks
  • ICH Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System - continual improvement, operational excellence

Together, the three support the concept of lifecycle validation.

Analogy: validation is like building a house

Stage 1 (Design) = the architect draws the plans and sets the construction specifications.
Stage 2 (Qualification) = the builder constructs the house and it is inspected, confirming it meets the design.
Stage 3 (Continued verification) = the owner maintains the house and monitors its structure regularly so that it stays safe over the long term.

The 1987 thinking focused only on "acceptance" (Stage 2) and ignored design quality and long-term monitoring.

Important boundaries of scope

This guidance does not cover computerized system validation (CSV), process validation of medical devices, or dietary supplements. It does, however, explicitly apply to:

  • Sterile injectable products (vials, prefilled syringes)
  • Active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) processes
  • Biologics (including antibodies and vaccines)

For an aseptic fill-finish CDMO, this guidance is one of the most important regulatory documents.

Background — Why FDA Updated the 1987 Guidance

Background - Why FDA Updated the 1987 Guidance

Original Text

In the Federal Register of May 11, 1987 (52 FR 17638), FDA issued a notice announcing the availability of a guidance entitled Guideline on General Principles of Process Validation (the 1987 guidance). Since then, we have obtained additional experience through our regulatory oversight that allows us to update our recommendations to industry on this topic.

This revised guidance conveys FDA's current thinking on process validation and is consistent with basic principles first introduced in the 1987 guidance. The revised guidance also provides recommendations that reflect some of the goals of FDA's initiative entitled "Pharmaceutical CGMPs for the 21st Century — A Risk-Based Approach," particularly with regard to the use of technological advances in pharmaceutical manufacturing, as well as implementation of modern risk management and quality system tools and concepts.

Key Statement: "This revised guidance replaces the 1987 guidance."

FDA has the authority and responsibility to inspect and evaluate process validation performed by manufacturers. The CGMP regulations for validating pharmaceutical (drug) manufacturing require that drug products be produced with a high degree of assurance of meeting all the attributes they are intended to possess.

Regulatory Authority Citations

  • 21 CFR 211.100(a) — Written procedures; deviations. Requires written procedures for production and process controls, and documentation that instructions were followed.
  • 21 CFR 211.110(a) — Sampling and testing of in-process materials and drug products. Requires control procedures to monitor output and validate performance of manufacturing processes that may be responsible for causing variability.

Commentary  |  The Shift in Thinking from 1987 to 2011

Limitations of the 1987 guidance

The 1987 process validation guidance treated validation as a "point-in-time event": run three validation batches (PPQ runs) before the start of commercial production, and then you are done. The problems with this thinking were:

  • After validation is complete, does the process remain in a state of control? There was no mechanism for ongoing monitoring
  • Changes in raw material suppliers, aging equipment and staff turnover - the impact of these changes on the process was not tracked
  • Over-reliance on batch release testing rather than process understanding

Impact of the 21st Century cGMP initiative

In 2002 FDA launched the "Pharmaceutical CGMPs for the 21st Century - A Risk-Based Approach" initiative, the direct predecessor of the 2011 guidance. Key changes:

  • Old thinking: process validation = a compliance obligation, done once and filed away
  • New thinking: process validation = accumulation of process knowledge and continual improvement
  • Tools: introduction of PAT (process analytical technology), design space and QbD

21 CFR 211.100(a) vs 211.110(a) - Practical Differences

211.100(a) is about whether there are written procedures - the focus is documentation. During an inspection, FDA will ask to see SOPs and batch manufacturing records (BMR).

211.110(a) is about whether process variability is monitored - the focus is scientific evidence. During an inspection, FDA will ask to see in-process testing (IPT) data, statistical process control (SPC) charts and OOS investigations.

A CDMO should prepare separate response strategies for these two provisions when preparing for audits.

Process Validation and Drug Quality

Process Validation and Drug Quality - Why End-Product Testing Is Not Enough

Original Text

Effective process validation contributes significantly to assuring drug quality. The basic principle of quality assurance is that a drug should be produced that is fit for its intended use. This principle incorporates the understanding that the following conditions exist:

  • Quality, safety, and efficacy are designed or built into the product.
  • Quality cannot be adequately assured merely by in-process and finished-product inspection or testing.
  • Each step of a manufacturing process is controlled to assure that the finished product meets all quality attributes including specifications.
Fundamental Principle: "Quality cannot be adequately assured merely by in-process and finished-product inspection or testing."

This statement represents a foundational shift in pharmaceutical quality philosophy — moving from testing quality in to building quality in.

Three Pillars of Quality Assurance

Commentary  |  Quality Cannot Be "Inspected In"

The core philosophical shift: Testing vs. Building

These three principles represent the most important shift in quality philosophy in the pharmaceutical industry:

  • Old thinking (Testing-in): after production, take many samples and test; release if they pass - like using exam scores to "measure" learning outcomes
  • New thinking (Building-in): quality is built in at the design stage, the process is controlled, and every batch is acceptable - like "a well-designed teaching system that lets every student learn"

This is especially important for sterile injectables, because sterility cannot be fully confirmed by destructive sampling.

Why does end-product testing have fundamental limitations?

Take a batch of 10,000 injectable units as an example:

  • The USP sterility test typically samples 20 units - coverage of only 0.2%
  • If the contamination rate is 0.1% (that is, 10 units), the sample may miss it entirely
  • Endotoxin is unevenly distributed, so a local excursion above the limit may be diluted away
  • Sub-visible particle counts based on sampling carry statistical uncertainty

Therefore, a "controlled process" provides stronger quality assurance than "extensive testing".

The regulatory meaning of "Fit for Intended Use"

"Fit for intended use" is a core definition of GMP, derived from ICH Q6A/Q6B. In practice, it means:

  • Clinical patients receive a drug with a consistent dose
  • Sterile products introduce no infection risk to immunocompromised patients
  • Appearance and characteristics are acceptable to patients
  • Stability meets the needs of use over the entire shelf life

Restaurant analogy

Imagine a restaurant:

  • Old approach: the chef cooks at random, and a waiter tastes every dish before serving and throws away any that fail - high waste and a high escape rate
  • New approach: standardized recipes + well-trained chefs + standardized prep procedures + temperature-controlled equipment - every dish is acceptable from the start

Process validation ensures that the "restaurant system" (rather than the "taster") guarantees food safety.

Practice Questions

  2. How is the principle "Quality is built in" reflected in one specific process step at your site? Give an example.
  3. If a client asks the CDMO to add more end-product tests before batch release, how would you respond using the logic of this guidance?

Approach to Process Validation — Definition & Three-Stage Lifecycle

Approach to Process Validation - Definition and the Three-Stage Lifecycle

FDA Official Definition of Process Validation

"Process validation is defined as the collection and evaluation of data, from the process design stage through commercial production, which establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product."

"Process validation is defined as the collection and evaluation of data, from the process design stage through commercial production, which establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product."

Three-Stage Lifecycle Model

Process validation involves a series of activities taking place over the lifecycle of the product and process. This guidance describes process validation activities in three stages:

  • Stage 1 — Process Design: The commercial manufacturing process is defined during this stage based on knowledge gained through development and scale-up activities.
  • Stage 2 — Process Qualification: During this stage, the process design is evaluated to determine if the process is capable of reproducible commercial manufacturing.
  • Stage 3 — Continued Process Verification: Ongoing assurance is gained during routine production that the process remains in a state of control.

This guidance describes activities typical of each stage, but in practice, some activities might occur in multiple stages.

Key Requirement: "Before any batch from the process is commercially distributed for use by consumers, a manufacturer should have gained a high degree of assurance in the performance of the manufacturing process such that it will consistently produce APIs and drug products meeting those attributes relating to identity, strength, quality, purity, and potency."

The assurance should be obtained from objective information and data from laboratory-, pilot-, and/or commercial-scale studies. Information and data should demonstrate that the commercial manufacturing process is capable of consistently producing acceptable quality products within commercial manufacturing conditions.

Commentary  |  The Definition, Word by Word

Five key phrases in the definition

  1. Collection and evaluation of data - emphasizes scientific data, not subjective judgment
  2. From the process design stage - validation begins in development, not just before production
  3. Through commercial production - it continues after launch
  4. Scientific evidence - quantifiable, reproducible and statistically supported
  5. Capable of consistently delivering - emphasizes "consistency"; success in a single batch is not validation

Identity, Strength, Quality, Purity, Potency

The five "ISQPP" quality attributes come from section 501 of the FD&C Act and are the statutory criteria for judging whether a drug meets requirements:

  • Identity: confirms it is the active ingredient claimed
  • Strength (assay/content): the content of the active ingredient matches the label
  • Quality: overall quality meets the predetermined standards
  • Purity: no unacceptable impurities
  • Potency: appropriate therapeutic activity (especially important for biologics)

Practical meaning of "Some activities might occur in multiple stages"

FDA acknowledges that the three stages overlap in practice. For example:

  • Scale-up data from process design (Stage 1) may also serve as supporting evidence for Stage 2
  • PPQ batch data from Stage 2 can provide the basis for establishing the Stage 3 baseline
  • Variation found in Stage 3 may trigger a return to Stage 1 for process redesign

This is the essence of the "lifecycle" concept - not a one-way linear flow, but a cycle of continuous feedback.

Stage 1 — Process Design: Key Activities

  • Define Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs)
  • Identify Critical Process Parameters (CPPs) and their ranges
  • Conduct risk assessment to identify variability sources
  • Perform Design of Experiments (DoE) studies at lab and/or pilot scale
  • Establish the design space (if applicable, per ICH Q8)
  • Develop process understanding through development studies
  • Scale-up activities — from lab to pilot to commercial scale
  • Establish control strategy

Stage 2 — Process Qualification: Key Activities

  • Qualification of equipment, utilities, facilities (IQ/OQ)
  • Process Performance Qualification (PPQ) — commercial-scale runs
  • Demonstrate reproducibility across multiple batches
  • Evaluate worst-case conditions (if applicable)
  • Confirm all CQAs are met consistently
  • Generate data sufficient to support commercial distribution

Stage 3 — Continued Process Verification: Key Activities

  • Ongoing statistical monitoring of process performance
  • Annual Product Review (APR) / Product Quality Review (PQR)
  • Statistical Process Control (SPC) charts for CPPs and CQAs
  • Investigation of adverse trends before they become OOS
  • Maintain validated state through change control
  • Evaluate impact of material, equipment, process changes

Commentary  |  The Three Stages in Practice

Stage 1 in a CDMO

For a CDMO, Stage 1 is usually led by the client (the drug company's R&D department), and by the time the CDMO takes over the following already exist:

  • Process description and development report
  • Preliminary CQA list
  • Lab-scale/pilot-scale data

The CDMO's responsibility is to conduct a technology transfer assessment of this knowledge and confirm that it can be reproduced under commercial equipment/facility conditions. This assessment is itself part of Stage 1 knowledge.

Stage 2 - Scientific Judgment on the Number of PPQ Batches

One of the biggest breakthroughs of the 2011 guidance: it abolished the rigid "three batches" requirement in favor of "scientific judgment". Factors affecting the number of PPQ batches:

  • Process complexity and degree of variability
  • Statistical analysis of batch-to-batch consistency
  • Adequacy of prior development data
  • Risk level of critical steps

In practice, three batches are still the starting point, but high-risk processes may need more, while processes of low complexity with sufficient precedent may need fewer.

Stage 3 - From "Obligation" to "Competitive Advantage"

Stage 3 (continued process verification) is the weakest link for many companies and has been an FDA inspection focus in recent years. An excellent Stage 3 system should include:

  • A real-time process monitoring dashboard
  • SPC control charts (Shewhart, CUSUM)
  • Periodic evaluation of process capability indices (Cpk)
  • A mechanism where trend analysis triggers investigations

For a CDMO, an outstanding Stage 3 system is a differentiator that attracts high-end clients.

The lifecycle "feedback loop"

The three stages do not flow in one direction; there are feedback mechanisms:

  • Stage 3 detects an abnormal product trend → triggers CAPA → may require returning to Stage 1 to re-evaluate CPPs
  • Stage 2 PPQ data → establish the statistical control baseline for Stage 3
  • Stage 1 design space → defines the monitoring range and alert limits for Stage 3

This feedback mechanism ensures that "validation knowledge" does not stop accumulating once commercial production begins.

Understanding Variation — The Scientific Foundation

Understanding Variation - The Scientific Basis of Process Validation

Original Text

A successful validation program depends upon information and knowledge from product and process development. This knowledge and understanding is the basis for establishing an approach to control of the manufacturing process that results in products with the desired quality attributes. Manufacturers should:

  • Understand the sources of variation
  • Detect the presence and degree of variation
  • Understand the impact of variation on the process and ultimately on product attributes
  • Control the variation in a manner commensurate with the risk it represents to the process and product

Each manufacturer should judge whether it has gained sufficient understanding to provide a high degree of assurance in its manufacturing process to justify commercial distribution of the drug product.

Key Concept: Variation control must be "commensurate with the risk it represents" — this is the direct integration of ICH Q9 risk management principles into process validation.

Sources of Variation in Manufacturing

Commentary  |  Four Steps of Variation Management

A four-step framework for variation management

The four steps FDA requires, mapped to practical tools:

  • 1. Understand the sources of variation → Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram, FMEA risk assessment, DoE (design of experiments)
  • 2. Detect the presence and degree of variation → IPT (in-process testing), PAT (process analytical technology), SPC control charts
  • 3. Understand the impact of variation on the product → multivariate analysis, process capability analysis, correlation studies
  • 4. Control variation commensurate with risk → ICH Q9-based control strategy, process specifications, alert and action limits

The essence of "Commensurate with Risk"

This phrase means FDA accepts the principle of proportionate risk: not every source of variation needs the same degree of control. For example:

  • High risk: a filtration step that directly affects sterility → 100% integrity testing
  • Medium risk: mixing time that affects content uniformity → statistical sampling monitoring
  • Low risk: deviation in the print position of the label → periodic visual spot checks

When resources are limited, this principle helps you focus validation effort where it matters most.

How is "High Degree of Assurance" quantified?

The guidance does not define a numerical standard for "high degree of assurance", but the industry generally accepts:

  • Process capability: Cpk ≥ 1.33 (long-term capability), Ppk ≥ 1.33 (PPQ batches)
  • Rejection rate: PPQ batch OOS rate = 0, or statistically acceptable
  • Batch-to-batch consistency: the between-batch CV of critical attributes is within predetermined ranges
  • Trend stability: no non-random patterns on SPC control charts

Ultimately, the manufacturer's quality system and scientific judgment determine what counts as "sufficient assurance".

Variation management: a weather forecast analogy

Variation in a process is like changes in the weather:

  • Understanding the sources = meteorologists study air pressure, humidity and ocean currents
  • Detecting the degree = thermometers, rain gauges and weather radar
  • Understanding the impact = predicting the agricultural losses from a typhoon's path
  • Controlling by risk = a typhoon warning (high risk) vs a reminder about cloudy skies (low risk)

No one expects weather variation to be eliminated completely, but a good weather system lets us respond to change "within a controlled range".

Interconnected Guidance Framework

Commentary  |  What the Regulations Mean for Inspections

Common Form 483 observations under 211.100(a)

Common Form 483 observations that FDA issues under this provision include:

  • The batch manufacturing record (BMR) does not match the validated process
  • Process parameter deviations not recorded in accordance with SOPs
  • No written procedures for critical steps
  • Process changes not reviewed and approved in writing

Response strategy: make sure every CPP has a written specification and that deviations are recorded promptly.

Common Form 483 observations under 211.110(a)

Form 483 observations under this provision include:

  • The in-process testing (IPT) plan is inadequate to monitor critical steps
  • Process performance data are not trended
  • Unexpected batch-to-batch variation in production batches is not investigated
  • Insufficient process validation data to support commercial production

Response strategy: build a complete IPT system + periodic SPC reviews + process capability reports.

The ICH Q8/Q9/Q10 triangle

The three ICH guidelines form a complementary triangle that supports process validation:

  • ICH Q8 (Design): determines "what to do" - design space, CPP ranges, CQA criteria
  • ICH Q9 (Risk): decides "what to control and how deeply" - risk assessment drives the control strategy
  • ICH Q10 (System): ensures "continued effectiveness" - the quality system underpins lifecycle management

If any one of the three is missing, the process validation lifecycle cannot operate completely.

Nonbinding vs. actual enforcement strength

Although this guidance is labeled "nonbinding recommendations", the consequences of not following it include:

  • Form 483 observations (inspection warnings)
  • Warning Letter
  • Import Alert
  • Consent Decree

In practice, adherence to this guidance is equivalent to a legally mandatory requirement.

CDMO Implications & Practical Application

CDMO Practical Application - Special Considerations for Contract Manufacturing Organizations

How This Guidance Applies in a CDMO Context

While this guidance is written primarily for manufacturers, contract development and manufacturing organizations (CDMOs) have unique considerations when implementing this framework. As the manufacturer of record for their clients' products, CDMOs bear direct regulatory responsibility for process validation compliance.

Key CDMO-Specific Validation Challenges

Quality Agreement Validation-Relevant Elements

  • Responsibility matrix for Stage 1, 2, 3 activities
  • Approval rights for Validation Master Plan (VMP) and individual PVPs
  • Data ownership and access rights (especially for regulatory submissions)
  • Change control notification requirements (client to CDMO)
  • Revalidation triggers and responsibilities
  • Stage 3 data reporting frequency and format

Commentary  |  Positioning the CDMO's Validation Responsibility

The CDMO is the manufacturer that "holds validation responsibility"

In FDA's eyes, a CDMO is a manufacturer and is directly responsible for the validation status of its facilities and processes. The client (the MAH) is also responsible for ensuring that its outsourced manufacturing complies with regulations, but it cannot shift all responsibility to the CDMO.

In practice, a CDMO usually:

  • Owns the validation documents for facilities and equipment (Stage 2 IQ/OQ)
  • Co-owns/co-manages process validation documents with the client (PPQ, Stage 3)
  • Clearly divides responsibilities between the two parties through a quality agreement

Technology transfer is the core of Stage 1

For a CDMO, technology transfer is essentially the Stage 1 process of "acquiring process design knowledge". Key elements to be transferred:

  • Process description and process flow diagram (PFD)
  • CQA/CPP list and specifications
  • Control strategy documents
  • Analytical methods and validation reports
  • Batch record (BMR) templates
  • Material balance and batch calculations

The technology transfer assessment report (TTR) is the key deliverable for completing Stage 1.

Matrix validation strategy for multi-client sites

An aseptic fill-finish CDMO usually uses a matrix/bracketing approach for equipment qualification to avoid repeating the full set of IQ/OQ for every product. Key principles:

  • Select a "worst-case" product to represent the whole product group
  • Matrix design for the fill volume range
  • Separate validation for each container type (vial, PFS, cartridge)
  • Boundary validation of the filling speed range

When a new client product is added, assess whether it falls within the validated matrix; if it falls outside, supplementary validation is triggered.

The CDMO-client validation responsibility can be compared to a bank "joint account":

  • Both parties must "sign" to carry out major operations (such as process changes)
  • The CDMO manages the "account infrastructure" (equipment, facilities, environment)
  • The client provides the "deposit knowledge" (development data, quality standards)
  • The quality agreement is the "account contract"

A failure by either party can jeopardize the whole account (the compliance status of the batch/product).

Statutory Requirements for Process Validation

Statutory Requirements for Process Validation | FDCA § 501(a)(2)(B) & cGMP Framework

Original Text — FDA Guidance (January 2011)

Process validation for drugs (finished pharmaceuticals and components) is a legally enforceable requirement under section 501(a)(2)(B) of the Act (21 U.S.C. 351(a)(2)(B)), which states the following:

A drug . . . shall be deemed to be adulterated . . . if . . . the methods used in, or the facilities or controls used for, its manufacture, processing, packing, or holding do not conform to or are not operated or administered in conformity with current good manufacturing practice to assure that such drug meets the requirements of this Act as to safety and has the identity and strength, and meets the quality and purity characteristics, which it purports or is represented to possess.

FDA regulations describing current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) for finished pharmaceuticals are provided in 21 CFR parts 210 and 211.

The CGMP regulations require that manufacturing processes be designed and controlled to assure that in-process materials and the finished product meet predetermined quality requirements and do so consistently and reliably. Process validation is required, in both general and specific terms, by the CGMP regulations in parts 210 and 211.

§ 211.100(a) — "There shall be written procedures for production and process control designed to assure that the drug products have the identity, strength, quality, and purity they purport or are represented to possess."

This regulation requires manufacturers to design a process, including operations and controls, which results in a product meeting these attributes.

Commentary & Practical Application

Legal Standing: Why Is Process Validation Not Optional?

Many people mistakenly regard process validation as merely a "best practice." In fact, FDCA Section 501(a)(2)(B) states directly that a drug is deemed adulterated if its manufacturing methods do not comply with cGMP. This means:

  • Non-compliance with cGMP = the product can be subject to mandatory recall or seizure
  • FDA can issue a warning letter or refuse import without a court order
  • In a CDMO setting, if the contract requires cGMP compliance, the validation obligations apply automatically

Analogy: Restaurant Hygiene Rules vs. Pharmaceutical cGMP

Just as a health authority requires a restaurant to control food temperatures rather than merely "hoping" the cooks pay attention, the FDA legal framework requires manufacturers to demonstrate systematically that the process delivers the required quality, not by luck but by design. Process validation is the mechanism of this "systematic demonstration."

CDMO Practical Point: Allocation of Regulatory Responsibility

In contract manufacturing, regulatory responsibility remains with the NDA/ANDA holder (the MAH), but the CDMO, as the actual manufacturer, must provide complete process validation data. During an inspection, FDA audits the CDMO facility directly, and any deficiency can prevent the client's product from being shipped.

§100

21 CFR § 211.100(a) — Production and Process Controls

Production and Process Controls | The Statutory Basis for Written Procedure Design

Regulatory Text & Context

The foundation for process validation is provided in § 211.100(a), which states:

"There shall be written procedures for production and process control designed to assure that the drug products have the identity, strength, quality, and purity they purport or are represented to possess..."

This regulation requires manufacturers to design a process, including operations and controls, which results in a product meeting these attributes.

The CGMP regulations require that manufacturing processes be:

  • Designed to meet predetermined quality requirements
  • Controlled to meet these requirements
  • Operated consistently and reliably

Process validation is required in both general and specific terms by the CGMP regulations in parts 210 and 211. Focusing exclusively on qualification efforts without also understanding the manufacturing process and associated variations may not lead to adequate assurance of quality.

Key principle: Manufacturers must design a process that reliably delivers the intended quality — not merely test whether a batch passes.

Commentary: The Fundamental Difference Between "Design" and "Testing"

The Core Philosophy of §211.100(a)

What this regulation emphasizes is not "passing final testing" but that the process design itself must ensure quality. It is the regulatory basis for FDA's shift from the old "test quality in" approach to "build quality in."

  • Old thinking: Complete production → test → release if it passes
  • New thinking (FDA requirement): Understand the process → design the control strategy → the process itself ensures quality → testing serves as confirmation

Analogy: Bridge Structural Engineering

A bridge is not validated by driving a heavy truck over it once a day and calling it safe if it does not collapse. Engineers use materials science, load calculations, and design codes to ensure that the bridge has sufficient strength at the design stage. The logic of pharmaceutical process validation is the same: understand the process, design the controls, then use validation data to confirm that the design works.

Implications for Aseptic Filling

In aseptic filling operations, §211.100(a) means that parameters such as filling speed, nitrogen overlay, and stopper insertion force are not set arbitrarily. Every written procedure should have a scientific rationale that can be linked to a product quality attribute (such as sterility assurance level or container closure integrity).

§110

21 CFR § 211.110 — In-Process Sampling & Specifications

In-Process Sampling and Specifications | A Regulatory Tool for Controlling Variability

Original Text — §211.110(a) & (b)

§ 211.110(a) — Sampling and testing of in-process materials and drug products requires that control procedures ". . . be established to monitor the output and to validate the performance of those manufacturing processes that may be responsible for causing variability in the characteristics of in-process material and the drug product."

Under this regulation, even well-designed processes must include in-process control procedures to assure final product quality.

In addition, the CGMP regulations regarding sampling set forth a number of requirements for validation:

  • Samples must represent the batch under analysis (§ 211.160(b)(3))
  • The sampling plan must result in statistical confidence (§ 211.165(c) and (d))
  • The batch must meet its predetermined specifications (§ 211.165(a))
§ 211.110(b) — Two principles for in-process specifications:
  1. In-process specifications "shall be consistent with drug product final specifications."
  2. In-process specifications "shall be derived from previous acceptable process average and process variability estimates where possible and determined by the application of suitable statistical procedures where appropriate."

This requirement establishes the need for manufacturers to analyze process performance and control batch-to-batch variability.

Commentary: The Dual Purpose of In-Process Controls

§211.110(a): Why Does a "Good Design" Still Need In-Process Controls?

Even with a well-designed process, variation in raw material lots, equipment wear, and differences in operator practice can all introduce variability. FDA explicitly requires that in-process control procedures be able to:

  • Monitor output: detect deviation trends in real time rather than discovering them only at final release
  • Validate performance: continuously confirm that the process is operating within validated conditions

For aseptic filling, this covers in-line controls such as environmental monitoring, container integrity testing, and fill volume checks.

§211.110(b): Practical Meaning of the Two Principles

Principle 1 (consistent with final specifications): If the final product pH specification is 6.8-7.2, the in-process pH specification must not be set at 6.5-7.5; otherwise an intermediate could pass yet drift outside the final specification in later steps.

Principle 2 (derived statistically): In-process specifications cannot be set by gut feeling; they must be based on estimates of the historical process mean and variability. This directly echoes the statistical analysis capability needed for Stage 3 continued process verification (CPV).

Analogy: In-Flight Instrument Monitoring

Pre-flight ground checks (analogous to design confirmation before validation) are certainly important, but during flight the pilot must continuously monitor altitude, speed, and engine parameters (analogous to in-process controls). With only a pre-takeoff check and no in-flight monitoring, no deviation arising en route could be detected in time. §211.110(a) requires exactly this "in-flight monitoring" mechanism.

Specific Applications for an Aseptic Filling CDMO

Taking a lyophilized injectable as an example, the corresponding items under §211.110 in practice are:

  • Fill weight: sampled every N units, with specifications statistically consistent with the final content specification
  • Lyophilization endpoint: in-process measurement of residual moisture (Karl Fischer), with specifications derived from historical batch variability
  • Shift sampling plan: must have a statistical basis and cannot sample only "convenient" locations

Periodic Review and Process Maintenance | The Regulatory Basis for Stage 3 Continued Verification

Regulatory Basis for Continuous Process Monitoring

§ 211.180(e) requires that information and data about product quality and manufacturing experience be periodically reviewed to determine whether any changes to the established process are warranted.

Ongoing feedback about product quality and process performance is an essential feature of process maintenance.

In addition, the CGMP regulations also describe facilities and equipment requirements that are integral to process validation:

§ 211.42 — Facilities must be of suitable size, construction, and location to facilitate proper operations.
§ 211.63 — Equipment must be of appropriate design, adequate size, and suitably located to facilitate operations for its intended use.
§ 211.68 — Automated, mechanical, and electronic equipment must be calibrated, inspected, or checked according to a written program designed to assure proper performance.

Summary: The CGMP regulations require that manufacturing processes be designed and controlled to assure that in-process materials and the finished product meet predetermined quality requirements and do so consistently and reliably.

Commentary: The "Second Half" Obligation of Process Validation

§211.180(e) and the Link to Stage 3 CPV

This regulation is the regulatory basis for "Stage 3: Continued Process Verification (CPV)" in the 2011 FDA guidance. It requires:

  • Periodically: not only when problems occur
  • Review of quality data and manufacturing experience: including OOS/OOT trends, complaints, and batch rejection rates
  • Determining whether changes are needed: proactively identifying improvement opportunities rather than passively waiting for problems

Analogy: Routine Car Servicing vs. Waiting for a Breakdown

The spirit of §211.180(e) is like a routine servicing schedule: the car manufacturer requires an oil change every 10,000 km not because the engine has already failed, but to maintain designed performance. Pharmaceutical manufacturers likewise need to give their processes a regular "health check" to confirm that they are still operating within a state of control.

Validation Implications of the Equipment-Related Provisions (§211.42/63/68)

These three regulations form the regulatory basis for equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ):

  • §211.42 → facility design / HVAC qualification
  • §211.63 → equipment design qualification (DQ) and installation qualification (IQ)
  • §211.68 → calibration program / instrument qualification (OQ)

In an aseptic filling environment, these three regulations together underpin cleanroom certification, filling machine qualification, and the calibration requirements for all measuring instruments.

General Considerations for Process Validation | Scientific Understanding, QbD Principles, and a Risk-Based Approach

FDA's Foundational Framework

In all stages of the product lifecycle, good project management and good archiving that capture scientific knowledge will make the process validation program more effective and efficient.

Core Message: Understanding > Qualification (Understand > Qualify)

This passage from FDA is the philosophical core of the entire 2011 guidance: qualification alone, without understanding the process, is not sufficient to assure quality.

The impact on industry is as follows:

  • Traditional approach: complete IQ/OQ/PQ in a fixed format; report received = validation complete
  • FDA expectation: understand the process thoroughly before validation, knowing which variables affect quality and to what extent

"All materials, equipment, environment, personnel, and procedures may change"

This sentence is especially important: FDA states explicitly that even when raw material suppliers change, equipment ages or is replaced, and operators turn over, the manufacturer remains responsible for keeping the process in a state of control. This is the fundamental reason continued process verification (CPV) exists, and the reason a CDMO must build complete process understanding during technology transfer.

Criticality as a Continuum: Risk-Based Thinking

Criticality as a Continuum: Risk-Based Thinking | Putting ICH Q9 and QbD into Practice

FDA's Risk-Proportionate Approach

The terms attribute(s) and parameter(s) are not categorized with respect to criticality in this guidance. With a lifecycle approach to process validation that employs risk-based decision making throughout that lifecycle, the perception of criticality as a continuum rather than a binary state is more useful.

All attributes and parameters should be evaluated in terms of their:

  • Roles in the process
  • Impact on the product or in-process material
  • Reevaluated as new information becomes available

The degree of control over those attributes or parameters should be commensurate with their risk to the process and process output. A higher degree of control is appropriate for attributes or parameters that pose a higher risk.

"The Agency recognizes that terminology usage can vary and expects that each manufacturer will communicate the meaning and intent of its terminology and categorization to the Agency."

Many products are single-source or involve complicated manufacturing processes. Homogeneity within a batch and consistency between batches are goals of process validation activities. Validation offers assurance that a process is reasonably protected against sources of variability that could affect production output, cause supply problems, and negatively affect public health.

Commentary: Breaking the Binary "Critical / Non-Critical" Mindset

The Practical Meaning of "Continuum" Thinking

The traditional approach tends to classify process parameters into two categories, "critical" or "non-critical," then applies strict control to the "critical" ones and almost no control to the "non-critical" ones. FDA states clearly here that this binary classification is not sufficient.

A more useful framework is:

  • Each parameter has an impact score (its effect on the CQAs)
  • The stringency of control is proportional to the impact score
  • As new data accumulate, the impact score can be updated dynamically

Analogy: Risk Management for an Aircraft Engine

  • Homogeneity within a batch: content, potency, and appearance are consistent across every dosage unit within the same batch. This is especially critical for mixing and filling processes.
  • Consistency between batches: different batches produced under the same conditions perform consistently. This is the regulatory requirement for "reproducibility."

QbD Principles, Design Space & Understanding Variability

Quality by Design Principles, Design Space, and Understanding Variability | Integrating the ICH Q8/Q9/Q10 Framework

Scientific Understanding as Validation Foundation

FDA's 2011 guidance aligns with the Quality by Design (QbD) framework established under ICH Q8/Q9/Q10. The core principles embedded in Sections I-II include:

  • Design of Experiments (DOE): Systematic studies to understand how process variables interact and impact product quality attributes.
  • Risk Analysis: Formal tools (FMEA, fishbone diagrams, risk ranking) to identify and prioritize sources of variability before validation begins.
  • Prior Knowledge: Platform knowledge, development data, clinical manufacturing data, and literature all inform the validation strategy.
  • Design Space: The multidimensional combination of input variables and process parameters that have been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality. Operating within the design space is not considered a change.
  • Control Strategy: The planned set of controls derived from current product and process understanding that ensures process performance and product quality.
Key insight from the guidance: "Process understanding is the foundation for reducing validation burden." Manufacturers who deeply understand their processes need fewer confirmatory experiments because they can predict how the process will behave under variation.

Manufacturers should use ongoing programs to collect and analyze product and process data to evaluate the state of control of the process. These programs may identify process or product problems or opportunities for process improvements.

Manufacturers of legacy products can take advantage of the knowledge gained from the original process development and qualification work as well as manufacturing experience to continually improve their processes. Implementation of these recommendations for legacy products and processes would likely begin with Stage 3 activities.

Commentary: How Does QbD Reduce the Validation Burden?

The Three Core Elements of the QbD Framework

  • Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP): defines the quality characteristics the product must achieve (intended use, dosage form, route of administration, potency, etc.)
  • Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs): product attributes that must be controlled, derived from the QTPP (such as content uniformity, sterility, visible particles)
  • Critical Process Parameters (CPPs): process parameters that significantly affect CQAs and must be monitored/controlled

The Relationship Between "Design Space" and Validation

The design space defined in ICH Q8 is the core tool of QbD. When a manufacturer can:

  1. demonstrate through DOE that operation anywhere within the design space meets the CQAs
  2. establish a robust process design space

then operating adjustments within the design space are not regarded as a change in the regulatory sense and do not require additional validation. This is the concrete mechanism behind "process understanding = reduced validation burden."

Special Considerations for Legacy Products

Many older commercial products (legacy products) were not originally developed under a QbD framework, and their validation documentation may consist only of three PPQ batch reports, lacking in-depth process understanding documentation. FDA suggests that such products begin at Stage 3 (CPV) and gradually build up process knowledge, rather than requiring all Stage 1-2 activities to be redone. For a CDMO, taking over such a product requires assessing the knowledge density of the existing documentation to determine whether it provides a sufficient basis for subsequent process management.

Analogy: Map vs. Satellite Navigation

Traditional three-batch PPQ validation is like navigating with an old map: you know one route, and as long as you follow it you reach the destination, but if the road is closed you do not know the alternatives. The QbD framework builds a satellite navigation system: you understand the whole road network (the design space), so any reasonable route adjustment does not require "revalidating the entire journey."

Identification Methods & Risk Assessment Tools

How Do You Identify CQAs?

  1. Starting from the QTPP, list all possible quality attributes
  2. Assess the severity of the impact on patient safety/efficacy if each attribute goes out of specification
  3. High-severity attributes = CQAs (must be monitored and have clear specifications)
  4. Example: for a sterile injectable, the severity of "sterility" is the highest, so it is necessarily a CQA

How Do You Identify CPPs?

  1. List all process parameters (the input variables of each unit operation)
  2. Through DOE or scientific reasoning, assess each parameter's effect on each CQA
  3. Significant effect = CPP; negligible effect = general process parameter (recorded, but with a lower level of control)
  4. The level of control is proportional to the risk level (the risk continuum principle)

  • Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram: identifies all potential influencing factors
  • FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis): quantifies the risk priority number (RPN)
  • Cause and Effect Matrix (C&E Matrix): systematically evaluates parameter-attribute relationships

An Important Nuance: Not All CPP Excursions Reject a Batch

It is important to distinguish between a CPP excursion and a CQA failure. A CPP out of range does not automatically mean the batch fails — it triggers a risk assessment to determine whether the out-of-range CPP actually impacted any CQA. This is where thorough process understanding (Stage 1 data) becomes critical: manufacturers who understand the quantitative relationship between CPP and CQA can make science-based batch disposition decisions.

An Important Distinction: CPP Deviation ≠ Batch Rejection

A CPP excursion outside the operating range does not automatically mean the batch is rejected. It triggers a deviation investigation + impact assessment: did the CPP deviation actually affect the CQAs? This assessment must be based on sufficient process understanding data (the CPP-CQA relationship curves from Stage 1 studies). This is another dimension of "process understanding reduces the validation burden": a deep understanding of the process makes batch disposition decisions more scientifically grounded, rather than conservatively rejecting every batch with a deviation.

Stage 1: Process Design — Overview

FDA source text pp.11 | Defining the commercial manufacturing process

B. Stage 1 — Process Design (FDA Original Text)

"Process design is the activity of defining the commercial manufacturing process that will be reflected in planned master production and control records. The goal of this stage is to design a process suitable for routine commercial manufacturing that can consistently deliver a product that meets its quality attributes."

Stage 1 is where manufacturers make fundamental decisions about how a product will be manufactured at commercial scale. The outputs of Stage 1 are codified in the master production and control records (MPCRs) — the living documents that govern every commercial batch.

The two major sub-sections within Stage 1 are:

  1. Building and Capturing Process Knowledge and Understanding — the science-based learning phase
  2. Establishing a Strategy for Process Control — converting knowledge into a defensible control approach

Early process design experiments do not need to be performed under full CGMP conditions, but must follow sound scientific methods and good documentation practices, consistent with ICH Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System.

Commentary: Definition and Position of Stage 1

What is Stage 1?

Stage 1 (process design) is the knowledge-building phase of the entire process validation lifecycle. In this stage you define the commercial manufacturing process: which parameters to use, what ranges, and how to control them.

Analogy: Stage 1 is like the architectural design phase before building a house. This is where you decide the structure, materials and load-bearing specifications, and all later construction (Stage 2) and occupancy monitoring (Stage 3) follow from it.

Quick Recap of the Three-Stage Lifecycle

  • Stage 1 - Process Design: Build process knowledge and a control strategy
  • Stage 2 - Process Qualification: Demonstrate at commercial scale that the process is reproducible
  • Stage 3 - Continued Process Verification: Continuously monitor the state of control of the process after launch

FDA's core logic: the more solid the Stage 1 work, the fewer batches Stage 2 needs and the fewer problems arise in Stage 3.

Exceptions to the CGMP Requirements

Early process design experiments (such as small-scale laboratory studies) do not need to be run under full CGMP conditions, but there is one important exception:

Viral clearance studies and impurity clearance studies must be overseen by the quality unit (QU) even when performed at small scale, to ensure the scientific soundness of the methods and the reliability of the conclusions.

Reason: these studies are used to project product quality at commercial scale directly, so there is no room for carelessness.

Building and Capturing Process Knowledge

FDA source text pp.11–12 | Sources of knowledge, variability, and documentation

1. Building and Capturing Process Knowledge and Understanding

Product development activities provide key inputs to the process design stage, such as the intended dosage form, the quality attributes, and a general manufacturing pathway. Process information available from product development activities can be leveraged in the process design stage.

The functionality and limitations of commercial manufacturing equipment should be considered in the process design, as well as predicted contributions to variability posed by:

  • Different component lots
  • Production operators
  • Environmental conditions
  • Measurement systems in the production setting

However, the full spectrum of input variability typical of commercial production is not generally known at this stage. Laboratory or pilot-scale models designed to be representative of the commercial process can be used to estimate variability.

It is essential that activities and studies resulting in process understanding be documented. Documentation should reflect the basis for decisions made about the process. For example, manufacturers should document the variables studied for a unit operation and the rationale for those variables identified as significant.

Commentary: Sources of Process Knowledge and Variability Management

Four Major Sources of Process Knowledge

  • Development data: Results of formulation development, dosage form studies and preliminary feasibility trials
  • Literature: Academic papers, regulatory guidance and technical standards
  • Prior experience: Manufacturing history of similar products within the company, and platform processes
  • Manufacturing history: Existing batch records, deviation investigations and CAPA outcomes

Four Sources of Variability

FDA specifically names these four major sources of variability in commercial manufacturing:

  • Component lots: For example, lot-to-lot differences in API particle size
  • Operators: Differences in how individuals perform operations, and consistency of training
  • Environmental conditions: Temperature, humidity and seasonal variation of the HVAC system
  • Measurement systems: Instrument calibration and precision of analytical methods

Key insight: at the time of Stage 1, the full range of variability at commercial scale is usually not completely known. This is normal; it is estimated from laboratory or pilot-scale work.

CDMO Practice: The Challenge of Knowledge Transfer

In a CDMO setting, process knowledge is usually transferred in from the sponsor (client). This creates particular challenges:

  • The sponsor's development data may be incomplete or inconsistently formatted
  • The sponsor's "prior experience" may be trade secret and not fully shared
  • The CDMO's own equipment (for example, the filling machine model) may differ from the original development equipment
  • Environmental conditions (such as facility classification and HVAC design) may also differ

Therefore, the CDMO's Stage 1 must re-evaluate the impact of these differences on the process; it cannot simply carry over the sponsor's old data.

DOE Studies and Risk Analysis Tools

FDA source text pp.11–12 | Revealing relationships, multivariate interactions, and establishing ranges

Design of Experiment (DOE) in Process Design

"Design of Experiment (DOE) studies can help develop process knowledge by revealing relationships, including multivariate interactions, between the variable inputs (e.g., component characteristics or process parameters) and the resulting outputs (e.g., in-process material, intermediates, or the final product)."

Risk analysis tools can be used to screen potential variables for DOE studies to minimize the total number of experiments conducted while maximizing knowledge gained.

The results of DOE studies can provide justification for establishing ranges of:

  • Incoming component quality
  • Equipment parameters
  • In-process material quality attributes
"FDA does not generally expect manufacturers to develop and test the process until it fails."

Other activities such as laboratory or pilot-scale experiments also assist in evaluation of certain conditions and prediction of commercial process performance. Computer-based or virtual simulations of unit operations can provide process understanding and help avoid problems at commercial scale.

It is important to understand the degree to which models represent the commercial process, including any differences that might exist, as this may impact the relevance of information derived from the models.

Commentary: DOE Methodology and Process Understanding

The Core Value of DOE

DOE (design of experiments) is not blind trial and error; it uses statistical methods to study the effects and interactions of multiple factors at the same time.

Comparison:

  • Traditional OFAT (one factor at a time): Changes only one variable per run, cannot reveal interactions, and requires a large number of experiments
  • DOE: Changes multiple variables simultaneously and uses the fewest experimental runs to identify the most important factors and their combined effects

Example of DOE in Aseptic Processing

Taking aseptic filling as an example, a DOE can study at the same time:

  • Fill speed × needle spacing
  • Solution temperature × fill weight
  • Nitrogen purge pressure × headspace oxygen

Outputs can be fill weight accuracy, particle counts and headspace oxygen content, that is, CQAs.

Through DOE, you identify which CPPs have a significant effect on CQAs (main effects) and which combinations show interactions (interaction effects).

Risk Tools -> Screening Step for DOE

First use risk tools (such as FMEA, fishbone diagrams and risk matrices) to list all potential factors, then screen those that go into the DOE. This can greatly reduce the number of experiments:

Typical flow:

List all factors
Ishikawa / Brainstorming → FMEA/risk scoring
Screen out high-risk factors → Design the DOE
High-risk factors selected → Analyze results
Determine CPPs

FDA's Position: "No Need to Test to Failure"

FDA states clearly that it does not expect manufacturers to seek out the failure boundary of the process.

Practical implication: what you need is to establish enough operating space to buffer normal variability, not to find the "most extreme acceptable condition". Regulators look at whether your understanding is sufficient to set control ranges reasonably, not at how many failed batches you have run.

Commentary: The Relationship Between NOR, PAR and Design Space

Analogy: Driving on a Highway

Imagine driving on a highway:

  • PAR = the legal speed range (for example, 60-120 km/h); driving anywhere within it is legal and proven safe
  • NOR = your company's fleet management rule (for example, 80-100 km/h); this is the normal day-to-day operating band, leaving a buffer for unexpected situations

Normal driving (commercial production) is within the NOR (80-100). Occasionally going outside the NOR but still within the PAR (for example, 110 km/h) to overtake? That needs to be assessed, but it is not necessarily a violation. Going beyond the PAR (over 120 km/h)? That is a real problem.

Design Space

The design space defined in ICH Q8(R2) is a higher-level concept than PAR:

  • Design space = the multidimensional combination space of multiple CPPs and material attributes within which operation assures product quality
  • PAR = the acceptable range of a single parameter (one-dimensional)
  • The design space must account for interactions between parameters (multidimensional)

Movement within the design space (such as adjusting a CPP) is not considered a change and does not require regulatory submission or approval. Moving outside the design space requires a post-approval change (PAC).

Practical Advice on Setting the NOR

The NOR should be set in the middle of the PAR and narrower than it, to ensure that:

  • Normal production variability (instrument precision, operator differences) does not exceed the NOR
  • There is sufficient buffer between the NOR and the PAR boundary
  • The distance from the NOR to the PAR boundary is at least 3 standard deviations of normal process variability (as a rule of thumb)

If the NOR is set too close to the PAR boundary, occasional normal fluctuations in production will trigger deviations, creating an unnecessary investigation burden.

Process Design Space and Operating Space

Multidimensional combinations of inputs — from ICH Q8(R2) to FDA 2011 Guidance

Design Space Concept in Process Design

The process design stage is the appropriate time to establish the design space — the multidimensional combination and interaction of input variables (material attributes and process parameters) that have been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality.

While FDA does not mandate design space for all products, Stage 1 data provides the scientific foundation if a sponsor chooses to pursue this regulatory pathway. The practical benefit is regulatory flexibility: movement within the design space does not constitute a change and does not require prior regulatory approval.

Key distinctions in the design space hierarchy:

  • Knowledge Space: The full range explored during development (including failure modes)
  • Design Space: The multidimensional subspace where product quality is assured (ICH Q8)
  • Normal Operating Range (NOR): The tighter range used for routine commercial production control

Moving outside the design space requires a regulatory submission. Moving within it does not. This creates a direct financial and operational incentive to conduct thorough Stage 1 studies — more design space means more manufacturing flexibility.

Scale-Up Considerations

From lab/pilot scale to commercial manufacturing — what changes and what doesn't

Scale-Up: From Lab and Pilot to Commercial Scale

Laboratory or pilot-scale models designed to be representative of the commercial process can be used to estimate variability. However, several critical differences must be accounted for when scaling up:

  • Equipment functionality and limitations: Commercial equipment capabilities may differ from lab/pilot equipment
  • Surface area-to-volume ratios: Often change significantly at scale
  • Heat and mass transfer characteristics: Mixing dynamics change at larger volumes
  • Environmental contribution to variability: Commercial environments have greater inherent variability than controlled labs
  • Operator variability: Multiple operators, shifts, and training levels at commercial scale vs. expert lab personnel

Platform processes and prior knowledge can accelerate Stage 1 development. If a manufacturer has successfully validated a similar process previously, that knowledge can be used to justify reduced experimentation — provided the differences between the platform and the new process are documented and addressed.

Computer-based or virtual simulations of certain unit operations can provide process understanding and help avoid problems at commercial scale, but the degree to which models represent the commercial process must be understood.

Commentary: A Systematic Approach to Scaling Up the Process

Making Good Use of Platform Processes

One of a CDMO's core competitive advantages is "platform process knowledge":

  • For example, a CDMO that already has aseptic filling history for 10 mAb products knows the range in which the PAR of the fill speed CPP typically falls on the same make of filling machine
  • This prior knowledge can shorten the Stage 1 work for a new product, but it must be documented which knowledge can be borrowed and which needs to be re-confirmed
  • FDA allows reduced experimentation for known processes, but requires the manufacturer to explain the differences through a comparability assessment

Special Challenges in Scaling Up Lyophilization

Lyophilization is one of the most difficult processes to scale up:

  • Shelf uniformity: Temperature differences between the edge and the center are more pronounced in large lyophilizers
  • Loading effect: Full load vs. half load gives different sublimation rates
  • Equipment transfer: From laboratory lyophilizer to commercial lyophilizer, parameters cannot be applied directly

The ICH Q8 annex notes that a design space can span different scales, but this must be supported by studies of scale-dependent parameters.

Establishing a Strategy for Process Control

FDA source text p.12 | Translating process knowledge into a defensible control approach

2. Establishing a Strategy for Process Control

"Process knowledge and understanding is the basis for establishing an approach to process control for each unit operation and the process overall. Strategies for process control can be designed to reduce input variation, adjust for input variation during manufacturing (and so reduce its impact on the output), or combine both approaches."

Process controls address variability to assure quality of the product. Controls can consist of material analysis and equipment monitoring at significant processing points (§ 211.110(c)).

FDA expects controls to include both examination of material quality and equipment monitoring. Special attention to control the process through operational limits and in-process monitoring is essential in two scenarios:

  1. When the product attribute is not readily measurable due to limitations of sampling or detectability (e.g., viral clearance or microbial contamination)
  2. When intermediates and products cannot be highly characterized and well-defined quality attributes cannot be identified

More advanced strategies, which may involve the use of Process Analytical Technology (PAT), can include timely analysis and control loops to adjust the processing conditions so that the output remains constant.

These controls are established in the master production and control records (§ 211.186(a) and (b)(9)).

Commentary: A Three-Layer Control Strategy

Two Main Directions for a Control Strategy

FDA states clearly that a process control strategy can be approached from two directions (or a combination of them):

  • Direction 1: Reduce input variation
    For example: set tight IQC specifications for raw materials to ensure consistency of incoming CMAs; filter excipients before the main filtration to reduce impurities; use automated compounding to reduce operator differences.
  • Direction 2: Adjust for input variation during manufacturing
    For example: use PAT (process analytical technology) to monitor the concentration of the compounded solution in real time and automatically adjust the mixing time; use a closed-loop control system during filling to automatically correct fill weight deviations.

Enhanced Control in Two Special Situations

Situation 1: Product attributes that are difficult to measure

Sterility is the classic example: you cannot run a sterility test on every unit (it is destructive and not representative), so you must rely on:

  • Strict environmental control (EM monitoring)
  • Filter integrity testing
  • Process simulation (media fill/APS)

Situation 2: Intermediates that cannot be fully characterized

The higher-order structure of biologic intermediates (such as cell harvest fluid and purification intermediates) is difficult to measure completely, so the process must rely on tight control of process parameters (such as pH, temperature and time) as a surrogate for quality.

Potential of PAT in Aseptic Manufacturing

PAT (process analytical technology) is a more advanced control strategy. Applications in aseptic manufacturing include:

  • In-line concentration monitoring (NIR/Raman): Confirms compounding concentration in real time, without waiting for off-line HPLC results
  • In-line particle size monitoring: Confirms the API particle size distribution during compounding
  • Automated 100% visual inspection: High-speed cameras plus AI image evaluation, more reliable than manual inspection
  • Closed-loop fill weight control: Automatic correction, reducing the frequency of manual adjustments

The adoption of PAT is usually decided in Stage 1 and its reliability is verified in Stage 2, which makes it a high-return area for Stage 1 investment.

Capturing the basis for decisions — what to document and why it matters

Documentation Requirements in Stage 1

"It is essential that activities and studies resulting in process understanding be documented. Documentation should reflect the basis for decisions made about the process. For example, manufacturers should document the variables studied for a unit operation and the rationale for those variables identified as significant."

Good documentation at Stage 1 serves multiple functions:

  • Provides the scientific basis for CPP identification and range-setting
  • Enables regulatory reviewers to understand the design logic
  • Feeds into Stage 2 (PPQ) protocol design — what to test and why
  • Enables Stage 3 (CPV) monitoring plan design — which parameters to track
  • Supports post-approval change management — knowing what was studied before
  • Facilitates technology transfer (to CDMO or new site) by capturing the institutional knowledge

The Process Design Report (PDR) or Development Report is the primary output document of Stage 1. It typically includes:

  • Process flow diagram and unit operation descriptions
  • CPP and CMA identification rationale (with supporting studies)
  • DOE study summaries and key findings
  • NOR and PAR ranges with scientific justification
  • Control strategy rationale
  • Scale-up assessment
  • Risk assessment summaries

Commentary: Structure of the Process Design Report

The Regulatory Importance of "Documenting the Decision Rationale"

One of the questions FDA asks most often during inspections is: "Why did you choose this range?"

If the answer is "the engineer's judgment" or "historical practice", without scientific data and a written rationale, this will be a major 483 observation.

The right way to document:

  • "The fill temperature NOR is set at 20-25 °C, based on a DOE study (Study Report XYZ-001) showing that protein aggregates increase significantly above 28 °C (p<0.05), and that below 18 °C increased solution viscosity affects fill accuracy. The PAR of 15-28 °C is fully supported by data."

Technology Transfer

Transferring Stage 1 knowledge from development to manufacturing — CDMO context

Knowledge Transfer to Manufacturing

Stage 1 culminates in a formal technology transfer from development to manufacturing. This transfer is more than a document handover — it is the migration of all accumulated process understanding that enables manufacturing personnel to produce the product correctly and consistently.

Key elements of a complete technology transfer package include:

  • Process Design Report (PDR) — complete Stage 1 documentation
  • Draft Master Batch Record (MBR) — reflecting all Stage 1 learnings
  • CPP/CMA list with ranges — NOR and PAR for each parameter
  • Control strategy document — in-process controls, monitoring plan
  • Analytical method transfers — all test methods used in Stage 1
  • Reference standards and materials — API samples, standards
  • Training records — knowledge transfer to manufacturing operators
  • Equipment qualification status — IQ/OQ/PQ of commercial equipment
  • Site comparability assessment — if transferring between facilities

The effectiveness of technology transfer directly determines the success of Stage 2 Process Performance Qualification (PPQ). Gaps in Stage 1 knowledge become failures in Stage 2 PPQ batches.

Commentary: A Practical Framework for CDMO Technology Transfer

Four Phases of CDMO Technology Transfer

Phase 1: Document Review
Receive the sponsor's PDR, development reports and existing data; perform a gap analysis
↓
Phase 2: Process Assessment

Common Reasons for Technology Transfer Failure

  • Knowledge not fully documented: The sponsor holds tacit knowledge that is not recorded in the PDR, so the CDMO has no way of knowing it
  • Equipment differences not assessed: The sponsor uses make A filling machine and the CDMO uses make B, and it is assumed that the CPP ranges can be applied directly
  • Analytical method transfer not confirmed: The sponsor's HPLC method was not verified on the CDMO's instruments, making the data non-comparable
  • Insufficient number of engineering runs: Reproducibility of the process under CDMO conditions was not fully confirmed before going directly into PPQ
  • Insufficient personnel training: CDMO operators did not adequately understand the science behind the Stage 1 decisions and simply followed SOPs mechanically

The Real Cost of Technology Transfer Failure

The cost of a failed Stage 2 PPQ batch is very high:

  • One failed commercial-scale batch can mean the loss of hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars in materials and manufacturing cost
  • PPQ failure delays the NDA/BLA submission and postpones product launch by 1-2 years
  • FDA may launch a for-cause compliance inspection over frequent PPQ failures

Conclusion: investing sufficient time and resources in Stage 1 (the engineering runs of technology transfer) is the most effective risk management for a CDMO.

For a CDMO, Stage 1 encompasses both the knowledge received through technology transfer and the additional studies conducted at the CDMO facility to validate applicability to the CDMO's specific equipment, environment, and processes.

The goal is the same as FDA's expectation: arrive at Stage 2 with sufficient process understanding to design a meaningful PPQ protocol, execute it successfully, and establish an ongoing Stage 3 monitoring program that detects drift before it becomes a failure.

  • All CPPs and CMAs are identified and documented with scientific rationale

  • Personnel trained and qualified
  • Engineering run data reviewed and approved
  • PPQ protocol drafted and approved (includes sampling plan, acceptance criteria)

Summary: The Three Core Outcomes of Stage 1

Outcome 1: Process Understanding

At the end of Stage 1, you should be able to answer:

  • Which process parameters are critical (CPPs)? Why?
  • Which material attributes are critical (CMAs)? Why?
  • What is the acceptable range (PAR) of these parameters? What is the normal operating range (NOR)?
  • What interactions exist between different parameters?

Outcome 2: Control Strategy

At the end of Stage 1, you should have a clear control strategy that describes:

  • Critical control points for each unit operation
  • IQC specifications for raw materials (based on CMA studies)
  • In-process monitoring frequency and methods
  • Whether PAT is used (and its validation plan)
  • Operational limits and action limits

Outcome 3: PPQ Readiness (Stage 2 Readiness)

The most important output of Stage 1 is giving the Stage 2 PPQ a scientific foundation:

  • What should PPQ test? -> From the CPPs and CQAs identified in Stage 1
  • How many batches? -> Based on the variability range and risk level assessed in Stage 1
  • What acceptance criteria? -> From the CQA specifications and PAR set in Stage 1
  • The more solid the Stage 1 work, the fewer batches PPQ requires and the lower the regulatory risk.

Many companies (especially under rapid-development pressure) treat Stage 1 as "necessary paperwork". FDA and industry practice both show that:

  • Every unit of currency invested in process understanding work in Stage 1 can save 5-10 units of failed-batch cost in Stage 2
  • Every unit saved in Stage 1 (by skipping DOE or documentation) can cost 10 units in deviation investigations and CAPA in Stage 3

For a CDMO's head of operations, the rigor of Stage 1 is a demonstration of quality culture and the foundation of commercial competitiveness.

FDA Source Text

1. Design of a Facility and Qualification of Utilities and Equipment

Proper design of a manufacturing facility is required under part 211, subpart C, of the CGMP regulations on Buildings and Facilities. It is essential that activities performed to assure proper facility design and commissioning precede PPQ. Here, the term qualification refers to activities undertaken to demonstrate that utilities and equipment are suitable for their intended use and perform properly. These activities necessarily precede manufacturing products at the commercial scale.

Qualification of utilities and equipment generally includes the following activities:

  • Selecting utilities and equipment construction materials, operating principles, and performance characteristics based on whether they are appropriate for their specific uses.
  • Verifying that utility systems and equipment are built and installed in compliance with the design specifications (e.g., built as designed with proper materials, capacity, and functions, and properly connected and calibrated).
  • Verifying that utility systems and equipment operate in accordance with the process requirements in all anticipated operating ranges. This should include challenging the equipment or system functions while under load comparable to that expected during routine production. It should also include the performance of interventions, stoppage, and start-up as is expected during routine production. Operating ranges should be shown capable of being held as long as would be necessary during routine production.
Commentary

The Three-Layer Logic of Facility Design and Equipment Qualification

Definition of "Qualification"

FDA defines qualification here as a set of activities that demonstrate that equipment and utilities are suitable for their intended use and perform properly. This is not a paperwork exercise. It is a systematic activity that must be supported by objective evidence.

Practical Application in an Aseptic Fill-Finish Facility

In an aseptic fill-finish CDMO, the equipment and systems to be qualified include:

  • Filling line equipment: filling pumps (peristaltic / time-pressure), stoppering machines, capping machines
  • Utilities: HVAC (ISO 5/7/8 cleanrooms), water for injection (WFI), compressed air, nitrogen overlay systems
  • Sterilization equipment: autoclaves, depyrogenation tunnels (dry-heat)
  • Barrier systems: RABS / isolators

Key Point: Facility Qualification Must Be Completed Before PPQ

FDA states clearly that facility design assurance activities (including commissioning) must precede PPQ. The logic is straightforward: you cannot confirm that a process is stable in a facility whose utilities have not yet been confirmed to work properly.

The three qualification activities correspond to the familiar IQ / OQ / PQ framework (see the table below). Although FDA does not use these abbreviations in this guidance, the concepts map directly.

IV-B: Process Performance Qualification (PPQ)
FDA Source Text

2. Process Performance Qualification

The process performance qualification (PPQ) is the second element of Stage 2, process qualification. The PPQ combines the actual facility, utilities, equipment (each now qualified), and the trained personnel with the commercial manufacturing process, control procedures, and components to produce commercial batches. A successful PPQ will confirm the process design and demonstrate that the commercial manufacturing process performs as expected.

Success at this stage signals an important milestone in the product lifecycle. A manufacturer must successfully complete PPQ before commencing commercial distribution of the drug product. The decision to begin commercial distribution should be supported by data from commercial-scale batches. Data from laboratory and pilot studies can provide additional assurance that the commercial manufacturing process performs as expected.

The approach to PPQ should be based on sound science and the manufacturer's overall level of product and process understanding and demonstrable control. The cumulative data from all relevant studies (e.g., designed experiments; laboratory, pilot, and commercial batches) should be used to establish the manufacturing conditions in the PPQ. To understand the commercial process sufficiently, the manufacturer will need to consider the effects of scale. However, it is not typically necessary to explore the entire operating range at commercial scale if assurance can be provided by process design data. Previous credible experience with sufficiently similar products and processes can also be helpful. In addition, we strongly recommend firms employ objective measures (e.g., statistical metrics) wherever feasible and meaningful to achieve adequate assurance.

Commentary

The Essence of PPQ: Integrated Verification

What Does PPQ Integrate?

PPQ brings all of the following elements together and runs them at commercial scale:

  • Qualified facility
  • Qualified utilities
  • Qualified equipment
  • Trained personnel
  • Commercial manufacturing process
  • Control procedures
  • Commercial components

This is why PPQ must follow equipment qualification: you cannot qualify a process using unqualified equipment.

Core Position: Only Commercial-Scale Data Count

FDA states clearly: "the decision to begin commercial distribution must be supported by data from commercial-scale batches." Laboratory and pilot data can provide only "additional assurance" and cannot serve as the primary basis.

This directly addresses a common industry misconception: data from three 10-liter experimental batches cannot substitute for commercial-scale PPQ.

Scale Effects

FDA requires manufacturers to consider the "effects of scale". This is especially important in aseptic fill-finish:

  • Compounding tank agitation: scaling from 50 L to 500 L changes the mixing dynamics completely
  • Filtration time: the filter area and differential pressure at commercial scale are completely different from small-scale trials
  • Filling speed: fill volume uniformity at high speed differs from that of low-speed small batches
  • Environmental exposure time (LST/BIER): the maximum exposure time of a commercial batch may be much longer than that of a small batch

Analogy: A Chef vs. Restaurant Mass Production

A chef makes three perfect dishes in the kitchen as a trial (Stage 1 development). Before the restaurant opens, however, it must be shown that the same recipe, in a commercial kitchen, prepared by trained cooks using commercial-scale equipment, consistently produces the same quality. That is PPQ. You cannot say "the trial went well, so it will surely be fine".

IV-C: PPQ Protocol Requirements
FDA Source Text

3. PPQ Protocol

A written protocol that specifies the manufacturing conditions, controls, testing, and expected outcomes is essential for this stage of process validation. We recommend that the protocol discuss the following elements:

  • The manufacturing conditions, including operating parameters, processing limits, and component (raw material) inputs.
  • The data to be collected and when and how it will be evaluated.
  • Tests to be performed (in-process, release, characterization) and acceptance criteria for each significant processing step.
  • The sampling plan, including sampling points, number of samples, and the frequency of sampling for each unit operation and attribute. The number of samples should be adequate to provide sufficient statistical confidence of quality both within a batch and between batches. The confidence level selected can be based on risk analysis as it relates to the particular attribute under examination. Sampling during this stage should be more extensive than is typical during routine production.
  • Criteria and process performance indicators that allow for a science- and risk-based decision about the ability of the process to consistently produce quality products. The criteria should include:
    • A description of the statistical methods to be used in analyzing all collected data (e.g., statistical metrics defining both intra-batch and inter-batch variability).
    • Provision for addressing deviations from expected conditions and handling of nonconforming data. Data should not be excluded from further consideration in terms of PPQ without a documented, science-based justification.
  • Design of facilities and the qualification of utilities and equipment, personnel training and qualification, and verification of material sources (components and container/closures), if not previously accomplished.
  • Status of the validation of analytical methods used in measuring the process, in-process materials, and the product.
  • Review and approval of the protocol by appropriate departments and the quality unit.
Commentary: In-Depth Analysis of PPQ Protocol Elements

PPQ Protocol: The Power of a Written Commitment

Why Is a Written Protocol So Important?

The PPQ protocol is a pre-specified commitment. Before execution you must define which tests will be run, what data will be collected and what the acceptance criteria are. This prevents cherry-picking, that is, deciding on the criteria after the work is done. During an FDA inspection, the protocol is compared with what was actually executed.

PPQ Protocol Element Checklist (8 Items)

  • Manufacturing conditions: operating parameters, processing limits and raw material (component) inputs
  • Data collection plan: what is collected, when, and how it is evaluated
  • Tests and acceptance criteria: in-process tests, release tests and characterization tests
  • Sampling plan: sampling points, number of samples and frequency (more intensive than routine production)
  • Process performance criteria: scientific/risk-based basis for decisions, including statistical methods
  • Confirmation of facility/equipment qualification status (if not yet completed)
  • Confirmation of analytical method validation status
  • Review by relevant departments and QU approval records for the protocol

Strict Requirements for Excluding Data

FDA emphasizes: "PPQ data must not be excluded from further consideration without a documented, scientifically sound justification." This means you cannot casually discard a data point just because it "looks like an outlier". A complete OOS/OOT investigation must support the decision.

Example Elements of an Aseptic Fill-Finish PPQ Protocol

  • Operating parameters: filling speed (e.g., 300 vials/min), fill volume (e.g., 10.0 mL ± 0.2 mL), stoppering force (e.g., 20-40 N), capping torque
  • In-process tests: fill weight checks every 30 minutes, settle plate EM, viable air sampling, container closure integrity (preliminary CCIT)
  • Release tests: sterility (§71.3a / direct inoculation), visible particulates (100% light inspection), pH, potency, CCIT
  • Acceptance criteria: all batches meet finished product specifications; aseptic process simulation (APS) growth-positive units = 0; process capability index Ppk ≥ 1.33 for each batch

Enhanced Sampling in PPQ

The sampling plan, including sampling points, number of samples, and the frequency of sampling for each unit operation and attribute. The number of samples should be adequate to provide sufficient statistical confidence of quality both within a batch and between batches. The confidence level selected can be based on risk analysis as it relates to the particular attribute under examination. Sampling during this stage should be more extensive than is typical during routine production.

In most cases, PPQ will have a higher level of sampling, additional testing, and greater scrutiny of process performance than would be typical of routine commercial production. The level of monitoring and testing should be sufficient to confirm uniform product quality throughout the batch. The increased level of scrutiny, testing, and sampling should continue through the process verification stage as appropriate, to establish levels and frequency of routine sampling and monitoring for the particular product and process.

Considerations for the duration of the heightened sampling and monitoring period could include, but are not limited to, volume of production, process complexity, level of process understanding, and experience with similar products and processes.

Commentary: Why Does PPQ Need More Samples Than Routine Production?

The Fundamental Rationale for Enhanced Sampling

Core Logic: You Are Learning, Not Confirming

The sampling plan for routine production is designed on the premise that the process has already been shown to be stable. The premise of PPQ is exactly the opposite: you do not yet know whether this commercial-scale process is stable, and you are collecting enough data to demonstrate that it is. Hence more samples are needed.

Three Statistical Reasons

  1. Intra-batch uniformity: enough samples are needed to detect variation within a batch (e.g., differences in fill volume between the start, middle and end of the filling run)
  2. Inter-batch variability: data across multiple batches are needed to estimate between-batch variation and establish tolerance intervals
  3. Statistical confidence level: the more samples, the more precise the estimate of process capability and the narrower the confidence interval

Analogy: How Weather Forecasting Works

A routine weather forecast needs data from only three weather stations, because decades of data have already built the model. But if you are building a forecast model for a new city for the first time, you need to take dense measurements at many locations and many times. This is the logic of enhanced sampling in PPQ.

Enhanced Sampling Can Be Adjusted Based on Risk Analysis

FDA says the "confidence level can be based on risk analysis", which means the sampling intensity can differ between attributes:

  • High-risk attributes (e.g., sterility, visible particulates) → highest sampling density
  • Medium-risk attributes (e.g., fill volume, pH) → standard increased sampling
  • Low-risk attributes (e.g., appearance, labeling) → moderate enhancement is sufficient

Aseptic Fill-Finish: Examples of Enhanced PPQ Sampling

Take a PPQ batch of 10,000 vials as an example:

  • Fill volume: routine, 10 vials every 30 minutes → PPQ, 20 vials every 15 minutes (covering the start, middle and end of the filling run)
  • Settle plates: routine, exposed for 50% of the filling time → PPQ, exposed for the entire run (100%)
  • CCIT: routine, n=30 per batch → PPQ, n=100 per batch
  • Visible particulates: 100% light inspection (same in both cases, since this is already a full inspection)

Criteria and process performance indicators that allow for a science- and risk-based decision about the ability of the process to consistently produce quality products. The criteria should include:

  • A description of the statistical methods to be used in analyzing all collected data (e.g., statistical metrics defining both intra-batch and inter-batch variability).

In addition, we strongly recommend firms employ objective measures (e.g., statistical metrics) wherever feasible and meaningful to achieve adequate assurance.

The number of samples should be adequate to provide sufficient statistical confidence of quality both within a batch and between batches. The confidence level selected can be based on risk analysis as it relates to the particular attribute under examination.

Commentary: Statistical Tools in Depth

Two Core Statistical Tools for PPQ

Tool 1: Tolerance Interval (TI)

Definition: an interval that, at a given confidence level (e.g., 95%), covers at least a given proportion (e.g., 99%) of the process output.

Purpose: to confirm that most of the product produced by the process (not just the samples) falls within the specification limits.

Difference from specifications: a specification tells you the "acceptable range"; a tolerance interval tells you how much of the actual process distribution falls within that range.

Interpretation example: if the 95/99 tolerance interval (95% confidence, covering 99% of the product) is [9.8 mL, 10.2 mL] and the specification is 9.5–10.5 mL, the process passes. If the tolerance interval extends beyond the specification, the process capability is insufficient.

Tool 2: Process Performance Index (Ppk)

Definition: Ppk = min[(USL - X̄) / 3σ, (X̄ - LSL) / 3σ], where σ is the overall standard deviation of the samples (including between-batch variation)

Difference from Cpk: Cpk uses within-process (within-subgroup) variation, whereas Ppk uses overall variation (including between-batch variation). It is more conservative and better suited to the "long-term capability estimate" of PPQ.

Commonly applied standard: industry generally expects Ppk ≥ 1.33 at the PPQ stage (corresponding to a sigma level of about 4), or a stricter standard set according to risk and product attributes.

Application in aseptic fill-finish: Ppk should be calculated for critical quality attributes (CQAs) such as fill volume, container closure integrity (CCIT results) and pH.

Analysis of Intra-Batch vs. Inter-Batch Variation

FDA explicitly requires the statistical methods to address "intra-batch and inter-batch variability":

  • Intra-batch: variation across time points/locations within the same batch (e.g., drift in fill volume within a batch)
  • Inter-batch: differences in means/standard deviations between batches (e.g., whether the mean fill volume is consistent from batch to batch)

Both must be within specification and under control for the process to be considered truly stable.

FDA Source Text — Execution Requirements

4. PPQ Protocol Execution and Report

Execution of the PPQ protocol should not begin until the protocol has been reviewed and approved by all appropriate departments, including the quality unit. Any departures from the protocol must be made according to established procedure or provisions in the protocol. Such departures must be justified and approved by all appropriate departments and the quality unit before implementation (§ 211.100).

Normal operating conditions should include the utility systems (e.g., air handling and water purification), material, personnel, environment, and manufacturing procedures.

Commentary: Compliance Requirements for Execution

Three Major Compliance Requirements for PPQ Execution

Requirement 1: Approve First, Then Execute

The PPQ protocol must be reviewed and approved by all relevant departments (including the QU) before execution begins. This is not a token signature but formal written approval. Starting execution before approval is a violation (§211.100).

Requirement 2: Deviations Must Be Approved in Advance

If a deviation from the protocol is needed during execution, it must be handled according to established procedures or as provided in the protocol, and approved by all relevant departments and the QU before implementation. "Act first, explain later" is not permitted in PPQ.

Requirement 3: Execution Under Real Commercial Conditions

PPQ batches must:

  • Follow the commercial manufacturing process and routine procedures (no special procedures)
  • Be executed by the personnel who normally operate the process (not "the best employees")
  • Be run under normal operating conditions (HVAC, WFI, raw materials and environment all in their normal state)

Common Mistake: Special Arrangements for PPQ

Some companies use their most senior staff, clean the equipment especially thoroughly, or select the best raw material lots during PPQ. All of these violate the requirement for "normal conditions". PPQ results should represent process capability at average performance, not at the best case. If the process can pass only under special arrangements, it is inherently unstable.

Analogy: The Driving Test

PPQ is like a driving test. You cannot send a top racing driver to take the test for you (not the routine operators), and you cannot use a specially modified track (not normal conditions). The examiner wants to see how an ordinary driver performs on an ordinary road.

PPQ Report Requirements
FDA Source Text — Report Requirements

PPQ Report

A report documenting and assessing adherence to the written PPQ protocol should be prepared in a timely manner after the completion of the protocol. This report should:

  • Discuss and cross-reference all aspects of the protocol.
  • Summarize data collected and analyze the data, as specified by the protocol.
  • Summarize and discuss all manufacturing nonconformances such as deviations, atypical events, out-of-specification results, and relate these to the conclusions about the state of control of the process.
  • Describe corrective actions taken or needed.
  • State the conclusion(s) regarding whether the data analyzed supports the conclusion that the process is in a state of control or not. If not, the report should indicate what corrective actions are needed and the timeline for such actions.
  • Include review and approval by all appropriate departments and the quality unit.
Commentary: Six Elements of the PPQ Report

The PPQ Report: The Final Written Conclusion

PPQ Report Structure Checklist (6 Items)

  • Cross-reference to the protocol: compare each protocol element one by one and confirm its execution status
  • Data summary and analysis: analyze all data using the statistical methods required by the protocol
  • Summary of all nonconformances: deviations, unexpected events and OOS results, linked to the conclusions on process control
  • Description of corrective actions: CAPA taken or required
  • Clear conclusion: the process is in a state of control, or it is not (with a follow-up action plan)
  • Review and approval by the relevant departments and the QU

Key Point: The Conclusion Must Be Clear, Not Vague

There are only two possible conclusions for a PPQ report:

  1. The process is in a state of control (commercial distribution may begin)
  2. The process is not in a state of control (the required corrective actions and timelines must be stated)

Vague conclusions (e.g., "most results meet the requirements") are not acceptable.

All Nonconformances Must Be Recorded and Linked to the Conclusion

FDA requires that all deviations, unexpected events and OOS results must be:

  • Fully summarized in the report (no selective presentation)
  • Explicitly linked to the conclusion on the state of process control (e.g., does this deviation affect the judgment that the process is in a state of control?)

This is consistent with OOS investigation requirements: data cannot be disregarded without scientific justification.

What "Timely Preparation" of the PPQ Report Means in Practice

FDA says the report should be prepared in a timely manner after the protocol is completed. The industry generally interprets this as:

  • Draft: within 30 days after the batches are completed
  • QU-approved version: within 60 days
  • Batch release must wait for PPQ report approval (at least a preliminary conclusion is needed)

In a CDMO, customers are usually eager to know the PPQ results, which creates pressure to finish the report quickly, but the completeness of the report should not be sacrificed for speed.

PAT: Process Analytical Technology
FDA Source Text — PAT Approach

PPQ for PAT-Enabled Processes

A manufacturing process that uses PAT may warrant a different PPQ approach. PAT processes are designed to measure in real time the attributes of an in-process material and then adjust the process in a timely control loop so the process maintains the desired quality of the output material.

The process design stage and the process qualification stage should focus on the measurement system and control loop for the measured attribute. Regardless, the goal of validating any manufacturing process is the same: to establish scientific evidence that the process is reproducible and will consistently deliver quality products.

In the case of a strategy using PAT, the approach to process qualification will differ from that used in other process designs. Further information on PAT processes can be found in FDA's guidance for industry on PAT — A Framework for Innovative Pharmaceutical Development, Manufacturing, and Quality Assurance.

Commentary: The Different PPQ Logic for PAT Processes

The Fundamental Difference of PAT Processes

What is PAT (Process Analytical Technology)?

PAT is a real-time process control strategy: while the process is running, critical material attributes (CMAs) are measured continuously, and process parameters are actively adjusted based on the real-time measurements so that the process output stays at the target quality level.

Common example: near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy monitors blend uniformity, and mixing stops automatically when the uniformity criterion is reached (endpoint control rather than fixed time)

The Shift in Focus of PPQ for PAT Processes

Traditional process PPQ focus: confirm the stability of output under fixed process conditions

PAT process PPQ focus: confirm the reliability of the measurement system and the control loop

Specifically:

  • Is the NIR model accurate? (measurement system validation)
  • Does the control logic respond correctly? (control loop testing)
  • Is the response under abnormal conditions as expected? (failure mode testing)

Potential PAT Applications in Aseptic Fill-Finish

  • Real-time fill volume monitoring: in-line weighing detects the fill volume of every vial in real time and stops the line automatically when an abnormality occurs
  • Compounding concentration monitoring: UV/Vis or NIR monitors API concentration in real time, and compounding stops when the target is reached
  • Real-time container integrity detection: high voltage leak detection (HVLD) checks seal integrity on 100% of units

PAT applications in aseptic fill-finish are still being rolled out gradually, but the trend is clear.

The Unchanged Core Objective

Whether traditional PPQ or a PAT approach is used, the objective is the same: "to establish scientific evidence that a process is reproducible and will consistently deliver quality products". PAT changes how this objective is achieved, not the objective itself.

What Happens When PPQ Fails
Regulatory Basis

FDA's Position on PPQ Failure

From the guidance:

"If not [in a state of control], the report should indicate what corrective actions are needed and the timeline for such actions."

And from the execution requirements:

"A manufacturer must successfully complete PPQ before commencing commercial distribution of the drug product."

The guidance also states that departures from the protocol must be justified and approved before implementation (§ 211.100). Nonconforming data cannot be excluded without documented, science-based justification.

Regarding concurrent release: products manufactured during Stage 2, if acceptable, can be released for distribution — but this is contingent on meeting specifications AND demonstrating process control.

Commentary: Compliance Consequences of PPQ Failure and the Response

Definition and Consequences of PPQ Failure

What Is "PPQ Failure"?

PPQ failure is not just "one vial did not pass". It means that the conclusion of the PPQ report cannot support the judgment that "the process is in a state of control". This can occur in situations such as:

  • Ppk < 1.33 across multiple batches (systematic lack of capability)
  • Excessive inter-batch variation (the process is not reproducible)
  • An unresolved OOS whose root cause points to a process design flaw
  • A critical deviation occurs and it cannot be shown that product quality was unaffected

Regulatory Consequences of PPQ Failure

  • No commercial distribution: commercial distribution cannot begin unless PPQ is successfully completed
  • Disposition of batches already produced: if a PPQ batch cannot be released because it fails specifications, a thorough investigation must be conducted under §211.192
  • Repeat PPQ: after the necessary process improvements, PPQ must be re-executed (in full or in part)
  • Notification to FDA: if an NDA/ANDA has been filed, a major process change may require a supplement

Standard Handling Procedure After PPQ Failure

  1. Deviation investigation: identify all nonconformances and perform root cause analysis (RCA)
  2. Scoping: determine whether the failure is localized (operator error) or systemic (process design flaw)
  3. CAPA development: establish corrective and preventive actions with timelines
  4. Process improvement: modify the process design or control strategy per the CAPA (this may require returning to Stage 1)
  5. Repeat PPQ: execute full or partial PPQ again and write a new report
  6. Regulatory assessment: evaluate whether the filing documents need to be updated

CDMO Perspective: Commercial Impact of PPQ Failure

In a CDMO, the impact of PPQ failure goes far beyond the technical: delays to the customer's launch timeline, emergency procurement costs, strained customer relationships and possible breach-of-contract clauses. These are real commercial consequences. This is exactly why the quality of process design in Stage 1 is so critical: a well-done front-end design reduces the risk of failure in Stage 2.

Analogy: Structural Testing of a Bridge

PPQ is like the final structural test before a bridge opens. If the test fails, the bridge cannot be opened to traffic (no commercial distribution). You must find where the structural problem lies (root cause), fix it and test again. You cannot say "never mind, open it first and hope it doesn't collapse". In drug manufacturing, that would endanger patient safety.

Commentary: Conditions and Risks of Concurrent Release

What Is Concurrent Release?

Definition

Concurrent release means releasing individual PPQ batches that meet specifications to the market before PPQ is fully complete (for example, while the cross-batch analysis and report are still in progress). FDA allows this practice in Stage 2, subject to conditions.

Conditions for Concurrent Release

  • The batch must meet all finished product specifications (release testing passed)
  • The manufacturer has sufficient understanding of the process to predict batch quality
  • Adequate scientific and risk assessment must support the decision
  • The plan for Stage 3 (continued process verification) must already be in place

Where We Are in the Lifecycle

FDA's 2011 Process Validation Guidance establishes a three-stage lifecycle approach. Stages 1 and 2 build knowledge and demonstrate initial process capability. Stage 3 is where that capability is sustained and confirmed across the full commercial production horizon.

Unlike traditional "three-batch validation," the lifecycle model recognizes that validation is never truly finished — it is an ongoing commitment to process understanding.

"The goal of the third validation stage is continual assurance that the process remains in a state of control (the validated state) during commercial manufacture."
— FDA Process Validation Guidance, 2011, p.14

Lifecycle Positioning — Commentary

Logic of the Three-Stage Lifecycle

  • Stage 1 (Process Design): understand the process and identify the critical process parameters (CPPs) and critical quality attributes (CQAs)
  • Stage 2 (Process Qualification, PPQ): perform at commercial scale to confirm the process can repeatedly produce product that meets specifications
  • Stage 3 (CPV): monitor continuously during commercial production to confirm the process remains in a state of control

Analogy: Driving Test vs. Daily Driving

Stage 2 PPQ is like passing the driving test (you pass the exam and earn the licence). Stage 3 CPV is driving on the road every day: a real safety record comes from sustained behavior, not from a single exam.

The traditional "three-batch validation" mindset is equivalent to saying that once you have passed the test, you never need to show again that you can drive. The 2011 FDA guidance explicitly rejects this thinking.

Key Point: Why Is CPV the Key to Closing the Loop?

Stage 1 and 2 data are limited (typically only 3-10 batches). Data that truly represent commercial process variability become available only after many batches have been produced in Stage 3. CPV turns validation from "static proof" into "dynamic assurance".

Block 2 — Stage 3 Definition & Regulatory Framework

D. Stage 3 — Continued Process Verification

The goal of the third validation stage is continual assurance that the process remains in a state of control (the validated state) during commercial manufacture. A system or systems for detecting unplanned departures from the process as designed is essential to accomplish this goal.

Adherence to the CGMP requirements, specifically, the collection and evaluation of information and data about the performance of the process, will allow detection of undesired process variability. Evaluating the performance of the process identifies problems and determines whether action must be taken to correct, anticipate, and prevent problems so that the process remains in control (§ 211.180(e)).

An ongoing program to collect and analyze product and process data that relate to product quality must be established (§ 211.180(e)). The data collected should include relevant process trends and quality of incoming materials or components, in-process material, and finished products. The data should be statistically trended and reviewed by trained personnel. The information collected should verify that the quality attributes are being appropriately controlled throughout the process.

"We recommend that a statistician or person with adequate training in statistical process control techniques develop the data collection plan and statistical methods and procedures used in measuring and evaluating process stability and process capability."

Stage 3 Definition — Commentary

Core Definition: CPV (Continued Process Verification)

Continued process verification means continually confirming, through systematic data collection, statistical analysis and trend monitoring during commercial production, that the process remains in the validated state of control.

  • Objective: detect unplanned process deviations
  • Means: statistical methods + review by trained personnel
  • Regulatory basis: 21 CFR § 211.180(e)
  • Data scope: raw materials, in-process materials, finished product, process parameters

Regulatory Link: § 211.180(e)

This provision requires manufacturers to establish written procedures and periodically evaluate manufacturing records to confirm that batches consistently meet established standards. The CPV program is the primary mechanism for meeting this requirement.

FDA expressly recommends that a statistician or person with adequate training in statistical process control (SPC) design the data collection plan. In other words, CPV is not a matter of QA arbitrarily choosing a few sampling points; it requires statistical design.

Analogy: Blood Pressure Monitoring vs. a Health Check-up

PPQ (Stage 2) is like a full health check-up: thorough but infrequent. CPV is like measuring blood pressure every day: less in-depth, but it tracks the trend continuously and warns you before a problem worsens.

If you only have check-ups and never measure blood pressure, a hypertensive crisis can quietly develop between two check-ups. The same is true of a process.

Block 3 — Data Collection & Sampling Program

Data Collection Program Requirements

We recommend continued monitoring and sampling of process parameters and quality attributes at the level established during the process qualification stage until sufficient data are available to generate significant variability estimates. These estimates can provide the basis for establishing levels and frequency of routine sampling and monitoring for the particular product and process.

Monitoring can then be adjusted to a statistically appropriate and representative level. Process variability should be periodically assessed and monitoring adjusted accordingly.

Procedures should describe how trending and calculations are to be performed and should guard against:

  • Overreaction to individual events (false positive signals leading to unnecessary investigation)
  • Failure to detect unintended process variability (missing real signals)

Production data should be collected to evaluate process stability and capability. The quality unit should review this information. If properly carried out, these efforts can identify variability in the process and/or signal potential process improvements.

Variation can also be detected by timely assessment of:

  • Defect complaints
  • Out-of-specification findings
  • Process deviation reports
  • Process yield variations
  • Batch records
  • Incoming raw material records
  • Adverse event reports

Data Collection Plan — Commentary

Principles of Sampling Plan Design

FDA recommends a two-phase sampling strategy:

  • Early commercialization phase: maintain the sampling frequency and number used during PPQ, and keep collecting data until there are enough to estimate process variability
  • Post-stabilization phase: based on the statistical analysis, adjust to a routine level that is "statistically appropriate and representative"

Key phrase: statistically appropriate and representative — more is not always better, and less is not simply cheaper; let the data decide.

Overview of Data Sources

CPV is not just about looking at test results. It also covers:

  • Process parameters (temperature, pressure, speed, fill volume, etc.)
  • In-process test results
  • Finished-product release data
  • Raw material CoA and release trends
  • Complaints and adverse event reports
  • Batch yield variability
  • Deviation and CAPA records

In a CDMO aseptic fill-finish environment, also include: environmental monitoring (EM) data, media fill (MFT) results, and container closure integrity testing (CCIT) trends.

Statistical Process Control — Key Indices

FDA references ASTM E2281-03 "Standard Practice for Process and Measurement Capability Indices" as a useful resource for measuring and evaluating process stability and process capability.

Two primary indices used in pharmaceutical CPV:

Process Capability Index (Cpk) — measures how well a process performs relative to specifications, assuming the process is in statistical control (within-subgroup variation only).

Process Performance Index (Ppk) — measures actual observed process performance including all sources of variation (total variation: within + between subgroups).

General industry benchmarks:

  • Cpk / Ppk ≥ 1.33 → capable process (generally acceptable)
  • Cpk / Ppk ≥ 1.67 → highly capable (Six Sigma target)
  • Cpk / Ppk < 1.00 → process not capable, action required
  • Cpk / Ppk 1.00–1.33 → marginal, enhanced monitoring needed

Cpk vs. Ppk in Depth

Cpk Formula

Cpk = min[ (USL - X̄) / (3σ_within),
           (X̄ - LSL) / (3σ_within) ]

σ_within = R̄ / d2  (from control chart)

Example:
  USL = 505 mg, LSL = 495 mg, X̄ = 501 mg
  σ_within = 1.2 mg

  CPU = (505 - 501) / (3 × 1.2) = 4/3.6 = 1.11
  CPL = (501 - 495) / (3 × 1.2) = 6/3.6 = 1.67
  Cpk = min(1.11, 1.67) = 1.11

Ppk Formula

Ppk = min[ (USL - X̄) / (3σ_total),
           (X̄ - LSL) / (3σ_total) ]

σ_total = S  (overall standard deviation)

Key difference:
  σ_within uses R̄/d2 (only random short-term noise)
  σ_total uses all data variation (includes shifts,
  drifts, lot-to-lot, operator effects, seasonal, etc.)

If Ppk << Cpk → large between-batch variation exists
  → investigate batch-to-batch sources

Analogy: Sprint vs. Marathon

Cpk is like a sprint time: performance under the best conditions over the shortest period. Ppk is like the average result across all races in a year, including the effects of weather, fitness, venue and every other real-world condition.

For regulatory submissions (NDA/BLA), FDA pays more attention to Ppk because it reflects the full picture of real commercial production.

Block 5 — Control Charts: Xbar-R & Other Tools

Statistical Tools for CPV

Many tools and techniques, some statistical and others more qualitative, can be used to detect variation, characterize it, and determine the root cause. FDA recommends that the manufacturer use quantitative, statistical methods whenever appropriate and feasible.

Scrutiny of intra-batch as well as inter-batch variation is part of a comprehensive continued process verification program under § 211.180(e).

Key statistical tools used in pharmaceutical CPV:

  • Xbar-R charts — monitor process mean and range over time
  • Individual-MR (I-MR) charts — for individual measurements (n=1)
  • P-charts / U-charts — for attribute data (defect rates)
  • CUSUM charts — cumulative sum, sensitive to small shifts
  • EWMA charts — exponentially weighted moving average
  • Histogram + Normality tests — assess distribution shape
  • Box plots — compare variation across batches/lines/operators
  • Scatter plots / Regression — explore CPP-CQA relationships

Control charts detect signals via Western Electric rules (or similar):

  • 1 point beyond 3σ (UCL/LCL)
  • 2 of 3 consecutive points beyond 2σ
  • 4 of 5 consecutive points beyond 1σ
  • 8 consecutive points on one side of centerline (run rule)
  • Trends: 6+ consecutive points moving in one direction

Control Charts in Detail — Commentary

Xbar-R Chart Structure

Xbar Chart (mean chart):
  UCL_X = X̄̄ + A2 × R̄     ← upper control limit
  CL_X  = X̄̄               ← center line (grand mean)
  LCL_X = X̄̄ - A2 × R̄     ← lower control limit

R Chart (range chart):
  UCL_R = D4 × R̄           ← upper control limit
  CL_R  = R̄                ← center line (mean range)
  LCL_R = D3 × R̄           ← lower control limit (= 0 when n≤6)

Common coefficients (n=5):
  A2 = 0.577, D3 = 0, D4 = 2.114

Illustration (fill weight, mg):
  UCL_X ─────────────────── 502.8
        ●   ●           ●
  CL_X  ─── ─── ─── ─── ─── 501.0
              ●   ●
  LCL_X ─────────────────── 499.2

  Batch:   1   2   3   4   5

I-MR Chart — A Common Scenario in Aseptic Filling

When there is only one measurement per batch (for example, one container per batch tested for protein content), use an I-MR chart:

  • I chart (individuals chart): monitors each batch result
  • MR chart (moving range chart): monitors batch-to-batch variation

Applicable scenarios: moisture content trends of lyophilized product, sterility test pass rate (by batch), BI (biological indicator sterilization challenge) results

Analogy: Traffic Signals

Control limits (UCL/LCL) are the red light: when exceeded, you must stop and investigate. Specification limits (USL/LSL) are the edge of the cliff: by the time you reach them, it is too late. The purpose of SPC is to act when you "see the yellow light", not to react after you have hit the cliff.

Control limits are usually much narrower than specification limits. If the control limits equal the specification limits, SPC loses its early-warning function.

Block 6 — Sources of Variation & Trend Analysis

Detecting and Characterizing Variation

Good process design and development should anticipate significant sources of variability and establish appropriate detection, control, and/or mitigation strategies, as well as appropriate alert and action limits.

However, a process is likely to encounter sources of variation that were not previously detected or to which the process was not previously exposed. Many tools and techniques, some statistical and others more qualitative, can be used to detect variation, characterize it, and determine the root cause.

Production line operators and quality unit staff should be encouraged to provide feedback on process performance. FDA recommends that the quality unit meet periodically with production staff to evaluate data, discuss possible trends or undesirable process variation, and coordinate any correction or follow-up actions by production.

Sources of Variation to Monitor

  • Intra-batch variation (within a single lot)
  • Inter-batch variation (lot-to-lot)
  • Seasonal variation (temperature, humidity effects)
  • Shift-to-shift / operator-to-operator variation
  • Equipment drift (wear, calibration drift)
  • Incoming material variability (supplier lots)
  • Line clearance and setup variation
  • Environmental monitoring trends (viable, non-viable)

Sources of Variation and Trend Analysis — Commentary

FDA Key Point: Regular QA and Production Meetings

FDA expressly recommends that the quality unit meet regularly with production personnel to evaluate data and discuss trends. This is not a formal regulatory requirement, but FDA considers such cross-functional communication essential for detecting problems early.

Practical recommendation: hold a Monthly CPV Review Meeting, with an agenda that includes:

  • Control chart updates and review of anomalous points
  • Monthly Cpk/Ppk calculation
  • Discussion of OOT cases
  • CAPA implementation status tracking
  • Priority monitoring items for next month

Analogy: Weather Forecast vs. Weather Record

The purpose of trend analysis is to "forecast", not just to "record". If you see EM data rising slowly for 3 consecutive months in summer, even if no limit has been exceeded, you should start an investigation before it is. This is the essence of Stage 3 CPV: preventive rather than reactive.

Block 7 — CPV Program Elements Checklist

Comprehensive CPV Program

A robust Continued Process Verification program encompasses multiple interconnected elements that collectively ensure ongoing process control. Based on the FDA guidance and industry best practices, a complete CPV program must address:

  • Written procedures describing how trending and calculations are performed
  • Sampling plans with defined frequency and number of units
  • Data collection systems for process parameters and quality attributes
  • Statistical methods for evaluating process stability and capability
  • Alert and action limits distinct from specification limits
  • In-process controls monitoring during manufacturing
  • Finished product testing trend analysis
  • Periodic Product Quality Reviews (PQR/APR)
  • Feedback mechanisms from operators and QA
  • CAPA system integration for corrective actions
  • Management review of CPV data

Data gathered during Stage 3 might suggest ways to improve and/or optimize the process. A description of the planned change, a well-justified rationale for the change, an implementation plan, and quality unit approval before implementation must be documented (§ 211.100).

CPV Program Elements — Full Checklist

13-Element CPV Program Checklist

  • CPV plan (written procedure, including scope and responsibilities)
  • List of monitored parameters (full coverage of CPPs and CQAs)
  • Sampling plan (frequency, quantity, sampling points, statistical rationale)
  • Statistical method specifications (control chart types, Cpk/Ppk calculation methods)
  • Rationale for setting control limits (UCL/LCL) and alert limits
  • Trend report format and review frequency
  • OOT definition and investigation trigger criteria
  • OOT to OOS escalation path
  • CAPA integration mechanism (investigation, root cause, corrective and preventive action)
  • Periodic CPV review meetings (agenda, frequency, participants)
  • Integration with the annual product quality review (APR/PQR)
  • Change management integration (process improvements must go through change control)
  • Training requirements (statisticians, operators, QA)

PQR/APR — Annual Product Quality Review

Product Quality Review (PQR), called the Annual Product Review (APR) under US CGMP, is the annual summary mechanism of CPV and requires:

  • Review of production and release data for all batches
  • Analysis of OOS, OOT and deviation trends
  • Evaluation of CAPA effectiveness
  • Evaluation of raw material suppliers
  • Update of stability data
  • Process capability trends (year-on-year comparison of Cpk/Ppk)
  • Identification of improvement opportunities and triggers for revalidation

Regulatory basis: 21 CFR § 211.180(e) (US), EU GMP Chapter 1 §1.10 (EU)

Block 8 — OOT vs. OOS Investigation

Out-of-Trend vs. Out-of-Specification

Variation can be detected by the timely assessment of defect complaints, out-of-specification findings, process deviation reports, process yield variations, batch records, incoming raw material records, and adverse event reports.

FDA distinguishes between two types of concerning findings in CPV data:

Out-of-Trend (OOT): A result or series of results that deviate from expected statistical behavior — exceeding control chart limits or trend analysis thresholds — even if still within specification limits. OOT signals a potential change in process behavior before it becomes a quality problem.

Out-of-Specification (OOS): A result that falls outside established specification limits. OOS requires a formal investigation per 21 CFR § 211.192 and FDA's OOS guidance (2006).

The key principle: OOT investigations should catch problems before they become OOS events. A robust CPV program uses OOT as a leading indicator system.

OOT vs. OOS Decision Matrix

OOT to OOS Decision Flow

Step 1: detect the OOT signal (control limit exceeded or trend rule triggered)

Step 2: initiate the OOT investigation: is it a calculation error or an instrument problem?

Step 3: confirm the OOT is valid and perform root cause analysis

Step 4: determine whether the specification limit has been reached or there is a risk of escalation to OOS

Step 5: if escalated to OOS, start a formal § 211.192 investigation

Step 6: implement CAPA and continue monitoring the effect of the improvement

Analogy: Thermometer vs. Emergency Room

OOT is like a body temperature of 37.5°C (slightly elevated): it needs attention, fluids and rest, but not yet a trip to the emergency room. OOS is like a temperature of 40°C: you must seek medical care immediately, as this is an emergency.

A good CPV system steps in while you are "only at 37.5°C" to prevent it worsening to 40°C. This is why the design of the OOT system is so important.

The Challenge of Defining OOT

Unlike OOS, OOT has no clear regulatory definition, so each company must define its trigger criteria in its SOP. Common approaches:

  • Any point outside UCL/LCL
  • N consecutive points on the same side of the center line (N is set by the SPC rules; 8 is commonly used)
  • M consecutive points steadily rising or falling (6 is commonly used)
  • Linear regression slope of stability data exceeding a set threshold
  • Cpk below a predefined alert value (e.g., <1.33)

Block 9 — Process Changes & Revalidation Triggers

Managing Process Changes in Stage 3

Data gathered during Stage 3 might suggest ways to improve and/or optimize the process by altering some aspect of the process or product, such as the operating conditions (ranges and set-points), process controls, component, or in-process material characteristics.

All proposed changes require:

  • A description of the planned change
  • A well-justified rationale for the change
  • An implementation plan
  • Quality unit approval before implementation (§ 211.100)

Depending on how the proposed change might affect product quality, additional process design and process qualification activities could be warranted.

Note: Certain manufacturing changes may call for formal notification to the Agency before implementation, as directed by existing regulations (see 21 CFR 314.70 and 601.12 — Prior Approval Supplement, CBE-30, CBE-0 filing categories).

Process Changes and Revalidation — Commentary

Regulatory Filing Requirements (US FDA)

In addition to internal change management, process changes must be assessed for regulatory filing:

  • Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): major changes, which may be implemented only after FDA approval (e.g., site change, major process change)
  • CBE-30: moderate changes, filed 30 days before implementation
  • CBE-0 / Annual Report: minor changes, reported in the annual report

Regulatory basis: 21 CFR § 314.70 (small-molecule NDA); 21 CFR § 601.12 (biologics, BLA)

Analogy: Car Modification vs. Factory Specification

A car as it leaves the factory (= a process that passed PPQ) has been validated. If you replace the engine (= a major process change), you need to redo the safety testing (= revalidation). Merely adjusting tire pressure (= a minor adjustment) may only need to be documented and checked for safety. The key question is: how much does the change affect "driving safety" (= product quality)?

Block 10 — Facility, Utilities & Equipment Maintenance

Maintaining Qualified State of Facility and Equipment

Maintenance of the facility, utilities, and equipment is another important aspect of ensuring that a process remains in control. Once established, qualification status must be maintained through routine monitoring, maintenance, and calibration procedures and schedules (21 CFR part 211, subparts C and D).

The equipment and facility qualification data should be assessed periodically to determine whether re-qualification should be performed and the extent of that re-qualification.

Maintenance and calibration frequency should be adjusted based on feedback from these activities.

Key regulatory references:

  • 21 CFR Part 211 Subpart C — Buildings and Facilities
  • 21 CFR Part 211 Subpart D — Equipment
  • 21 CFR § 211.67 — Equipment cleaning and maintenance
  • 21 CFR § 211.68 — Automatic, mechanical, and electronic equipment

Equipment Maintenance and Maintaining the Qualified State — Commentary

Continuity of Equipment Qualification

Equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) is not a one-time event; it must be maintained through the following mechanisms:

  • Preventive maintenance (PM) program: periodic replacement of consumable parts, lubrication, cleaning
  • Calibration program: periodic calibration of measuring equipment to maintain metrological traceability
  • Periodic performance verification (PV): periodic testing to confirm the equipment still meets the original qualification criteria
  • Requalification interval assessment: determine the requalification interval based on the equipment history

Basis for Deciding Requalification Frequency

FDA recommends adjusting maintenance and calibration frequency based on "feedback information", which means:

  • Calibration history shows the equipment is stable: the calibration interval may be extended appropriately
  • Calibration history shows frequent drift: the calibration interval should be shortened
  • CPV data show process parameter drift associated with a particular piece of equipment: triggers unplanned requalification
  • After major repair or replacement of critical parts: requalification must be performed

This reflects "risk-based equipment management": resources invested are proportional to risk.

Special Considerations for CDMO Aseptic Fill-Finish

  • Filling needles, diaphragms, peristaltic pump tubing: monitor service life and its relationship to fill-volume drift
  • HVAC systems: ongoing trends of airflow balance, differential pressure, temperature and humidity records
  • Autoclave: F0 value trends, thermocouple calibration
  • Lyophilizer: vacuum gauge calibration, periodic shelf temperature uniformity testing
  • Tunnel (depyrogenation tunnel): periodic verification of the temperature profile
  • RABS/isolator: periodic glove integrity testing, H2O2 residual trends

Block 11 — Concurrent Release of PPQ Batches

V. Concurrent Release of PPQ Batches

In most cases, the PPQ study needs to be completed successfully and a high degree of assurance in the process achieved before commercial distribution of a product. In special situations, the PPQ protocol can be designed to release a PPQ batch for distribution before complete execution of the protocol steps and activities, i.e., concurrent release. FDA expects that concurrent release will be used rarely.

Concurrent release might be appropriate for:

  • Processes used infrequently for various reasons
  • Drugs for which there is limited demand (e.g., orphan drugs, minor use and minor species veterinary drugs)
  • Drugs with short half lives (e.g., radiopharmaceuticals, including positron emission tomography drugs)
  • Drugs that are medically necessary and are being manufactured in coordination with the Agency to alleviate a short supply
"Conclusions about a commercial manufacturing process can only be made after the PPQ protocol is fully executed and the data are fully evaluated."

If Stage 2 qualification is not successful, additional design studies and qualification may be necessary. The new product and process understanding obtained from the unsuccessful qualification study(ies) can have negative implications if any lot was already distributed.

Concurrent Release — Commentary

Definition of Concurrent Release

Concurrent release means allowing PPQ batches to be distributed commercially before the PPQ protocol has been fully executed. It is an exception to normal practice, not the norm.

FDA states explicitly: "FDA expects concurrent release to be used rarely." The word "rarely" is a strong regulatory signal.

Risk Warning on Concurrent Release

FDA points out that if Stage 2 qualification is not successful (that is, PPQ fails) and some batches have already been distributed to the market under concurrent release, the consequences are serious, because product made by a non-reproducible process may already have been released.

This is the greatest regulatory risk of concurrent release: once the final PPQ conclusion is that the "process is not qualified", all batches already released concurrently face the possibility of recall.

Analogy: Preview Screening vs. Official Premiere

Concurrent release is like letting the audience in while the film is still being edited: possible in theory, but if the finished cut turns out to have an incoherent plot, the audience experience has already been damaged, and refunds are difficult. The normal practice is to complete the whole film first (complete PPQ) and start selling tickets (commercial release) only after quality is confirmed.

Block 12 — Concurrent Release: Conditions & Safeguards

Requirements for Concurrent Release

Circumstances and rationale for concurrent release should be fully described in the PPQ protocol. Even when process performance assessment based on the PPQ protocol is still outstanding, any lot released concurrently must comply with:

  • All CGMPs
  • Regulatory approval requirements
  • PPQ protocol lot release criteria

Lot release under a PPQ protocol is based upon meeting confidence levels appropriate for each quality attribute of the drug.

When warranted and used, concurrent release should be accompanied by:

  • A system for careful oversight of the distributed batch
  • Rapid customer feedback mechanisms
  • Customer complaints and defect reports rapidly assessed to determine root cause

Concurrently released lots must also be assessed in light of any negative PPQ study finding or conclusions and appropriate corrective action must be taken (§§ 211.100(a), 211.180(e), and 211.192).

FDA recommends that each batch in a concurrent release program be evaluated for inclusion in the stability program. Stability test data should be promptly evaluated to ensure rapid detection and correction of any problems.

Complete Requirements for Concurrent Release — Commentary

The 6 Essential Conditions for Concurrent Release

  • 1. Advance planning: the rationale and circumstances for concurrent release must be fully described in the PPQ protocol in advance (they cannot be explained after the fact)
  • 2. Full CGMP compliance: even though the protocol is not yet complete, every batch must fully comply with CGMP requirements
  • 3. Batch release criteria: the release criteria specified in the PPQ protocol must be met (usually a higher statistical confidence than for routine release)
  • 4. Post-marketing surveillance system: a rapid feedback mechanism is needed, including prompt evaluation of complaints and adverse event reports
  • 5. Inclusion in the stability program: each concurrently released batch should be included in the stability program, and stability data must be evaluated promptly
  • 6. Response to a negative PPQ conclusion: if the final PPQ conclusion is a failure, appropriate corrective action must be taken for the batches already released

Application of "Confidence Level" in PPQ Release

Routine commercial release is usually based on specification compliance (Yes/No). Concurrent release of PPQ batches requires a higher level of statistical assurance, for example:

  • Achieve a 95% confidence level that 95% of individual units will be within specification (95/95 tolerance interval)
  • Or meet a specific criterion of Ppk ≥ 1.33
  • Or a weighted statistical evaluation of multiple critical attributes

The specific criteria must be defined in the PPQ protocol in advance and cannot be decided after the evaluation.

Rapid Post-Marketing Monitoring System

Concurrent release must be accompanied by enhanced post-marketing monitoring:

  • Set up a dedicated rapid-response complaint process (shorter than the normal response time)
  • Establish a distribution tracking system (know where every batch is)
  • Maintain communication with FDA (especially for coordination in drug shortage situations)
  • Real-time review of stability data (not waiting for the annual review)
  • Any negative PPQ finding is immediately assessed for its impact on batches already released

Block 13 — CDMO Aseptic Fill-Finish: CPV Implementation

Stage 3 in a CDMO Context

Contract Development and Manufacturing Organizations (CDMOs) face unique challenges in implementing Stage 3 Continued Process Verification. Unlike integrated pharmaceutical companies, CDMOs must manage CPV programs across multiple client products, often with different regulatory filing requirements, specification systems, and client oversight expectations.

Key considerations for CDMO CPV implementation:

  • Client-specific CPV plan requirements (sponsor's statistical standards may differ)
  • Data ownership and access (client vs. CDMO data systems)
  • Multi-product facility shared equipment trend data
  • Regulatory filing coordination (who files PAS/CBE for process changes?)
  • PQR/APR compilation responsibility (CDMO provides data, client files)
  • Stage 3 monitoring during tech transfer from another manufacturer
  • Enhanced CPV requirements for aseptic processes (EM trends, MFT, CCIT)

CPV Implementation Strategy for CDMO Aseptic Fill-Finish

CPV Monitoring Items Specific to Aseptic Fill-Finish

  • Environmental monitoring (EM) trends: viable air, settle plates, surface microbiology, non-viable particles; monthly trend charts to identify seasonality or facility degradation
  • Media fill (MFT/APS): trend of the results of each run, with long-term monitoring of the pass rate
  • Container closure integrity (CCIT): residual oxygen trends after autoclaving, dye ingress test results
  • Visible particulates (VPI): per-batch particulate rate trend, with statistics classified by particle type
  • Sterility test (SAL): although it is 100% pass/fail, it still needs trend monitoring (even with zero positives, the continued validity of the test method must be confirmed)
  • Residual solvents: applicable to formulations using organic solvents (e.g., lipid nanoparticles)

Recommended Prioritization of the CDMO CPV Program

High priority (must be established before the first commercial batch):

  • Fill volume CPV (Xbar-R chart, monthly Cpk/Ppk calculation)
  • EM trending program (monthly trend report, OOT definition)
  • Batch yield trend

Medium priority (establish within the first year):

  • Critical equipment parameter trends (fill speed, steam pressure, lyophilization cycle)
  • Raw material CoA critical parameter trends
  • CCIT trends

Continuous optimization: based on early CPV data results, identify the most important sources of variation and prioritize their monitoring.

VI. Documentation

Documentation — The Foundation of Validation

Documentation at each stage of the process validation lifecycle is essential for effective communication in complex, lengthy, and multidisciplinary projects. Documentation is important so that knowledge gained about a product and process is accessible and comprehensible to others involved in each stage of the lifecycle.

Information transparency and accessibility are fundamental tenets of the scientific method. They are also essential to enabling organizational units responsible and accountable for the process to make informed, science-based decisions that ultimately support the release of a product to commerce.

"Information transparency and accessibility are fundamental tenets of the scientific method."
— FDA Guidance, January 2011

Why Documentation Is Central to Validation

FDA regards documentation as part of validation itself, not merely administrative work. The logic is simple: work that is not documented is, from a regulatory standpoint, work that was never done.

Core Principle

Process validation spans many years, many departments and many people. Documentation ensures:

  • Knowledge continuity — development knowledge is not lost when staff change
  • Scientific transparency — anyone can understand the basis for decisions
  • Regulatory auditability — FDA can trace the complete validation history during an inspection
  • Basis for commercial release — QA can make an informed release decision

Analogy: Documentation as Courtroom Evidence

Validation is like a court case: your process is the "defendant" and FDA is the "judge". No matter how stable your process actually is, without documentation to back it up you have no case: no evidence, no win.

CGMP Documentation Requirements by Stage

The degree and type of documentation required by CGMP vary during the validation lifecycle. Documentation requirements are greatest during Stage 2, process qualification, and Stage 3, continued process verification. Studies during these stages must conform to CGMPs and must be approved by the quality unit in accordance with the regulations (see §§ 211.22 and 211.100).

Viral and impurity clearance studies, even when performed at small scale, also require quality unit oversight.

Key Regulatory Citations

  • 21 CFR § 211.22 — Responsibilities of the Quality Control Unit
  • 21 CFR § 211.100 — Written procedures; deviations
  • 21 CFR § 211.186 — Master production and control records

Intensity of CGMP Documentation Requirements by Stage

Stage 1 — Process Design

Documentation requirements are relatively flexible. Main outputs:

  • Master Batch Record for commercial manufacture (§ 211.186)
  • Process flow diagrams
  • Documents comparing the process at each scale
  • Supporting procedures

Stage 2 — Process Qualification

Documentation requirements are at their highest, with full CGMP compliance:

  • PPQ protocol (requires prior QA approval)
  • Batch manufacturing records
  • Deviation reports
  • PPQ final report (requires QA approval)

Stage 3 — Continued Process Verification (CPV)

Documentation requirements are ongoing and systematic:

  • CPV plan
  • Periodic statistical trending reports
  • Process capability index (Cpk) records
  • CAPA records (if triggered)

Important Reminder: Viral/Impurity Clearance Studies

Even viral clearance or impurity clearance studies performed at small scale still require Quality Unit oversight and documentation approval; laboratory scale is no reason to lower documentation standards.

Process Flow Diagrams — A Key Stage 1 Output

CGMP documents for commercial manufacturing (i.e., the initial commercial master batch production and control record (§ 211.186) and supporting procedures) are key outputs of Stage 1, process design.

We recommend that firms diagram the process flow for the full-scale process. Process flow diagrams should describe:

  • Each unit operation and its placement in the overall process
  • Monitoring and control points
  • Component inputs and other processing material inputs (e.g., processing aids)
  • Expected outputs (i.e., in-process materials and finished product)

It is also useful to generate and preserve process flow diagrams of the various scales as the process design progresses to facilitate comparison and decision making about their comparability.

Process Flow Diagrams: The Core Stage 1 Deliverable

A process flow diagram is more than a "schematic". It is the scientific decision-making tool FDA expects to see, demonstrating the comparability assessment from small scale to commercial scale.

A Complete Process Flow Diagram Should Include

  • Unit operations — the sequence and position of each step
  • Monitoring points — online and offline measurement points
  • Control points — control actions triggered by CPPs
  • Material inputs — raw materials, excipients, processing aids
  • Intermediate outputs — intermediate specifications
  • Finished product outputs — final CQA requirements

Analogy: Maps at Different Scales

Keeping the flow diagrams for each scale (1 L → 50 L → 500 L → commercial batch) is like keeping maps at different scales: zoom in for detail, zoom out for the big picture. You need both to demonstrate scale-up comparability to FDA.

CDMO Practical Application

For aseptic filling operations, the process flow diagram should cover: compounding → filtration (0.22 µm) → filling → capping → sterilization (if applicable), with the environmental monitoring points (EMPs) and in-process control (IPC) test points marked at each step.

VII. Analytical Methodology

Analytical Methods in Process Validation

Process knowledge depends on accurate and precise measuring techniques used to test and examine the quality of drug components, in-process materials, and finished products.

Validated analytical methods are not necessarily required during product- and process-development activities or when used in characterization studies. Nevertheless, analytical methods should be scientifically sound (e.g., specific, sensitive, and accurate) and provide results that are reliable.

There should be assurance of proper equipment function for laboratory experiments. Procedures for analytical method and equipment maintenance, documentation practices, and calibration practices supporting process-development efforts should be documented or described.

New analytical technology and modifications to existing technology are continually being developed and can be used to characterize the process or the product. Use of these methods is particularly appropriate when they reduce risk by providing greater understanding or control of product quality.

However, analytical methods supporting commercial batch release must follow CGMPs in parts 210 and 211. Clinical supply production should follow the CGMPs appropriate for the particular phase of clinical studies.

Analytical Methodology: Different Standards for Development vs. Commercial Release

FDA clearly distinguishes the analytical method requirements for these two situations, a point many companies confuse.

Key Principle: Methods Must Be in Place Before PPQ

Although FDA says full method validation is not mandatory during development, in practice all commercial release methods must be validated before entering PPQ (Stage 2). Otherwise the credibility of the PPQ data will be questioned, and the whole validation effort loses its meaning.

Emerging Analytical Technologies (PAT)

FDA encourages the use of emerging analytical technologies (such as process analytical technology, PAT), especially where they can:

  • Improve understanding of the process or product
  • Reduce quality risk
  • Provide more real-time process control

See also: FDA PAT Guidance (September 2004)

CDMO Practice: In-Process Testing vs. Release Testing

  • In-process control (IPC) testing: pH, osmolality, filter differential pressure; research-grade methods may be used during development
  • Finished product release: potency, content uniformity, sterility; methods must already be validated per USP/ICH

Validation Document Hierarchy

FDA Expected Document Structure

While the guidance does not mandate specific document titles, the following hierarchy represents FDA's expected documentation framework for a complete process validation lifecycle:

Validation Master Plan (VMP)
↓
Stage 1: Process Development Report
↓
Stage 2: PPQ Protocol + PPQ Report
↓
Stage 3: CPV Plan + Periodic Reports
↓
Supporting Records: Batch Records, Deviation Reports, OOS Reports, Lab Notebooks

Each level feeds into the next — the VMP sets the framework, Stage reports provide evidence, and supporting records are the raw data.

Description of Each Document Level

Validation Master Plan (VMP)

Sets the framework for the entire validation lifecycle. It covers validation scope, methodology, organizational responsibilities and timelines. It is usually one of the core documents submitted in the marketing application (NDA/BLA).

Stage 1: Process Development Report

Documents all the knowledge that led to the commercial process design, including DoE results, CQA/CPP identification, design space definition and scale-up assessment. This is the scientific basis for Stage 2.

Stage 2: PPQ Protocol + PPQ Report

PPQ protocol: predefines acceptance criteria, number of batches and statistical methods; it must be approved by QA before execution.
PPQ report: records the results of all PPQ batches and concludes whether the acceptance criteria were met; it must be approved by QA.

Stage 3: CPV Plan + Periodic Reports

CPV plan: defines monitoring parameters, frequency, statistical methods and the conditions that trigger CAPA.
Periodic reports: usually issued quarterly or semiannually, presenting process capability trends as the basis for continual improvement.

CDMO Documentation Checklist

Pre-PPQ Documentation Readiness

Before executing any PPQ batch, the following documents must be in place and approved by the Quality Unit:

  • Process Development Report / Technology Transfer Summary completed
  • Master Batch Record (MBR) approved and issued
  • PPQ Protocol reviewed and approved by QA
  • All analytical methods validated (per USP <1225> or ICH Q2)
  • Equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) completed for all critical equipment
  • Cleaning validation completed (or cleaning equivalency study)
  • Process Flow Diagram reviewed and current
  • Risk assessment (FMEA or equivalent) documented
  • Acceptance criteria pre-defined and statistically justified
  • Sampling plan documented

During and After PPQ Execution

During PPQ:

  • Batch production records (BPRs) completed in real time, with no retrospective entries
  • All deviations recorded immediately and deviation reports opened
  • OOS results investigated per 21 CFR § 211.192
  • Environmental monitoring data fully recorded
  • In-process test results recorded in the BPR
  • Electronic records comply with 21 CFR Part 11 (if applicable)

Post-PPQ:

  • PPQ final report (including statistical analysis) completed and approved by QA
  • QA review completed before release of all batches
  • Conformance with the VMP confirmed
  • CPV plan initiated; Stage 3 formally begins
  • Change control initiated for any change

21 CFR Part 11 Considerations for Electronic Records

If electronic systems are used to record PPQ data, you must ensure that the audit trail function is enabled, electronic signatures meet requirements, and computerized system validation (CSV) has been completed.

Glossary — Original Text

FDA Glossary (as published)

Capability of a process

Ability of a process to produce a product that will fulfill the requirements of that product. The concept of process capability can also be defined in statistical terms. (ISO 9000:2005)

Commercial manufacturing process

The manufacturing process resulting in commercial product (i.e., drug that is marketed, distributed, and sold or intended to be sold). For the purposes of this guidance, the term commercial manufacturing process does not include clinical trial or treatment IND material.

Concurrent release

Releasing for distribution a lot of finished product, manufactured following a qualification protocol, that meets the lot release criteria established in the protocol, but before the entire study protocol has been executed.

Continued process verification

Assuring that during routine production the process remains in a state of control.

Performance indicators

Measurable values used to quantify quality objectives to reflect the performance of an organization, process or system, also known as performance metrics in some regions. (ICH Q10)

Process design

Defining the commercial manufacturing process based on knowledge gained through development and scale-up activities.

Process qualification

Confirming that the manufacturing process as designed is capable of reproducible commercial manufacturing.

Process validation

The collection and evaluation of data, from the process design stage through commercial production, which establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality products.

Quality

The degree to which a set of inherent properties of a product, system, or process fulfils requirements. (ICH Q9)

State of control

A condition in which the set of controls consistently provides assurance of continued process performance and product quality. (ICH Q10)

In-Depth Analysis of the Terms

Process Capability

Statistical meaning: commonly expressed as the Cpk (process capability index). A Cpk of 1.33 or higher is generally regarded as an acceptable benchmark for commercial manufacturing. In CPV reports, Cpk is the core indicator for judging whether the process remains in a state of control.

Commercial Manufacturing Process

Key exclusion: clinical trial batches (IND material) are not a commercial manufacturing process. This means validation requirements for clinical batches can be lower than for commercial batches, but once an NDA/BLA is filed, complete validation data for the commercial manufacturing process must be submitted.

Concurrent Release

This is a mechanism to be used with caution. It allows certain batches to be released before PPQ is fully complete, provided that the batch meets the predetermined release criteria and the release decision is scientifically justified. It is not recommended as routine practice.

Continued Process Verification (CPV)

CPV is the formal name for Stage 3. It is not a one-time activity but a permanent monitoring system. As long as the product remains in commercial production, CPV must keep running. This is also one of the biggest changes in FDA's 2011 framework: validation is no longer "completed" but continues indefinitely.

Performance Indicators

Under the CPV framework, typical performance indicators include batch yield, OOS rate, deviation rate and Cpk trends. These indicators must be predefined in the CPV plan and presented regularly in the periodic reports.

Process Design vs. Process Qualification vs. Process Validation

Three easily confused terms:

  • Process design = Stage 1, defines the process
  • Process qualification = Stage 2, confirms the process
  • Process validation = the sum of all three stages; the complete concept

State of Control

This is the ultimate goal of CPV. It means more than "the product meets specification"; it means "the process is consistently predictable". Only when data points on an SPC (statistical process control) chart fall within the control limits with no special trends does it represent a true state of control.

Documentation IS Validation

"If it wasn't documented, it wasn't done."
— GMP Industry Principle

The FDA's 2011 Process Validation Guidance makes clear that documentation is not merely administrative overhead — it is the substance of validation itself. For regulatory purposes:

  • An undocumented validation does not exist. No matter how scientifically sound the work, if it cannot be demonstrated through records, it has no regulatory standing.
  • Documentation enables accountability. The Quality Unit's ability to make science-based release decisions depends entirely on complete, accurate, and accessible records.
  • Documentation enables learning. The lifecycle approach to process validation requires that knowledge is captured and transferred — documentation is the only mechanism for doing so across time, teams, and regulatory submissions.
  • Documentation protects patients. The entire purpose of CGMP documentation is to create an auditable trail that demonstrates a manufacturer's commitment to consistent product quality — ultimately protecting patient safety.

This principle applies at every stage: the development knowledge captured in Stage 1 documents becomes the scientific justification for Stage 2 PPQ design; the PPQ evidence becomes the foundation for Stage 3 CPV; and the ongoing CPV records become the evidence presented in regulatory submissions and inspections.

Documentation Is the Essence of Validation

"If it isn't recorded, it didn't happen."
— A widely accepted principle in the GMP industry

One of the core messages of FDA's 2011 process validation guidance: documentation is not an administrative burden; documentation is validation itself. From a regulatory perspective:

No documentation = validation does not exist

No matter how stable the process actually is or how rigorous the scientific work, if it cannot be demonstrated by records, it is regulatorily equivalent to not having been done. During an FDA inspection, the investigator looks at documents, not at your verbal explanations.

The Logic Chain of Three-Stage Documentation

  • Stage 1 documents (development knowledge) → become the scientific basis for designing Stage 2 PPQ
  • Stage 2 documents (PPQ evidence) → become the baseline for Stage 3 CPV
  • Stage 3 documents (CPV records) → become the data source for the next marketing application or change submission

The documents of the three stages form a closed loop; if any link is missing, the whole validation architecture is incomplete.

Electronic Records & 21 CFR Part 11

21 CFR Part 11 — Core Requirements

When validation records are maintained electronically, 21 CFR Part 11 applies. Key requirements include:

  • Audit Trail — All record creation, modification, and deletion must be time-stamped and attributed to the individual who made the change.
  • Electronic Signatures — Must be legally binding, attributable to a unique individual, and cannot be repudiated.
  • System Validation (CSV) — The computerized system used to maintain records must itself be validated (Computer System Validation).
  • Access Controls — Role-based permissions prevent unauthorized modification.
  • Record Integrity — Data must be protected from alteration or loss over the required retention period.
  • Backup and Recovery — Validated backup procedures and tested recovery capability.

The FDA's expectation is that electronic records provide at least the same level of reliability and trustworthiness as paper records they replace.

Key Practical Points for Electronic Records at a CDMO

CSV (Computerized System Validation)

Any electronic system used to store PPQ/CPV data (LIMS, MES, EBR) must have completed CSV. The validation scope includes:

  • User requirements specification (URS)
  • Functional specification (FS)
  • IQ / OQ / PQ (or an equivalent GAMP 5 framework)
  • Audit trail verification

Data Integrity

In recent years, data integrity violations have been among the most common Form 483 observations in FDA inspections. The ALCOA+ principles must run through all record systems:

  • Attributable — traceable to the person who generated the data
  • Legible — clear and readable
  • Contemporaneous — recorded at the time of the activity
  • Original — original data
  • Accurate — accurate
  • + Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available
