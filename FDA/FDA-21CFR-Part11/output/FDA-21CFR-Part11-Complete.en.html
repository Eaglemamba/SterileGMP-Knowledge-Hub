0.1 Regulatory Background: Why Part 11 Exists

Original Text

21 CFR Part 11 (Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures) was promulgated by FDA in 1997 (62 FR 13430). It establishes criteria under which the agency considers electronic records and electronic signatures to be trustworthy, reliable, and generally equivalent to paper records and handwritten signatures executed on paper.

Prior to Part 11, FDA regulations required paper records and handwritten signatures for most manufacturing, laboratory, and quality activities. As computerized systems became ubiquitous in pharmaceutical manufacturing during the 1990s, a regulatory framework was needed to:

  • Define when electronic records are acceptable alternatives to paper
  • Establish minimum standards for system security and data integrity
  • Ensure electronic signatures carry the same legal weight as handwritten signatures
  • Give FDA inspection authority over computer systems maintaining regulated records
"The agency considers electronic records, electronic signatures, and handwritten signatures executed to electronic records to be trustworthy, reliable, and generally equivalent to paper records and handwritten signatures executed on paper."
— 21 CFR §11.1(a)

Scope trigger: Part 11 applies to records in electronic form that are created, modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or transmitted under any requirements set forth in agency regulations. The key phrase is "under any requirements" — Part 11 only applies where another FDA regulation already requires the record to exist.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The 1997 context: In the 1990s, computerization swept through the pharmaceutical industry. SCADA systems, LIMS and MES began to replace paper records on a large scale, yet FDA regulations (such as 21 CFR 211.188 on batch records) still referred to "written records" and "handwritten signatures". This gap created regulatory uncertainty: were electronic records acceptable for compliance purposes or not? Part 11 is the rule that filled that gap.

Equivalence principle: Part 11 does not require you to use electronic methods. It says that if you choose to use them, an electronic record that meets Part 11 is equivalent to a paper record. It is a "permissive" framework, not a "mandatory" one.

Analogy

Think of Part 11 as the "notarization standard for electronic contracts". To sign a contract, you can write on paper or use an e-signature platform (such as DocuSign). Electronic methods are not mandatory, but if you choose them, they must meet certain security standards (identity verification, tamper resistance, time stamps) to have legal effect. Part 11 is FDA's version of that standard.

Key Notes

★ The key phrase for Part 11 scope is "under any requirements set forth in agency regulations": Part 11 governs a record only if another FDA regulation requires that record to be kept.

★ Paper records transmitted electronically are not covered: if a company keeps paper batch records as the official records and merely scans them to PDF and sends them by email, Part 11 does not apply to that PDF (§11.1(b) expressly excludes "electronic transmission of paper records").

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO uses paper batch records and, once complete, scans and uploads them to SharePoint. Does this SharePoint system need to comply with Part 11?
  2. The same CDMO now switches to an eBR (electronic batch record) system, where operators enter data directly on a tablet and sign electronically. Does Part 11 apply now?
0.2 The "Predicate Rule" Concept

Original Text

From FDA's 2003 Guidance on Scope and Application of 21 CFR Part 11:

"Part 11 applies to records in electronic form that are created, modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or transmitted under any requirements set forth in agency regulations and to electronic records submitted to FDA. We will refer to such regulations as 'predicate rules'."

Key predicate rules for pharmaceutical manufacturers:

FDA further clarified: Part 11 applies when the regulated entity chooses to use electronic records for predicate rule records. If paper records are maintained as the official record (with electronic records only supplementing), Part 11 may not fully apply.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Predicate rule decision tree:

Question 1: Does another FDA regulation require this record to exist? → No → Part 11 does not apply (even if the record is electronic)

Question 2: Is the record created/maintained in electronic form (rather than a scan of a paper record)? → No → Part 11 does not apply

Question 3: Has the company chosen the electronic record as its official record? → No → Part 11 may not fully apply (room for discretion under the 2003 guidance)

All yes → Part 11 applies

Analogy

The predicate rule is like the "parent rule", and Part 11 is the "supporting technical specification". The parent rule (such as 21 CFR 211.188) says "you must have a batch record"; Part 11 says "if you keep that batch record electronically, you must meet the following requirements". Without a predicate rule requirement, Part 11 has nothing to attach to.

CDMO Practical Notes

Amaran Biotech example: Suppose Amaran Biotech performs aseptic filling for clients and uses the following systems:

  • eBR system (Veeva Vault MES): predicate rule = §211.188 (batch records) → Part 11 applies
  • LIMS (LabWare): predicate rule = §211.194 (laboratory records) → Part 11 applies
  • SCADA (Emerson DeltaV): predicate rule = §211.68 (automatic, mechanical and electronic equipment data) → Part 11 applies
  • BMS (temperature and humidity monitoring): predicate rule = §211.68 + EM data under the FDA Aseptic Guidance → Part 11 applies
  • HR system (employee payroll): no FDA predicate rule → Part 11 does not apply
0.3 FDA 2003 Guidance: Narrowed Enforcement Scope

Original Text

In August 2003, FDA issued guidance "21 CFR Part 11; Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures — Scope and Application" which significantly narrowed enforcement. Key provisions:

1. Enforcement Discretion for Legacy Systems: FDA exercises enforcement discretion for requirements §11.10(a) (validation), §11.10(b) (readable copies), §11.10(c) (protection), §11.10(d) (access), §11.10(e) (audit trails), §11.10(f) (operational checks), §11.10(g) (authority checks), §11.10(h) (device checks), §11.10(k) (system documentation), and §11.30 for legacy systems when: (a) the system was operational before August 20, 1997, AND (b) the system uses paper as the official record (electronic records supplement paper).

2. Validation Exception: FDA does not intend to take regulatory action based solely on §11.10(a) validation deficiencies for legacy systems that otherwise comply.

3. Copies Requirement: FDA clarified §11.10(b) — companies must be able to provide complete and accurate copies of records in a form that FDA can use during inspections. The format does not need to be specified in advance, but must be accessible.

4. What Part 11 Does NOT Require: Part 11 does NOT mandate:

  • Use of electronic records (paper is always an option)
  • Use of electronic signatures
  • Any specific technology solution
  • FDA pre-approval of systems before use

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Background to the 2003 guidance: After Part 11 was issued in 1997, industry's reaction was that it was "too strict". This was especially true for older systems already operating before 1997 (legacy systems), where requiring full compliance would impose a huge financial and operational burden. In 2003, FDA issued guidance stating, in effect: "We understand that, for legacy systems, we will not take regulatory action solely because they technically fail to meet certain provisions." However, this is "enforcement discretion", not an "exemption from regulation".

Key Notes

★ Criteria for a legacy system (both must be met): (1) the system was already in use before August 20, 1997; AND (2) paper is the official record and electronic records are only supplementary. Modern pharmaceutical plants rarely have a true "legacy system": most systems have been replaced or upgraded, or are fully electronic, and so do not meet the "paper as the official record" condition.

★ Why it matters that Part 11 is "not mandatory": If your electronic record system does not meet Part 11 during an FDA inspection, FDA cannot issue a Form 483 solely for "violating Part 11". There must be a violation of the underlying predicate rule (for example, an incomplete batch record = §211.188 violation). However, a Part 11 deficiency usually points to a data integrity problem and can escalate to a Warning Letter.

Original Text

While ALCOA+ is a data integrity principle framework (not a regulation), its elements map directly to Part 11 requirements:

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

How ALCOA+ relates to Part 11: ALCOA+ is the principles framework that regulators such as WHO, FDA and MHRA repeatedly emphasize in their data integrity guidance; Part 11 is the specific FDA regulation for electronic records and signatures. The two are not substitutes but complementary:

  • ALCOA+ tells you "what the data should look like" (conceptual layer)
  • Part 11 tells you "what specific controls an electronic system must have" (implementation layer)
  • GAMP 5 tells you "how to meet Part 11 requirements through the validation process" (methodology layer)

Inspection Tips

When an FDA investigator asks "How do you ensure data integrity?", you can answer using the ALCOA+ framework and then point out specifically how each element is implemented in your Part 11 systems. This structured answer is more convincing than simply saying "we have a Part 11 validated system".

Sample answer framework:
"Our eBR system has been validated for Part 11. For Attributable: the system requires each operator to use an individual account (§11.10(d)), and signatures show the full printed name, time stamp and meaning of the signature (§11.50). For Original: all data are automatically time-stamped by the system, and the audit trail is system-generated and cannot be modified by users (§11.10(e))..."

Practice Questions

  1. If a LIMS audit trail can be switched off by the system administrator, which ALCOA+ principle is violated? Which Part 11 provision does it correspond to?
  2. "Can a paper record be Original?" How is this explained under the ALCOA+ and Part 11 frameworks, respectively?

Chapter Summary

  • Part 11 does not mandate electronic records: It only specifies that "if you choose electronic records/electronic signatures, you must meet these standards". Paper is always a compliant option.
  • The predicate rule is the precondition for applicability: If no other FDA regulation requires the record to be kept, Part 11 does not apply. To decide whether a system is subject to Part 11, first find the predicate rule.
  • The 2003 guidance narrowed the scope of enforcement: Legacy systems that predate 1997 (with paper as the official record) enjoy enforcement discretion, but modern CDMO systems usually do not meet the legacy system criteria.
  • ALCOA+ and Part 11 are complementary: The specific technical requirements of Part 11 (audit trails, validation, access control) map directly to the ALCOA+ elements; used together, the two frameworks build a complete data integrity argument.

Section 1: Subpart A — General Provisions & Definitions

§§ 11.1–11.3

21 CFR Part 11 — §§ 11.1–11.3 | FDA | eCFR current as of April 2026

Learning Objectives

  • Work through §11.1 (Scope) clause by clause and determine accurately when Part 11 is triggered
  • Understand how §11.2 (Implementation) treats "official electronic records" differently from "electronic submissions"
  • Master the key definitions in §11.3, especially the regulatory test for a "closed system" versus an "open system"
  • Distinguish three concepts: "electronic signature", "digital signature" and "handwritten signature executed to electronic record"
1.1 §11.1 — Scope

Original Text

§ 11.1(a) — The regulations in this part set forth the criteria under which the agency considers electronic records, electronic signatures, and handwritten signatures executed to electronic records to be trustworthy, reliable, and generally equivalent to paper records and handwritten signatures executed on paper.

§ 11.1(b) — This part applies to records in electronic form that are created, modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or transmitted, under any requirements set forth in agency regulations. This part also applies to electronic records submitted to the agency under requirements of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and the Public Health Service Act, even if such records are not specifically identified in agency regulations. However, this part does not apply to paper records that are, or have been, transmitted by electronic means.

§ 11.1(c) — Where electronic signatures and their associated electronic records meet the requirements of this part, the agency will consider the electronic signatures to be equivalent to full handwritten signatures, initials, and other general signings as required by agency regulations, unless specifically excepted by regulation(s) effective on or after August 20, 1997.

§ 11.1(d) — Electronic records that meet the requirements of this part may be used in lieu of paper records, in accordance with §11.2, unless paper records are specifically required.

§ 11.1(e) — Computer systems (including hardware and software), controls, and attendant documentation maintained under this part shall be readily available for, and subject to, FDA inspection.

"This part does not apply to paper records that are, or have been, transmitted by electronic means."
— 21 CFR §11.1(b), key exclusion clause

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The two triggers in §11.1(b):

Condition 1: The record is in electronic form (not paper) and is subject to any of the following operations: creation, modification, maintenance, archiving, retrieval or transmission.

Condition 2: The electronic record is only caught when it is kept "under FDA regulations" (the predicate rule).

Exclusion (important!): The "electronic transmission of paper records" is not covered by Part 11. Example: a paper batch record is scanned to PDF and sent to Quality Assurance → that PDF is not an "electronic record"; it is merely an electronic copy of paper, and Part 11 does not apply.

§11.1(e) Inspection Implications

§11.1(e) gives FDA investigators full authority to inspect Part 11 systems, including hardware, software and system documentation (validation documents, SOPs, access control lists). This means an FDA inspection does not look only at production records; investigators may also request:

  • System validation reports (IQ/OQ/PQ)
  • User account lists and permission settings
  • Audit trail records
  • System SOPs and administrator manuals
  • Backup and disaster recovery test records

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO uses an Excel spreadsheet on a shared network drive to record environmental monitoring data (temperature/humidity). Is this a Part 11 "electronic record"? If so, which requirements must be met?
  2. Why is §11.1(e) an important compliance risk point for system administrators?
1.2 §11.2 — Implementation

Original Text

§ 11.2(a) — Records maintained but not submitted to FDA:
Persons may use electronic records in lieu of paper records or electronic signatures in lieu of traditional signatures, in whole or in part, provided that the requirements of this part are met.

§ 11.2(b) — Records submitted to FDA:
Persons may use electronic records in lieu of paper records or electronic signatures in lieu of traditional signatures, in whole or in part, provided that:

  • (1) The requirements of this part are met; and
  • (2) The document or parts of a document to be submitted have been identified in public docket No. 92S-0251 as being the type of submission the agency accepts in electronic form.

This docket will identify specifically what types of documents or parts of documents are acceptable for electronic submission. The agency will periodically publish in the Federal Register a list of the types of documents it will accept electronically.

Practical impact: Most internal manufacturing records (eBR, LIMS) fall under §11.2(a) — maintained but not submitted to FDA. Records submitted electronically to FDA (e.g., eCTD submissions, eDMFs) must also meet the electronic submission criteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The two record categories under §11.2:

Category A: internally maintained records (not submitted to FDA): eBR, LIMS and SCADA data. These only need to meet the requirements of Part 11 itself. This is the situation a CDMO encounters most often.

Category B: records submitted electronically to FDA: eCTD (electronic Common Technical Document), electronic IND, electronic NDA/BLA. In addition to meeting Part 11, you must confirm that FDA accepts electronic submission of that specific record type (the docket 92S-0251 list).

For most manufacturing CDMOs, day-to-day operating records fall under Category A. Clients' regulatory submission materials fall under Category B (usually handled by the client and its regulatory affairs department, not by the manufacturing side).

Analogy

§11.2(a) is like your "internal bookkeeping": as long as it complies with company policy and tax law, you can keep the books however you like. §11.2(b) is like a "tax return filed with the tax authority": besides being compliant, you must use the format and filing channel the tax authority prescribes.

1.3 §11.3 — Definitions

Original Text

§11.3(c) Biometric: A method of verifying an individual's identity based on measurement of the individual's physical feature(s) or repeatable action(s) where those features and/or actions are both unique to that individual and measurable.

§11.3(d) Closed system: An environment in which system access is controlled by persons who are responsible for the content of electronic records that are on the system.

§11.3(e) Digital signature: An electronic signature based upon cryptographic methods of originator authentication, computed by using a set of rules and a set of parameters such that the identity of the signer and the integrity of the data can be verified.

§11.3(f) Electronic record: Any combination of text, graphics, data, audio, pictorial, or other information representation in digital form that is created, modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or distributed by a computer system.

§11.3(g) Electronic signature: A computer data compilation of any symbol or series of symbols executed, adopted, or authorized by an individual to be the legally binding equivalent of the individual's handwritten signature.

§11.3(h) Handwritten signature: The scripted name or legal mark of an individual handwritten by that individual and executed or adopted with the present intention to authenticate a writing in an electronic form. The act of signing with a writing or marking instrument such as a pen or stylus is preserved. The scripted name or legal mark, while conventionally applied to paper, may also be applied to other devices that capture the name or mark.

§11.3(i) Open system: An environment in which system access is not controlled by persons who are responsible for the content of electronic records that are on the system.

Tutorial Commentary

Distinguishing the Three Types of "Signature"

1. Electronic signature: The broadest term. Any computer data compilation of any symbol or series of symbols executed, adopted or authorized by an individual to be the legally binding equivalent of the individual's handwritten signature. It includes ID + password combinations, digital certificates, biometrics and so on.

2. Digital signature: A subset of electronic signatures, based on cryptographic methods (asymmetric encryption, PKI). It can verify both the identity of the signer and the integrity of the document (any tampering invalidates the signature). Part 11 requires its use in open systems (§11.30).

3. Handwritten signature executed to electronic record: A signature written by hand on an electronic device using a stylus pad. It preserves the handwriting of the signature, but the medium is an electronic device. Part 11 also regulates this type of signature (when signing an electronic record).

Practical Implications of the Biometric Definition

The definition of biometrics in §11.3(c) stresses two conditions: (1) unique to an individual; (2) measurable.

Qualifying biometrics: fingerprints, retina/iris scans, facial recognition, voiceprints, and handwriting dynamics (not just the result, but the speed/pressure/stroke order of the signature).

Not qualifying (or debatable): a static scanned image of a signature (it can be copied and is not unique).

Biometric electronic signatures fall under §11.200(b) and do not need two distinct identification components, but you must ensure they can "be used only by their genuine owners".

Determining Closed vs. Open System: Key Concepts

The key test: WHO controls access?

Closed system = the persons responsible for the content of the electronic records (usually the company's IT/QA) control system access.

Open system = access is controlled by "parties other than those responsible for the content of the records".

1.4 Closed vs. Open System — Practical Implications

Original Text

The classification of a system as closed or open determines the applicable controls under Part 11:

FDA's rationale: In a closed system, physical and administrative access controls protect records. In an open system (accessible via public networks), records can be intercepted during transmission — encryption protects confidentiality, and digital signatures protect integrity.

The access control test: Ask "who controls the user accounts for this system?" If the answer is the same organization responsible for the records (the pharmaceutical company), it is typically a closed system. If a third party (cloud provider, ISP, external IT vendor) controls access, it is an open system.

Gray areas in practice: Many modern cloud-based systems blur the line. A cloud LIMS where the pharmaceutical company manages all user accounts (even though hosted externally) may be argued as a closed system if the company has administrative control over who can access the system. However, the transmission layer (internet) introduces open system elements. FDA has not issued definitive guidance on Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) systems — risk-based assessment is recommended.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Closed system: Like your company's own internal vault: the company controls the keys and the entry list. Even if the vault is in an outside building, it is still "closed" as long as you control who can get in.

Open system: Like leaving documents in a public library: even if you have a locked compartment, the librarian (a third party) has a master key and can open any compartment. That is the essence of "open".

Part 11 Challenges of Cloud Systems

Common practice for SaaS systems (such as Veeva Vault, MasterControl, LabVantage):

The vendor provides: (1) a system validation support package (IQ/OQ protocols); (2) a SOC 2 Type II audit report (third-party security assessment); (3) contract terms guaranteeing access control and audit trail functionality.

The pharmaceutical company (CDMO) is responsible for: (1) vendor qualification; (2) supplementary PQ testing of Part 11-specific functions; (3) a risk assessment covering how the system is classified (closed vs. open) and how encrypted transmission is ensured.

During an FDA inspection, either position may be accepted, but a documented risk assessment and corresponding controls must be in place.

Practical Application (CDMO Focus)

System classification decision tree (Amaran Biotech application):

Step 1: Identify who controls user accounts (where are the system administrators?)

Step 2: Identify the data transmission path (internal network only vs. through a public network?)

Step 3: If it is a cloud system, evaluate the access control terms in the vendor contract

Step 4: Document the classification decision and rationale (documented risk assessment)

Step 5: Based on the classification, decide which additional controls are needed (encryption? digital signature?)

Step 6: Record the system classification in the validation documents (URS, risk assessment)

Chapter Summary

  • The §11.1 exclusion: Electronic transmission of paper records (scanning, sending PDFs) is not subject to Part 11; the record must be "created/maintained in electronic form" for Part 11 to apply.
  • Closed vs. open systems are determined by who controls access: Technology (internal network/cloud) is not the criterion; the deciding factor is whether "the persons responsible for the content of the records control system access".
  • Keep the three signature types apart: electronic signature (broadest) ⊃ digital signature (a cryptographic method, used in open systems); handwritten signature executed to electronic record (stylus pad) is also regulated by Part 11.
  • Open systems need additional controls: On top of the 11 controls in §11.10, they also need encryption and digital signature standards (§11.30), to protect confidentiality and integrity during transmission.

2.1 §11.10 — Controls for Closed Systems: Overview of 11 Requirements

Original Text

Persons who use closed systems to create, modify, maintain, or transmit electronic records shall employ procedures and controls designed to ensure the authenticity, integrity, and, when appropriate, the confidentiality of electronic records, and to ensure that the signer cannot readily repudiate the signed record as not genuine.

Such procedures and controls shall include the following:

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three core objectives (authenticity + integrity + confidentiality):

Authenticity: Confirming that the record was in fact created by the person who claims to have created it. Assured by identity verification for electronic signatures.

Integrity: Confirming that the record has not been tampered with since it was created. Assured by the audit trail (§11.10(e)) and signature/record linking (§11.70).

Confidentiality: Required only "when appropriate"; not every record needs to be kept confidential. GMP records usually do not need encryption (within a closed system), but records containing trade secrets or personal data may.

High-Risk Controls for FDA 483 Observations

Of the 11 controls, the ones where FDA inspectors most often observe deficiencies (ranked):

1st: (e) Audit trail: not enabled, incomplete, or modifiable

2nd: (d) Access control: shared accounts, accounts of departed employees not disabled

3rd: (a) Validation: system not validated or inadequately validated

4th: (j) Written policies: no SOP defining accountability for electronic signatures

5th: (i) Training: system training records missing or incomplete

Analogy

Think of the 11 controls in §11.10 as the layers of protection in a secure building:

(a) Validation = structural safety inspection of the building (the foundation); (d) Access = the badge/door access system; (e) Audit trail = 24-hour CCTV monitoring; (f) Operational checks = workflow lock-in (steps cannot be skipped); (g) Authority checks = different authorizations for different areas; (j) Policies = the employee code-of-conduct handbook

2.2 §11.10(a) — Validation: Computer System Validation (CSV)

Original Text

§11.10(a): Validation of systems to ensure accuracy, reliability, consistent intended performance, and the ability to discern invalid or altered records.

While §11.10(a) does not specify a validation methodology, industry has converged on the GAMP 5 (Good Automated Manufacturing Practice) framework published by ISPE for implementing CSV requirements.

GAMP 5 Software Categories:

Key CSV documents for Part 11 compliance:

  • Computer System Validation Master Plan (CVMP)
  • User Requirements Specification (URS)
  • Functional Requirements Specification (FRS)
  • Installation Qualification (IQ) — is the system installed correctly?
  • Operational Qualification (OQ) — does the system function as designed?
  • Performance Qualification (PQ) — does the system perform correctly in intended use?
  • Traceability Matrix — linking requirements to test scripts
  • Summary Validation Report and System Release for Use

Change Control requirement: Validated systems must maintain their validated state. Any change (software update, configuration change, hardware replacement) requires documented change control, impact assessment, and revalidation as appropriate.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why is validation needed? §11.10(a) requires that the system be able to "discern invalid or altered records". This means the system itself must be reliable enough to ensure that the records in it can be trusted. An unvalidated system may: (1) calculate incorrectly without raising an error; (2) allow records to be modified without leaving a trace; (3) fail under certain conditions and produce erroneous data.

Validation focus for Part 11: Part 11-specific validation test scripts must test whether:

  • The audit trail function is enabled and cannot be switched off
  • Access control restricts users correctly by role
  • Electronic signatures display name, date/time and meaning
  • Record export is complete and accurate
  • Backup and restore functions work properly

FDA 2003 Guidance: Validation Exception for Legacy Systems

In its 2003 guidance, FDA stated that for legacy systems already in use before August 1997 and with paper as the official record, it exercises "enforcement discretion" regarding the §11.10(a) validation requirement. However, this exception is very narrow: a modern CDMO system will almost never meet both conditions.

Practical Application (CDMO Focus)

Amaran Biotech LIMS validation strategy (GAMP Category 4 system):

1. URS: write up all Part 11 requirements (audit trail, access control, electronic signatures, etc.) as user requirements

2. Vendor assessment: review the LIMS vendor's GAMP documentation package (DQ, IQ/OQ protocol templates)

3. IQ: confirm correct installation, that the version meets specification, and that licensing documents are complete

4. OQ: test all Part 11 functions (audit trail, access control, electronic signatures, export function)

5. PQ: verify system performance in the actual workflow (run real QC testing processes)

6. Release the system for use (System Release) and establish a continuous monitoring plan

2.3 §11.10(e) — Audit Trails: The Most Critical Part 11 Requirement

Original Text

§11.10(e): Use of secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails to independently record the date and time of operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete electronic records. Record changes shall not obscure previously recorded information. Such audit trail documentation shall be retained for a period at least as long as that required for the subject electronic records and shall be available for agency review and copying.

The 8 Critical Elements of a §11.10(e) Compliant Audit Trail:

  1. SECURE: Cannot be turned off, modified, or deleted by users. Only documented administrators with authorization can adjust settings, and such changes are themselves logged.
  2. COMPUTER-GENERATED: Automatically created by the system — NOT manually entered by users. This is the fundamental distinction from paper logbooks.
  3. TIME-STAMPED: Each entry records date and time, synchronized to a reliable time source (NTP). Time zone documentation required for multi-site systems.
  4. INDEPENDENTLY RECORDING: Audit trail is a separate log, not part of the editable data record. Cannot be in the same file as the record that can be edited.
  5. CREATE/MODIFY/DELETE: ALL three operations must be captured. The audit trail must record every original entry, every change (old value → new value, reason, user, timestamp), and every deletion.
  6. NOT OBSCURING: Changes must show BOTH old and new values. Overwriting old data without capturing original = non-compliant.
  7. RETAINED: Retained for at least as long as the associated electronic record. If a batch record is retained 3 years, its audit trail is retained 3 years.
  8. AVAILABLE FOR FDA REVIEW: FDA inspectors must be able to access and copy audit trails during inspections. Must be exportable in a readable format.
"Record changes shall not obscure previously recorded information."
— 21 CFR §11.10(e), critical anti-falsification provision

Tutorial Commentary

Most Common FDA 483 Deficiencies on Audit Trails

1. Audit trail not enabled: "Audit trail functionality was not enabled on [system]." The system has an audit trail function, but it was switched off by default by the administrator or the vendor.

2. Only new values recorded, not old values: "The audit trail does not capture the original value prior to change." The system shows only the modified value and the original data cannot be traced.

3. Deletions not recorded: "Deleted records are not captured in the audit trail." Someone deleted a record, but the audit trail does not show it.

4. Reason for change missing: "Reason for change not required when modifying electronic records." The system allows changes without a reason.

5. Audit trail can be modified by users: "Non-administrative users have the ability to modify audit trail entries."

6. Audit trail not reviewed periodically: "No procedure for periodic review of audit trail records as part of quality oversight."

Analogy

An audit trail is like a bank's "transaction history". Every deposit, withdrawal and transfer in your bank account is recorded automatically (computer-generated) and time-stamped, and you cannot modify or delete these records (secure). In a dispute, the bank can provide the complete transaction history (available for review). The audit trail of a GMP electronic record follows the same logic, except that it governs pharmaceutical operations rather than financial transactions.

Audit Trail Inspection Drill

Scenario: An FDA investigator asks to see the eBR audit trail for batch #AMB-2026-0411.

You should be able to show:

  1. The original value of each input field (who entered it and when)
  2. All modification records (old value, new value, who changed it, when, and the reason for change)
  3. All electronic signature records (signer's name, date/time, meaning of the signature)
  4. Any attempted but failed operations (access denied records)
  5. Administrator action records (system configuration changes)

You should be able to export these records as PDF or CSV so the investigator can take a copy away.

2.4 §11.10(d) & (g) — Access Controls and Authority Checks

Original Text

§11.10(d) — Limiting system access:
Limiting system access to authorized individuals.

This requires:

  • Individual user accounts (no shared/generic accounts)
  • Role-based access control (RBAC) matching job function and training
  • Periodic user access reviews
  • Timely deprovisioning when personnel change roles or leave
  • Physical access controls for workstations

§11.10(g) — Authority checks:
Use of authority checks to ensure that only authorized individuals can use the system, electronically sign a record, access the operation or computer system input or output device, alter a record, or perform the operation at issue.

Authority checks go beyond access (§11.10d) to specifically govern:

  • WHO can perform electronic signatures (validated signing authority)
  • WHO can approve specific records (role-based approval authority)
  • WHO can modify approved records (typically only QA with documented justification)
  • Separation of duties (operator cannot approve their own work)

Shared accounts — fundamental incompatibility with Part 11:
If two people share an ID/password, the signature is no longer unique to one individual (violating §11.100a) and actions cannot be attributed (violating §11.50 and ALCOA Attributable principle).

Tutorial Commentary

Shared Accounts Are a Systemic Problem

When an FDA inspection finds shared accounts, it is not just a Part 11 §11.10(d) problem but a domino effect that triggers multiple compliance issues:

→ §11.100(a): the electronic signature is not unique to one individual

→ §11.50: the signature cannot identify the actual signer (violating the "printed name" requirement)

→ ALCOA Attributable: the record cannot be attributed to an individual

→ 21 CFR 211.68: responsibility of the operator is unclear

FDA usually elevates a shared account observation to a "data integrity deficiency", which may lead to a Warning Letter.

The Difference Between Access Control and Authority Control

§11.10(d) access control: The question of "Can you get into this system?" Like building access: do you have a badge?

§11.10(g) authority checks: The question of "Once inside the system, which operations are you allowed to perform?" Like the different floors in a building requiring different levels of authorization: ordinary employees can go to the offices, but cannot enter the server room or the finance office.

2.5 §11.10(b)(c)(f)(h)(i)(j)(k) — Remaining Controls

Original Text

§11.10(b) — Readable copies: The ability to generate accurate and complete copies of records in both human readable and electronic form suitable for inspection, review, and copying by the agency.

§11.10(c) — Record protection: Protection of records to enable their accurate and ready retrieval throughout the records retention period. Protection from: unauthorized alteration/deletion, data corruption, disaster loss, storage media degradation.

§11.10(f) — Operational system checks: Enforce permitted sequencing of steps and events, as appropriate. Example: in an eBR, an operator cannot sign "dispensing complete" before "weight check passed."

§11.10(h) — Device checks: Determine, as appropriate, the validity of the source of data input or operational instruction. Includes instrument calibration verification and data transmission integrity checks.

§11.10(i) — Training: Persons who develop, maintain, or use electronic record/electronic signature systems have the education, training, and experience to perform their assigned tasks. Training must be role-specific, documented, and current.

§11.10(j) — Written policies: Establishment of, and adherence to, written policies that hold individuals accountable and responsible for actions initiated under their electronic signatures. SOPs must define what constitutes an electronic signature, prohibited actions (sharing credentials), and consequences for misuse.

§11.10(k) — Systems documentation controls: Adequate controls over distribution, access to, and use of documentation for system operation and maintenance; revision and change control procedures maintaining an audit trail of changes to documentation, identifying date of change, nature of change, and individuals making the change.

Tutorial Commentary

Inspection Implications of §11.10(b) Readable Copies

During an FDA inspection, the investigator is entitled to ask for "accurate and complete copies" of a batch record to take away. §11.10(b) requires your system to be able to:

  • Immediately export the complete batch record (including all fields, charts and attachments)
  • Provide it in a format FDA can read (PDF, paper printout)
  • Ensure the exported copy is fully consistent with the original record in the system (without omitting any audit trail entry)

Common problem: the PDF exported by the system lacks the audit trail pages, or the format cannot display all fields completely. FDA will flag all of these as §11.10(b) deficiencies.

§11.10(c) Record Protection and Backup Strategy

Record protection must cover the entire "retention period". The retention period is set by the predicate rule:

  • Drug product batch records (§211.180): at least 1 year after the expiration date, with a minimum of 3 years
  • Biologics batch records: 10 years or longer after the expiration date
  • Validation documents: the period the system is in use + a number of years after retirement

Protective measures: regular backups (daily incremental + weekly full), offsite backup (disaster recovery), backup testing (quarterly verification of restore capability), and storage media management (preventing media degradation).

§11.10(f) Operational System Checks: A Key eBR Function

In an electronic batch record, operational system checks ensure that process steps are executed in the correct sequence. This is an important advantage of electronic systems over paper: on a paper batch record, an operator can sign ahead of time, whereas an eBR system can technically enforce "step B can be opened only after step A is completed and signed". This directly prevents the problem of backdating signatures.

2.6 §11.30 — Controls for Open Systems

Original Text

§11.30 Controls for open systems:
Persons who use open systems to create, modify, maintain, or transmit electronic records shall employ procedures and controls designed to ensure the authenticity, integrity, and, as appropriate, the confidentiality of electronic records from the point of their creation to the point of their receipt.

Such procedures and controls shall include those identified in §11.10, as appropriate, and additional measures such as:

  • Document encryption, using appropriate encryption methods
  • Use of appropriate digital signature standards to ensure, as appropriate, record authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality

Why open systems need more:

  • In a closed system, physical and administrative controls protect records — the data "stays inside"
  • In an open system, records traverse public networks — interception is possible
  • Encryption protects confidentiality: even if intercepted, data cannot be read
  • Digital signatures protect integrity: any modification invalidates the signature, alerting recipients

Practical example: A CRO submitting clinical data to a pharma sponsor via a cloud platform. The CRO cannot control access to the AWS infrastructure — this is an open system. Data submission must use TLS encryption (in transit) and SHA-256 digital signatures to meet §11.30.

Tutorial Commentary

Technical Implementation of the Additional Open System Controls

Document encryption:

Encryption in transit: TLS 1.2+ / HTTPS. The browser's "padlock icon" indicates TLS encryption. All SaaS GMP systems should enforce HTTPS.

Encryption at rest: AES-256 encryption of stored records. If the database is accessed without authorization, the encrypted content cannot be read.

Digital signature standards:

FDA-recognized standards include FIPS 140-2 validated cryptographic modules and RSA/ECDSA signature algorithms. A digital signature binds the signer's specific public key to the content of the document: any modification to the document causes signature verification to fail, providing a technical assurance of integrity.

§11.30 vs §11.10: Cumulative, Not a Replacement

Many people mistakenly think an open system needs only "encryption and digital signatures" and not the other §11.10 controls. This is wrong. The text of §11.30 reads: "shall include those identified in §11.10, as appropriate, and additional measures". That is, the 11 controls in §11.10 still apply, and §11.30 adds encryption and digital signatures on top.

Practical Application (CDMO Focus)

Compliance strategy for a cloud LIMS (open system):

1. Confirm the SaaS vendor provides TLS-encrypted transmission (HTTPS)

2. Confirm encryption of data at rest (at-rest encryption, usually AES-256)

3. Confirm the vendor's SOC 2 Type II report covers access control, encryption and audit trails

4. Assess the system classification (whether it is an open system) and document it in the URS and risk assessment

5. Define the vendor's Part 11-related responsibilities explicitly in the SLA/contract

6. Supplement OQ/PQ with tests of open system-specific functions (encrypted connection, signature integrity verification)

Chapter Summary

  • The §11.10(e) audit trail is the core Part 11 requirement: It must be computer-generated, time-stamped, secure and tamper-proof, record create/modify/delete operations, retain old and new values, be retained for as long as the subject record, and be available for FDA review and copying. Any gap is a high-risk FDA 483 observation.
  • §11.10(a) validation is the foundation of compliance: An unvalidated system cannot ensure the accuracy and reliability of its records. Under the GAMP 5 framework, core CDMO systems such as LIMS/eBR/SCADA are all Category 4 and require full IQ/OQ/PQ.
  • Shared accounts are a fundamental violation: They violate §11.10(d) access control + §11.100(a) signature uniqueness, and are also a serious ALCOA Attributable failure that may escalate to Warning Letter level.
  • Open systems need cumulative controls: §11.30 = all 11 controls of §11.10 + encryption + digital signatures. This protects the confidentiality and integrity of records transmitted over public networks.

3.1 §11.50 — Signature Manifestations: Three Mandatory Elements

Original Text

§11.50(a): Signed electronic records shall contain information associated with the signing that clearly indicates all of the following:

(1) The printed name of the signer;
The full human-readable name of the signer — not just a user ID, employee number, or initials. The name must be legible and unambiguous.

(2) The date and time when the signature was executed;
The exact timestamp when the signature was applied — NOT the date the record was created. This timestamp must be automatically generated by the system (computer-generated), not manually entered by the signer.

(3) The meaning (such as review, approval, responsibility, or authorship) associated with the signature.
The purpose of the signature must be explicitly stated. Examples:

  • "Reviewed by" — QC analyst reviewing test data
  • "Approved by" — QA manager approving batch record for release
  • "Authored by" — operator entering process data
  • "Verified by" — second-person verification step
  • "Accountability signature" — accepting responsibility for a critical operation

§11.50(b): The items identified in paragraphs (a)(1), (a)(2), and (a)(3) of this section shall be subject to the same controls as for electronic records and shall be included as part of any human readable form of the electronic record (such as electronic display or printout).

"The meaning (such as review, approval, responsibility, or authorship) associated with the signature."
— 21 CFR §11.50(a)(3), the often-neglected third element

Common deficiencies observed by FDA:

  • System displays only user ID (not printed name) in audit trail or on record
  • Date/time is manually entered by the signer (not computer-generated)
  • Meaning is generic ("signed") rather than specific ("approved for release")
  • Signature manifestation not included in printed/exported copies of records
  • Time zone not documented for systems used across multiple sites/time zones

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

All three elements are indispensable: The three elements of §11.50 must "all appear together" in the signature-related record to meet the requirement.

Element 1: Printed name: "Printed" does not mean the name must come out of a printer; it means a "clearly legible, full name string", corresponding to the "legibility" of a handwritten signature. "John Doe" is compliant; "JD" or "jd01" is not.

Element 2: Automatic time stamp: The time stamp must be generated by the system and cannot be a date field typed in by the user. If the system lets the user edit the signing time, it violates the "computer-generated" requirement (the same logic applies to §11.10(e)).

Element 3: Meaning of the signature (the most often overlooked): Many systems display only "Signed by: John Doe at 2026-04-11 09:23", without stating "why it was signed". Without this element, the record cannot show where responsibility lies: "I signed" and "I approved this decision" have completely different legal meanings.

Analogy

Imagine a court judgment. It must include: (1) the judge's full name (not a court code); (2) the date and time of the judgment (recorded by the clerk, not filled in by the judge); (3) the nature of the judgment ("appeal dismissed" vs. "found guilty" vs. "transfer of jurisdiction approved"). If any one of the three is missing, the legal effect of the judgment is incomplete. The logic of the three elements of an electronic signature under §11.50 is exactly the same.

3.2 §11.70 — Signature/Record Linking: Anti-Falsification

Original Text

§11.70: Electronic signatures and handwritten signatures executed to electronic records shall be linked to their respective electronic records to ensure that the signatures cannot be excised, copied, or otherwise transferred to falsify an electronic record by ordinary means.

The problem §11.70 solves — "Signature Cut-and-Paste" Fraud:
Without linking, a valid electronic signature from a legitimate record could be extracted and inserted into a falsified record. For example:

  • Extract QA Director's approval signature from a passed batch record
  • Insert it into a failed batch record that was changed to show passing results
  • Result: falsified batch record appears to have QA Director's approval

Technical implementations that satisfy §11.70:

  • Cryptographic binding: The signature is computed over the record content (digital signature/hash). Any modification to the record invalidates the signature — tampering is immediately detectable.
  • Database linking with record version control: The signature record contains an immutable foreign key reference to the specific version of the electronic record (identified by a hash or unique version ID). If the record changes, the signature no longer matches the current version.
  • Record locking: Once signed, the record is frozen — no edits allowed without going through a formal amendment workflow that requires re-signature, creating a new audit trail entry.

FDA inspection test: Inspectors may ask: "If I print this batch record, can the signature block be cut and pasted into another document?" The system design must make this technically impossible or immediately detectable.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The anti-falsification logic of §11.70: If an electronic signature is just an "image" or a "text string" attached next to the record, anyone with basic computer skills can copy the image/string → paste it into another document → obtain a forged document that appears to be authorized.

§11.70 requires the signature to be "linked", meaning the signature must be technically "bound" to the content of a specific version of the record, so that the combination of "signature + record A" cannot technically be transferred into a valid combination of "signature + record B".

Analogy

The traditional wax seal is a good analogy: the wax covers the opening of the letter and carries the holder's seal. If you try to open it and re-seal it, the wax seal is bound to break and cannot be restored, so the forgery is obvious at a glance. The hash value of a digital signature is the modern wax seal: if the record content changes, the hash value no longer matches, and the "broken seal" is immediately visible.

How §11.70 Is Assessed During Inspections

Common methods FDA investigators use to assess §11.70 compliance:

Method 1: Ask for a batch record to be printed, then ask: "If I scan the signature on this printed version and paste it into another document, can the system detect it?" The answer should be: "It does not need to detect it, because the printed version is merely a human-readable copy; the signature binding mechanism inside the system means the signature in the electronic record cannot be transplanted."

Method 2: Try to modify a signed record and see whether the system blocks the change or forces re-signing.

Method 3: Review the system's technical documentation to confirm the design of the signature mechanism (hash values, version locking, database linkage).

3.3 §11.100 — General Requirements: Uniqueness, Identity Verification & FDA Certification

Original Text

§11.100(a) — Uniqueness:
Each electronic signature shall be unique to one individual and shall not be reused by, or reassigned to, anyone else.

This uniqueness requirement means:

  • No shared accounts (two people sharing one username/password)
  • No reassignment of identifiers (when an employee leaves, their user ID cannot be given to a new employee)
  • Even after deactivation, the same ID cannot be reused for a different person

§11.100(b) — Identity Verification:
Before an organization establishes, assigns, certifies, or otherwise sanctions an individual's electronic signature, or any element of such electronic signature, the organization shall verify the identity of the individual.

Acceptable identity verification methods:

  • Government-issued photo ID review during HR onboarding
  • In-person verification by HR or supervisor
  • Biometric enrollment process

§11.100(c) — FDA Certification Letter (the "Part 11 letter"):
Persons using electronic signatures shall, prior to or at the time of such use, certify to the agency in writing that the electronic signatures in their system are intended to be the legally binding equivalent of traditional handwritten signatures.

Upon agency request, such certification shall be submitted in paper form with a traditional handwritten signature to the docket established by the agency for such purposes (Docket No. 92S-0251).

This is a company-level certification, not per-system or per-user. One letter per company covers all systems and all users. The letter should reference the FDA docket number and state the company's intent that all electronic signatures used in FDA-regulated activities are intended to be legally binding equivalents of handwritten signatures.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Impact of §11.100(a) Uniqueness

Managing accounts of departed employees: When an employee leaves, the account must be disabled (not deleted). A disabled account must not be reassigned to a new employee, even if the employee ID follows the same format. Reason: the audit trail already holds a large number of records linked to that account; if the account were reassigned, it would later be impossible to determine which real person performed the historical operations.

Correct practice: Disable the account → retain the account (set to "inactive" status) → create a brand-new account for the new employee (even if the name or ID prefix is similar)

The §11.100(c) FDA Certification Letter in Detail

What the letter is: A statement filed by the company with FDA saying, "Our company uses electronic signatures, and we understand and confirm that these electronic signatures are legally equivalent to handwritten signatures."

When to send it: "prior to or at the time of such use": before the first use of electronic signatures, or at the time of use. In practice, send it before the Part 11 system goes live.

Who to send it to: The FDA Office of Regional Operations (ORO), referencing Docket No. 92S-0251.

Format: Paper (not an electronic submission!), with the handwritten signature of a company official (ironically, the letter declaring electronic signatures valid must itself carry a handwritten signature).

One letter covers the whole company: If Amaran Biotech has three systems (eBR, LIMS, SCADA), only one letter is needed, not one per system.

  2. A QA director has left, and the company wants to transfer her account (qa-director) to her successor. Does this meet the requirements of §11.100(a)? What is the correct approach?
3.4 §11.200 — Electronic Signature Components and Controls

Original Text

§11.200(a) — Non-biometric electronic signatures:
Electronic signatures that are not based upon biometrics shall:

(1) Employ at least two distinct identification components such as an identification code and password.

When BOTH components are required:

  • When used by an individual other than in the context of continuous or serial use: Must use BOTH identification components (ID code + password)
  • At the start of every non-continuous session (new login, after session timeout): Must use BOTH components

When ONE component is sufficient:

  • When an individual uses a series of signings during a single, continuous period of controlled system access: Subsequent signings need only employ ONE component (e.g., just re-entering the password)

(2) Be used only by their genuine owners; and

(3) Be administered and executed to ensure that attempted use of an individual's electronic signature by anyone other than its genuine owner requires collaboration of two or more individuals.

"When used by an individual other than in the context of continuous or serial use, each electronic signing shall employ both identification components."
— 21 CFR §11.200(a), the two-component rule

§11.200(b) — Biometric electronic signatures:
Electronic signatures based upon biometrics shall be designed to ensure that they cannot be used by anyone other than their genuine owners.

Biometric signatures (fingerprint, retinal scan, facial recognition, voice recognition) do not require the two-component rule because the biometric IS inherently individual and cannot be transferred. The system design must ensure the biometric cannot be spoofed.

Tutorial Commentary

Determining Continuous vs. Non-Continuous Sessions

Single continuous period of controlled system access: The operator logs in to the system (using user ID + password) and performs multiple signing actions in the same session; for subsequent signings, only the password needs to be entered again (one component is sufficient).

Non-continuous situations (re-login): In the following cases, both identification components must be used again:

  • After the session times out and the user is logged out automatically
  • After the operator logs out voluntarily and logs in again
  • When logging in from a different computer or device
  • After the account has been locked and then unlocked

Common design in practice: Initial login = user ID + password (two components); signing within the same session = password only (one component); session timeout (usually 15-30 minutes of inactivity) = user ID + password required again.

The Anti-Collusion Design Requirement of §11.200(a)(3)

This requirement states that an attempt to use another person's electronic signature must require "collaboration of two or more individuals" to succeed. It is a technical safeguard: the system should be designed so that one unauthorized person alone cannot use someone else's electronic signature without detection.

This is why the user ID + password combination is called "two distinct identification components" in Part 11: to misuse another person's signature, you need to know both the user ID AND the password, which requires information from two parties; in theory, one person alone cannot obtain all of the information.

Analogy

The continuous-session rule of §11.200 is like the "two-key" system of a bank vault: to open the door the first time, both keys are needed, the branch manager's (user ID) and the supervisor's (password). Once the vault is open, within the same working day only the supervisor's key is needed to continue accessing it. But if the vault is closed (the session ends), both keys must be used again the next time it is opened.

3.5 §11.300 — Controls for Identification Codes/Passwords

Original Text

Persons who use electronic signatures based upon use of identification codes in combination with passwords shall employ controls to ensure their security and integrity. Such controls shall include:

(a) Maintaining uniqueness:
Maintaining the uniqueness of each combined identification code and password, such that no two individuals have the same combination of identification code and password.

(b) Periodic review and revision:
Ensuring that identification code and password issuances are periodically checked, recalled, or revised (e.g., to cover such events as password aging, with appropriate token revisions as necessary).

(c) Loss management:
Following loss management procedures to electronically de-authorize lost, stolen, missing, or otherwise potentially compromised tokens, cards, and other devices that bear or generate identification code or password information, and to issue temporary or permanent replacements using suitable, rigorous controls.

(d) Transaction safeguards:
Use of transaction safeguards to prevent unauthorized use of passwords and/or identification codes, and to detect and report in an immediate and urgent manner any attempts at their unauthorized use to the system security unit, and, as appropriate, to organizational management.

(e) Initial and periodic device testing:
Initial and periodic testing of devices, such as tokens or cards, that bear or generate identification code or password information to ensure that they function properly and have not been altered in an unauthorized manner.

Tutorial Commentary

Implementation Framework for the Five §11.300 Control Elements

(a) Uniqueness: The system technically does not allow two users to set the same user ID + password combination. Most modern systems already do this automatically (unique user IDs, with passwords stored as hashes to prevent collisions). However, an SOP is needed to explicitly prohibit reuse of old user IDs.

(b) Periodic revision: The password management policy should include:

  • Password validity period (recommended: 60-90 days)
  • Password complexity requirements (length ≥ 8, upper and lower case, numbers, special characters)
  • Password history (prohibit reuse of the last 10-24 passwords)
  • Forced change at first login (for initial passwords set by IT)

(c) Loss management (the most critical SOP):

  • Immediate reporting procedure (employees report lost/stolen credentials immediately)
  • Immediate account deactivation (not after the facts are established, but at once)
  • Issue new credentials after re-verification of identity
  • Event recording and investigation

(d) Transaction safeguards: Technical implementation:

  • Automatic account lockout after N consecutive failed login attempts (usually 3-5)
  • Lockout events are immediately notified to system security personnel (by email or system alert)
  • All authentication attempts (successful and failed) are recorded in the audit trail

(e) Device testing: Applies to hardware tokens (such as RSA SecurID, smart cards, USB keys): test before initial issue + periodic testing that they work properly + check for signs of tampering.

Common FDA Observations on Password Control

Common FDA 483 observations:

  • "System administrator passwords are not changed periodically"
  • "Initial passwords are not forced to be changed by users at first login"
  • "No written procedure defines the reporting and handling of lost/stolen credentials"
  • "Failed logins do not trigger account lockout"
  • "The system allows users to set simple passwords (such as '12345')"

Note: §11.300 does not expressly say "passwords must be changed every 90 days", but the requirement that codes be "periodically checked, recalled, or revised" makes periodic password changes standard industry practice, and FDA inspectors will expect to see a written password policy.

Chapter Summary

  • All three §11.50 elements are indispensable: An electronic signature must automatically display (1) the full printed name, (2) a system-generated time stamp, and (3) a clear meaning of the signature (review/approval/authorship/verification). The "meaning of the signature" is the element most often overlooked and a focus of FDA inspections.
  • §11.70 signature linking prevents falsification: The signature must be technically bound to a specific version of the record, so that "signature cut-and-paste" falsification is technically impossible or immediately detectable. Cryptographic hash values and record locking are the mainstream implementations.
  • The §11.100(c) FDA certification letter is a company-level obligation: Before first using electronic signatures, the company must send a paper letter to FDA (Docket No. 92S-0251) stating that electronic signatures are the legal equivalent of handwritten signatures. One letter covers all of the company's systems.
  • §11.200 two-component authentication: Non-biometric signatures need two components, user ID + password. Subsequent signings in a continuous session may use one component (password only); every new session (including after a timeout) requires both components again. §11.300 further requires a complete password control framework (uniqueness, periodic revision, loss handling, transaction safeguards, device testing).
