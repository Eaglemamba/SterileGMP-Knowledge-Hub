Learning Objectives

  • Understand the scope of this guidance: it covers only changes to the container closure system (CCS) for glass vials and stoppers
  • Master FDA's three-tier reporting framework for post-approval CMC changes: Prior Approval Supplement (PAS), CBE-30, and Annual Report
  • Understand the risk-based principles for regulatory assessment and how they link to ICH Q9(R1) and ICH Q12

I. Introduction

Original Text

This guidance represents the current thinking of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA or Agency) on this topic. It does not establish any rights for any person and is not binding on FDA or the public. You can use an alternative approach if it satisfies the requirements of the applicable statutes and regulations. To discuss an alternative approach, contact the FDA office responsible for this guidance as listed on the title page.

FDA is issuing this guidance to collate recommendations for appropriate reporting categories and the content of postapproval change submissions across numerous FDA guidance documents. This guidance conveys recommendations to holders of approved new drug applications (NDAs), biologics license applications (BLAs), and abbreviated new drug applications (ANDAs) regarding the reporting and implementation of some common changes to container closure system (CCS) components consisting of glass vials and stoppers for approved sterile drug products, including biological products, administered parenterally. This guidance also discusses pathways available to application holders to obtain Agency feedback. Additionally, this guidance discusses risk-based tools available to facilitate the implementation of changes to CCSs consisting of glass vials and stoppers. This guidance does not apply to CCS types other than glass vials and stoppers.

"This guidance does not apply to CCS types other than glass vials and stoppers."

Footnote 1: This guidance has been prepared by the Office of Pharmaceutical Quality in the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research in cooperation with the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research at the Food and Drug Administration.

Footnote 2: In March 2021, FDA published the guidance for industry COVID-19 Container Closure System and Component Changes: Glass Vials and Stoppers, which provided recommendations regarding the reporting and implementation of some common changes to container closure system (CCS) components consisting of glass vials and stoppers for approved sterile drugs. That guidance was intended to remain in effect only for the duration of the public health emergency related to COVID-19 declared by the Secretary of Health and Human Services under section 319 of the Public Health Service Act (section 319 public health emergency), which has now expired. FDA is issuing this final guidance because many of the recommendations set forth in the 2021 guidance are applicable outside the context of the section 319 public health emergency.

Footnote 3: Although this guidance does not principally address data recommendations for original applications pending approval, the risk-based regulatory approaches discussed in this guidance, other guidance documents referenced in References, and the data recommendations provided in the tables in Appendix may be useful during product development and for original application submission. Should an applicant need to change CCS components before product approval, the applicant should proactively seek feedback from the appropriate FDA review division to mitigate the effect of the change on the review timeline of the pending application.

In general, FDA's guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead, guidances describe the Agency's current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Container Closure System (CCS): The CCS is the last barrier between the drug product and the outside environment. It consists of the glass vial, stopper, aluminum seal, and so on. Its functions include: (1) maintaining sterility, (2) protecting the drug product from light, oxygen, and moisture, and (3) not interacting harmfully with the drug formulation.

Reporting Categories: FDA classifies post-approval change reporting into three levels:

  • PAS (Prior Approval Supplement): Requires FDA approval before implementation; used for high-risk changes
  • CBE-30 (Changes Being Effected in 30 Days): May be implemented 30 days after submission
  • Annual Report: Reported in the annual report after implementation; used for low-risk changes

Analogy

Think of the CCS as choosing dinnerware for a restaurant. A high-end restaurant (the injectable drug manufacturer) is extremely strict about its dinnerware (the CCS): it must be clean, must not react chemically with the food, and must not release harmful substances. If the restaurant wants to switch to a new set of dinnerware (change the CCS supplier), you cannot simply use it because it looks the same; you must first test whether the new dinnerware is safe at various temperatures and pH levels. This is why FDA requires a systematic change assessment.

Key Notes

The COVID-19 catalyst: This final 2024 guidance originated from interim guidance issued during the 2021 COVID-19 emergency. During the pandemic, a global glass vial shortage (especially of Type I borosilicate glass) forced drug manufacturers to urgently find alternative suppliers, so FDA issued interim guidance to speed up review of CCS changes. After the pandemic, FDA concluded that these recommendations had long-term applicability and formalized them in this guidance.

Limited scope: This guidance applies only to glass vials and stoppers. It does not cover other CCS types such as pre-filled syringes, cartridges, or plastic containers.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: As a clinical-stage sterile fill-finish CDMO, your client holds the NDA/BLA and you are the filling site. When the client's glass vial supplier requests a change (such as moving to a different manufacturing site), the client must assess the reporting category for the change and submit a supplement. Your role as the CDMO is to provide process validation data (such as CCIT and media fill data) to support the client's application.

Dual sourcing strategy: The lessons of the pandemic led many companies to adopt a dual-supplier strategy for the CCS. This guidance provides a clear regulatory pathway, allowing drug manufacturers to plan the approval process for a second supplier in advance.

Practice Questions

  1. Why did FDA choose to formalize the COVID-19 interim guidance? What long-term supply chain risk considerations does this reflect?
  2. This guidance applies only to glass vials and stoppers. If a CDMO client wants to change a component of a pre-filled syringe, which other FDA guidances should be consulted?
  3. How do the regulatory responsibilities of NDA, BLA, and ANDA holders differ for CCS changes? Why are requirements for biologics (BLA) generally stricter?
II. Regulatory Approaches to CMC Changes

Original Text

As described in section 506A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 U.S.C. 356a), 21 CFR 314.70, 21 CFR 314.97, and 21 CFR 601.12, an applicant must notify FDA about changes in each condition (e.g., the product, production process, quality controls, equipment, facilities, or labeling) established in an approved application beyond the variations already provided for in the application. The reporting category for such changes depends on the potential to have an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product as these factors may relate to the safety or effectiveness of the product. Therefore, changes can range from those that require approval before implementation (prior approval supplement (PAS)), those that require notification 30 days before (changes being effected in 30 days (CBE-30)), or upon implementation (changes being effected), and those where notification can be provided after implementation (annual report). Some of these identified changes, depending on the circumstances, may be more appropriately submitted as an original application instead of as supplements.

FDA has conveyed additional clarification regarding reporting of CMC changes to an NDA, BLA, and ANDA in guidance documents specific to various application types and product dosage forms (see References). The content in section IV. Common Changes Related to Glass Vials and Stoppers related to changes to glass vials and stoppers for finished drug and biological products includes content derived from the referenced guidance documents.

FDA applies a risk-based approach to the evaluation of proposed CMC changes to a CCS (e.g., changes in components, composition, container type, suppliers, and manufacturers), taking into consideration the characteristics of the specific product. The suitability and compatibility of a CCS depends not only on the properties of a container, but also on the properties of other CCS components and their interactions with the drug product formulation over its intended shelf life. Applicants must validate the effects of the change prior to distribution of the drug, and, as appropriate, conduct additional qualification tests or submit information to address product-specific risks as part of that assessment. For example, this additional information to be submitted to FDA could include studies to assess: the effect of formulation attributes such as high pH; the effect of factors that increase the potential for glass delamination, particulate matter, leachables, or interactions of labile molecules with leachables (such as susceptibility of some proteins to interact with metal ions); or the effect of the lyophilization process on product quality. Finally, applicable tests and studies as outlined in United States Pharmacopeia (USP) chapters should be conducted to demonstrate the suitability of the glass vial following a change; such tests must be conducted where needed to ensure continued compliance with compendial standards for strength, quality, or purity of the drug.

FDA may consider available risk evaluations and product quality risk mitigation strategies to support a lower reporting category for specific changes. FDA recommends applicants use the risk management principles described in the ICH guidance for industry Q9(R1) Quality Risk Management (May 2023), and tools described in ICH guidance for industry Q12 Technical and Regulatory Considerations for Pharmaceutical Product Lifecycle Management (May 2021), where appropriate. Whether or not a change is required to be reported to FDA, in accordance with applicable regulations, FDA expects all changes to be appropriately managed by an establishment's/facility's pharmaceutical quality system under the applicable current good manufacturing practice regulations in 21 CFR parts 210, 211, and 600. Applicants should also refer to the recommendations in the ICH guidance for industry Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System (April 2009).

A supplement for a CCS-related change should include information to support the proposed change and can incorporate certain information by reference to a drug master file (DMF), where appropriate. However, when incorporating information by reference to a DMF, the supplement should also include sufficient product-specific information to support the change.

Applicants are ultimately responsible for assessing the effects of the proposed change(s) on product quality, and ensuring that information to support the change is provided for a complete submission of a supplement or annual report. DMF holders must notify affected authorized parties of any DMF changes, additions, or deletions. DMF holders also should provide application holders with sufficient information to determine the appropriate reporting category for their applications based on the potential to have an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product as these factors may relate to the safety or effectiveness of the product. This notification should occur well before making any changes to permit authorized parties to submit application changes within an appropriate time frame. For additional information on DMF submission guidelines, refer to 21 CFR 314.420, 21 CFR 601.51(a), and the draft guidance for industry Drug Master Files (October 2019).

This guidance is not intended to alter existing submission processes; applicants should follow the recommendations in current guidance documents for reporting CMC changes to an approved application (see References).

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The three-tier reporting framework is like the approval process for a home renovation:

  • PAS = structural change (such as removing a wall): You must obtain a building permit before starting work, because the change may affect structural safety
  • CBE-30 = plumbing or electrical change (such as moving an outlet): Notify the authority first; if there is no objection within 30 days, work can begin
  • Annual Report = repainting: It does not affect safety, so it is enough to record it in the annual maintenance report after the work is done

From the drug product perspective: switching to a glass vial made of an entirely new material (PAS) versus moving the same supplier to a different site (AR) carries very different risk.

Key Notes

The core role of ICH Q9(R1) and Q12: FDA explicitly recommends using the Quality Risk Management principles of ICH Q9(R1) and the product lifecycle management tools of ICH Q12 (such as Established Conditions and PACMPs). This means that if a manufacturer can demonstrate adequate risk assessment and mitigation strategies, FDA may agree to a lower reporting category (for example, from PAS down to CBE-30).

Multidimensional assessment of CCS compatibility: The suitability of a CCS depends not only on the container itself; the following must also be considered:

  • Glass delamination, especially for high-pH formulations
  • Leachables and their interaction with the drug product
  • Particulate matter, which may originate from the glass or the stopper
  • Interaction of proteins with metal ions, which requires particular attention for biologics
  • The effect of the lyophilization process on product quality

USP standards are mandatory: The text uses "must be conducted" rather than "should", which means USP-compliant testing is a mandatory requirement and cannot be omitted. The key USP chapters include <381> (elastomeric closures), <660> (glass containers), <1207> (package integrity), and <1660> (inner surface durability of glass).

Key Notes: The DMF Chain of Responsibility

The DMF holder's notification obligation: A DMF holder (usually the CCS component supplier) must notify all affected authorized parties of any change to the DMF. The notice must also be given sufficiently before the change, so that the drug manufacturer has time to submit the corresponding supplement.

Implications for CDMOs: As a CDMO, you do not hold the NDA/BLA directly, but your client relies on your information to determine the reporting category. If your glass vial supplier notifies you of a process change, you need to pass that information to the client promptly so they can assess whether a supplement must be submitted to FDA.

Practical Application

CDMO supplier change management process:

  1. Receive the notification: The glass vial supplier informs you that its manufacturing site will move from Plant A to Plant B
  2. Initial risk assessment: Assess the scope of the change: site only, or has the process changed as well?
  3. Look up the reporting category: Refer to the appendix tables of this guidance (covered in detail in Section 3)
  4. Notify the client (application holder): Provide the change details and the recommended reporting category
  5. Prepare supporting data: CCIT, stability data, media fill results, and so on
  6. Client submits the supplement: The client submits the supplement of the corresponding level to FDA

This process is consistent with the CCS integrity requirements of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022): change management is part of the Contamination Control Strategy.

Practice Questions

  1. If your glass vial supplier changes both the manufacturing site and the glass composition, which reporting category should you choose under FDA's principles, and why?
  2. FDA states that "applicants must validate the effects of the change prior to distribution". In a CDMO setting, how is this validation responsibility divided between the client and the CDMO?
  3. A DMF holder must notify authorized parties of changes "in advance". If the DMF holder fails to do so in time, what regulatory risks do the CDMO and the drug manufacturer face?
  4. Compare FDA's three-tier reporting system with the change management requirements of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022). What principles do the two share for managing CCS changes?

Key Concepts

Tiered Reporting System:

Risk-Based Approach: The core principle by which FDA assesses CCS changes is the "potential adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product". The higher the risk, the stricter the reporting category.

Drug Master File (DMF): A DMF is a confidential technical file that a CCS supplier (such as a glass vial manufacturer) submits to FDA, containing information on composition, manufacturing process, quality control, and more. A drug manufacturer may reference the DMF in a supplement but must still provide sufficient product-specific information.

Section Summary

  • Clearly defined scope: This guidance applies only to CCS changes for glass vials and stoppers, for sterile injectable drug products with an NDA, BLA, or ANDA
  • Risk-tiered reporting: CCS changes are classified by risk as PAS (high), CBE-30 (moderate), or Annual Report (low); the reporting category depends on the potential effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, and potency of the product
  • Integration of ICH tools: FDA explicitly recommends using the tools of ICH Q9(R1) (quality risk management) and ICH Q12 (lifecycle management) to support proposals for a lower reporting category
  • DMF chain of responsibility: DMF holders must notify authorized parties of all changes in advance, and the drug manufacturer is ultimately responsible for assessing the effect of the change on product quality
  • CCS compatibility is multidimensional: The assessment covers glass delamination, leachables, particulate matter, protein interactions, and the effect of the lyophilization process, and must comply with USP compendial standards
  • The COVID-19 legacy: This guidance originated in the 2021 pandemic emergency guidance and reflects FDA's long-term attention to the resilience of the CCS supply chain

III. Common Changes Related to Glass Vials and Stoppers

Original Text

The tables in the Appendix of this guidance include information derived from existing FDA guidance documents that contain recommendations for reporting category and submission content related to common CMC changes to CCS components (glass vials and stoppers) for finished drug and biological products. The tables include the type of data recommended to support a specific change (as discussed in the guidance for industry Container Closure Systems for Packaging Human Drugs and Biologics; Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Documentation (May 1999)), as well as the recommended reporting category. Some recommendations regarding data submission and reporting categories may differ based on the nature of the product and the potential risk to product quality as it may relate to safety and effectiveness of the product. The quantity of data that should be included in the submission depends on the application type and available body of knowledge.

"The quantity of data that should be included in the submission depends on the application type and available body of knowledge."

Footnote 12: The quantity of data recommended in the Appendix tables for NDAs, ANDAs, and BLAs is derived from related guidance documents. For example, the number of batches is derived from principles outlined in scale-up and postapproval changes guidance documents (e.g., 1 to 3 batches for ANDAs and NDAs depending on the body of knowledge). Where guidance documents do not provide specific information on the quantity of data expected to support the change, the applicant may contact FDA to obtain feedback.

Changes should be supported with confirmatory batch data. To assess the change, FDA may request that the applicant provide additional information in the supplement or referenced DMF.

Footnote 13: For the purposes of this guidance, confirmatory data means comparative product batch data (on product manufactured prior to and after the proposed change) that is sufficient to demonstrate no adverse effect on product quality due to the proposed CCS change.

An applicant seeking to make more than one related change concurrently should submit a supplement with data appropriate to address the potential risk from the cumulative effect of multiple changes. Where the recommended reporting categories for the individual changes differ, the submission category selected should be the one associated with the highest risk change (e.g., PAS) of the categories recommended for the individual changes.

When the CCS changes described in the Appendix tables necessitate a corresponding change in finished product specifications, the applicant should provide all studies and data appropriate to support the affected specification change(s). The reporting category depends on the potential to have an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product as these factors may relate to the safety or effectiveness of the product. For example, tightening the acceptance criteria for a quality attribute may warrant a lower reporting category than widening the acceptance criteria.

Footnote 14: The ICH guidances for industry Q6A Specifications: Test Procedures and Acceptance Criteria for New Drug Substances and New Drug Products: Chemical Substances (December 2000) and Q6B Specifications: Test Procedures and Acceptance Criteria for Biotechnological/Biological Products (August 1999) define specifications as "a list of tests, references to analytical procedures, and appropriate acceptance criteria that are numerical limits, ranges, or other criteria for the tests described."

Before introducing a new CCS component that differs from the specified CCS component type/grade in product labeling (e.g., "packaged in type 1 glass"), a supplement to change product labeling may be appropriate. When the CCS new component differs from that specified in the applicable USP monograph, appropriate engagement with USP regarding an update to the product monograph may be necessary to ensure continued compliance with the monograph.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Confirmatory Data: This is the core evidence FDA expects in assessing a CCS change. Confirmatory data are comparative batch data: product made before and after the change is compared to show that the change has no adverse effect on product quality. This is not simply "passing the tests"; it means demonstrating comparability between the pre-change and post-change product.

Body of Knowledge: FDA allows the amount of data submitted to be adjusted according to the existing body of knowledge. If the applicant has already accumulated extensive product and CCS compatibility data (for example, from long-term stability studies and experience with many batches), less additional data may be needed. This extends the concept of Established Conditions in ICH Q12.

Cumulative Effect: When several related changes are made at the same time, the risk of each change cannot be assessed separately; the cumulative effect of the changes must be assessed. The reporting category is that of the highest-risk individual change.

Analogy

The "highest risk" principle for concurrent changes is like an upgrade in airport security: if you carry both a laptop (standard screening) and a bottle of liquid over 100 ml (enhanced screening), you cannot send the two items through separate lanes; you must go through the strictest lane. Likewise, if you change the glass vial supplier (possibly an AR) and the stopper material (PAS) at the same time, the whole supplement must be submitted at the PAS level.

Key Notes

The foundational role of the FDA CCS Guidance (1999): The data types recommended in this guidance's appendix tables derive from the 1999 guidance Container Closure Systems for Packaging Human Drugs and Biologics. That 1999 guidance remains the cornerstone document for FDA's CCS assessment. Understanding its evaluation framework of protection, safety, compatibility, and performance is a prerequisite for using this 2024 guidance correctly.

Tightening vs Widening Specifications: This is an important principle that is often overlooked. Tightening acceptance criteria (for example, reducing the particulate limit from NMT 600 to NMT 300 particles per container) is generally regarded as a low-risk change because it represents a stricter quality requirement, whereas widening (for example, from NMT 300 to NMT 600 particles per container) is high risk because it may lower product quality.

Key Notes: Link to the SUPAC Guidances

The SUPAC series: The batch numbers recommended in the appendix tables (for example, 1-3 batches for NDA/ANDA) derive from the principles of the SUPAC (Scale-Up and Postapproval Changes) series of guidances. Although SUPAC-IR (immediate-release solid oral dosage forms) and SUPAC-SS (nonsterile semisolid dosage forms) target mainly oral products, this guidance also draws on their reporting principles for CCS changes. For injectables, the recommendations in this guidance are more specific and stricter.

NDA/ANDA vs BLA differences: CCS changes for biologics (BLA) generally call for a higher reporting category than those for chemical drugs (NDA/ANDA). This is because biologics (such as proteins and antibodies) are more sensitive to interactions with CCS components; for example, metal ions leached from glass vials may cause protein aggregation.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: preparing confirmatory data

Suppose a client's NDA product needs to switch from supplier A's Type I glass vials to supplier B. As the CDMO, the confirmatory data you need to prepare include:

  1. Pre-change data: Release and stability data for the last 3 batches made with supplier A vials
  2. Post-change data: Release and stability data (accelerated plus long-term) for at least 1-3 batches made with supplier B vials
  3. Comparative analysis: Demonstrate no significant difference in CQAs (such as pH, particulates, visible particles, assay, and potency) before and after the change
  4. CCIT data: Container closure integrity testing to confirm seal integrity

Timeline planning tip: Accelerated stability data take at least 3 months, and long-term data take 12-24 months. A CCS supplier change plan should therefore start 1-2 years ahead.

Practical Application: Labeling Compliance

The knock-on effect of labeling changes: If your product labeling states "packaged in Type I glass" and the new CCS component is a different type or grade, a separate labeling supplement is needed in addition to the CCS change supplement. Also, if the new component differs from what the USP monograph specifies, you may need to work with USP to update the monograph.

Implication for CDMOs: when helping a client assess a CCS change, always check product labeling and USP monograph compliance at the same time, so that no additional regulatory submission is missed.

Practice Questions

  1. Your client wants to change the glass vial supplier (no composition change; AR suggested) and the stopper size (CBE-30 suggested) at the same time. What is the correct way to submit? Why can the two supplements of different categories not be submitted separately?
  2. How is the "body of knowledge" applied in practice? Give an example of the difference in data volume needed for a CCS change between a product with an extensive body of knowledge and a newly approved product.
  3. Why must confirmatory data be "comparative"? Is it sufficient to submit only post-change batch data showing compliance with specifications?
  4. How does the principle of tightening vs widening specifications apply to particulate matter limits after a CCS change? Illustrate with specific numbers.

Section Summary

  • The appendix tables are the core tool: They consolidate recommendations from multiple FDA guidances on reporting categories and required data for CCS changes, and are a key reference for CDMOs in daily work
  • Definition of confirmatory data: Comparative batch data from before and after the change, sufficient to show that the CCS change has no adverse effect on product quality
  • Concurrent changes follow the highest-risk category: When several related changes are made together, the cumulative effect must be assessed and the supplement submitted under the category of the highest-risk individual change
  • Knock-on effects of specification changes: If a CCS change also affects finished product specifications, supporting data must be submitted at the same time; tightening a specification generally warrants a lower reporting category than widening it
  • Labeling and compendial compliance: A CCS change may also require a labeling change and a USP monograph update, and the CDMO should help the client assess the full regulatory impact
  • Data volume depends on the body of knowledge: 1-3 batches for NDA/ANDA, and possibly more for BLA depending on product characteristics; applicants may contact FDA for specific recommendations on data volume

IV.A Risk-Based Considerations for Supplement Reporting Categories

Original Text

For CCS and component changes that are the subject of this guidance, FDA intends to consider risk-based approaches using existing tools and capabilities to facilitate providing timely feedback, guidance, and assessment of proposed changes as discussed below.

FDA may expedite the assessment of a supplement or determine that a different reporting category is appropriate, taking into account public health priorities and the applicant's rationale and associated risk assessment and mitigation strategy for the proposed change. Should a different reporting category be determined to be appropriate, FDA will notify the applicant and convey its rationale for the determination.

Consistent with applicable regulations and existing guidance documents related to postapproval CMC changes for NDAs, ANDAs, and BLAs, FDA may consider available information and approaches to mitigate the risk to product quality when determining whether a lower reporting category for supplements is appropriate. Applicants should consider using tools in ICH Q12 (e.g., established conditions, postapproval change management protocols (PACMPs)) to support proposals for reduced reporting categories based on an increased understanding of the risk to product quality when making a change related to the glass vial or stopper.

Before submitting a supplement with a lower reporting category than what is required in existing regulations and recommended in existing guidance, applicants should contact FDA for feedback and concurrence. For Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER)-regulated products, applicants should contact the regulatory project management staff (regulatory project manager or regulatory business process manager) within the appropriate CDER review office responsible for the subject product application. For Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER)-regulated products, applicants should contact the appropriate CBER review office. Additionally, if the CDER- or CBER-regulated product could enter, or is currently in, drug shortage, then the respective applicant should include drugshortages@fda.hhs.gov for products regulated by CDER and cbershortage@fda.hhs.gov for products regulated by CBER on those communications.

Footnote 17: For additional information, see 21 CFR 314.70(b)(4). For FDA policies describing expedited review requests related to prior approval supplements to NDAs and BLAs, see MAPP 5310.3 Rev 1 Requests for Expedited Review of New Drug Application and Biologics License Application Prior Approval Supplements Submitted for Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Changes; for FDA policies describing prioritization of the review related to original ANDAs, amendments, and supplements thereof, see MAPP 5240.3 Rev. 5 Prioritization of the Review of Original ANDAs, Amendments, and Supplements; for FDA policies on drug shortage management, see MAPP 4190.1 Rev. 3 Drug Shortage Management.

Footnote 18: Depending on the regulatory jurisdiction of the product, the applicant can contact the responsible review division within CDER or CBER (as applicable) to gain additional feedback on specific changes, the information needed to support the change, and the filing approach, as needed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Applying ICH Q12 tools to CCS changes:

  • Established Conditions (ECs): As defined in ICH Q12, the process conditions that directly affect product quality and require regulatory reporting. Clearly distinguishing ECs from non-ECs helps focus resources on the changes that are truly critical.
  • PACMPs (Post-Approval Change Management Protocols): Pre-approved change plans that define the assessment methods and acceptance criteria for future changes. Similar in concept to the Comparability Protocol (CP) but broader.

Drug shortage triggers expedited review: If a product is facing or may face a drug shortage, FDA will consider expediting review of the supplement or accepting a lower reporting category. This is an important strategic lever: when a CDMO's client argues for a lower reporting category, supply chain risk and potential shortage can be used as one of the arguments.

Analogy

Downgrading a reporting category on a risk basis is like adjusting a credit card limit: the bank (FDA) decides your credit limit (the strictness of the reporting category) based on your credit history (body of knowledge plus risk assessment). If you have always repaid on time and have a good record (a sound quality system, extensive product knowledge, and thorough risk assessment), the bank may be willing to give you a higher limit (a lower reporting category). But you must talk to the bank first (contact FDA for feedback and concurrence); you cannot decide on a downgrade yourself.

Key Notes

"Contact FDA first" is mandatory: FDA explicitly requires that, before submitting a reporting category lower than the regulation requires or the guidance recommends, you must first contact FDA to obtain feedback and concurrence. This is not an optional step: submitting a lower-category supplement without FDA's concurrence may lead to a refuse-to-file decision or a request to resubmit.

Contact channels for CDER vs CBER:

  • CDER products: contact the Regulatory Project Manager (RPM) or the Regulatory Business Process Manager
  • CBER products: contact the relevant CBER review office
  • Drug shortages: additionally copy drugshortages@fda.hhs.gov (CDER) or cbershortage@fda.hhs.gov (CBER)

Practical Application

CDMO strategy: using ICH Q12 to reduce reporting burden:

Suppose your CDMO fills the same 2R glass vial specification for several clients. If you need to switch to a new glass vial supplier (for example, from Schott to Nipro), the strategy for using ICH Q12 tools is as follows:

  1. Establish Established Conditions: Clearly define which vial attributes are ECs (such as composition, dimensions, and surface treatment) and which are not (such as packaging configuration)
  2. Prepare a risk assessment: Use ICH Q9(R1) tools (such as FMEA) to assess the risk of the supplier switch
  3. Contact FDA: Submit a proposal to the RPM for a lower reporting category, with the risk assessment and mitigation strategy attached
  4. If there is a drug shortage risk: Notify the Drug Shortage Program at the same time to seek expedited review

This strategy is especially suited to dual sourcing scenarios: once the framework is set up in advance, future supplier switches can be implemented faster.

IV.B Comparability Protocols

Original Text

As explained in the guidance for industry Comparability Protocols for Postapproval Changes to the Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Information in an NDA, ANDA, or BLA (October 2022), a comparability protocol (CP) is a comprehensive, prospectively written plan for assessing the effect of a proposed CMC postapproval change(s) on the identity, strength, quality, purity, and potency of a drug product or a biological product. It is a tool for applicants to obtain feedback from FDA on prospective scientific approaches, submitted as a part of an original application or PAS, to facilitate expeditious implementation of postapproval changes. CPs allow FDA to review a description of one or more proposed CMC postapproval changes, supporting information (including any analysis and risk assessment activities), the plan for implementing the change(s), and, if appropriate, the proposed reduced reporting category for the change(s). By delineating the specific approach to be used to evaluate one or more future changes and the rationale for that approach, the applicant can gain the Agency's approval of the plan well in advance of the need to implement the change(s). This process can facilitate a more efficient submission process for the applicant and review process for FDA.

"A CP can then be used for a one-time change(s) or be used repeatedly for a specified type of change over the life cycle of a product."

If the original application or PAS containing the CP is approved, the change usually can be implemented under a lower reporting category than would normally be expected based on recommendations in guidance (e.g., changes otherwise requiring a prior approval supplement could be implemented under a CBE-30). A CP can then be used for a one-time change(s) or be used repeatedly for a specified type of change over the life cycle of a product. A CP can also be submitted to cover an identical change(s) that affects multiple applications (grouped supplements, trans-BLA submissions). A CP is a well-suited tool for making changes to glass vials and stoppers, because a change to one component can be implemented to many products sharing that container or closure, and the protocol could be used multiple times as the need arises for additional changes to accommodate the dynamic supply chain evolution.

Footnote 19: See the guidance for industry Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Changes to an Approved Application: Certain Biological Products (June 2021).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Comparability Protocol (CP): A CP is a pre-written, FDA-approved protocol that describes how a future CCS change will be assessed and tested, and how it will be judged acceptable. The core value of a CP is "approve the plan first, implement quickly later".

Three key features of a CP:

  • Single-use or reusable: A CP can be designed for a single change (such as switching from supplier A to supplier B) or for repeated use (such as "this CP applies to any future glass vial supplier switch")
  • Applicable across products: A CP can cover multiple products that share the same CCS component, through grouped supplements or trans-BLA submissions
  • Lower reporting category: Once a CP is approved, a change that would otherwise require a PAS can be implemented as a CBE-30

Grouped Supplements / Trans-BLA Submissions: When a CCS change affects multiple NDA/BLA products, a grouped supplement can cover several applications at once. This greatly reduces duplicated regulatory submission work.

Analogy

A Comparability Protocol is like a pre-approved travel checklist. Suppose you are an international sales representative and every business trip needs company approval. You can agree a general travel plan with your manager (FDA) in advance: defining the trip conditions, expense limits, and reporting format. Once the manager approves it, for each future trip that meets the conditions you only need to fill in the checklist and attach proof, and you can leave quickly, without applying for full trip approval each time.

For a CCS change: you define in advance "what kind of supplier switch is acceptable", "what tests are needed", and "what the acceptance criteria are". After FDA approves, each future supplier switch only needs to follow the CP and be submitted as a CBE-30 rather than a PAS.

Key Notes

The CP is one of the most powerful regulatory tools for a CDMO:

FDA states explicitly in this guidance that a CP is particularly well suited to CCS changes, for these reasons:

  1. One-to-many: A glass vial change may affect all products that use the same vial type. A CP can cover all of them at once.
  2. Dynamic supply chain: Glass vial suppliers continually change sites and upgrade processes. A reusable CP lets each change be implemented quickly.
  3. Time benefit: PAS review can take 6-12 months. With a CP, the change can be downgraded to a CBE-30 (30 days) or even an Annual Report.

Relationship to the ICH Q12 PACMP: The CP is FDA's specific mechanism, and the PACMP is the conceptual framework of ICH Q12. Their goal is the same: to reduce the regulatory burden of post-approval changes through advance planning. FDA explains how the two correspond in its ICH Q12 implementation guidance.

Formulas and Calculations: CP Return-on-Investment Analysis

CP development cost ≈ NT$500,000-1,000,000 (drafting + internal review + FDA submission)
Cost per PAS submission ≈ NT$300,000-500,000 (data preparation + review fees + cost of waiting)
PAS review waiting time ≈ 6-12 months (the new supplier may not be usable during this period)

Assume the CP covers 5 products and 3 supplier changes over the next 5 years:
  Without a CP: 5 products × 3 changes × NT$400,000 = NT$6,000,000 (PAS cost)
  With a CP: NT$1,000,000 (CP development) + 5 × 3 × NT$50,000 (CBE-30 cost) = NT$1,750,000

Savings: 6,000,000 - 1,750,000 = NT$4,250,000
Time saved: each change shortened from 6-12 months to 30 days

Practical Application

CP strategy planning for CDMOs:

As a clinical-stage CDMO, we recommend that clients start CP planning at the following points:

  • At NDA/BLA submission: Include the CP in the original application. This is the ideal time: FDA approves the CP together with the application, so no additional PAS is needed
  • Early commercialization: Once the product enters commercial production and supply chain needs are foreseen (such as dual sourcing), submit a PAS that includes a CP
  • Before a supply chain crisis: Do not wait until a supplier has a problem. Proactively establishing a CP is part of a supply chain resilience strategy

Suggested CP content (for CCS changes):

  1. Define the scope of change: which types of CCS change the CP covers
  2. Test plan: specifications and methods for CCIT, extractables/leachables, and stability studies
  3. Acceptance criteria: the conditions under which a change is acceptable
  4. Justification of the reporting category: why the downgrade is reasonable
  5. List of applicable products (grouped supplements)

Key Notes: Special Upgrade for Lyophilized Products

Risk escalation for lyophilized products: In Table A, switching between molded and tubing glass is a CBE-30 for ordinary injectables but is upgraded to a PAS for lyophilized products. Implication for CDMOs: if you fill lyophilized products, the regulatory threshold for CCS changes is usually higher than for liquid injectables. When planning CCS dual sourcing, the time and cost of lyophilization process validation must be included in the calculation.

Key Concepts: Method vs Parameters

The dividing line between method changes and parameter changes:

Table E clearly illustrates a key regulatory distinction:

  • Method change = PAS: For example, changing the sterilization method from moist heat to dry heat, or from moist heat to irradiation. This is a fundamental process change.
  • Parameter change = CBE-30: For example, adjusting the temperature or time within the same sterilization method (dry heat). This is a fine adjustment within the same method framework.

This "method vs parameter" logic runs through the whole of Table E and Table F.

Why stopper siliconization needs a PAS: Siliconization changes the chemical properties of the stopper surface, directly affecting the extractables/leachables profile and CCS integrity. This is equivalent to a "material property change" rather than a simple "process parameter adjustment".

Analogy

The difference between method and parameter is like "changing the cooking method" vs "adjusting the heat" in cooking:

  • Method change (PAS): From boiling to deep frying: a completely different cooking method, so the texture and nutritional content of the finished dish will differ
  • Parameter change (CBE-30): Boiling temperature adjusted from 100°C to 95°C: still boiling, just a fine adjustment of conditions

Similarly, a change to the stopper siliconization process is a PAS because it is equivalent to "adding a new layer of sauce to the dish": it changes the surface chemistry, not just the process parameters.

Key Notes

Changes directly relevant to CDMOs: Most of the change types in Table E occur in a CDMO environment:

  • Vial sterilization/depyrogenation: If the CDMO performs vial depyrogenation itself (such as a dry heat tunnel), any method change needs a PAS and a parameter change needs a CBE-30
  • Stopper washing: A change to the CDMO's own stopper washing process needs only a CBE-30
  • Stopper sterilization: A method change (such as from autoclave to gamma irradiation) needs a PAS; a parameter adjustment within the same method needs a CBE-30

These are all changes a CDMO may encounter in daily operations, especially during equipment upgrades or process optimization.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The BLA "supplier control downgrade" is like an outsourced supplier adjusting its internal processes. Suppose your company outsources IT services to a cloud provider. If the provider changes its server brand internally (its "sterilization method change"), you as the customer need to know and assess the impact. But if the provider merely adjusts the servers' operating parameters (a "parameter change") and service quality is unchanged, you may not need to do anything, because this falls within the scope of their service quality assurance.

Key Notes: Integrated Decision Framework for the Six Tables

The CDMO's table-lookup decision flow:

  1. Step 1: Confirm the client's application type → NDA/ANDA (use A/C/E) or BLA (use B/D/F)
  2. Step 2: Confirm the type of change → Properties (A/B), Source/Site (C/D), Manufacturing (E/F)
  3. Step 3: Find the specific change → read the Reporting Category
  4. Step 4: Check Specific Considerations → are there any upgrade/downgrade triggers?
  5. Step 5: Assess whether there are multiple changes → the highest-risk category governs
  6. Step 6: Decide whether to use a CP/ICH Q12 tools → apply for a downgrade (contact FDA first)

Practical Application

Integrated case: comprehensive CCS change assessment:

Your CDMO fills a mAb product (BLA) for a biopharmaceutical company. The company decides to:

  1. Switch to a new stopper supplier (different material) → Table B → PAS
  2. Have the new supplier provide RTU stoppers (previously unprocessed) → Table B → CBE-30 (possibly down to AR)
  3. Because the stoppers are RTU, the CDMO no longer washes stoppers itself → Table F → CBE-30 (possibly down to AR)

Under the cumulative change principle, the overall reporting category = PAS (the highest governs). However, a Comparability Protocol can be used to plan ahead so that similar future changes are downgraded to CBE-30.

Key Concepts: Table E vs Table F Comparison

Key differences in process changes between NDA/ANDA and BLA:

Most notable difference: stopper siliconization: NDA/ANDA requires a PAS, but BLA requires only a CBE-30. This is because the 2021 BLA guidance classifies this kind of supplier-level change more leniently.

The BLA "supplier control" downgrade principle: The Specific Considerations column of Table F repeatedly shows a pattern: "if the change occurs at the supplier (vendor provides pre-xxx and is changing the xxx process)", the category can be lowered to AR. This reflects FDA's trust in the supplier's quality management capability: process adjustments inside the supplier have a more indirect effect on the drug product manufacturer.

Key Notes: Cross-Table Strategic Insights

Five regulatory strategies every CDMO should remember:

  1. Lowest threshold for dual sourcing: Same supplier, different site + same specifications = Annual Report (Table C/D)
  2. BLA is not necessarily stricter: For some changes (such as molded/tubing switching and the siliconization process), BLA is actually more lenient than NDA/ANDA
  3. Supplier control = possible downgrade: Changes that occur at the supplier (vendor-controlled process) usually have a downgrade path
  4. Lyophilized product = systematic upgrade: CCS property changes for lyophilized products are almost always upgraded to PAS
  5. The Comparability Protocol is the strongest lever: A pre-approved CP can lower a PAS to a CBE-30, with very high ROI for multiple products and repeated changes

Link to PIC/S Annex 1 (2022): PIC/S Annex 1 Section 8.17–8.21 requires container closure integrity testing (CCIT) to be performed before first use and periodically. The tables in this guidance repeatedly require CCIT data, which is fully consistent with Annex 1. CDMOs should ensure the CCIT procedures are incorporated into the Contamination Control Strategy (CCS Strategy).

Section Summary

  • The six tables are the CDMO's everyday regulatory tools: Tables A-F cover the three main categories of change for glass vials and stoppers (Properties, Source/Site, Manufacturing/Processing), separately for NDA/ANDA and BLA
  • PAS triggers: Material change, container type change, glass composition change, stopper material change, sterilization method change, siliconization change (NDA/ANDA), molded/tubing switch for lyophilized products
  • CBE-30 triggers: Molded/tubing switch (non-lyophilized NDA/ANDA), stopper dimension change, stopper supply method change, sterilization parameter change, stopper washing process change
  • Annual Report pathways: Same supplier, different site (Table C/D); supplier switch with the same specifications (Table A Footnote 2); molded/tubing switch for BLA (Table B)
  • The unique BLA downgrade mechanism: For supplier-controlled process changes (vendor provides pre-xxx), there are mostly conditional pathways down to AR
  • The cumulative change principle: For multiple concurrent changes, the highest-risk category governs. A Comparability Protocol can be planned in advance and greatly reduces the regulatory burden of future changes
  • Systematic upgrade for lyophilized products: For changes involving glass vial wall thickness/heat transfer properties, lyophilized products are usually upgraded to PAS automatically
  • The central role of CCIT data: Most changes require CCIT data, consistent with the requirements of PIC/S Annex 1 Section 8
