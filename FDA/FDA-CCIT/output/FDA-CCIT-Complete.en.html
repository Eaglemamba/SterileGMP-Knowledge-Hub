I. Purpose and Scope

Original Text

This guidance document provides recommendations to you, manufacturers, for using methods other than sterility testing to confirm container and closure system integrity as a part of the stability protocol for sterile biological products, human and animal drugs, and medical devices. This guidance document finalizes the draft guidance of the same title dated January 1998 (January 28, 1998, 63 Federal Register (FR) 4272).

Manufacturers of drugs and biologics purporting to be sterile must test each batch or lot, as the case may be, to ensure that the product in question conforms to sterility requirements. 21 CFR 211.167(a); 21 CFR 610.12. Such drugs and biologics are also subject to stability testing requirements. 21 CFR 211.166. The stability testing requirements include maintaining a written testing program designed to assess stability characteristics. Manufacturers of medical devices must validate processes, including sterilization for a device purporting to be sterile. 21 CFR 820.75. Stability testing should be part of the design validation of such devices. In vitro diagnostic products for human use are required to be labeled with stability information. 21 CFR 809.10. For products labeled as sterile, we consider sterility to be a stability characteristic.

The purpose of stability testing is to provide evidence on how the quality of a substance or product varies with time under the influence of a variety of environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and light, which enables you to establish or modify recommended storage conditions, retest periods, and shelf life or dating period, as the case may be. This guidance document applies only to the replacement of the sterility test with an appropriate container and closure system integrity test in the stability written testing program (referred to in this guidance as the "stability protocol"), recommending an alternative to sterility testing for supporting the continued capability of containers to maintain sterility. The guidance document does not apply to sterility testing methods for product sterility testing prior to release, as container and closure system integrity tests cannot demonstrate a product's initial sterility.

This guidance document provides information that we recommend you consider when you propose using alternative methods to sterility testing to confirm the integrity of a container and closure system throughout the product's shelf life or dating period. The recommendations in this guidance document apply to both pre- and post-approval stability protocols for sterile biological products, human and animal drugs, including investigational and bulk drugs. For medical devices, the recommendations in this guidance document apply to stability protocols for those devices labeled as sterile.

If you currently perform sterility testing as a stability-indicating test as part of a stability protocol, you may continue to do so. If your product is approved for an alternative to sterility testing as a component of your stability protocol, this document is not intended to recommend additional testing requirements.

FDA's guidance documents, including this guidance, do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead, guidances describe the FDA's current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word should in FDA's guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required.

1 This guidance document was prepared by an intercenter working group with representatives from the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research, Center for Devices and Radiological Health, Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, and the Center for Veterinary Medicine.

2 "Dating period" being the term used for biologics, as defined at 21 CFR 600.3(l), and "shelf life" being the term used for other drugs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Container and Closure System Integrity: The ability of a system made up of a container and closure to prevent microbial ingress and maintain the sterility of the product. This is the core concept of CCI (Container Closure Integrity), which was later fully systematized in the USP <1207> series.

Stability Protocol: Under 21 CFR 211.166, a manufacturer must maintain a written stability testing program. This guidance addresses only the sterility testing portion of that program and recommends replacing it with CCIT.

Sterility as a Stability Characteristic: FDA explicitly positions sterility as one of the stability characteristics, meaning the stability program must include measures that confirm the product remains sterile throughout its shelf life.

Analogy

Imagine you store food in a vacuum-sealed bag in the refrigerator. Stability testing is like periodically checking whether the food has spoiled (temperature, appearance, smell), and "sterility as a stability characteristic" means you must also periodically confirm that the bag is not torn or leaking. Rather than opening the bag every time to take a sample for culture (the sterility test, which destroys the seal once the bag is opened), it makes more sense to check the integrity of the bag with an instrument (CCIT).

Key Notes

Key limitation: not applicable to release sterility testing. The Guidance explicitly states that CCIT cannot replace the sterility test performed before product release (Release Sterility Testing, USP <71>). The reason is that CCIT can only demonstrate that "the container does not leak"; it cannot demonstrate that "the product itself is sterile". Release sterility testing verifies whether the process itself has successfully produced a sterile product.

Broad scope of application: It covers biological products, human drugs, animal drugs and medical devices, in both pre-approval and post-approval stability programs, including investigational drugs at the IND (Investigational New Drug) stage.

Practical Application

CDMO stability program design: For a sterile fill-finish CDMO, this guidance means that the sterility test originally required at each time point of the stability program (e.g., 3M, 6M, 9M, 12M, 18M, 24M, 36M) can be replaced with CCIT. This brings multiple benefits:

  • Saves samples: The sterility test is destructive and consumes 20 or more units each time; if a non-destructive CCIT method is used, the tested samples can continue to be used for other stability attributes
  • Shortens timelines: The sterility test requires an incubation period of at least 14 days; CCIT can be completed within minutes to hours
  • Reduces false-positive risk: False positives in sterility testing (from operator contamination) have long been an industry pain point; CCIT eliminates this variable

II. Introduction

Original Text

Products labeled as sterile are expected to be free from viable microbial contamination throughout the product's entire shelf life or dating period. For products labeled as sterile, we consider sterility to be a stability characteristic. As a result, the stability protocol should include confirmation of continuing sterility throughout the product's shelf life or dating period. The minimum sterility testing generally performed as a component of the stability protocol for sterile products is at the initial time point (release) and final testing interval (i.e., expiration). Additional testing is often performed at appropriate intervals within this time period (e.g., annually). However, as discussed below sterility tests for the purpose of demonstrating continuing sterility have limitations, with respect to the method's reliability, accuracy, and the conclusions that may be derived from the results. Because of the limitations of sterility tests described below, sterility tests are not recommended as a component of a stability program for confirming the continued sterility throughout a product's shelf life or dating period. Alternative methods may be more reliable in confirming the integrity of the container and closure system as a component of the stability protocol for sterile products.

This guidance document does not suggest specific test methods and acceptance criteria (except for references to USP methods), nor does it provide comprehensive lists of tests. You should determine these details based on good scientific principles for each specific container and closure system taking into consideration particular product formulations and, where applicable, routes of administration.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Continuing Sterility: The product must remain sterile for the entire period from release to the end of shelf life. This is not a one-time demonstration but an ongoing assurance. The sterility-related testing in the stability program exists to provide this assurance.

Limitations of the Sterility Test: Here FDA previews what Section IV will explain in detail: the sterility test has fundamental limitations in reliability, accuracy and the conclusions that can be drawn from it. This is the scientific basis for promoting CCIT as a replacement.

Analogy

The traditional sterility test in a stability program is like "opening the refrigerator door from time to time to check whether the food has gone bad": every time the door is opened, outside bacteria may get in (false positive), and you can only see the condition at the moment the door is open. CCIT is like installing a temperature sensor and a seal monitor on the refrigerator door, so you can confirm that the seal is intact without opening it.

Key Notes

Baseline testing frequency: FDA notes that the minimum requirement for sterility testing in a stability program is to test at the "initial time point (release)" and the "final testing interval (expiry)", with testing typically once a year in between. This sets the minimum frequency baseline that should be maintained once CCIT is used as the replacement.

No specific method prescribed: FDA deliberately does not specify a particular CCIT method or acceptance criteria, leaving this responsibility to the manufacturer to decide based on scientific principles. This is consistent with the philosophy of PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) Section 8.21: "container closure integrity should be confirmed using a validated and appropriately sensitive method" **(PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 8.21)**.

Role of USP <1207>: When FDA issued this Guidance in 2008, USP <1207> was still under development (formally published in 2016). Today USP <1207> is the main technical reference for selecting and validating CCIT methods, and it classifies container closure integrity test methods into Deterministic Methods and Probabilistic Methods.

III. Definitions

Original Text

The definitions presented here are for the purposes of this guidance only.

A container and closure system refers to the entirety of packaging components that together contain and protect the product.

A packaging component means any single part of a container and closure system. Typical components are containers (e.g., ampules, vials, bottles), container liners, closures (e.g., screw caps, stoppers), closure liners, stopper overseals, container inner seals, administration ports (e.g., on large-volume parenterals), overwraps, administration accessories, and container labels.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Container and Closure System: More than just "a bottle plus a cap"; it is the sum of all packaging components. FDA uses the phrase "entirety of packaging components" to stress that CCI must consider the system as a whole rather than a single component.

Packaging Component: Any single part of the container and closure system. FDA lists a complete set of examples:

  • Containers: Ampules, Vials, Bottles
  • Closures: Screw Caps, Stoppers
  • Ancillary components: Container Liners, Closure Liners, Stopper Overseals (aluminum seals/flip-off caps), Administration Ports, Overwraps, Container Labels

Analogy

A container and closure system is like a "restaurant's food storage system": not just the bowl and the lid, but also the plastic wrap, sealing strips, outer packaging box and every other component that protects the food. You would not check only whether the bowl is cracked and ignore holes in the plastic wrap. Likewise, CCIT must evaluate the integrity of the entire system, not just a single component.

Key Notes

Practical significance of the definition: This definition directly affects the choice of CCIT method. For example:

  • Vial system: Consists of a Glass Vial + Rubber Stopper + Aluminum Crimp Seal. The CCIT method must be able to detect leakage at the interfaces among all three components
  • Pre-filled Syringe: Consists of a Glass Barrel + Plunger Stopper + Tip Cap/Needle Shield. Every joint interface is a potential leak path
  • Bag system: Consists of a Film Body + Port + Overwrap. The welds and the port joints are the critical test points

PDA Technical Report No. 27 (Pharmaceutical Package Integrity) provides more detailed technical guidance on CCI test methods for various container systems **(PDA TR27)**.

Practical Application

CDMO container closure system inventory management: As a CDMO, you may use many different container closure systems for different clients. It is recommended to build a complete container closure system matrix that includes:

  • The list of container closure system components used for each product
  • The validated CCIT method corresponding to each system
  • Test parameters and acceptance criteria
  • An assessment of the applicability of a Bracketing Matrix: whether products with the same container type can share validation data (see Section VII and ICH Q1D)

Section Summary

  • Core position: FDA recommends replacing the sterility test with CCIT in the stability program, because the sterility test has fundamental limitations in reliability, accuracy and the conclusions that can be drawn from it
  • Scope: Covers pre-approval and post-approval stability programs for biological products, human and animal drugs and medical devices, but does not apply to release sterility testing
  • Regulatory basis: 21 CFR 211.166 (stability testing), 21 CFR 211.167 (release testing) and 21 CFR 610.12 (sterility testing of biological products) form the regulatory framework
  • Definition clarified: The Container and Closure System is the whole of all packaging components, and CCIT must evaluate the entire system rather than a single component
  • Method flexibility: FDA does not specify a particular method; manufacturers choose the CCIT method suited to their container system based on scientific principles (the USP <1207> classification framework can now be used as a reference)
  • Takeaway for CDMOs: Replacing the sterility test with CCIT saves stability samples, shortens testing timelines and reduces false-positive risk, making it an important opportunity to optimize the stability program

IV. Background

Original Text

Sterility tests have long been used to verify that products maintain their sterility throughout the product's entire shelf life or dating period. However, sterility testing has scientific and practical limitations, which are well known. Some of these are:

  1. Sterility tests will only detect viable microorganisms present at the time of the test;
  2. Viable organisms present at the time of the test can only be detected if they are capable of growth in the specified culture media;
  3. Sterility tests may be subject to potential interference due to adventitious microbial contamination introduced at the time of testing, resulting in false positive readings; and
  4. Sterility tests are always destructive of the samples tested and do not offer the opportunity to reexamine the same samples in the event of either positive or negative findings.

Some Centers of FDA have sought to address these shortcomings and communicated the information to you in the guidance document: "Guidance for Industry for the Submission Documentation for Sterilization Process Validation in Applications for Human and Veterinary Drug Products" (December 3, 1993, 58 FR 63996). Section V. A., page 16 of the guidance states:

"The ability of the container-closure system to maintain the integrity of its microbial barrier, and, hence, the sterility of a drug product throughout its shelf life, should be demonstrated. [...] As previously stated, sterility testing at the initial time point is not considered sufficient to demonstrate the microbial integrity of a container-closure system. [...]."

In the Federal Register of July 10, 1996 (61 FR 36466), we published a notice regarding the final guideline entitled "Quality of Biotechnological Products: Stability Testing of Biotechnological/Biological Products", prepared under the auspices of the International Conference on Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH Final Guideline). The ICH Final Guideline is intended to provide guidance to applicants regarding the type of stability studies that should be provided in support of marketing applications for biotechnological/biological products.

3 For veterinary products, generally, see: VICH GL17, "Stability Testing of New Biotechnological/Biological Veterinary Medicinal Products," 66 FR 19177, April 13, 2001, and VICH GL3(R), "Stability Testing of New Veterinary Drug Substances and Medicinal Products (Revision)," 71 FR 19525, April 14, 2006.

4 The ICH Final Guideline is intended to supplement the tripartite ICH guideline entitled, "Stability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products," the notice for which is published in the Federal Register of September 22, 1994 (59 FR 48754). A revision of the September 22, 1994, ICH guideline with the same title (Q1A (R)) (66 FR 56332, November 7, 2001) was issued to add information to certain sections and to provide clarification to other sections. A second revision of the guideline, "Guidance for Industry Q1A (R2) Stability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products," (68 FR 65717, November 21, 2003) was issued to harmonize intermediate storage conditions for zones I and II with long-term storage conditions for zones III and IV.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four limitations of the sterility test:

  1. Point-in-time: It can only detect viable microorganisms present "at the moment of testing". If a container has a micro-leak at 12 months but bacteria have not yet entered, the sterility test may be negative, yet the product may already be contaminated at 18 months
  2. Media Selectivity: It can only detect microorganisms that can grow in the specified media. USP <71> uses two media, FTM and TSB, but some extremophiles or strict anaerobes may not be detected
  3. False Positive: Adventitious contamination introduced during handling can cause a false positive and trigger an expensive investigation (OOS Investigation)
  4. Destructive: The test sample is consumed, so the same sample cannot be retested or used for subsequent testing

Analogy

The limitations of the sterility test are like "checking whether a house is burglar-proof by opening its windows":

  • You can only see whether a burglar is in the house "right now" (point-in-time limitation)
  • You can only recognize the burglars you know about (media selectivity)
  • The act of opening the window may itself let a burglar in (false positive)
  • Every inspection requires removing a window that cannot be put back (destructive)

By contrast, CCIT is like scanning the doors and windows from the outside with an infrared sensor to look for gaps: you can judge the integrity of the defenses without damaging any structure.

Key Notes

Historical context: As early as the 1993 Sterilization Process Validation Guidance, FDA stated that "sterility testing at the initial time point alone is not sufficient to demonstrate the microbial integrity of the container closure system". It took FDA 15 years, from 1993 to 2008, to formally issue this CCIT Guidance, reflecting the gradual build-up of scientific consensus.

ICH stability framework: The ICH guideline cited in this paragraph later evolved into ICH Q1A(R2) "Stability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products" **(ICH Q1A(R2), 2003)** and ICH Q5C "Stability Testing of Biotechnological/Biological Products". These ICH guidelines established the international framework for stability testing, and this FDA CCIT Guidance supplements that framework.

Practical Application

The cost of a false positive, a CDMO pain point: A single false-positive sterility test can trigger the following chain reaction:

  • Start of an OOS investigation (usually 15-30 working days)
  • The batch may need to be quarantined pending the investigation conclusion
  • If the investigation cannot be closed within 30 days, the client's product supply may be affected
  • Investigation labor cost (multiple departments involved: QA, QC, Production)
  • Reduced client confidence, affecting the ongoing business relationship

Replacing the sterility test in the stability program with CCIT can greatly reduce this risk.

V. Alternatives

Original Text

Alternatives to sterility testing as part of the stability protocol, such as replacing the sterility test with container and closure system integrity testing, might include any properly validated physical or chemical container and closure system integrity test (e.g., bubble tests, pressure/vacuum decay, trace gas permeation/leak tests, dye penetration tests, seal force or electrical conductivity and capacitance tests, etc.), or microbiological container and closure system integrity tests (e.g., microbial challenge or immersion tests). Such tests may be more useful than sterility testing in demonstrating the potential for product contamination over the product's shelf life or dating period. The advantages of using such container and closure system integrity tests in lieu of sterility tests in the stability protocol for sterile products include:

  1. Such alternate methods may detect a breach of the container and/or closure system prior to product contamination;
  2. Some of the alternate methods used to evaluate container and closure integrity can conserve samples that may be used for other stability tests;
  3. Alternative test methods may require less time than sterility test methods which require at least seven days incubation; and
  4. The potential for false positive results may be reduced with some alternative test methods when compared to sterility tests.

Occasionally, applicants have proposed the use of a preservative effectiveness test in lieu of the appropriate sterility test for products containing antimicrobial preservatives. However, these tests only measure the effectiveness of preservatives against a panel of five different test organisms. This method cannot confirm product sterility since it does not confirm the presence or absence of contamination, but rather only demonstrates the microbiological effectiveness of the preservative system against the five test organisms in question. For these reasons, preservative effectiveness tests are not acceptable alternative tests for monitoring container and closure system integrity or for demonstrating maintenance of sterility. However, such tests are appropriate to perform as part of the stability protocol on multi-dose containers at the end of the product's shelf life or dating period, to verify antimicrobial preservative effectiveness and preservative content.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Classification of CCIT methods: FDA divides the alternative methods into two categories, which correspond to the later classification framework of USP <1207>:

1. Physical/Chemical Methods, corresponding to the Deterministic Methods of USP <1207>:

  • Bubble Test: The container is immersed in liquid and pressure is applied; the test observes whether bubbles are generated
  • Pressure/Vacuum Decay: Measures the rate of pressure change inside the container; a deviation indicates a leak
  • Trace Gas Permeation/Leak Test: Uses a tracer gas such as helium and detects leaks with a mass spectrometer. Extremely sensitive
  • Dye Penetration Test: The container is immersed in a dye solution and checked for dye ingress. This is a probabilistic method
  • Seal Force Test: Measures the gripping/compression force of the closure
  • Electrical Conductivity/Capacitance: Uses electrical measurements to detect seal defects

2. Microbiological Methods, corresponding to the Probabilistic Methods of USP <1207>:

  • Microbial Challenge/Immersion Test: The container is immersed in a liquid containing microorganisms, and the test observes whether microorganisms have entered

Analogy

The different CCIT methods are like different ways of "checking a tire for air leaks":

  • Bubble Test: Put the tire in water and look for bubbles: simple and intuitive, but with limited sensitivity
  • Pressure Decay: Use a pressure gauge to monitor whether the tire pressure drops over time: quantitative and accurate
  • Helium Leak Test: Fill with a special tracer gas and detect it with a precision instrument: can find extremely small leaks
  • Dye Penetration: Coat the outside of the tire with a colored liquid and see whether it seeps in: visual but time-consuming
  • Microbial Challenge: Leave the tire in muddy water for a while and see whether any gets inside: closest to real-world risk but inefficient

Key Notes

The four advantages of CCIT (corresponding to the four limitations of the sterility test):

  1. Proactive: Container leaks can be detected before microorganisms enter, shifting from "discovering contamination reactively" to "discovering risk proactively"
  2. Non-destructive: Some methods (such as Vacuum Decay and Helium Leak) do not damage the sample, so it can continue to be used for other stability attributes after testing
  3. Rapid: Completed within minutes to hours, compared with at least 7 days for the sterility test (USP <71> specifies 14 days for the bacterial media)
  4. Low False Positive: Physical methods do not involve handling microorganisms, so the false-positive risk is greatly reduced

The Preservative Effectiveness Test (PET) cannot replace CCIT: FDA explicitly rejected the proposal to replace the sterility test with PET (USP <51>). PET only tests the efficacy of the preservative against 5 specific microorganisms; it cannot confirm that the product is sterile or evaluate container closure integrity. However, PET should still be included in the stability program for multi-dose containers.

Key Notes: Modern Classification under USP <1207>

When this Guidance was issued in 2008, USP <1207> had not yet been established. Today USP <1207> systematizes CCI test methods as follows:

  • Deterministic Methods: Based on physical/chemical principles, with deterministic results (pass/fail). They include Helium Leak Test, Vacuum Decay, High-Voltage Leak Detection (HVLD), Laser-based Headspace Analysis and others **(USP <1207.1>)**
  • Probabilistic Methods: Based on statistical probability, with statistical uncertainty in the results. They include Dye Ingress and Microbial Immersion/Aerosol Challenge **(USP <1207.2>)**

PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) explicitly requires that Deterministic Methods be used preferentially **(PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 8.21)**. For a CDMO, it is advisable to implement deterministic methods first, in order to meet the latest regulatory expectations.

VI. Implementation

Original Text

When seeking to implement container and closure system integrity testing as an alternative to sterility testing as a component of the stability protocol for sterile products, we recommend that you consider the following:

  1. A container and closure system integrity test may replace sterility testing in a stability program at time points other than the product sterility test prior to release;
  2. Container and closure system integrity tests do not replace sterility testing methods for product sterility testing prior to release;
  3. Any validated container and closure system integrity test method should be acceptable provided the method uses analytical detection techniques appropriate to the method and is compatible with the specific product being tested. A test method is adequately validated if it has been proven through scientifically accepted studies to be capable of detecting a breach in container and closure system integrity; and
  4. An appropriate container and closure system integrity test should be conducted annually and at expiration, or as otherwise required by applicable regulations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Analysis of the four key points of Implementation:

1. Time point restriction: CCIT can replace the sterility test at the post-release time points of the stability program (e.g., 3M, 6M, 12M, 24M, 36M), but the sterility test at the release time point (T=0) cannot be replaced. This is because the release sterility test verifies "whether the process has successfully produced a sterile product", a question CCIT cannot answer.

2. Release sterility testing cannot be replaced: This core limitation bears repeating. The USP <71> sterility test remains the statutory requirement for release.

3. Validation requirements: The CCIT method must be validated, and the validation must demonstrate that the method can detect breaches in container closure integrity. The standard of "adequately validated" is met by demonstration through scientifically accepted studies.

4. Testing frequency: At least once a year and at expiry. This frequency matches the time points of most stability programs (usually 3M, 6M, 9M, 12M, 18M, 24M, 36M).

Analogy

Imagine you run a warehousing company that stores valuable goods for clients. Checking the gas before leaving the premises (the release sterility test) is mandatory: you must confirm the goods are intact at handover. But for the periodic patrols afterwards (the stability program), you may choose to use surveillance cameras (CCIT) instead of opening the door and looking every time (the sterility test). The cameras must first be calibrated and tested (validation), and the patrol frequency is at least once a year, plus when the contract ends (expiry).

Key Notes

Validation points, linking to USP <1207>:

  • Method Suitability: Confirm that the selected CCIT method is compatible with the target container closure system and is not affected by interference from the product formulation **(USP <1207.1>)**
  • Limit of Detection: Determine the smallest leak size the method can detect. For deterministic methods this is usually expressed as a Leak Rate (e.g., atm·cc/sec) or as the Maximum Allowable Leakage Limit (MALL)
  • Positive Controls: Use samples with known defects (e.g., laser-drilled vials) to verify the detection capability of the method
  • Product Compatibility: Confirm that product characteristics (e.g., viscosity, surface tension, dissolved gas content) do not affect the test results

Enhanced requirements in PIC/S Annex 1 (2022): PIC/S Annex 1 Section 8.21 further requires that CCI test methods have "appropriate sensitivity" and that deterministic methods be used preferentially. This is more specific than the requirements of this 2008 FDA Guidance **(PIC/S Annex 1 2022, Section 8.21)**.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Regulatory filing routes:

  • Human Drugs, CBE Supplement (§ 314.70): A "Changes Being Effected" (CBE) Supplement is a type of filing that allows a change to be implemented without prior FDA approval. Implementation can begin after submission, although FDA reserves the right to require modifications after review. This reflects FDA's view of the CCIT replacement as a low-risk change
  • Biological Products, CBE Supplement (§ 601.12): A similar CBE route applies to biological products
  • Medical Devices, PMA Supplement (§ 814.39): Medical devices must submit a PMA Supplement, with a stricter review standard

Bracketing Matrix: See ICH Q1D "Bracketing and Matrixing Designs for Stability Testing" **(ICH Q1D, 2003)**. If multiple products use the same container closure system, a bracketing strategy can be used to select representative combinations for validation, without repeating the full validation for every product.

Media-filled Containers: Using culture media instead of the actual product for CCIT validation avoids interference from product components (such as preservatives) with the test results.

Analogy

Efficiency of the Bracketing Matrix: Consider a shoe factory that wants to test the waterproofing of its shoes. If you have 10 different styles that all use the same Gore-Tex waterproof layer and the same stitching technique, you do not need to soak all 10 styles in water for 48 hours. It is enough to test the largest and smallest sizes and the most complex and simplest styles (extreme conditions) to represent the whole. This is the concept of Bracketing: a scientifically designed subset represents the whole.

Key Notes

Key rules for sample management:

  • Reusable: Samples that have passed CCIT can continue to be used for other stability attributes at the same test time point (e.g., assay, pH, appearance), provided that the CCIT method is non-destructive and does not alter the sample
  • Not usable across time points: Samples tested by CCIT at the 12-month time point must not be kept until 24 months and then used for other stability tests. This rule ensures that the samples for each time point are taken directly from the original stability storage conditions
  • Statistically appropriate sample size: The number of samples must be "statistically appropriate", but FDA does not specify a number. In practice, the recommendations of USP <1207> and PDA TR27 are usually followed

What the supplement should include:

  1. A description of the CCIT method and how it evaluates microbial integrity
  2. Method validation data (for the specific container closure system or product type)
  3. If Media-filled Containers are used, supporting data
  4. If a Bracketing Matrix is used, the scientific rationale for the bracketing design

Formula

Efficiency calculation for a bracketing strategy:

Full validation plan: 4 products × 3 batches × 30 samples = 360 units
Bracketing plan: 2 products (6R + 20R) × 3 batches × 30 samples = 180 units

Savings ratio = (360 - 180) / 360 = 50%
Savings = validation time + sample cost + analytical labor

(The actual number of samples should be adjusted according to the statistical design and FDA expectations)

Section Summary

  • Four limitations of the sterility test: Point-in-time, media selectivity, false-positive risk and destructiveness. These scientific limitations are the fundamental reason for the CCIT replacement
  • CCIT method classification: Physical/chemical methods (Bubble Test, Pressure/Vacuum Decay, Trace Gas, Dye Penetration, Seal Force, Electrical) and microbiological methods (Microbial Challenge), corresponding to the Deterministic vs. Probabilistic classification of USP <1207>
  • Four advantages of CCIT: Proactive (detection before contamination), non-destructive (saves samples), rapid (minutes), and low false-positive rate
  • PET cannot replace CCIT: The Preservative Effectiveness Test only tests preservative efficacy and cannot confirm sterility or container integrity
  • Implementation limits: CCIT cannot replace the release sterility test; the method must be validated; testing at least annually and at expiry
  • Filing routes: Human Drugs and Biologics use a CBE Supplement; Medical Devices use a PMA Supplement
  • Efficiency tools: The Bracketing Matrix (ICH Q1D) can greatly reduce the validation workload for multiple products; Media-filled Containers can resolve product interference issues
  • Sample management: Samples can be used for other tests at the same time point after non-destructive CCIT, but must not be reused across time points
  • Link to modern standards: USP <1207> (2016) and PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) have further systematized the classification of CCI methods and require preferential use of deterministic methods
