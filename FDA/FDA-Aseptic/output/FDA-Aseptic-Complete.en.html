Section I — Introduction

Original Text

This guidance is intended to help manufacturers meet current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) regulations under 21 CFR parts 210 and 211 when manufacturing sterile drug and biological products using aseptic processing. This document replaces the 1987 Aseptic Processing Guideline.

For NDA, ANDA, and BLA products, this guidance should be read in conjunction with the Guideline for the Submission of Documentation for Sterilization Process Validation.

FDA's guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead, guidances describe the Agency's current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word "should" in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required.

CFR text boxes are included in this guidance to aid readers in identifying applicable regulatory requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Is This Guidance?

This document is a formal guidance issued by FDA in 2004, replacing the earlier 1987 aseptic processing guideline. Its core purpose is to help manufacturers understand FDA's current expectations for aseptic processing and how they map to the specific requirements of the CGMP (current good manufacturing practice) regulations (21 CFR parts 210 and 211).

For a CDMO, this is a foundational document that must be thoroughly understood when accepting contracts to manufacture sterile products. Whether the client's product is a brand-name drug (NDA), a generic (ANDA) or a biologic (BLA), this guidance is the common language for audits and technical assessments.

Analogy: "Should" vs. a Mandatory Requirement - Traffic Lights vs. a Suggested Route

Imagine driving somewhere: a red light is a legal requirement, and running it gets you fined. The "suggested route" from your navigation app is not mandatory, but if you deviate and something goes wrong, you have to explain yourself.

The word "should" in FDA guidance works like that suggested route. It is not technically mandatory, but if you choose not to follow it, you must be able to show an alternative approach of equivalent effect during an inspection. Otherwise FDA is likely to cite it as a deficiency. In other words, for an experienced CDMO, "should" is in practice almost the same as "must".

Note: What the CFR Text Boxes Mean

The CFR text boxes (boxed excerpts of regulatory text) embedded in this guidance are a deliberate reminder device. When you see a CFR text box, the content that follows has moved from "recommendation" to a mandatory 21 CFR requirement with the force of law. When reading this guidance, be sure to distinguish between ordinary paragraphs (advisory) and CFR text boxes (mandatory).

Section II-A — Background: Regulatory Framework

Original Text

A. Regulatory Framework

This guidance pertains to CGMP regulations under 21 CFR parts 210 and 211 for the manufacture of sterile drug and biological products using aseptic processing.

Supplementary requirements for biological products are found in 21 CFR parts 600–680.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Regulatory Hierarchy

The United States regulates sterile drug products through a "dual-track" regulatory framework:

  • 21 CFR parts 210 & 211: the foundational CGMP requirements for all drug products, covering general requirements such as facilities, equipment qualification, process controls and quality control.
  • 21 CFR parts 600-680: additional requirements specific to biological products (such as vaccines, blood products and gene therapies), overseen by CBER.

Conventional small-molecule sterile injectables are mainly subject to parts 210/211, whereas biologics (such as mAb injectables) must comply with both sets of regulations. As a CDMO, when accepting projects from different types of clients, you must confirm which regulatory scope applies in order to plan your GMP strategy correctly.

Analogy: The Regulatory Hierarchy Is Like Building Codes

21 CFR 210/211 is like the city's general "building code" that every building must follow, while 21 CFR 600-680 is like the "special supplementary code" for hospitals or nuclear plants, which demands higher specifications on top of the general standard. If your CDMO takes on both conventional injectables and biologics, it is like complying with two building codes in the same facility, so the design and management are naturally more complex.

Note: The CDER vs. CBER Division of Oversight

Within FDA, CDER (Center for Drug Evaluation and Research) regulates most drug products (including biosimilars), while CBER (Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research) regulates vaccines, blood products and some cell and gene therapy products. When planning inspection readiness and documentation strategy, a CDMO needs to confirm which center is responsible for the client's product.

Section II-B — Background: Technical Framework

Original Text

B. Technical Framework

Terminal sterilization involves filling and sealing product containers under high-quality environmental conditions. Products are not initially sterile, but the final sealed container undergoes a sterilization process (e.g., heat or irradiation). High assurance of sterility is achieved by thoroughly validating the sterilization process parameters and the absence of significant variation in the manufacturing process. This approach offers a high level of sterility assurance, limiting the possibility of error.

Aseptic processing involves separate sterilization of the drug product, container, and closure, followed by their aseptic assembly. Because the product cannot be terminally sterilized in its final container, each of the components and the dosage form must be individually sterilized and protected from contamination throughout the entire filling process.

For example, the glass containers may be subjected to dry heat; the rubber closures to moist heat (autoclaving); and liquid dosage forms to filtration through a sterilizing-grade filter. Each of these processes requires validation and stringent control to provide an extremely high-quality environment.

Any manual or mechanical manipulation of sterilized items prior to or during aseptic assembly poses a contamination risk. Unlike terminal sterilization, aseptic processing involves considerably more variables that must be controlled.

A significant proportion of all recalls attributable to lack of sterility assurance during the period 1980–2000 involved products produced by aseptic processing.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: An Intuitive Picture of the Two Sterilization Strategies

Terminal sterilization is like a surgeon who, after finishing an operation, puts the whole pack of instruments into an autoclave. No cleanroom is needed for the handling itself, because the whole pack is sterilized by heat at the end and the risk is eliminated by that single step.

Aseptic processing is like a surgeon who sterilizes each instrument individually before the operation and then carefully assembles them in an ultra-clean operating room. Every movement, every person and every opening of a door is a potential source of contamination, so the whole process demands extreme environmental control and operational discipline.

Which approach is safer? Terminal sterilization, of course. That is why FDA states explicitly: use aseptic processing only when terminal sterilization is not feasible.

Core Concept: Three Separate Sterilization Paths in Aseptic Processing

Because the final container cannot be sterilized, each component must follow its own sterilization path:

  • Glass containers: dry heat sterilization in a high-temperature oven, which also removes pyrogens (depyrogenation)
  • Rubber closures: moist heat sterilization (autoclaving) with pressurized steam, ensuring a high sterility assurance level
  • Liquid dosage forms: sterilizing filtration through a 0.22 µm sterilizing-grade filter to remove microorganisms

The three paths converge in the aseptic fill room, where final assembly takes place in an ISO 5 (grade A/B) environment. Each path must be validated independently, and the failure of any one step threatens sterility assurance.

Important Warning: Lessons from Recalls in 1980-2000

FDA specifically points out that, among products recalled between 1980 and 2000 because of inadequate sterility assurance, the great majority were produced by aseptic processing. This historical data is no coincidence; it reflects the inherent complexity and high risk of aseptic processing.

For a CDMO, this means the GMP system for aseptic processing must be built on preventive design rather than after-the-fact remediation. A recall is not only a loss for the client, but also a major blow to the CDMO's reputation.

CDMO Business Application

When a CDMO business team presents its aseptic manufacturing capabilities to clients, the most frequently asked question is: "Can you do terminal sterilization?" There is a sound commercial logic behind this question: clients know that terminal sterilization offers higher sterility assurance, lower recall risk and less regulatory inspection pressure.

If a CDMO has only aseptic processing capability, it must be able to explain clearly: (1) why the product is not suitable for terminal sterilization (for example, thermal stability of a protein); and (2) how its aseptic process compensates for this inherent risk through environmental control, process design and media fill (aseptic process simulation). This is a key persuasion point in technical selling.

Section III — Scope

Original Text

This guidance covers selected issues in aseptic processing, but does not address every aspect of the subject. It focuses primarily on finished drug product CGMP issues. This guidance updates the 1987 guideline with respect to:

  • Personnel qualification
  • Cleanroom design
  • Process design
  • Quality control
  • Environmental monitoring
  • Production records review

The use of isolators for aseptic processing is also discussed. This guidance does not address terminal sterilization of drug products.

A key principle underlying this guidance is that sterile drugs should be manufactured using aseptic processing only when terminal sterilization is not feasible.

Some final packaging may afford unique advantages not achievable with terminal sterilization (e.g., some dual-chamber syringes); in such cases, the manufacturer can explore adjunct processing steps that may augment sterility assurance of the product. A list of references is included.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Six Updated Topic Areas of This Guidance

Compared with the 1987 edition, this guidance systematically updates the following six areas, each of which corresponds to an important module of day-to-day CDMO GMP management:

  • Personnel Qualification: gowning evaluation, aseptic technique training, behavioral controls
  • Cleanroom Design: HVAC systems, airflow patterns, pressure differentials, cleanroom classification
  • Process Design: fill process design, personnel flow, equipment layout risk assessment
  • Quality Control: sterility testing, media fill (process simulation), filter integrity
  • Environmental Monitoring: particle counts, microbiological sampling, trend analysis
  • Production Records Review: batch record completeness, investigation of abnormal events

These six topics form the core structure of the sections that follow, and the rest of the learning revolves around them.

Key Principle: Aseptic Processing Is a "Last Resort"

FDA emphasizes explicitly: use aseptic processing only when terminal sterilization is not feasible. This is not just a technical recommendation; it is a design philosophy that runs through the entire guidance.

In practice, for many protein drugs, liposomes and mRNA products, high-temperature sterilization would destroy the active ingredient, so terminal sterilization is genuinely not feasible. However, FDA requires manufacturers to evaluate and document in advance why terminal sterilization cannot be used, and not to adopt aseptic processing without that evaluation. This evaluation is a required part of a CDMO's technical proposal documentation.

Analogy: The Special Status of Dual-Chamber Syringes

Dual-chamber syringes are an interesting special case. Like a sandwich cookie, two components are stored separately and mixed only before use. This design makes terminal sterilization infeasible by the very structure of the package, because the components in the two chambers may tolerate sterilization conditions differently.

For such special packaging, FDA does not prohibit it outright, but expects manufacturers to explore adjunct processing steps. For example, components that can tolerate sterilization should still be terminally sterilized wherever possible, to strengthen the overall sterility assurance level. This reflects FDA's risk-based regulatory thinking.

The Emergence of Isolators: A New Generation of Aseptic Barrier Technology

In the 1987 guideline, isolator technology was not yet mature. The 2004 version specifically brings the isolator into the discussion, because it provides a higher level of separation between personnel and the environment than a conventional cleanroom: the operator stays entirely outside the barrier and works through glove ports (a glove box), greatly reducing the risk of contamination introduced by personnel.

Isolator technology has now become an important tool for improving sterility assurance in the industry, and later sections discuss its design and qualification requirements in dedicated sections.

CDMO Business Application

When a CDMO undergoes a technical assessment by a new client, understanding the scope statement of this guidance has important business implications:

(1) Capability assessment mapping: The six updated topics of this guidance are exactly the six areas FDA pays most attention to during inspections, and they form the core checklist for demonstrating CDMO capability. In business development, documented evidence of capability in these six areas is the key to winning client trust.

(2) Responsibility for the feasibility assessment: The principle that aseptic processing is used only when terminal sterilization is not feasible means that, before accepting a project, the CDMO needs to help the client document and confirm this assessment. If FDA finds at a later inspection that the assessment is missing, the sponsor and the contract manufacturer may both face liability risk.

(3) Isolator investment decision: For a CDMO considering an upgrade of its aseptic manufacturing facility, introducing isolator technology is not only a technical matter but also a differentiation strategy, since it can attract high-end clients with the strictest sterility assurance requirements (such as cell therapy and highly potent drugs).

Section Summary

  • Regulatory status: This guidance replaces the 1987 edition and helps manufacturers understand FDA's current expectations for aseptic processing; "should" in the guidance means a recommendation rather than a requirement, but in practice it is equivalent to an industry standard.
  • Regulatory framework: The main regulatory basis for aseptic processing CGMP is 21 CFR 210 & 211; biologics must additionally comply with 21 CFR 600-680; CFR text boxes denote mandatory requirements.
  • Terminal sterilization vs. aseptic processing: Terminal sterilization gives higher sterility assurance and fewer variables; aseptic processing requires separate sterilization of the drug product, container and closure, followed by assembly in a high-cleanliness environment, and has historically carried a higher recall risk.
  • Three sterilization paths: glass containers (dry heat), rubber closures (moist heat/autoclave), liquid formulations (sterilizing filtration); each path requires independent validation.
  • Core principle: Aseptic processing is a "last resort", used only when terminal sterilization is not feasible; manufacturers must document the basis for this assessment.
  • This guidance covers: six topics (personnel qualification, cleanroom design, process design, quality control, environmental monitoring and production records review), plus isolator technology; it does not cover terminal sterilization.
  • CDMO implications: Understanding this framework supports capability demonstration, feasibility assessment of new products, facility upgrade decisions and inspection risk management.

Section IV — Buildings and Facilities (Overview)

Separate or defined areas in an aseptic processing facility should be appropriately controlled to attain different degrees of air quality. Design involves satisfying microbiological and particle criteria defined by the equipment, components, products exposed, and operational activities conducted in the area.

Guide - Overview and Regulatory Citations

The design of an aseptic manufacturing facility is not one-size-fits-all. FDA expects different areas to meet different levels of air quality, depending on the equipment, materials and product exposed in the area and the activities performed there.

Core Concept: The Regulatory Framework

The CFR provisions covered in this section fall into four layers:

  • Facility design layer: 211.42(b)/(c)(10) - layout and flow, hardware requirements for aseptic areas
  • HVAC and filtration layer: 211.46(b)/(c) - pressure, temperature and humidity, filtration systems
  • Equipment and maintenance layer: 211.63, 211.65(a), 211.67(a) - design, materials, cleaning
  • Procedures and validation layer: 211.113(b) - procedures for preventing microbiological contamination, and sterilization validation

Understanding the scope of each provision is the first step in responding to an FDA Form 483 observation.

Analogy: Kitchen Safety Zones

Picture the kitchen of a Michelin-starred restaurant: the plating and pass area (like Class 100, no contamination tolerated), the chef's prep area (Class 10,000) and the dishwashing area (Class 100,000). The cleanliness requirements, personnel entry rules and airflow in each zone are all designed to run from "cleanest" to "less clean", so contamination never "flows backward".

CDMO Commercial Perspective

These CFR citations are not only compliance requirements; they directly drive facility construction cost. The six hardware requirements listed in 211.42(c)(10) (smooth surfaces, temperature and humidity control, HEPA-filtered air under positive pressure, environmental monitoring, cleaning and disinfection systems, equipment maintenance systems) each represent substantial capital expenditure (CapEx). When a CDMO quotes a client, the higher the facility classification, the higher the cost amortized into each batch.

How to Read the Table

  • US classes (Class 100/1,000/10,000/100,000) are the older system, which FDA guidance still uses

Analogy: Cleanliness Grades in a Hospital

A hospital helps explain the four classes:

  • Class 100 (ISO 5) = operating room: the strictest; surgical instruments contact the patient directly, and any contamination can be fatal
  • Class 1,000 (ISO 6) = corridor outside the operating room: immediately adjacent to the operating room, with controlled personnel movement
  • Class 10,000 (ISO 7) = ward corridor: some control, but more people movement is allowed
  • Class 100,000 (ISO 8) = outpatient waiting room: general activity, with basic controls still in place

Just as nobody walks into an operating room in street clothes, aseptic manufacturing uses graded zones to keep the most critical exposed area free from contamination.

Key Difference: "Action Level" vs. "Specification Limit"

The values in Table 1 are Action Levels, not absolute pass/fail specification limits. When an action level is exceeded, the firm must immediately investigate the cause and take corrective action (CAPA), but this does not automatically lead to batch rejection. However, the microbiological action level for the Class 100 area (1 cfu/m³) is in practice equivalent to "zero tolerance".

The particle limit differs by a factor of 10 between each class, which makes it easy to remember:

Class 1,000 → 35,200 /m³（×10）

Building a Class 100 (ISO 5) cleanroom can cost 5 to 10 times more than a Class 100,000 (ISO 8) one, mainly because it needs denser HEPA coverage, more sophisticated airflow design (unidirectional flow), more rigorous environmental monitoring infrastructure and more frequent validation activities. When designing a facility, a CDMO must assess the actual floor area needed at each classification and avoid "over-classification", which creates unnecessary operating cost.

IV.A - Critical Area: Class 100 (ISO 5)

IV.A — Critical Area (Class 100 / ISO 5)

Definition and Regulatory Basis

A critical area is one in which the sterilized drug product, containers, and closures are exposed. The primary objective of this environment is to maintain the sterility of components as required by 21 CFR 211.42(c)(10).

Activities in critical areas include:

  • Aseptic connections
  • Sterile ingredient additions
  • Filling operations
  • Closing/stoppering operations

The exposed product is vulnerable and will not be subsequently sterilized in its immediate container. Any contamination introduced at this stage cannot be remediated.

Particle Quality Requirements

No more than 3,520 particles ≥ 0.5 µm per cubic meter at sites not more than 1 foot from the work site, within the airflow, during filling and closing operations. Measurements should be taken at the highest risk sites. Regular monitoring shall occur during each production shift.

A remote counting system is recommended — this approach is less invasive and more comprehensive than portable equipment that requires an operator in proximity to the work site.

Some operations (e.g., powder filling) generate product-intrinsic particles that do not pose a contamination risk. In such cases, air can be sampled to characterize extrinsic particle contamination separately from product particles.

Airflow: Unidirectional Flow

HEPA-filtered air should be supplied at a velocity sufficient to sweep particles away from the filling/closing area, forming a unidirectional airflow pattern (also called laminar flow). Typical velocity:

  • 0.45 m/s (90 ft/min) ± 20%

Proper design prevents turbulence and stagnant air zones. Turbulence is a key mechanism by which lower-quality ambient air can be entrained into the critical zone.

In Situ Air Pattern Analysis

In situ air pattern analysis (smoke studies / airflow visualization) should be conducted under dynamic conditions — with personnel present, equipment operating, and production activities simulated. Videotape is useful for assessing and documenting airflow patterns. This analysis confirms that unidirectional flow is maintained and that no stagnant zones exist near exposed product.

"Air monitoring samples of critical areas should normally yield no microbiological contaminants."

Guide - Critical Area (Class 100 / ISO 5)

The Critical Area is the most important area in the whole aseptic manufacturing facility. Drug product, containers and closures are exposed here and are not sterilized again afterward. If contamination occurs here, the batch must be rejected. FDA scrutinizes the critical area most closely during inspections.

Three Core Requirements for the Critical Area

  • Particles: no more than 3,520 particles/m³ at ≥0.5 µm, equivalent to ISO 5, measured within 1 foot of the work site
  • Microorganisms: none should normally be detected (action level 1 cfu/m³, but the practical target is zero)
  • Airflow: unidirectional flow, with air velocity of 0.45 m/s (90 ft/min) ±20%

Analogy: Precision Chip Production in a Clean Factory

A Class 100 clean area is like the exposure room of a semiconductor fab: circuits are imprinted on the wafer there, and a single dust particle can ruin the whole wafer. Manufacturers invest enormous sums in an ultra-clean environment, and operators must fully gown before entering. Likewise, the engineering investment in an aseptic filling area exists to protect that one vial of drug product that cannot be "redone".

The Physics of Unidirectional Flow

HEPA-filtered air "sweeps" particles downward at a uniform velocity (0.45 m/s), forming an "air curtain" that protects the exposed product. Key points:

  • Velocity too low (<0.36 m/s): particles are not swept away effectively and may linger
  • Velocity too high (>0.54 m/s): excessive turbulence may actually create eddies
  • Smoke studies are performed under dynamic conditions to confirm there are no "dead spots" or eddies

Remote Counting Systems vs. Handheld Counters

FDA explicitly recommends a remote sampling system (remote counting system). A handheld counter requires the operator to stand close to the work area, and the operator is themselves a source of particles and microorganisms, which compromises how representative the measurement is. A remote system can monitor continuously without the operator present, giving more objective and more complete data.

Particles generated by the product itself during powder filling (such as lyophilized powder for injection) do not represent a contamination risk; a separate sampling strategy can be designed to distinguish extrinsic particles.

Inspection Focus: Common Smoke Study Deficiencies

Common smoke study (airflow visualization) deficiencies cited in FDA Form 483 observations include: performing the study only under static (no personnel) conditions, not covering all filling positions, and incomplete or low-resolution video records. Define dynamic conditions explicitly in the validation plan (number of personnel, equipment status, simulated operations).

IV.B - Supporting Clean Areas

IV.B — Supporting Clean Areas

Various supporting areas exist within an aseptic processing facility, each with different classifications and functions. Many support areas are used to prepare, hold, or transfer:

  • Nonsterile components and formulated products
  • In-process materials
  • Equipment
  • Containers and closures

Classification depends on the activities performed and the degree of exposure risk. FDA recommendations by zone:

Adjacent to Aseptic Processing Line

FDA recommends that the area immediately adjacent to the aseptic processing line meet at minimum Class 10,000 (ISO 7) under dynamic conditions. This area may alternatively be classified as Class 1,000 (ISO 6), or the entire aseptic filling room may be maintained at Class 100 (ISO 5).

Less Critical Activities

Class 100,000 (ISO 8) is appropriate for less critical support activities, such as:

  • Equipment cleaning and washing
  • Preparation of non-exposed components
  • Personnel gowning anteroom (in some designs)

The classification of each area must reflect the worst-case activities performed within it. Higher-risk activities in a room drive the classification requirement upward.

Guide - Supporting Clean Areas

An aseptic manufacturing facility does not have just one "cleanest" core area. The "supporting areas" surrounding the core also need risk-based classification. Their main function is to supply the critical area with cleaned and prepared materials and equipment.

Classification Logic for Supporting Areas

FDA's classification recommendations are flexible, and firms may choose stricter standards:

  • Minimum requirement: the area immediately adjacent to the aseptic filling line should be at least Class 10,000 (ISO 7)
  • Upgrade option A: upgrade the adjacent area to Class 1,000 (ISO 6)
  • Upgrade option B: maintain the entire aseptic filling room at Class 100 (ISO 5)
  • Lower-classification areas: activities such as equipment washing can be performed in Class 100,000 (ISO 8)

Choosing an upgrade option can reduce operational risk but requires higher construction and operating costs.

Analogy: The Buffer Corridor of an Operating Room

Outside an operating room (Class 100) there is usually a "clean corridor" (Class 10,000), and beyond that a "semi-clean corridor" (Class 100,000). This design filters and blocks contamination step by step from the outside in, rather than letting it enter the operating room directly from the external environment. The "supporting clean area" concept in an aseptic facility is exactly the same.

The Risk-Driven Principle of Classification

The classification of each room must reflect the highest-risk activity performed in it. If a Class 100,000 washing room is occasionally used to hold sterilized but unsealed containers, the classification of that room must be re-evaluated. During inspections FDA checks that the use of each room is consistent with its actual monitoring data.

CDMO Facility Planning Considerations

For a CDMO, the classification of supporting areas directly affects design flexibility: keeping the whole filling room at Class 100 (option B) simplifies operating procedures but greatly increases construction cost; the minimum requirement (ISO 7 adjacent area) saves cost but requires stricter material and personnel flow controls. Classification design for a multi-product facility is especially complex, as it must meet the needs of the most demanding product.

IV.C - Clean Area Separation

IV.C — Clean Area Separation

Pressure Differential Requirements

Adequate separation between areas of different cleanliness levels prevents contamination migration. Airflow moves from higher to lower cleanliness areas. A positive pressure differential of at least 10–15 Pascals (Pa) should be maintained between adjacent rooms of differing classification (with doors closed).

When doors are opened, outward airflow should be sufficient to minimize ingress of lower-quality air. Door open time must be strictly controlled.

For same-classification adjacent rooms, maintaining a pressure differential still provides beneficial directional separation. For an unclassified adjacent room, the aseptic processing room should maintain at least 12.5 Pa overpressure at all times.

If pressure drops below the minimum threshold, the environmental quality must be restored and confirmed before production continues.

Continuous Monitoring and Documentation

Pressure differentials shall be:

  • Monitored continuously throughout each shift
  • Frequently recorded
  • All alarms must be documented
  • All deviations must be investigated
Air Change Rate Requirements

Minimum air change rates by classification:

  • Class 100,000 (ISO 8): at least 20 air changes per hour
  • Class 10,000 (ISO 7) and Class 100 (ISO 5): higher rates are required (specific values determined by validation and design)

The facility monitoring system should be capable of rapidly detecting atypical changes in environmental conditions.

Guide - Clean Area Separation and Pressure Differential Management

Pressure differential management is the "first line of defense" against contamination in an aseptic facility. As long as the correct pressure cascade is maintained between areas of different classification, air naturally flows from "clean" to "less clean" and not the reverse.

Three Levels of Pressure Differential Requirements

  • Adjacent rooms of different classification: at least 10-15 Pa (doors closed)
  • Aseptic processing room vs. unclassified area: at least 12.5 Pa positive pressure (at all times)
  • When a door is open: outward airflow should effectively prevent lower-quality air from flowing in, and door-open time must be strictly controlled

If the pressure differential falls below the minimum, it must be restored and the environmental quality confirmed before production can continue. This is a mandatory requirement, not a recommendation.

Analogy: Aircraft Cabin Pressurization

An aircraft cabin is kept at positive pressure (relative to the outside atmosphere) to keep cold outside air out; high-speed rail platform screen doors likewise use pressure difference to stop tunnel wind from blasting onto the waiting platform. The logic of positive pressure in an aseptic facility is the same: the cleanroom is held at positive pressure relative to the corridor, so even if there is a tiny leak at a door gap, the air is "pushed outward" rather than "sucking in" contaminated air. A differential of 10-15 Pa looks small, but it is enough to form a stable "air wall" at the door gap.

Inspection Focus: Continuity of Pressure Differential Records

During an inspection, FDA investigators will certainly request the pressure differential monitoring records (Pressure Differential Logs), including:

  • Continuous recording charts for each shift (not just one point reading per hour)
  • All alarm records and their investigation reports
  • Impact assessments for low-pressure events (effect on the batches concerned)

Incomplete alarm records or uninvestigated deviations are among the most common Form 483 observations.

Air Change Rate Quick Reference

Class 100,000 (ISO 8) minimum requirement: 20 ACH (Air Changes per Hour)

ACH = total supply airflow per hour (m³/hr) ÷ room volume (m³)

Class 10,000 (ISO 7) and Class 100 (ISO 5) need higher air change rates, with specific values determined by validation, typically ≥ 40-60 ACH for ISO 7; for an ISO 5 laminar flow workstation, the design velocity (0.45 m/s) governs rather than ACH.

CDMO Operations Perspective: Choosing a Pressure Differential Monitoring System

Continuous pressure differential monitoring requires differential pressure transducers integrated into a building management system (BMS) or environmental monitoring system (EMS). Sensor maintenance (calibration intervals) and alarm threshold settings directly affect compliance. Some CDMOs choose an EMS that complies with FDA 21 CFR Part 11 so that all data carry an electronic audit trail.

IV.D - Air Filtration

IV.D — Air Filtration

D.1 — Membrane Filters (Compressed Gas)

Compressed gas used in cleanroom operations (e.g., purging, overlaying) must be of appropriate purity — free from oil. After filtration, the microbiological and particle quality of compressed gas must be equal to or better than the environment into which it is introduced.

Common gases used: air, nitrogen, CO₂.

FDA recommendations for membrane filters:

  • Sterile membrane filters required for compressed gas used in aseptic areas
  • Recommended for autoclave air lines
  • Recommended for lyophilizer vacuum breaks
  • Recommended for tanks containing sterilized materials
  • Sterilized holding tanks should be kept under positive pressure or sealed

Condensate control: Gas filters must be dry — condensate causes filter blockage or microbial growth. Hydrophobic filters are recommended to prevent moisture ingress.

Integrity testing: Recommend integrity testing upon installation and periodically thereafter. Filters should be replaced at defined intervals.

D.2 — HEPA Filters

HEPA filter integrity must be maintained at all times. Specific requirements:

Leak Testing Frequency:

  • At installation (initial qualification)
  • Thereafter at suitable intervals — e.g., twice per year for aseptic processing rooms
  • Additionally required when:
    • Air quality monitoring shows unacceptable results
    • Facility renovations or modifications
    • Media fill (APS) failure investigation
    • Sterility failure investigation
  • Dry heat depyrogenation tunnels and ovens also require HEPA leak testing

Challenge Aerosol: Must meet viscosity and other specifications. DOP (dioctyl phthalate) and PAO (poly-alpha-olefin) are appropriate aerosols. The aerosol must not promote microbial growth.

Efficiency Test vs. Leak Test distinction:

  • Efficiency test: uses monodispersed 0.3 µm particles; assesses overall filter media performance
  • Leak test: uses polydispersed aerosol; scans entire filter face and frame with photometer probe at 1 inch from the filter face, at ≥ 1 cubic foot per minute flow rate

Leak Acceptance Criterion: A single probe reading equivalent to 0.01% of the upstream challenge concentration constitutes a significant leak → the filter must be replaced or repaired, followed by confirmatory retesting.

Velocity Uniformity: HEPA filter leak testing alone is insufficient. Velocity uniformity must also be monitored — velocities are measured at 6 inches from the filter face and at defined distances from the work surface. HEPA filters shall be replaced when non-uniformity is detected or when airflow patterns are adversely affected.

Drug manufacturers are responsible for ensuring that contractor activities meet specifications, even when testing is outsourced.

Guide - Air Filtration (Membrane Filters and HEPA)

Air filtration is the technical backbone of aseptic manufacturing. This section covers two types of filtration systems: membrane filters for compressed gases, and HEPA filters for cleanroom air handling. Both have strict integrity testing requirements.

D.1 Membrane Filters: The "Invisible Source of Contamination" in Compressed Gases

Compressed gases (nitrogen, CO₂, compressed air) are widely used to move materials and to blanket and protect drug product, but if the gas itself carries particles, oil mist or microorganisms, contamination is "injected" straight into the product.

  • Gas quality after membrane filtration must be ≥ the classification of the cleanroom served
  • Hydrophobic filters prevent moisture, because a wet membrane is a breeding ground for microorganisms
  • Autoclave inlet lines and lyophilizer vacuum-break lines must also be fitted with membrane filters

Analogy: Purity Requirements for Scuba Tank Air

Compressed air in a diver's tank must contain no oil mist (oil mist at high pressure can cause poisoning). Compressed gases in a pharmaceutical plant face exactly the same requirement: nitrogen blown into drug containers must be as clean as "operating room air". Any oil, moisture or microorganisms can become a vehicle for contamination.

D.2 HEPA Filters: Efficiency Test vs. Leak Test

Many people confuse these two tests, but their purposes are entirely different:

  • Efficiency Test：
    • Uses monodisperse 0.3 µm particles
    • Evaluates the overall filtration efficiency of the whole filter medium
    • Confirms that the medium meets the HEPA specification (≥99.97% at 0.3 µm)
  • Leak Test：
    • Uses a polydisperse aerosol (DOP or PAO)
    • A photometer probe scans the entire filter face and frame at 1 inch from the surface
    • Flow rate ≥1 ft³/min
    • The purpose is to find pinholes in the medium or leaks in the frame seal

Analogy: Bridge Inspection - the HEPA 0.01% Leak Standard

A bridge engineer tests not only the overall strength of the steel (= efficiency test) but also scans every inch of the welds and bolted joints (= leak test). The HEPA limit of 0.01% of upstream concentration is like a bridge's "crack tolerance limit": if any single-point reading exceeds it, the filter must be repaired or replaced, rather than being kept in use because the average passes. The standard is very strict: even if overall efficiency reaches 99.99%, a localized leak is still unacceptable.

Triggers for HEPA Testing Frequency

In addition to routine testing twice a year, any of the following events triggers additional testing:

  • Environmental monitoring failures (particle or microbial excursions)
  • Facility renovation or equipment changes (vibration may damage the medium or seals)
  • Media fill (APS) failure investigations
  • Sterility test failure investigations

Note: HEPA filters in a dry heat depyrogenation tunnel also need periodic leak testing. This is often overlooked!

Velocity Uniformity: A Necessary Complement to Leak Testing

A HEPA filter that passes the leak test does not necessarily deliver uniform airflow. If velocity in some areas of the filter face is markedly low, "low-velocity dead spots" form where particles settle instead of being carried away. FDA expects velocity measurements at 6 inches from the filter face and at a defined distance from the work surface, and the HEPA filter must be replaced if non-uniformity is found.

Responsibility for Outsourced Testing

Many CDMOs and pharmaceutical plants outsource HEPA leak testing to certified contractors. FDA states explicitly that even when testing is outsourced, the firm remains fully responsible for the results. This means the firm must review the contractor's SOPs, confirm that the aerosol used meets specifications, review the raw data in the test records, and keep complete records of contractor audits.

IV.E - Design

IV.E — Facility and Equipment Design

Core Design Principles

Aseptic processes are designed to minimize exposure of sterile articles to contamination hazards. Essential design considerations:

  • Limiting the duration of exposure of sterile items
  • Providing the highest possible environmental control
  • Optimizing process flow
  • Designing equipment to prevent entrainment of lower-quality air into Class 100 (ISO 5)
Personnel and Material Flow

Personnel and material flow should be optimized to minimize unnecessary activities. Equipment layout should provide good ergonomics for operators. Minimize the number of personnel present in the aseptic processing room and limit frequency of entries/exits to the critical area.

Any intervention or unplanned stoppage increases contamination risk. Therefore:

  • On-line weight checks eliminate repeated manual weighing activities
  • SIP (Sterilize-in-Place) eliminates aseptic connections requiring manual setup
  • Robotics and automation further reduce human intervention risk
Product Transfer and Protection

Products must be transferred under appropriate cleanroom conditions. For lyophilization:

  • Partially sealed containers must be transferred in critical area conditions only
  • Class 100 (ISO 5) must be maintained between the filling line and the lyophilizer
  • Curtains and rigid plastic shields may serve as barriers

Isolator systems significantly enhance product protection and are increasingly recommended for high-risk operations.

Double-door or integrated sterilizers support direct product flow without exposure to lower-classified environments. Airlocks and interlocking doors provide better air balance control and prevent simultaneous opening of adjacent doors.

Materials must be disinfected or sterilized when entering higher-classified areas.

Stoppered vials exiting the aseptic zone prior to capping (crimping) require local protection until the crimp seal is applied.

Cleanroom Construction and Surface Requirements

Cleanrooms should function as integrated, seamless units. Requirements:

  • Seamless, rounded floor-to-wall junctions (coved corners) to prevent particle accumulation
  • Smooth, hard surfaces throughout
  • Minimal unnecessary equipment and fixtures
  • Sanitary fittings and valves
  • Drains are inappropriate for classified areas except Class 100,000 (ISO 8)
  • No horizontal surfaces or ledges that could accumulate particles
  • Equipment must not obstruct airflow patterns
Equipment Sterilization and Aseptic Setup

Equipment shall be designed for:

  • Ease of sterilization
  • Ease of aseptic setup and assembly
  • Minimal complexity during aseptic interventions
Shutdown and Restart Procedures

Deviation and change control systems shall address planned or unplanned shutdowns of air handling or other utilities. Written procedures must specify the steps required to return the facility to operating conditions after any shutdown, and the environmental qualification activities required before production may resume.

Guide - Facility and Equipment Design

IV.E is the most practically dense part of the whole section. The design principles here directly affect the success rate of aseptic operations, environmental monitoring results and FDA inspection outcomes.

Design Philosophy: "Defensive Design"

FDA's design principles can be summarized as four "minimizes":

  • Minimize exposure time: the shorter sterile items spend in an open environment, the better
  • Minimize personnel intervention: every intervention is an opportunity for contamination
  • Minimize ingress of lower-quality air: equipment design must not obstruct unidirectional flow or create turbulence
  • Minimize unnecessary fixtures: superfluous shelves, piping and equipment collect dust and create cleaning dead spots

Analogy: The "Minimal Personnel" Principle in Surgery

Top operating rooms have an unwritten rule: "no non-essential personnel in the room". Each additional person adds a source of particles and microorganisms. An aseptic manufacturing room is no different. FDA explicitly expects fewer personnel in the filling room and limits on how often the critical area is entered. "People are the contamination source" is the core premise of aseptic design.

Tiers of Automation and Isolation Technology

Technologies FDA recommends for reducing personnel intervention, from lower to higher level:

  • In-line weight checking: eliminates manual weighing interventions (simple but effective)
  • SIP (steam-in-place / sterilize-in-place): eliminates aseptic piping connections, since the connection points themselves are a contamination risk
  • Robotics/automation: removes personnel from critical area operations entirely (high investment, highest protection)
  • Isolator: operation in a physically separated positive or negative pressure environment, offering the highest level of protection

Design Requirements for Lyophilization

Transfer of partially stoppered lyophilized product from the filling line to the lyophilizer is one of the highest-risk steps in aseptic manufacturing:

  • The entire transfer path must be maintained at Class 100 (ISO 5)
  • Connecting the filling line directly to the lyophilizer with an isolator greatly reduces this risk
  • Acrylic curtains or rigid partitions can serve as local protective barriers
  • Until crimping is complete, stoppered but uncrimped vials still require local protection

Cleanroom Construction Details: Why Must Corners Be Coved?

Right-angle (90°) corners harbor particles and microorganisms, and conventional cleaning cannot thoroughly clean a right-angle joint. FDA expects "coved corners where floors meet walls", in order to:

  • Eliminate cleaning dead spots so that disinfectant fully covers all surfaces
  • Reduce physical refuge for microorganisms
  • Allow easy visual inspection (no dark corners where dust collects)

Likewise, drains are not permitted in ISO 5/6/7 cleanrooms because drain pipes are a pathway for microorganisms; drains are allowed only in ISO 8 (Class 100,000) areas such as washing rooms.

Recovery After Shutdown: A Frequently Overlooked Compliance Requirement

After an unplanned HVAC shutdown (such as a power failure), production cannot restart immediately once power is restored. A written SOP must specify:

  • The "purge" time after HVAC is restored
  • The environmental verification monitoring that must be performed (particles + microorganisms)
  • Confirmation that pressure differentials have returned to the acceptable range
  • Documentation of the shutdown event and the results of the recovery verification

If FDA finds no such written procedure during an inspection, it will issue a Form 483 observation directly.

Commercial Decision Matrix for CDMO Facility Design

When a CDMO evaluates whether it can accept a new client's product, facility design decisions have far-reaching effects:

  • Isolator vs. conventional RABS: an isolator has higher initial cost but usually yields better environmental monitoring data, which helps improve APS (aseptic process simulation) pass rates
  • SIP piping design: higher upfront engineering cost, but fewer aseptic connection procedures and lower long-term operating risk
  • Robotic filling systems: high capital investment, but allow the CDMO to take on high-risk, high-value products (such as cell and gene therapies) at premium pricing

Every design upgrade balances "risk reduction" against "cost increase"; a CDMO's competitiveness often lies in the judgment applied to these trade-offs.

Section V.A — Personnel Training & Aseptic Technique

V.A Personnel — Training & Aseptic Technique

Key CFR Citations: 21 CFR 211.25(a) — each person shall have education, training, and experience; training shall be continuing and with sufficient frequency. 21 CFR 211.22(a) — QC unit has authority to approve/reject. 21 CFR 211.28(b) — personnel practice good sanitation and health habits.

A well-designed, maintained, and operated aseptic process minimizes personnel intervention. As operator activities increase, risk to product sterility also increases. It is critical for operators to use aseptic technique at all times.

Training topics required before entering the aseptic manufacturing area:

  • Aseptic technique
  • Cleanroom behavior
  • Microbiology fundamentals
  • Hygiene and health practices
  • Gowning procedure
  • Patient safety hazards
  • Specific written procedures (SOPs)

After initial training, an ongoing training program is maintained. Supervisory personnel routinely evaluate each operator's conformance. The QC unit provides regular oversight.

Key aseptic techniques operators must follow:

  • Contact sterile materials only with sterile instruments held under Class 100 (ISO 5) conditions between uses in sterilized containers. Replace as necessary throughout operation.
  • Sterile gloves should be regularly sanitized or changed. Personnel should not directly contact sterile products, containers, closures, or critical surfaces.
  • Move slowly and deliberately. Rapid movements create unacceptable turbulence and disrupt unidirectional airflow.
  • Keep entire body out of the path of unidirectional airflow. Disruption poses risk to product sterility.
  • Approach manipulation from the side, not above (in vertical unidirectional flow). Refrain from speaking when in direct proximity to the critical area.
  • Maintain proper gown control. No activity posing unreasonable contamination risk to the gown is permitted.
"As operator activities increase, risk to product sterility also increases." — FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance, Section V.A

V.A Personnel Training and Aseptic Technique (Commentary)

The core message of this section: people are the greatest source of contamination risk in aseptic processing. Every operator sheds millions of skin particles each day, and every word spoken or fast movement made can disrupt a carefully designed unidirectional airflow. FDA's answer is to control this risk through training, discipline, and ongoing oversight.

Core concept: three layers of training

  • Initial training: seven mandatory topics, none optional, completed before the operator enters the aseptic area.
  • Ongoing training: FDA expects a "sufficient" frequency; training cannot be a one-time event.
  • Supervisory evaluation: supervisors must periodically observe operators' compliance on the floor, and the QC unit provides routine oversight.

All three layers are essential. Failure of any one of them can become a Form 483 observation during an FDA inspection.

Real-life analogy: an aseptic operator is like a surgeon going into the operating room

Before heart surgery, every surgeon's movement is governed by rules: how to scrub, how a nurse helps put on gloves without touching the outer surface, keeping the arms above waist level once in the operating room, and so on. An aseptic manufacturing operator is held to exactly the same level of rigor: "move slowly and deliberately," "do not stand in the airflow path," "do not speak near the critical area." These practices are borrowed from the operating room because the logic of protecting the patient is the same.

CDMO audit focus: common training record deficiencies

  • Training records missing the date, signature, or trainer information
  • Inability to show that training is "ongoing" (no retraining records)
  • Operators performing tasks they have not been trained for
  • Supervisory on-the-floor evaluation records are missing or only perfunctory

Clients also typically confirm the completeness of the training system when they review the Technical Agreement.

Section V.A — Gowning Qualification Program

V.A Gowning — Requirements & Qualification

CFR Citations: 21 CFR 211.28(a) — personnel wear clean clothing appropriate for duties; protective apparel as necessary. 21 CFR 211.28(c) — only authorized personnel enter limited-access areas.

Only qualified and appropriately gowned personnel are permitted in the aseptic manufacturing area. The gown provides a barrier between the body and sterile materials.

The Agency recommends gowns that are sterilized and nonshedding, covering all skin and hair. Required components include:

  • Facemasks
  • Hoods
  • Beard and moustache covers (where applicable)
  • Protective goggles
  • Elastic gloves

Written procedures detail donning in an aseptic manner. Adequate barrier is provided by overlapping of gown components (e.g., gloves overlapping sleeves). Any torn or defective element must be changed immediately. Gloves must be sanitized frequently.

Gowning Qualification Program — assesses the ability of cleanroom operators to maintain gown quality. The Agency recommends microbiological surface sampling of several locations:

  • Glove fingers
  • Facemask
  • Forearm
  • Chest area

Periodic requalification is required. Annual requalification is normally sufficient for automated operations. Additional or more frequent requalification is required if adverse conditions occur.

Written procedures should address circumstances for retraining, requalification, or reassignment. Sanitizing gloves just prior to sampling is inappropriate, as it can prevent recovery of microorganisms present during manipulation.

V.A Gowning Qualification Program (Commentary)

Gowning is more than "putting on sterile garments." It is a barrier system that must be supported by data, and each operator must individually pass qualification before entering the aseptic manufacturing area.

Core concept: three key elements of gowning qualification

  • Standardized sampling locations: FDA recommends microbiological surface sampling of the fingers, face mask, forearms, and chest, which are the sites where microorganisms most easily accumulate.
  • Periodic requalification: passing once is not enough; qualification must be repeated periodically. If an adverse event occurs (such as a microbial excursion), the frequency must be increased.
  • No disinfection of gloves before sampling: FDA specifically identifies this as an improper practice. Disinfecting gloves before sampling kills the microorganisms actually picked up during operations, which distorts the results and masks the true risk.

Real-life analogy: the gowning procedure is like a surgeon putting on surgical attire

A surgeon puts on a surgical gown in a strict sequence: a nurse ties the gown (the surgeon cannot touch the outer surface), and finally a nurse helps put on the sterile gloves (so the outside of the gloves is not contaminated). The logic of aseptic gowning is exactly the same: the "layering order" in the SOP (for example, gloves over the cuffs) ensures no skin is exposed. If any step goes wrong, the barrier can fail, just as a surgeon who accidentally touches the outside of the surgical table must change gloves immediately.

CDMO Form 483 high-risk items: common gowning qualification deficiencies

  • No written SOP defining the gowning sequence and the sampling plan
  • No investigation or requalification after an excursion
  • Disinfecting gloves before sampling (compromises data integrity)
  • Gowning qualification records that are incomplete or not linked to batch production
  • Temporary staff (contract workers) entering the aseptic area without completing qualification

If an operator who failed gowning qualification has entered the filling room for a client's product, this may trigger batch rejection and a customer notification obligation.

V.B & V.C — Lab Personnel & Personnel Monitoring

V.B — Laboratory Personnel: The basic principles of training, aseptic technique, and personnel qualification also apply to those performing aseptic sampling and microbiological laboratory analyses. Systems cannot be considered in control if the validity of laboratory data is in question.

V.C — Monitoring Program: Personnel can significantly affect environment quality. A vigilant and responsive personnel monitoring program should be established.

Surface samples of each operator's gloves should be taken on a daily basis or in association with each lot. This is accompanied by sampling of other selected gown locations. The QC unit establishes more comprehensive monitoring for especially labor-intensive operations.

Maintaining contamination-free gloves and gowns throughout operations is the ongoing goal.

Sanitizing gloves just prior to sampling is inappropriate, as it can prevent recovery of microorganisms present during manipulation.

When operators exceed established levels or show an adverse trend, the following actions are required:

  • Investigation
  • Increased sampling
  • Increased observation
  • Retraining
  • Gowning requalification
  • Reassignment (if necessary)

V.B & V.C Laboratory Personnel and Personnel Monitoring Program (Commentary)

The personnel monitoring program is the "immune system" of aseptic processing. Its purpose is not to punish people but to detect trends early and intervene before a problem becomes a batch failure.

Core concept: two dimensions of the monitoring program

  • Routine monitoring: glove surface sampling for each operator every day or every batch, plus sampling of some gown surfaces, to build an individual baseline.
  • Enhanced monitoring triggers: when an alert level or action level is exceeded, an investigation, additional sampling, observation, retraining, and requalification must be initiated, with reassignment of duties if necessary.

V.B reminder: laboratory personnel (who perform aseptic sampling and microbiological analysis) are subject to the same requirements. If the validity of laboratory data is questioned, the credibility of the entire quality system collapses.

Real-life analogy: the monitoring program is like regular medical checkups for airline pilots

Commercial pilots must undergo regular medical examinations, and if their health falls short of the standard, they must stop flying until they recover. This is not distrust of pilots; passenger safety requires a systematic safeguard rather than reliance on self-assessment. The logic behind daily glove sampling of cleanroom operators is the same: build an objective, data-driven, continuous monitoring mechanism so that any problem is found early.

Practical warning: trend analysis matters more than a single excursion

FDA looks not only at single excursions but also at trends. If an operator's colony counts come close to the alert level five times in a row without exceeding the action level, some companies may take no action, but in FDA's eyes this is a deficiency: "an adverse trend was not identified and responded to." A sound monitoring program must define in the SOP the criteria for an "adverse trend" and the response procedure.

Section VI.A — Components

VI.A Components — Sterilization & Endotoxin Control

CFR Citations: 21 CFR 210.3(b)(3) — component includes any ingredient intended for use in manufacture. 21 CFR 211.80(a) — written procedures for receipt, identification, storage, handling, sampling, testing, approval/rejection. 21 CFR 211.84(d)(6) — each lot liable to microbiological contamination shall be subjected to microbiological tests before use. 21 CFR 211.113(b) — validation of sterilization processes.

A drug product can be contaminated through contaminated components. Examples include active ingredients, Water for Injection (WFI), and other excipients. It is important to characterize the microbial content (bioburden and endotoxin) of each component and establish acceptance limits.

Endotoxin load data is particularly significant because parenteral products must be nonpyrogenic. Written procedures and specifications for acceptance/rejection of each lot with respect to endotoxins must be established. Any component failing defined endotoxin limits should be rejected.

In aseptic processing, each component is individually sterilized, or components are combined and the mixture is sterilized. Knowledge of bioburden is important for assessing sterilization adequacy.

Methods for sterilizing components:

  • Filtration (widely used): Solution passed through a sterilizing membrane or cartridge filter. Used where the component is soluble and heat-labile. Variation: aseptic crystallization/precipitation or lyophilization as a sterile powder — but more handling equals higher contamination potential.
  • Dry heat sterilization: Suitable for heat-stable and insoluble components. Heat penetration/distribution studies especially significant for powders due to insulating effects.
  • Irradiation: Suitable for some components. Studies must demonstrate appropriateness.

VI.A Component Control (Commentary)

Contamination pathways in sterile drug products come not only from personnel and the environment: the components themselves can be the starting point of contamination. However well the later sterilization is carried out, if the input materials carry a high endotoxin load, the final product can still cause fever or septic shock in patients.

Core concept: two key indicators for material control

  • Bioburden: the number of viable microorganisms in the material. It is critical to sterilization process design: the higher the pre-sterilization bioburden, the more rigorous the sterilization conditions needed to ensure the sterility assurance level (SAL).
  • Endotoxin: lipopolysaccharide (LPS) fragments from the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria. Even after the bacteria are killed, endotoxin remains and is highly pyrogenic; when injected into the body it causes fever and, in severe cases, can be fatal.

Every batch of material must have clear acceptance criteria for bioburden and endotoxin; a batch that does not meet them must be rejected, with no compromise.

Real-life analogy: endotoxin is like food that looks fine but already contains toxins

Imagine a pot of cooked white rice left at room temperature for 12 hours. It looks and smells fine, but Bacillus cereus has multiplied heavily while it cooled and produced heat-stable toxins. Even if you reheat it to 100°C and kill the bacteria, the toxins remain, and eating it still causes food poisoning. Endotoxin works the same way: ordinary heat sterilization can kill bacteria, but killing bacteria is not the same as removing endotoxin. This is why injectable products require independent endotoxin testing of every batch of materials rather than relying on the sterilization process alone.

CDMO business risk: the chain of consequences of a material endotoxin OOS

  • The entire batch of product may have to be rejected (even if all subsequent processing steps have been completed)
  • Customer notification is required, affecting delivery schedules and the trust relationship
  • If the batch has already been released, it may trigger a market recall
  • Root cause analysis (RCA) must be performed and a CAPA submitted

Robust incoming material testing and supplier qualification are the first line of defense against these costly scenarios.

Section VI.B — Containers & Closures Preparation

VI.B Containers & Closures — Preparation

CFR Citations: 21 CFR 211.94(c) — containers and closures shall be clean and, where indicated, sterilized and processed to remove pyrogenic properties. 21 CFR 211.94(d) — standards, specifications, methods of testing written and followed.

Containers and closures must be sterile and nonpyrogenic for parenteral products. A validation study must demonstrate the ability to render them sterile and nonpyrogenic. Written procedures must specify revalidation frequency and time limits for holding sterile depyrogenated containers/closures.

Glass Containers:

  • A series of wash and rinse cycles to remove foreign matter. High purity rinse water is recommended. Final rinse water must meet WFI, USP specifications.
  • Depyrogenation adequacy validated by spiking containers with known quantities of endotoxin, measuring content after depyrogenation. Endotoxin solution allowed to air dry on surfaces. Positive controls measure percentage recovery.
  • Validation data must demonstrate at least 99.9% (3-log) endotoxin reduction.
  • Dry heat generally accomplishes both sterilization and depyrogenation for glass. Validation includes heat distribution/penetration studies, worst-case cycles, container characteristics (mass), and specific loading configurations.

Plastic Containers:

  • Nonpyrogenic by nature; WFI rinses can remove residual pyrogens.
  • Can be sterilized with EtO (ethylene oxide), irradiation, or other means.
  • For EtO: temperature, pressure, humidity, gas concentration, exposure time, degassing, aeration, and residuals must be specified and monitored. EtO is an effective surface sterilant. Biological indicators (BI) are important for EtO validation. Residuals (EtO and byproducts) must be within specified limits.

Rubber Closures (stoppers, syringe plungers):

  • Multiple wash/rinse cycles, then steam or irradiation sterilization.
  • Initial rinses: at least Purified Water, USP; final rinses with WFI.
  • Depyrogenation by multiple hot WFI rinses.
  • Minimize time between washing, drying, and sterilizing (residual moisture supports microbial growth and endotoxin generation).
  • Rubber is a poor heat conductor — extra attention to heat penetration validation.
  • Validation data must demonstrate successful endotoxin removal.
  • Siliconization of rubber stoppers: silicone must meet QC criteria; must not adversely affect product safety, quality, or purity.

Contract Sterilization Facilities: Same CGMP requirements apply. The finished dosage form manufacturer must review and assess the contractor's validation protocol and final validation report.

VI.B Container and Closure Preparation (Commentary)

Containers and closures must meet both standards: sterile and nonpyrogenic. Neither can be omitted, and both require validation data as support.

3-log depyrogenation validation: a 1000-fold reduction in endotoxin

FDA requires depyrogenation validation of glass containers to achieve at least a 3-log (99.9%) reduction in endotoxin.

Specific calculation: if 1,000 EU of endotoxin standard is inoculated onto the inner wall of a glass vial during validation, the residual after dry heat treatment must not exceed 1 EU.

1,000 EU → no more than 1 EU = 99.9% reduction = 3-log reduction.

A positive control confirms recovery and ensures that the LAL assay itself is valid. This is a minimum requirement, not a recommendation.

Real-life analogy: a 3-log reduction means cutting the toxin 1000-fold

In everyday terms: if you start with 1,000 units of endotoxin, only less than 1 unit may remain after depyrogenation. That is like compressing the space of a football field down to the area of a sheet of A4 paper. Only a reduction of this magnitude ensures that an injectable product will not cause a pyrogenic reaction once it enters the body. In customer audits and regulatory explanations, "1000-fold reduction" is the most intuitive way to explain it.

CDMO audit points: managing outsourced sterilization facilities

If sterilization/depyrogenation of containers and closures is outsourced, FDA expects that:

  • The drug manufacturer must review and evaluate the contractor's validation protocol and final validation report
  • The contractor is also subject to CGMP; outsourcing does not lower the requirements
  • The Quality Agreement must clearly assign responsibilities

In a client audit, auditors will usually ask to see the depyrogenation validation reports from the container closure supplier and the company's records of its review of those reports.

Section VI.B — Container Closure Integrity

VI.B Container Closure Integrity (CCI)

The container closure system must not permit penetration of microorganisms. Damaged or defective units must be detected and removed during final sealed product inspection.

Safeguards must preclude shipment of products lacking container closure integrity (CCI). Equipment suitability problems or incoming container/closure deficiencies can cause loss of integrity.

Functional defects in delivery devices — such as syringe device defects and delivery volume errors — should be monitored by in-process testing.

Defects outside specifications must be investigated per 21 CFR 211.192.

CFR Citations: 21 CFR 211.94(c); 21 CFR 211.94(d); 21 CFR 211.192 — investigation of unexplained discrepancies.

VI.B Container Closure Integrity (Commentary)

The container closure system is the last physical barrier to the sterility of a drug product. Even if the product was perfectly sterile at filling, if the seal has a pinhole or defect, external microorganisms can still enter over time.

Core concept: two protective functions of CCI

  • Blocking microbial ingress: the seal must prevent microbial penetration throughout the shelf life. This is the most fundamental function.
  • Delivery device functionality: products that include a delivery device, such as prefilled syringes, also require monitoring of functional attributes such as dose accuracy and plunger break-loose and glide force.

Real-life analogy: CCI is like the seal of a vacuum food storage bag

The purpose of a vacuum bag is to keep out air and microorganisms. But if the bag has pinholes invisible to the naked eye, it still looks "sealed" while the food spoils without your knowing. The logic of drug container closure failure is exactly the same, which is why CCI validation must use sensitive instrumental methods (such as dye ingress, vacuum decay, or high-voltage leak detection) and cannot rely on visual inspection alone.

CDMO quality risk: the chain reaction of a CCI failure

  • If found on the market: may trigger a Class I recall
  • If found in-house: the whole batch is at risk of destruction
  • The customer must be notified, affecting future contracts
  • FDA requires root cause investigation and CAPA, and may require revalidation of the filling and capping equipment

Section VII — Endotoxin Control

VII Endotoxin Control — Equipment & Processing

CFR Citations: 21 CFR 211.63, 211.65(a), 211.67(a) — equipment design, construction, cleaning. 21 CFR 211.167(a) — appropriate laboratory testing for each batch purporting to be sterile and/or pyrogen-free.

Endotoxin contamination of injectable products can occur as a result of poor CGMP controls. Certain patient populations are at greater risk:

  • Neonates
  • Those receiving other injections concomitantly
  • Those administered parenterals in atypically large volumes or doses

Areas to address in establishing endotoxin control:

  • Drug product components
  • Containers and closures
  • Storage time limitations
  • Manufacturing equipment

Adequate cleaning, drying, and storage of equipment will control bioburden and prevent endotoxin accumulation. Equipment should be easily assembled/disassembled, cleaned, sanitized, and sterilized. Endotoxins can be contributed by both upstream and downstream processing equipment.

Critical point: Sterilizing-grade filters and moist heat sterilization (autoclaving) have NOT been shown to be effective in removing endotoxin.

Endotoxin on equipment surfaces can be:

  • Inactivated by high-temperature dry heat
  • Removed by cleaning procedures

CIP (Clean-In-Place) procedures: initial rinses with high purity water and/or cleaning agents (acid, base, surfactant), followed by final rinses with heated WFI. Equipment should be dried following cleaning, unless proceeding immediately to a sterilization step.

VII Endotoxin Control (Commentary)

The core message of Section VII is that endotoxin control is a systematic task that runs through the entire process. From components, containers, and equipment to storage time, every step can be a point where endotoxin is introduced.

Core concept: four nodes of endotoxin control

  • Components: independent endotoxin testing of every batch, with acceptance criteria.
  • Containers & Closures: depyrogenation validation with the 3-log reduction requirement.
  • Storage time limits: the longer the hold time, the more microorganisms multiply and the more endotoxin they produce; time limits must be supported by data.
  • Equipment: cleaning (CIP), drying, and storage conditions determine the endotoxin level on equipment surfaces; both upstream and downstream equipment must be controlled.

Real-life analogy: endotoxin on equipment is like bacterial toxins on kitchenware

Imagine a cutting board. If it is not thoroughly cleaned and dried after each use, leftover food debris and moisture let bacteria multiply heavily, and the toxins left behind when the bacteria die remain on the board. Even if you later boil the board, those toxins are not necessarily destroyed. The logic of endotoxin control for pharmaceutical equipment is exactly the same: cleaning + drying + appropriate storage is what fundamentally controls endotoxin accumulation.

Key warning: autoclaving cannot remove endotoxin

FDA states explicitly that neither sterilizing-grade filters nor moist heat sterilization (autoclaving) effectively removes endotoxin. This is a common misconception.

  • Autoclaving (121°C, 15 min) kills bacteria, but endotoxin (LPS) is heat resistant and is not destroyed under these conditions.
  • Methods that remove endotoxin: dry heat (above 180°C, used for glassware) or chemical cleaning (acid, alkali, or surfactant followed by a WFI rinse).
  • For equipment, the CIP procedure (alkaline wash + final WFI rinse) is the primary means of depyrogenation.

In a CDMO's client audit, a claim that "equipment is pyrogen-free after autoclaving" would be a serious technical error and directly calls the site's technical competence into question.

Section VIII — Time Limitations

VIII Time Limitations — Aseptic Processing Phases

CFR Citation: 21 CFR 211.111 — time limits for completion of each phase of production shall be established; deviation acceptable if not compromising quality, must be justified and documented.

Time limits must be established for each phase of aseptic processing. Examples include:

  • Period between start of bulk product compounding and sterilization
  • Filtration processes
  • Product exposure while on processing line
  • Storage of sterilized equipment
  • Storage of sterilized containers/closures

Time limits must be supported by data. Bioburden and endotoxin load must be assessed when establishing time limits for the formulation processing stage.

Total time for product filtration must be limited to:

  • Prevent microorganisms from penetrating the filter (filter integrity)
  • Prevent significant increase in upstream bioburden
  • Prevent significant increase in upstream endotoxin load

Filters used upstream for clarification or particle removal also have maximum use times that must be established and followed.

Any deviation from established time limits must be documented and justified; it is acceptable only if product quality is not compromised.

VIII Time Limitations (Commentary)

The core logic of time limits: microorganisms multiply in any uncontrolled moist environment, and multiplication not only increases bioburden but also produces more endotoxin. FDA requires a data-supported time limit for every process stage, and any deviation beyond that limit must be documented with a scientific justification.

Core concept: six process points where time limits must be established

  • Start of compounding → completion of sterilization: the longer this period, the more bioburden and endotoxin accumulate.
  • Filtration time: if too long, microorganisms may penetrate the filter membrane (filter integrity risk).
  • Product exposure time on the filling line: the cumulative time the filling line is exposed to the environment must have a limit.
  • Storage time of equipment after sterilization: equipment held too long after sterilization may be recontaminated by environmental factors.
  • Storage time of containers and closures after sterilization: as above, a written time limit is needed.
  • Use time of upstream clarifying/particulate-removal filters: it is not only the final sterilizing filter that needs a time limit.

Real-life analogy: time limits are like shelf life and food safety time limits for ingredients

Fresh chicken can be kept for 2 days in a 4°C refrigerator but only 2 hours at room temperature. This is not an arbitrary rule; it is a safe upper time limit calculated from the bacterial growth rate (doubling time). Past that time, the food may not smell off, yet the bacterial count has already exceeded the safety standard. The logic of time limits in drug manufacturing is exactly the same: every "hold time" must be supported by microbial growth data rather than decided by guesswork based on experience.

CDMO audit focus: documentation of time limits

Common deficiencies related to time limits during FDA and client audits include:

  • The batch record does not record the actual start/end times of each stage
  • Time limits have no data support and are merely historical practice ("We've always done it this way")
  • Time limit deviations are not documented under 21 CFR 211.111
  • Filtration time limits apply only to the final sterilizing filter and ignore the upstream clarifying filter
  • Storage time limits for containers/equipment after sterilization are not clearly defined in the SOP

For a CDMO, the scientific basis for time limits (validation study reports) must be available to auditors at all times.

Section VIII — Time Limitations (pp. 20–21)

Original Text

21 CFR 211.111 states: "When appropriate, time limits for the completion of each phase of production shall be established to assure the quality of the drug product. Deviation from established time limits may be acceptable if such deviation does not compromise the quality of the drug product. Such deviation shall be justified and documented."

When appropriate, time limits must be established for each phase of aseptic processing (§ 211.111). Time limits should include, for example, the period between the start of bulk product compounding and its sterilization, filtration processes, product exposure while on the processing line, and storage of sterilized equipment, containers and closures. The time limits established for the various production phases should be supported by data. Bioburden and endotoxin load should be assessed when establishing time limits for stages such as the formulation processing stage.

The total time for product filtration should be limited to an established maximum to prevent microorganisms from penetrating the filter. Such a time limit should also prevent a significant increase in upstream bioburden and endotoxin load. Because they can provide a substrate for microbial attachment, maximum use times for those filters used upstream for solution clarification or particle removal should also be established and justified.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Are Time Limits Needed?

Every stage of an aseptic process carries a dynamic risk of microbial contamination. The longer the time, the greater the potential accumulation of bioburden, growth of endotoxin, or risk of microorganisms penetrating the filter membrane. 21 CFR 211.111 requires firms to establish data-supported time limits for each production phase, rather than guessing from experience.

The key stages that need time limits include:

  • Start of compounding → sterile filtration: prevents excessive bioburden accumulation
  • Sterile filtration → completion of filling: prevents recontamination of the sterilized liquid
  • Sterilized equipment/containers/stoppers → use: prevents post-sterilization recontamination or biofilm formation
  • Product exposure time on the production line: controls the risk from environmental exposure

Note: time limits must be supported by data, not just "what the SOP says"

FDA expects time limits to be supported by actual bioburden and endotoxin load data. A common audit finding is that a firm's time limits come from "industry practice" or "we have always done it this way", and the firm cannot produce hold time study data.

If an established time limit is exceeded, there must be documented records plus a scientific justification that quality is not affected; simply filing a deviation report is not enough.

Analogy: ingredient prep vs. serving deadline

Picture the kitchen of a high-end restaurant: from the moment seafood is prepared to the moment the dish is served, there is a strict time window. This is not arbitrary; it is based on food safety data, since bacteria double every few hours at a given temperature. Aseptic processing follows the same logic: a compounded solution or a sterilized container each has a "freshness window", and beyond it you need scientific proof that it is still safe.

Filtration time limits: protection on two levels

Filtration time limits serve a dual function:

  • Preventing microbial penetration (filter penetration): over a long filtration, microorganisms accumulating on the membrane may grow through or be forced through to the downstream side under pressure, compromising sterility.
  • Controlling upstream bioburden and endotoxin: even a prefilter used for clarification or particle removal provides a surface for microorganisms to attach to, so its use time must be limited to prevent endotoxin from accumulating on the membrane and being released under pressure.

This shows that even an upstream filter that is not sterilizing-grade needs a time limit on use, with a written justification.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

During CDMO technology transfer, establishing time limits is part of the validation plan. In the process characterization phase, a hold time study must be designed and executed. A typical design samples under worst-case conditions (highest compounding temperature, longest anticipated hold time) and monitors bioburden and endotoxin, so that scientific data support the time limits in the final SOP. Time limits without supporting data are a frequent source of FDA 483 observations.

Section IX — Validation Overview (p. 21)

Original Text

21 CFR 211.113(b): "Appropriate written procedures, designed to prevent microbiological contamination of drug products purporting to be sterile, shall be established and followed. Such procedures shall include validation of any sterilization process."

This section primarily discusses routine qualification and validation study recommendations. Change control procedures are addressed only briefly, but are an important part of the quality systems established by a firm. A change in facility, equipment, process, or test method should be evaluated through the written change control program, triggering an evaluation of the need for revalidation or requalification.

To ensure the sterility of products purporting to be sterile, sterilization, aseptic filling and closing operations must be adequately validated (§ 211.113). The goal of even the most effective sterilization processes can be defeated if the sterilized elements of a product (the drug formulation, the container, and the closure) are brought together under conditions that contaminate any of those elements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Complete Validation Framework

The validation system in Section IX rests on three pillars, each corresponding to a key link in the aseptic process:

  • A. Process simulations (media fill): validates the aseptic filling operation itself
  • B. Filtration efficacy: validates the effectiveness of sterilizing filtration
  • C. Sterilization of equipment, containers, and closures: validates the sterility of every product-contact component

None of the three pillars can be omitted, because of the "chain of failure" principle: even if every component has individually passed the most rigorous sterilization, contamination during assembly or combination defeats the whole sterility assurance system.

Note: change control is an extension of validation, not an exception

Many firms mistakenly think validation is a one-time exercise. FDA states clearly that any change to the facility, equipment, process, or test methods must be evaluated through a written change control system to determine whether revalidation or requalification is needed. This means the validation program is a "living document" that must be continually maintained and respond to change.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

For a CDMO, every technology transfer from a new client, and every change of equipment or facility for an existing product, is a trigger event that requires a revalidation assessment. Building a thorough trigger list into the change control SOP effectively reduces the risk of receiving an FDA 483 observation because the firm "did not realize revalidation was needed".

Section IX.A.1 — Process Simulations: Study Design (pp. 21–22)

Original Text

An aseptic processing operation should be validated using a microbiological growth medium in place of the product. This process simulation, also known as a media fill, normally includes exposing the microbiological growth medium to product contact surfaces of equipment, container closure systems, critical environments, and process manipulations to closely simulate the same exposure that the product itself will undergo. The sealed containers filled with the medium are then incubated to detect microbial contamination.

A media fill program should incorporate the contamination risk factors that occur on a production line, and accurately assesses the state of process control. Media fill studies should closely simulate aseptic manufacturing operations incorporating, as appropriate, worst-case activities and conditions. FDA recommends that the media fill program address applicable issues such as:

  • Factors associated with the longest permitted run (e.g., operator fatigue)
  • Representative number, type, and complexity of normal interventions, as well as nonroutine interventions (e.g., maintenance, stoppages, equipment adjustments)
  • Lyophilization, when applicable
  • Aseptic assembly of equipment (e.g., at start-up, during processing)
  • Number of personnel and their activities
  • Representative number of aseptic additions (e.g., charging containers and closures as well as sterile ingredients) or transfers
  • Shift changes, breaks, and gown changes (when applicable)
  • Type of aseptic equipment disconnections/connections
  • Aseptic sample collections
  • Line speed and configuration
  • Weight checks
  • Container closure systems (e.g., sizes, type, compatibility with equipment)
  • Specific provisions in written procedures relating to aseptic processing (e.g., conditions permitted before line clearance is mandated)

A written batch record, documenting production conditions and simulated activities, should be prepared for each media fill run. Media fills should not be used to justify practices that pose unnecessary contamination risks.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Design Philosophy of Media Fill

The fundamental logic of a media fill is to replace the actual drug product with microbiological growth medium and "pretend" to run through the complete aseptic filling process. If any step introduces contamination, the medium grows microorganisms, giving a clear, detectable signal.

The key design principle is "worst case". A media fill is not meant to make the process look perfect; it deliberately includes all high-risk activities to confirm that sterility assurance holds even under the most challenging conditions.

Analogy: a fire drill should not pick the best weather

If a fire drill is held only in daylight, in good weather, with everyone present, it cannot show how well people would respond to a late-night emergency. Likewise, a media fill run under the best environment, with the fewest personnel and the simplest operations, loses its validation value. FDA's requirement to include "worst-case operating conditions" is there to keep the drill meaningful.

Note: 13 design elements, and each one is an audit focus

The 13 media fill design elements listed by FDA serve as an item-by-item checklist during inspections. Common deficiencies include:

  • Shift changes/breaks not simulated, so only a single shift is run and the contamination risk at shift change is missed
  • Non-routine interventions not included, with equipment adjustments and repairs not simulated
  • Lyophilized products where the partial vacuum steps are not simulated
  • Incomplete or perfunctory batch records

CDMO/COO Operational Application

In a CDMO setting, one production line may serve different products for several clients, and the media fill design must represent the worst-case operations of all of them. In practice, it is advisable to build a worst-case matrix listing the special operating steps of each product (such as aseptic additions, manual filling, or differences in container size), so that one media fill design covers the shared validation needs of several products without sacrificing representativeness. This is also a good opportunity to show the client your expertise at the proposal stage.

Section IX.A.2 — Frequency and Number of Runs (pp. 22–23)

Original Text

When a processing line is initially qualified, individual media fills should be repeated enough times to ensure that results are consistent and meaningful. We recommend that at least three consecutive separate successful runs be performed during initial line qualification. Subsequently, routine semi-annual qualification conducted for each processing line will evaluate the state of control of the aseptic process.

All personnel who are authorized to enter the aseptic processing room during manufacturing, including technicians and maintenance personnel, should participate in a media fill at least once a year.

Each change to a product or line change should be evaluated using a written change control system. Any changes or events that have the potential to affect the ability of the aseptic process to exclude contamination should be assessed through additional media fills. For example, facility and equipment modifications, line configuration changes, significant changes in personnel, anomalies in environmental testing results, container closure system changes, extended shutdowns, or end product sterility testing showing contaminated products may be cause for revalidation of the system.

When data from a media fill indicate the process may not be in control, an investigation should be conducted. Once corrections are instituted, process simulation run(s) should confirm that deficiencies have been corrected. When an investigation fails to reach well-supported, substantive conclusions, three consecutive successful runs in tandem with increased scrutiny of the production process may be warranted.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Statistical Meaning of "Three Consecutive Successful Runs"

Requiring three consecutive successful runs rather than one has a statistical basis: a single success may be good luck, while three consecutive successes build confidence in process consistency. Conversely, if the three results diverge (for example, one success and one failure), it more clearly exposes that the process is not yet in control.

Note: Events that trigger additional media fills

The following events may trigger additional media fills or revalidation:

  • Major facility or equipment modifications
  • Changes to the line configuration
  • Significant changes in personnel (replacement of critical operators)
  • Adverse trends in environmental monitoring
  • Changes to the container/closure system
  • Resumption of production after a long shutdown
  • Contamination found in a sterility test of the finished product

This list should be built into the firm's change control trigger event matrix so that no trigger point is missed.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

For a CDMO, the requirement that "each person participates at least once a year" means media fill scheduling must be planned carefully, so that all technicians, QC staff, and maintenance engineers can take part in a scheduled media fill run. If the CDMO operates several lines, it is advisable to build a personnel participation tracking matrix to prevent some individuals from lapsing and needing an ad hoc make-up run. This is also one of the fixed annual audit costs a CDMO needs to explain to clients during contract negotiations.

Section IX.A.3 — Duration of Runs (p. 23)

Original Text

The duration of aseptic processing operations is a major consideration in media fill design. Although the most accurate simulation model would be the full batch size and duration, other appropriate models can be justified. The duration of the media fill run should be determined by the time it takes to incorporate manipulations and interventions, as well as appropriate consideration of the duration of the actual aseptic processing operation. Interventions that commonly occur should be routinely simulated, while those occurring rarely can be simulated periodically.

When aseptic processing employs manual filling or closing, or extensive manual manipulations, the duration of the process simulation should generally be no less than the length of the actual manufacturing process to best simulate contamination risks posed by operators.

For lyophilization operations, FDA recommends that unsealed containers be exposed to partial evacuation of the chamber in a manner that simulates the process. Vials should not be frozen, and precautions should be taken that ensure that the medium remains in an aerobic state to avoid potentially inhibiting the growth of microorganisms.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Which Risks Does Duration Affect?

The duration of a media fill directly determines whether it can capture "time-dependent" contamination risks, including:

  • Operator fatigue: after long periods of work, operators' aseptic awareness and precision of movement decline
  • Deterioration of the environment: after a RABS/isolator has been open for a long time, particles or microorganisms in the environment may accumulate
  • Accumulation of interventions: the longer the run, the more likely non-routine interventions (equipment adjustments, jam clearances, etc.) become

Therefore, although a full-length simulation is ideal, it should be designed reasonably according to the process characteristics. Processes dominated by manual operations (such as manual filling or manual stoppering) must be simulated for the full duration and cannot be shortened.

Note: Special requirements for lyophilization media fills

A lyophilization media fill has two key points:

  • The medium must not be frozen: low temperatures inhibit microbial growth, so potential contamination would go undetected
  • An aerobic condition must be maintained: if a vacuum step is performed, the medium must remain in an aerobic environment, otherwise aerobic organisms cannot grow, giving false negatives

In practice, the usual approach is to simulate the loading of the lyophilizer (open vials exposed inside the chamber), then return to atmospheric pressure and stopper the vials, rather than actually running a lyophilization cycle.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

To save medium cost or labor, many CDMOs tend to shorten the media fill duration. This is a risk point during FDA inspections: when a production batch is a 16-hour continuous operation but the media fill ran for only 4 hours, FDA will question whether the risk in the last 12 hours was adequately evaluated. Document the scientific rationale for the duration in the media fill protocol, including the argument for "how worst-case interventions are covered", rather than simply writing down a number of hours.

Section IX.A.4 — Size of Runs (p. 23)

Original Text

The simulation run sizes should be adequate to mimic commercial production conditions and accurately assess the potential for commercial batch contamination. The number of units filled during the process simulation should be based on contamination risk for a given process. A generally acceptable starting point for run size is in the range of 5,000 to 10,000 units. For operations with production sizes under 5,000, the number of media filled units should at least equal the maximum batch size made on the processing line.

When the possibility of contamination is higher based on the process design (e.g., manually intensive filling lines), a larger number of units, generally at or approaching the full production batch size, should be used. In contrast, a process conducted in an isolator can have a low risk of contamination and can be simulated with a lower number of units as a proportion of the overall operation.

Media fill size is an especially important consideration because some batches are produced over multiple shifts or yield an unusually large number of units. These factors should be carefully evaluated when designing the simulation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Run Size Affects Statistical Detection Sensitivity

The size of a media fill directly affects its statistical sensitivity. The larger the run, the lower the contamination rate it can detect. For example:

  • 1 positive in 10,000 vials = 0.01% contamination rate
  • 1 positive in 1,000 vials = 0.1% contamination rate

The latter is 10 times less sensitive. FDA allows smaller runs for isolator lines because their inherently low contamination risk makes the lower statistical sensitivity acceptable, but this requires scientific justification.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

Multiple-shift production is a common CDMO business model, but FDA expressly notes that multiple-shift batches need special consideration. If a batch spans 3 production shifts, the media fill design must cover the shift changes of all shifts, otherwise FDA will consider the contamination risk at shift change unevaluated. This is also one of the reasons each shift needs its own semiannual media fill: it ensures that the working habits and personnel behavior unique to each shift are validated.

Section IX.A.5–6 — Line Speed & Environmental Conditions (pp. 23–24)

Original Text

The media fill program should adequately address the range of line speeds employed during production. Each media fill run should evaluate a single line speed, and the speed chosen should be justified. For example, use of high line speed is often most appropriate in the evaluation of manufacturing processes characterized by frequent interventions or a significant degree of manual manipulation. Use of slow line speed is generally appropriate for evaluating manufacturing processes with prolonged exposure of the sterile drug product and containers/closures in the aseptic area.

Media fills should be adequately representative of the conditions under which actual manufacturing operations are conducted. An inaccurate assessment (making the process appear cleaner than it actually is) can result from conducting a media fill under extraordinary air particulate and microbial quality, or under production controls and precautions taken in preparation for the media fill. To the extent standard operating procedures permit stressful conditions (e.g., maximum number of personnel present and elevated activity level), it is important that media fills include analogous challenges. Stressful conditions do not include artificially created environmental extremes, such as reconfiguration of HVAC systems to operate at worst-case limits.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: High Speed vs. Slow Speed. Which Is the Worst Case?

This is an important and counterintuitive point:

  • High line speed as worst case: for processes with frequent interventions (for example, frequent stopper jams requiring manual clearance), the intervention rate is higher at high speed and each intervention is a contamination opportunity, so high speed is the worst case.
  • Slow line speed as worst case: for processes where product is exposed in the aseptic area for a long time, slow speed means each vial stays on the filling line longer, with more environmental exposure.

The media fill protocol must choose the line speed that represents the "worst case" for the process characteristics, and document the reason for the choice.

Note: "A media fill must not look cleaner than routine production"

FDA points to a common problem: to make sure a media fill passes, firms carry out special cleaning, reduce personnel, or strengthen environmental controls beforehand, so that the media fill environment is better than normal production. As a result, the media fill results cannot truly reflect the contamination risk of commercial production.

The correct approach is that media fill environmental conditions should be equivalent to normal commercial production, including number of personnel, activity level, and equipment status. This does not include deliberately worsening the environment (such as shutting down part of the HVAC), which would be artificially creating a worst case and is not reasonable.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

CDMO clients sometimes ask for "extra cleaning on the media fill day" to make sure the result looks good. This request must be declined and explained: FDA expressly opposes the practice, and if FDA finds that environmental monitoring data on the media fill day are clearly better than on routine production days, it will question the representativeness of the media fill and may even require it to be repeated. Keeping environmental conditions on the media fill day the same as on normal production days is the right approach to protect both parties.

Section IX.A.7 — Media (pp. 24–25)

Original Text

In general, a microbiological growth medium, such as soybean casein digest medium, should be used. Use of anaerobic growth media (e.g., fluid thioglycollate medium) should be considered in special circumstances. The media selected should be demonstrated to promote growth of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, and yeast and mold (e.g., USP indicator organisms).

Growth promotion units should be inoculated with a <100 CFU challenge. If the growth promotion testing fails, the origin of any contamination found during the simulation should nonetheless be investigated and the media fill promptly repeated.

The production process should be accurately simulated using media and conditions that optimize detection of any microbiological contamination. Each unit should be filled with an appropriate quantity and type of microbial growth medium to contact the inner container closure surfaces (when the unit is inverted or thoroughly swirled) and permit visual detection of microbial growth.

If the medium is handled properly and is promptly followed by the cleaning, sanitizing, and sterilization of equipment, subsequently processed products are not likely to be compromised.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Scientific Basis for Medium Selection

Soybean Casein Digest Medium (SCDM), also known as Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), is FDA's first choice because it supports growth of a broad range of microorganisms:

  • Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus)
  • Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli)
  • Yeasts (e.g., Candida albicans)
  • Molds (e.g., Aspergillus brasiliensis)

Anaerobic medium (Fluid Thioglycollate Medium, FTM) needs to be considered only in special cases (such as anaerobic processes or complex containers with many rubber parts).

Note: The growth promotion test is the quality gate of a media fill

FDA requires that every batch of media fill medium pass a growth promotion test using a low-level challenge of <100 CFU, to confirm that the medium really does support microbial growth. This ensures that:

  • A negative result (no growth) means there was truly no contamination, rather than that the medium failed
  • If the growth promotion test fails, even a media fill that appears free of contamination cannot be used as evidence that the process is in control, and the run must be repeated

Analogy: the medium is the "contamination detective's magnifying glass"

If the detective's magnifying glass is itself flawed (the medium cannot support microbial growth), then "no contamination found" does not mean a clean bill of health; it only means the tool has failed. The growth promotion test is the necessary step that periodically confirms the "detective's magnifying glass" is working properly.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

When medium is used in place of drug product, what worries firms most is whether the medium will contaminate the equipment and affect the next batch. FDA's answer is clear: as long as appropriate cleaning, disinfection, and, where necessary, sterilization are performed, subsequent production is unlikely to be affected. This shows that cleaning validation of the equipment after a media fill is also an indispensable step; if the cleaning SOP has been validated, there is no need for additional concern. This reminds CDMOs to make sure the media fill SOP and the cleaning validation procedure connect seamlessly.

Section IX.A.8 — Incubation and Examination (pp. 25–26)

Original Text

Media units should be incubated under conditions adequate to detect microorganisms that might otherwise be difficult to culture. Incubation conditions should be established in accord with the following general guidelines:

  • Incubation temperature should be suitable for recovery of bioburden and environmental isolates and should at no time be outside the range of 20–35°C. Incubation temperature should be maintained within ±2.5°C of the target temperature.
  • Incubation time should not be less than 14 days. If two temperatures are used, the units should be incubated for at least 7 days at each temperature (starting with the lower temperature).

Each media-filled unit should be examined by personnel with appropriate education, training, and experience. All suspect units should be brought to the immediate attention of the QC microbiologist. To allow for visual detection, we recommend substituting clear containers for amber or other opaque containers.

Written procedures regarding aseptic interventions should be clear and specific (e.g., intervention type; quantity of units removed), providing for consistent production practices. If written procedures and batch documentation are adequate to describe an associated clearance, the intervention units removed during media fills do not need to be incubated. In no case should more units be removed during a media fill intervention than would be cleared during a production run.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Handling of Units Removed After Interventions, a Regulatory Gray Area

Whether units removed after an intervention need to be incubated is an important detail of media fill management:

  • Conditions where incubation is not required: a written SOP specifies the number of units to be removed after an intervention, and the batch record confirms compliance.
  • Cases where incubation is required: the SOP lacks specific instructions, or the number removed exceeds what the SOP specifies.
  • Strict limit: the number of units removed after interventions in a media fill must not exceed the number that would be removed in an actual production batch.

FDA's concern is that firms might remove large numbers of "intervention units" to deliberately keep potentially contaminated units out of the incubation count.

Note: Handling of damaged units found after incubation

If damaged (non-integral) units are found during incubation, they must not simply be excluded from the count. Any exclusion must be fully justified, and the cause of the deviation explained in the media fill report. If there is a correlation between the damage and microbial contamination, an in-depth investigation is required. This requirement prevents firms from using "damage" as a reason to remove suspected positive units.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

A well-designed media fill intervention log is an essential tool for a CDMO. It records the type, timing, and number of units removed for each intervention, and is checked item by item against the media fill report. This is not only an FDA expectation but also a powerful document for showing clients how well the process is under control. When an FDA investigator goes through the media fill batch record, clear intervention records greatly reduce the likelihood of further questions.

Section IX.A.9 — Interpretation of Test Results (pp. 26–27)

Original Text

Any contaminated unit should be considered objectionable and investigated. The microorganisms should be identified to species level. The investigation should survey the possible causes of contamination. Any failure investigation should assess the impact on commercial drugs produced on the line since the last media fill.

Whenever contamination exists in a media fill run, it should be considered indicative of a potential sterility assurance problem, regardless of run size. Modern aseptic processing operations in suitably designed facilities have demonstrated a capability of meeting contamination levels approaching zero. Recommended criteria for assessing state of aseptic line control are as follows:

  • When filling fewer than 5,000 units: no contaminated units should be detected. One (1) contaminated unit is considered cause for revalidation, following an investigation.
  • When filling from 5,000 to 10,000 units: One (1) contaminated unit should result in an investigation, including consideration of a repeat media fill. Two (2) contaminated units are considered cause for revalidation.
  • When filling more than 10,000 units: One (1) contaminated unit should result in an investigation. Two (2) contaminated units are considered cause for revalidation.

A firm's use of media fill acceptance criteria allowing infrequent contamination does not mean that a distributed lot of drug product may contain a nonsterile unit. Invalidation of a media fill run should be a rare occurrence.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: "Near-Zero Contamination" Is the Standard for Modern Aseptic Processing

FDA states clearly that for a modern, well-designed aseptic processing facility, the media fill contamination rate should be close to zero. The acceptance criterion that allows "1 contaminated unit to be investigated" is not a normal operating goal for the firm but the minimum threshold of regulatory tolerance.

A more important principle is this: even if the contamination rate has not reached the threshold that triggers revalidation, repeated sporadic contamination is itself a warning sign that the process is out of control, and a trend investigation must be started rather than taking comfort that "the acceptance criteria are still met".

Note: Invalidating a media fill must be done with caution

FDA states that invalidation of a media fill should be a rare event. A media fill may be aborted only in circumstances where a commercial batch would also need to be handled in the same way. This prevents firms from "scrapping" unfavorable media fill results for various reasons without recording them. Any invalidation requires full documented support and justification.

Analogy: media fill acceptance criteria are not commercial batch release criteria

The media fill allows "conditional acceptance" of 1 contaminated unit, but this does not mean a commercial batch can be shipped containing one non-sterile unit. The two are entirely different concepts: a media fill is a process validation tool whose acceptance criteria reflect the statistical tolerance of a process capability assessment, whereas a commercial batch must be sterile in every single unit. FDA draws this important distinction explicitly here to avoid confusion among firms.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

The depth of a CDMO's investigation into a positive media fill is often a focus of FDA inspections. The investigation report should include at least: identification of the contaminating organism to species level, a systematic analysis of possible contamination sources (personnel, equipment, environment, materials), an impact assessment of all commercial batches since the previous media fill (including whether additional testing or a recall assessment of commercial batches is needed), and the specific content and verification plan of the corrective and preventive actions (CAPA). An incomplete investigation report is one of the most frequent 483 observations raised in FDA inspections.

Section IX.B — Filtration Efficacy (pp. 27–28)

Original Text

Filtration is a common method of sterilizing drug product solutions. A sterilizing grade filter should be validated to reproducibly remove viable microorganisms from the process stream, producing a sterile effluent. Currently, such filters usually have a rated pore size of 0.2 µm or smaller. Use of redundant sterilizing filters should be considered in many cases.

Validation should include microbiological challenges to simulate worst-case production conditions. Product bioburden should be evaluated when selecting a suitable challenge microorganism. The microorganism Brevundimonas diminuta (ATCC 19146) when properly grown, harvested and used, is a common challenge microorganism for 0.2 µm rated filters because of its small size (0.3 µm mean diameter). A challenge concentration of at least 107 organisms per cm2 of effective filtration area should generally be used.

Direct inoculation into the drug formulation is the preferred method because it provides an assessment of the effect of drug product on the filter matrix and on the challenge organism.

Factors that can affect filter performance include: (1) viscosity and surface tension, (2) pH, (3) compatibility of formulation components with the filter, (4) pressures, (5) flow rates, (6) maximum use time, (7) temperature, (8) osmolality, and (9) effects of hydraulic shock. Filter validation should be conducted using the worst-case conditions.

Integrity testing of the filter(s) should be routinely performed post-use. Forward flow and bubble point tests are two integrity tests that can be used.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is Brevundimonas diminuta Chosen as the Challenge Organism?

The choice of Brevundimonas diminuta (formerly Pseudomonas diminuta, ATCC 19146) has clear scientific logic:

  • Small size: its average diameter is only 0.3 µm, just above the 0.2 µm membrane pore rating, making it one of the smallest common bacteria that a 0.2 µm membrane can retain, and so it represents the most challenging penetration risk
  • Growth stability: under standard conditions it grows reproducibly to a uniform size distribution, ensuring the reproducibility of the challenge
  • Non-pathogenic: safe to handle in the laboratory

A challenge concentration of ≥ 10⁷ organisms/cm² of filter area provides a safety margin far above the bioburden expected in production.

Note: Integrity testing must be performed after use

Many firms perform integrity testing only before use (such as the bubble point test or forward flow test) and overlook the importance of post-use testing. FDA expressly requires integrity testing after filtration, because a pre-use test cannot detect membrane damage that occurs during filtration (such as micropore rupture caused by excessive differential pressure). If the post-use integrity test fails, the sterility of that filtered batch is in question, and a deviation investigation and risk assessment must be started.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

Filtration efficacy validation is often the validation item most easily overlooked when a CDMO takes on a client's project. Common problems include: using validation data supplied by the client without confirming that the data apply to the CDMO's specific process conditions (such as pressure, temperature, and filter lot), or using a membrane from a different lot/supplier than the one used in the validation study. FDA states clearly that validation data must be applicable to the user's specific product and conditions of use, since filtration performance can differ significantly under different conditions. A CDMO needs a thorough filter identity verification procedure to ensure the production filter matches the validated filter (same polymer, same pore size specification).

Section IX.C — Sterilization of Equipment, Containers, and Closures (pp. 28–30)

Original Text

Equipment surfaces that contact sterilized drug product or its sterilized containers or closures must be sterile (§ 211.67 and 211.113). Sterility of aseptic processing equipment should normally be maintained by sterilization between each batch.

Validation studies should be conducted to demonstrate the efficacy of the sterilization cycle. The specific load configurations, as well as biological indicator and temperature sensor locations, should be documented in validation records.

It is important to remove air from the autoclave chamber as part of a steam sterilization cycle. The insulating properties of air interfere with the ability of steam to transfer its energy to the load.

Empty chamber studies evaluate numerous locations throughout a sterilizing unit to confirm uniformity of conditions (e.g., temperature, pressure). Heat penetration studies should be performed using the established sterilizer loads.

A sterility assurance level (SAL) of 10-6 or better should be demonstrated for a sterilization process. Cycle specifications should be based on the delivery of adequate lethality to the slowest to heat locations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Does an SAL of 10⁻⁶ Mean?

A Sterility Assurance Level (SAL) of 10⁻⁶ means that, at most, one surviving microorganism is allowed per 1 million sterilized items. This is an extremely demanding standard, equivalent to a "one in a million failure rate".

To achieve an SAL of 10⁻⁶, the sterilization cycle must be designed around the location hardest to sterilize (the cold spot), ensuring that even the position where heat transfer is most difficult receives sufficient lethal heat. This is also why heat penetration studies and biological indicator testing are indispensable: data from temperature sensors alone are not enough.

Note: Air in steam is a deadly validation killer

Autoclave sterilization works by transferring energy through saturated steam. Air is a thermal insulator. If air remains in the chamber, items surrounded by air (such as sealed tubing or folded fabric packs) can be several degrees cooler than the chamber temperature. The reading may appear to reach 121°C, while the cold spot is actually only 115°C or even lower, so the SAL target cannot be achieved at all. Air removal steps (such as a pre-vacuum program) are therefore critical to steam sterilization cycle design, and their effectiveness must be demonstrated in validation.

Core Concept: Special Challenges of SIP Systems

Sterilize-in-place (SIP) systems (such as sterilizing the piping of a large compounding tank) face a particular validation challenge: a filter installed in the line creates a significant pressure drop across it, so the temperature downstream of the filter is clearly lower than upstream. FDA recommends placing biological indicators downstream of the filter to ensure the validation data reflect the location hardest to sterilize.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

The most common audit issues for CDMOs in sterilization validation include:

  • Load configuration changes without revalidation: the client changes to a different container size or load density, but no assessment is made of whether the heat penetration study must be repeated
  • Biological indicator placement that does not represent the worst case: they are placed where they have "always been placed", rather than re-evaluating the cold spot against the latest cavity mapping results
  • Incomplete instrument calibration records: calibration records for the temperature sensors used in validation are missing or out of date before and after the study

Building a sterilization validation matrix indexed by load configuration, recording the validation status, cold spot location, and biological indicator placement for each configuration, is the most effective reference tool for an inspection.

Section IX.C.2 — Equipment Controls and Instrument Calibration (pp. 30–31)

Original Text

For both validation and routine process control, the reliability of the data generated by sterilization cycle monitoring devices should be considered to be of the utmost importance. Devices that measure cycle parameters should be routinely calibrated. Written procedures should be established to ensure these devices are maintained in a calibrated state.

  • Temperature and pressure monitoring devices for heat sterilization should be calibrated at suitable intervals. The sensing devices used for validation studies should be calibrated before and after validation runs.
  • Dwell time monitoring devices should be periodically calibrated.
  • The microbial count of a biological indicator should be confirmed. If the reliability of a vendor's Certificate of Analysis is established through an appropriate qualification program, the D-value can be accepted in lieu of confirmatory testing of each lot.
  • For dry heat depyrogenation tunnels, devices used to measure belt speed should be routinely calibrated. Bacterial endotoxin challenges should be appropriately prepared and measured.

Equipment control should be ensured through placement of measuring devices at those control points that are most likely to rapidly detect unexpected process variability. Routine evaluation of sterilizer performance-indicating attributes, such as equilibrium (come up) time is important.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Is the D-value?

The D-value is a core parameter of sterilization science: the time (in minutes) at a specified temperature needed to reduce a microbial population by one log (that is, to kill 90% of the organisms).

For example, D₁₂₁ = 1.5 minutes means that at 121°C the number of microorganisms falls tenfold every 1.5 minutes. If the initial bioburden is 10⁶, reaching an SAL of 10⁻⁶ requires killing a further 12 log orders, so the time needed = 12 × the D-value. This is the scientific basis for calculating the sterilization cycle time.

Note: Validation sensors must be calibrated both before and after the study

This is a frequently overlooked requirement that FDA expressly states: temperature sensors used in validation studies must be calibrated both before the validation begins and after it is completed. If calibration is done only before the study, drift of the sensor during the study cannot be ruled out; the post-study calibration confirms that the temperature data are reliable throughout the validation. If the post-study calibration shows a deviation beyond specification, the validity of the entire validation result must be re-evaluated.

Analogy: "equilibration time (come-up time)" is like preheating an oven

When you put dough in the oven, you do not start timing the moment you plug it in; you wait until the oven has actually reached the set temperature and stabilized before counting the baking time. The "come-up time" in autoclave sterilization is the same concept: after steam enters the chamber, it takes a period of time for the temperature at every location in the chamber to reach the target uniformly, and this "ramp-up and stabilization time" must not be counted in the effective sterilization time. If the come-up time is abnormally prolonged, it usually indicates aging equipment, valve problems, or other engineering anomalies that require investigation.

CDMO/COO Operational Application

For a CDMO, maintaining the calibration system for sterilization instruments (temperature, pressure, time, belt speed) is a standard item in FDA inspections. It is advisable to establish an Instrument Calibration Master Plan and make sure all validation instruments have an independent calibration frequency at a higher standard than production instruments. In addition, if a client supplies special biological indicators (self-prepared or from a non-commercial source), make sure their D-value has been verified through an appropriate qualification procedure; the supplier CoA cannot be used as a substitute unless a supplier qualification program has been completed.

Section Summary

  • Time limits (Section VIII): every aseptic process stage (compounding → filtration → filling → storage) needs time limits supported by bioburden and endotoxin data; deviations must be documented and scientifically justified as not affecting quality; filtration time limits must protect against both microbial penetration and accumulation of upstream contaminants.
  • Process simulation design (IX.A.1): a media fill must include the 13 design elements, with worst case as the core, including the longest shift, all types of interventions, shift changes, and maintenance activities, and with complete batch records prepared; a media fill must not be used to justify high-risk practices.
  • Frequency and number of runs (IX.A.2): initial validation requires 3 consecutive successful runs; routine revalidation is semiannual for each line; all authorized personnel must participate at least once a year; major changes or failures require evaluation and may trigger additional media fills or revalidation.
  • Duration and size (IX.A.3–4): manual processes need full-duration simulation; the starting run size is 5,000–10,000 units; manually intensive operations need close to the full batch size; isolators may use smaller runs; lyophilization media must not be frozen and must remain aerobic.
  • Line speed, environment, and media (IX.A.5–7): test a single line speed in each media fill and justify it in writing; environmental conditions must be equivalent to normal production (no embellishment); the preferred medium is TSB/SCDM, with a growth promotion test at <100 CFU; the fill volume must contact the container walls and allow visual observation.
  • Incubation and interpretation (IX.A.8–9): incubate at 20–35°C, controlled within ±2.5°C, for at least 14 days (7 days at each of two temperatures); the goal is zero contamination; <5K units: 1 contaminated unit means revalidation; 5K–10K units: 1 requires investigation and 2 mean revalidation; contaminating organisms must be identified to species level; the impact on commercial batches must be assessed.
  • Filtration efficacy validation (IX.B): a 0.2 µm sterilizing-grade membrane requires a microbiological challenge with B. diminuta ATCC 19146 (≥10⁷/cm²); direct inoculation of the formulation is preferred; integrity testing (bubble point/forward flow) must be performed after use; validation data must apply to the user's specific product and conditions.
  • Sterilization validation (IX.C): equipment must be sterilized for every batch; validation must cover three layers, namely the cavity study, the heat penetration study, and the biological indicator challenge; an SAL of 10⁻⁶ must be achieved; steam sterilization must remove air; instruments must be calibrated both before and after validation; confirmation of the D-value is a prerequisite for using biological indicators.
  • Core CDMO risk points: time limits without supporting data, embellished media fill environmental conditions, shortened incubation time, improper handling of intervention units, missing filter identity verification, sterilization load changes without revalidation, and incomplete calibration records for validation instruments are all common FDA 483 observations.

A. Environmental Monitoring — 1. General Written Program

Original Text

In aseptic processing, one of the most important laboratory controls is the environmental monitoring program. This program provides meaningful information on the quality of the aseptic processing environment (e.g., when a given batch is being manufactured) as well as environmental trends of ancillary clean areas. Environmental monitoring should promptly identify potential routes of contamination, allowing for implementation of corrections before product contamination occurs (211.42 and 211.113).

Evaluating the quality of air and surfaces in the cleanroom environment should start with a well-defined written program and scientifically sound methods. The monitoring program should cover all production shifts and include air, floors, walls, and equipment surfaces, including the critical surfaces that come in contact with the product, container, and closures.

It is important that locations posing the most microbiological risk to the product be a key part of the program. It is especially important to monitor the microbiological quality of the critical area to determine whether or not aseptic conditions are maintained during filling and closing activities. Air and surface samples should be taken at the locations where significant activity or product exposure occurs during production.

Critical surface sampling should be performed at the conclusion of the aseptic processing operation to avoid direct contact with sterile surfaces during processing. Detection of microbial contamination on a critical site would not necessarily result in batch rejection. The contaminated critical site sample should prompt an investigation of operational information and data that includes an awareness of the potential for a low incidence of false positives.

Environmental monitoring methods do not always recover microorganisms present in the sampled area. Because false negatives can occur, consecutive growth results are only one type of adverse trend. Increased incidence of contamination over a given period is an equal or more significant trend to be tracked. In the absence of any adverse trend, a single result above an action level should trigger an evaluation and a determination about whether remedial measures may be appropriate.

All environmental monitoring locations should be described in SOPs with sufficient detail to allow for reproducible sampling. Written SOPs should also address:

  • (1) frequency of sampling
  • (2) when the samples are taken (during or at the conclusion of operations)
  • (3) duration of sampling
  • (4) sample size (e.g., surface area, air volume)
  • (5) specific sampling equipment and techniques
  • (6) alert and action levels
  • (7) appropriate response to deviations from alert or action levels

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Purpose of the EM Program

An EM program is more than a compliance formality; it is the "real-time health tracking system" of the aseptic process. FDA states clearly that the EM program has two core functions:

  • Real-time information: confirming that the environment meets aseptic conditions during the production of a specific batch
  • Trend data: tracking, over the long term, the microbial status of supporting clean areas (corridors, gowning rooms)

Most important is being "proactive": identifying potential risk pathways through trends before contamination occurs. This is fundamentally different from the after-the-fact sterility test of the product: EM is a process monitoring tool, not a substitute for batch release.

Analogy: An EM Program Is Like a Hospital's Infection Control System

Picture a hospital operating room: you do not wait for a patient to become infected before disinfecting. Instead, you continuously monitor colony counts in the environment, sample the air periodically, establish a "normal baseline", and investigate the cause immediately when readings deviate. The logic of an aseptic-process EM program is exactly the same: prevention-oriented and trend-driven.

Key Point: Timing of Critical Surface Sampling

FDA expressly requires that sampling of critical surfaces be performed "after" the aseptic operation is finished, not midway through it, to avoid the act of sampling itself contaminating sterile contact surfaces. This is often misunderstood in CDMO operations: some companies insert contact plate sampling in the middle of aseptic filling, which actually creates a contamination risk, and FDA auditors are very familiar with this mistake.

Key Point: A Negative Result Does Not Mean Safe

EM methods are inherently imperfect in detection efficiency, so false negatives are a structural problem, not an exception. FDA therefore requires tracking not only "consecutive positives" but also:

  • An increase in contamination frequency at the same location (even if every result is below the action level)
  • Spatial spread trends across shifts and rooms
  • Qualitative changes in the microbial flora (a shift from common skin flora to environmental or sporeforming organisms)

CDMO Implementation: The Seven Elements of the SOP

FDA explicitly lists seven elements that an EM SOP must cover. Auditors typically check them one by one during an inspection. The following are common CDMO shortcomings:

  • The sampling location map is not detailed enough to be reproducible → add a floor plan with coordinates
  • The SOP says only "sample after operations" without defining "within how long after operations" → add a specific time limit
  • No documented scientific basis for the alert/action level settings → add a report supporting the settings
  • The deviation response procedure does not distinguish a single-point excursion from a trend excursion → introduce a tiered response process
A.2 Establishing Levels and a Trending Program

Original Text

Microbiological monitoring levels should be established based on the relationship of the sampled location to the operation. The levels should be based on the need to maintain adequate microbiological control throughout the entire sterile manufacturing facility. One should also consider environmental monitoring data from historical databases, media fills, cleanroom qualification, and sanitization studies, in developing monitoring levels. Data from similar operations can also be helpful in setting action and alert levels, especially for a new operation.

Environmental monitoring data will provide information on the quality of the manufacturing environment. Each individual sample result should be evaluated for its significance by comparison to the alert or action levels. Averaging of results can mask unacceptable localized conditions. A result at the alert level urges attention to the approaching action conditions. A result at the action level should prompt a more thorough investigation.

Trend reports should include data generated by location, shift, room, operator, or other parameters. The quality control unit should be responsible for producing specialized data reports (e.g., a search on a particular isolate over a year period) with the goal of investigating results beyond established levels and identifying any appropriate follow-up actions. Significant changes in microbial flora should be considered in the review of the ongoing environmental monitoring data.

Written procedures should define the system whereby the most responsible managers are regularly informed and updated on trends and investigations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Essential Difference Between Alert Level and Action Level

This is one of the concepts most frequently tested in FDA inspections and must be thoroughly understood:

Important Warning: Do Not Average EM Results

FDA expressly warns that EM data from different locations must not be averaged (Averaging of results can mask unacceptable localized conditions). This is a common 483 observation in audits: some companies average the CFU counts from several sampling points in the same room before evaluating them, so that a severely contaminated local spot is "diluted" and masked.
Every result at every sampling location should be evaluated independently.

Analogy: Levels Are Like a Car's Dashboard Warning Lights

The Alert Level is like the orange light that comes on when the fuel gauge nears a quarter tank: you can keep driving, but you should look for a gas station. The Action Level is like the fuel gauge turning red: you must stop and refuel immediately, or you may break down. And "averaging" is like averaging the driver's fuel gauge readings before deciding whether to refuel, which completely defeats the purpose of a real-time warning.

Practical Recommendations for CDMO Trend Reports

FDA expects trend reports to cover multiple dimensions: location, shift, room, operator, and more. A CDMO's EM database should support the following types of queries:

  • The trend of a single operator's personnel sampling results over the past year
  • The time distribution of a specific organism (such as Bacillus cereus)
  • Shift-to-shift difference analysis (contamination frequency on night shift vs day shift)
  • A comparison of environmental data before and after media fill batches

In addition, FDA emphasizes that management must receive trend updates regularly, which means EM data is not just QC's job but a basis for facility management decisions.

A.3 Disinfection Efficacy

Original Text

The suitability, efficacy, and limitations of disinfecting agents and procedures should be assessed. The effectiveness of these disinfectants and procedures should be measured by their ability to ensure that potential contaminants are adequately removed from surfaces.

To prevent introduction of contamination, disinfectants should be sterile, appropriately handled in suitable (e.g., sterile) containers and used for no longer than the predefined period specified by written procedures. Routinely used disinfectants should be effective against the normal microbial vegetative flora recovered from the facility.

Many common disinfectants are ineffective against spores. For example, 70 percent isopropyl alcohol is ineffective against Bacillus spp. spores. Therefore, a sound disinfectant program also includes a sporicidal agent, used according to a written schedule and when environmental data suggest the presence of sporeforming organisms.

Disinfection procedures should be described in sufficient detail (e.g., preparation, work sequence, contact time) to enable reproducibility. Once the procedures are established, their adequacy should be evaluated using a routine environmental monitoring program. If indicated, microorganisms associated with adverse trends can be investigated as to their sensitivity to the disinfectants employed in the cleanroom in which the organisms were isolated.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three-Tier Structure of a Disinfectant Program

A disinfectant program that meets FDA expectations needs at least three tiers of protection:

  • Tier 1: Routine disinfectant: baseline disinfection against common organisms (e.g., 70% IPA)
  • Tier 2: Rotational disinfectant: a periodic change of mode of action to prevent strain adaptation (e.g., switching to a quaternary ammonium compound)
  • Tier 3: Sporicide: used periodically or triggered by EM data (e.g., hydrogen peroxide vapor, chlorine dioxide)

Important Warning: 70% IPA Cannot Kill Bacillus Spores

This is a rare point where FDA cites a specific example in the guidance, which shows how seriously FDA takes it in inspections. The genus Bacillus (such as B. cereus and B. subtilis) forms spores that are highly resistant to the environment, and 70% IPA is completely ineffective against them.

If EM data show Bacillus spp., this is an immediate signal to use a sporicide. If a facility's disinfection program has no sporicide rotation procedure, FDA will almost certainly issue a 483 observation.

Analogy: Disinfectant Rotation Is Like Designing an Antibiotic Course

Using only one disinfectant over a long period is like always giving the same antibiotic: bacteria "learn" and develop resistance. A rotation program uses different modes of action against microorganisms, preventing particular strains from establishing ecological dominance in the cleanroom. Sporicide use is the "intensive treatment for when ordinary agents are not enough".

Audit Points for a CDMO Disinfectant Program

When reviewing a disinfectant program, inspectors typically check:

  • Confirmation of sterility of the disinfectant (is there a COA or a record of in-house filter sterilization?)
  • The disinfectant's expiry and preparation records (preparation date, expiry date, preparer)
  • Whether contact time is defined in writing and can be verified
  • Whether the trigger conditions for sporicide use are clearly defined (e.g., a fixed quarterly application plus triggering by positive EM results)
  • Whether the validation report (disinfectant efficacy study) covers representative organisms
  • When a particular strain appears in EM trends, whether its susceptibility to the current disinfectants is evaluated
A.4a Monitoring Methods — Surface Monitoring

Original Text

Acceptable methods for monitoring the microbiological quality of the environment include:

a. Surface Monitoring

Environmental monitoring involves sampling various surfaces for microbiological quality. For example, product contact surfaces, floors, walls, and equipment should be tested on a regular basis. Touch plates, swabs, and contact plates can be used for such tests.

Tutorial Commentary

Cleaning Reminder After Surface Sampling

After sampling in the aseptic area with RODAC plates (contact plates), the sampled site must be disinfected, because the culture medium itself can provide nutrients for microbial growth, and any residue left on cleanroom surfaces becomes a contamination risk. This step is often omitted from SOPs and is a common source of audit findings.

A.4b Monitoring Methods — Active Air Monitoring

Original Text

b. Active Air Monitoring

Assessing microbial quality of air should involve the use of active devices including but not limited to impaction, centrifugal, and membrane (or gelatin) samplers. Each device has certain advantages and disadvantages, although all allow testing of the number of organisms per volume of air sampled. We recommend that such devices be used during each production shift to evaluate aseptic processing areas at carefully chosen locations.

Manufacturers should be aware of a device's air monitoring capabilities, and the air sampler should be evaluated for its suitability for use in an aseptic environment based on:

  • collection efficiency
  • cleanability
  • ability to be sterilized
  • disruption of unidirectional airflow

Manufacturers should ensure that such devices are calibrated and used according to appropriate procedures. The volume of air sampled should be sufficient to yield meaningful measurements of air quality in a given environment.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Implications of the Four Equipment Evaluation Criteria

FDA requires active sampling equipment to be evaluated on four dimensions. Common problems seen in CDMOs include:

  • Collection Efficiency: detection sensitivity differs greatly between devices in low-concentration environments, which matters especially in ISO Class 5 environments
  • Cleanability: the equipment must be cleanable inside the cleanroom with no dead spots; complex designs often make it hard to ensure cleaning validation
  • Ability to be Sterilized: equipment entering a Grade A/ISO 5 area, or its product-contact parts, must be capable of being sterilized
  • Disruption of Unidirectional Airflow: the size and shape of the equipment must not disrupt UDAF (unidirectional airflow), or it will create turbulence and contamination points

Operating Rules for CDMO Active Sampling

Active air sampling should be performed during every production shift; FDA emphasizes "every shift" rather than every day or every batch. For a CDMO running 24-hour continuous production, this means that night shift also needs trained operators to perform EM sampling, and night-shift data must be reviewed to the same standard; monitoring frequency on night shift cannot be reduced because of a staffing shortage.

Equipment calibration records (flow rate calibration, timer calibration) must be complete, and the calibration interval must be defined in writing. An inspector finding overdue calibration on equipment that is still in use is one of the situations that can draw an immediate 483.

A.4c Monitoring Methods — Passive Air Monitoring (Settling Plates)

Original Text

c. Passive Air Monitoring (Settling Plates)

Another method is the use of passive air samplers, such as settling plates (petri dishes containing nutrient growth medium exposed to the environment). Because only microorganisms that settle onto the agar surface are detected, settling plates can be used as qualitative, or semi-quantitative, air monitors.

Their value in critical areas will be enhanced by ensuring that plates are positioned in locations posing the greatest risk of product contamination. As part of methods validation, the quality control laboratory should evaluate what media exposure conditions optimize recovery of low levels of environmental isolates. Exposure conditions should preclude desiccation (e.g., caused by lengthy sampling periods and/or high airflows), which inhibits recovery of microorganisms.

The data generated by passive air sampling can be useful when considered in combination with results from other types of air samples.

Tutorial Commentary

Settling Plates vs Active Sampling

Settling plates "capture only organisms that settle naturally": they rely on gravity settling and cannot actively capture airborne particles, so FDA expressly characterizes them as qualitative or semi-quantitative tools that cannot replace active sampling as the primary monitoring method.

But settling plates have one advantage that active samplers cannot offer: they can be exposed continuously for the entire operation, capturing settling events at any point during the whole batch production, whereas active sampling usually lasts only a few minutes.

The Desiccation Problem: A Frequently Overlooked Trap

FDA specifically warns about desiccation: in a Grade A area, the high-velocity HEPA-filtered airflow can dry out the medium surface of a settling plate within hours, so that even if organisms land on it, they cannot grow, producing a false negative.

Solution: determine the maximum safe exposure time for each location during method validation; results from settling plates exposed longer than this limit may be meaningless. Some companies use lidded or half-open designs, or media containing neutralizers, to slow desiccation.

Settling Plate Placement Strategy

FDA stresses that settling plates should be placed at "the locations of highest product contamination risk". In filling operations, this usually means:

  • The area directly beneath the filling needles (but without interfering with the operation)
  • Near the stoppering area
  • Both sides of the area where the operator's hands move most frequently
  • The start and end points of robotic intervention paths

The settling plate location map must be included in the EM SOP, and actual placement must match the map; any deviation must be recorded and explained.

B. Microbiological Media and Identification

Original Text

Characterization of recovered microorganisms provides vital information for the environmental monitoring program. Environmental isolates often correlate with the contaminants found in a media fill or product sterility testing failure, and the overall environmental picture provides valuable information for an investigation.

Monitoring critical and immediately surrounding clean areas as well as personnel should include routine identification of microorganisms to the species (or, where appropriate, genus) level. In some cases, environmental trending data have revealed migration of microorganisms into the aseptic processing room from either uncontrolled or lesser controlled areas.

Genotypic methods have been shown to be more accurate and precise than traditional biochemical and phenotypic techniques. These methods are especially valuable for investigations into failures (e.g., sterility test; media fill contamination).

The microbiological culture media used in environmental monitoring should be validated as capable of detecting fungi (i.e., yeasts and molds) as well as bacteria and incubated at appropriate conditions of time and temperature:

  • Total aerobic bacterial count: 30–35°C for 48–72 hours
  • Total combined yeast and mold count: 20–25°C for 5–7 days

Incoming lots of environmental monitoring media should be tested for their ability to reliably recover microorganisms. Growth promotion testing should be performed on all lots of prepared media. Where appropriate, inactivating agents should be used to prevent inhibition of growth by cleanroom disinfectants or product residuals (e.g., antibiotics).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is Organism Identification Important?

Recording only the "CFU count" is not enough: knowing which organism it is allows you to judge:

  • Where the contamination comes from (skin flora? environmental flora? an external source?)
  • Whether it is the same organism found in a media fill or a sterility test failure (a key forensic tool)
  • Whether organisms are migrating from lower-control areas into the aseptic processing area
  • Whether the current disinfectants are effective against this organism

FDA requires routine identification to the species level, not just the genus level, because even different species within the same genus can differ greatly in pathogenicity, disinfectant resistance, and value for tracing the source.

Choosing Between Genotypic and Phenotypic Identification

Routine EM monitoring: traditional biochemical/phenotypic methods (such as API strips and VITEK) are acceptable; they are low-cost and fast, suitable for large volumes of routine identification.

When investigating failures: FDA recommends genotypic identification (such as 16S rRNA sequencing or MALDI-TOF), which is more accurate and allows strain-level comparison, confirming whether the organism contaminating a media fill and the EM isolate come from the same source, which is key evidence in an investigation report.

Incoming Media Control: Growth Promotion Testing in CDMO Practice

Each received lot of EM media (such as TSA plates and SDA plates) must undergo growth promotion testing (GPT) to confirm the medium's recovery capability. Common CDMO problems include:

  • The GPT strains do not include organisms commonly found in the facility (only the USP standard strains are used, with no environmental isolates added)
  • No neutralizing agent is added in sampling environments with residues of antibiotic products (such as lecithin + polysorbate 80 to neutralize quaternary ammonium compounds), resulting in false negatives
  • Media are not requalified during their shelf life, and no use-by time is set after opening

Special note: if the CDMO also manufactures antibiotic-containing products, antibiotic residues are very likely to inhibit colony growth on EM media. A suitable neutralizing agent must be added to the medium, and its neutralizing effectiveness must be validated.

Quick Reference: Incubation Conditions

Note: the conditions above come from the FDA guidance and are consistent with the settings in USP <61> and EP 2.6.12, but the incubation conditions actually adopted must be confirmed and documented in the EM method validation.

C. Prefiltration Bioburden

Original Text

Manufacturing process controls should be designed to minimize the bioburden in the unfiltered product. In addition to increasing the challenge to the sterilizing filter, bioburden can contribute impurities (e.g., endotoxin) to, and lead to degradation of, the drug product. A prefiltration bioburden limit should be established.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Control Prefiltration Bioburden?

The design assumption of sterile filtration is that the microbial load entering the filter (bioburden) is at a low level that can be reasonably controlled. If bioburden is too high, there are three consequences:

  • Increased filter challenge: exceeding the filter's design capacity may lead to breakthrough failure
  • Elevated endotoxin: even if the organisms are filtered out, cell wall fragments of Gram-negative bacteria (endotoxin) can pass through a 0.22μm filter into the product
  • Product degradation: high bioburden means high enzyme activity, and some organisms secrete proteases and nucleases that degrade the drug substance before filtration

Analogy: Prefiltration Control Is Like a Sedimentation Tank Before Water Purification

A waterworks does not push dirty water straight into a precision filter: it first lets it settle and pretreats it, reducing suspended solids and microbial load, so that the precision membrane can do its final job as the last barrier. Sterile filtration works the same way: prefiltration bioburden control is a measure that protects filter life and performance and is also the first line of defense for product quality.

Basis for Setting the Prefiltration Bioburden Limit

The prefiltration bioburden limit is usually set at 10 CFU/100 mL (it may be stricter depending on the product), and should be based on the challenge level of organisms used during filter validation. FDA requires that this limit be established in writing and that routine production have a corresponding monitoring program.

If a bioburden result exceeds the set limit, the cause must be investigated immediately: Is the raw material bioburden high? Was the exposure time during compounding too long? Is the compounding water (WFI/PW) microbiologically high? These are all items that must be systematically checked in the deviation report.

CDMO Prefiltration Bioburden Monitoring Program

During technology transfer or process development, the CDMO should confirm with the client the following for prefiltration bioburden:

  • Sampling timing (sample immediately before filtration to avoid extended exposure time)
  • Sample volume (usually 100 mL to ensure sufficient sensitivity)
  • Basis for setting the limit (must be documented)
  • Deviation response process (when the limit is exceeded, must the validity of the filtration validation be re-evaluated?)

Special note: biologic formulations (proteins, antibodies) are more susceptible to microbial effects than chemical drugs, because they are rich in nutrients and bioburden grows faster, so stricter compounding hold-time limits are needed.

D. Alternate Microbiological Test Methods

Original Text

Other suitable microbiological test methods (e.g., rapid test methods) can be considered for environmental monitoring, in-process control testing, and finished product release testing after it is demonstrated that the methods are equivalent or better than traditional methods (e.g., USP).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Rapid Microbiological Methods (RMM)

The traditional sterility test takes 14 days, and traditional EM counts take 3–7 days. Rapid microbiological methods (RMM) aim to cut this time sharply, from days to hours or even real-time results. In its 2004 guidance, FDA already permitted RMM for:

  • Environmental monitoring (EM)
  • In-process control testing
  • Finished product release testing

Prerequisite for Adopting RMM: Equivalent or Better

FDA's condition is very clear: the new method must be shown to be equivalent or superior to the traditional method (such as the USP standard method). This requires a complete method equivalency validation, including:

  • Accuracy and precision
  • A lower limit of sensitivity no worse than the traditional method
  • Specificity, with no misidentification
  • Comparative data against the traditional method (at least 30 parallel samples)

If used for finished product release, a supplemental NDA/ANDA/BLA submission is usually required, and in the US it can replace the compendial USP method only after FDA approval.

Business Considerations for CDMOs Adopting RMM

The potential advantages of rapid microbiological methods are of great commercial value to a CDMO:

  • Shorter batch release time: from 14 days for the traditional sterility test to a few hours (e.g., BacT/ALERT, BACTEC)
  • Faster response to process deviations: EM data are available the next day, enabling immediate correction
  • Competitive advantage: a CDMO that can offer faster release is more attractive to clients

Challenges: high initial validation cost, large equipment investment, and a complex regulatory review process. CDMOs therefore usually adopt RMM first for EM applications (where regulatory requirements are more relaxed), and then gradually extend it to finished product release testing.

E. Particle Monitoring

Original Text

Routine particle monitoring is useful in rapidly detecting significant deviations in air cleanliness from qualified processing norms (e.g., clean area classification). A result outside the established classification level at a given location should be investigated as to its cause.

The extent of investigation should be consistent with the severity of the excursion and include an evaluation of trending data. Appropriate corrective action should be implemented, as necessary, to prevent future deviations.

See Section IV.A for additional guidance on particle monitoring.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Particle Monitoring vs Microbial Monitoring

Particle counting and microbial EM are complementary but independent monitoring systems:

  • Particle monitoring: continuous real-time detection of non-viable particles ≥0.5μm and ≥5μm, used to confirm cleanroom classification, HEPA filter integrity, and airflow stability. It can be automated for real-time monitoring.
  • Microbial EM: periodic sampling to detect viable microorganisms, with results available only after 48 hours to 7 days, providing direct information on microbial contamination risk.

Elevated particle counts do not necessarily indicate microbial contamination, but they are a warning sign of increased contamination risk, because particles can carry microorganisms.

Integrating Particle Monitoring Systems in a CDMO

Modern CDMOs usually install a continuous particle monitoring system that monitors particle counts in the Grade A area in real time during filling operations. Main considerations:

  • Monitoring points should be chosen to reflect the highest-risk areas (such as near the filling needles)
  • The sampling tubing design should avoid excessive length (which causes measurement delay)
  • System alarm levels (Alert/Action) must be set according to the cleanroom grade and have a documented basis
  • Particle deviation records and EM data must be amenable to correlation analysis (same batch, same time period)
  • Alarm records from the connected DCS/SCADA are key electronic records that FDA requests during inspections

Section XI — Sterility Testing (Overview)

Original Text

21 CFR 211.167(a): For each batch of drug product purporting to be sterile and/or pyrogen-free, there shall be appropriate laboratory testing to determine conformance to such requirements. The test procedures shall be in writing and shall be followed.
21 CFR 211.165(e): The accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility of test methods employed by the firm shall be established and documented.
21 CFR 211.160(b): Laboratory controls shall include the establishment of scientifically sound and appropriate specifications, standards, sampling plans, and test procedures designed to assure that components, drug product containers, closures, in-process materials, labeling, and drug products conform to appropriate standards of identity, strength, quality, and purity.

Certain aspects of sterility testing are of particular importance, including control of the testing environment, understanding the test limitations, and investigating manufacturing systems following a positive test.

The testing laboratory environment should employ facilities and controls comparable to those used for aseptic filling operations. Poor or deficient sterility test facilities or controls can result in test failure. If production facilities and controls are significantly better than those for sterility testing, the danger exists of mistakenly attributing a positive sterility test result to a faulty laboratory even when the product tested could have, in fact, been nonsterile. Therefore, a manufacturing deficiency may go undetected.

The use of isolators for sterility testing minimizes the chance of a false positive test result.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Key Aspects of Sterility Testing

FDA identifies three particularly important aspects of sterility testing, and none can be omitted:

  • Control of the test environment — the laboratory itself must have environmental controls at the level required for aseptic operations
  • Understanding the limitations of the test — statistically, the test is not a highly sensitive screening tool
  • Systematic investigation after a positive result — the investigation cannot stop at the laboratory; it must trace back to the manufacturing process

All three points directly affect a CDMO's QC laboratory and QA department, and they are frequently raised in customer audits.

Warning: Laboratory Quality Must Not Be Lower Than the Production Environment

FDA's logic is clear: if environmental control in the production area is stricter than in the laboratory, then when a positive result occurs, a firm may wrongly blame the laboratory and release product that is in fact not sterile, causing process failures to be masked.

Practical recommendation: the QC microbiology laboratory should maintain operating conditions of at least ISO 5 (grade A/B), with regular environmental monitoring and traceable data.

CDMO COO Application: The Business Logic of Investing in an Isolator

FDA expressly recommends using an isolator for sterility testing to reduce the probability of false positives. For a CDMO, adopting an isolator is not only a technical upgrade but also commercial risk management: it reduces batch rejections, retesting costs and delivery delays caused by false positives, while demonstrating advanced laboratory control during customer audits and strengthening competitive differentiation.

Section XI-A — Microbiological Laboratory Controls

Original Text

A Sterility testing methods are required to be accurate and reproducible, in accordance with 211.194 and 211.165. USP <71> "Sterility Tests" is the principal source used for sterility testing methods, including information on test procedures and media.

As a part of methods validation, appropriate microbiological challenge testing will demonstrate reproducibility of the method to reliably recover representative microorganisms. If growth is inhibited, modifications (e.g., increased dilution, additional membrane filter washes, addition of inactivating agents) to the test method should be implemented to optimize recovery.

Ultimately, methods validation studies should demonstrate that the method does not provide an opportunity for false negatives.

It is essential that the media used to perform sterility testing be rendered sterile and demonstrated as growth promoting. Personnel performing sterility testing should be qualified and trained for the task. A written program should be in place to maintain updated training of personnel and confirm acceptable sterility testing practices.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Key Demonstrations in Method Validation

Method validation for sterility testing must be able to answer three questions:

  • Growth Promotion: Can the culture medium itself support the growth of the target microorganisms? (Confirmed for every lot of medium)
  • Bacteriostasis/Fungistasis Test: Does the product itself inhibit microbial growth? If so, modify the method (dilution, rinsing, adding a neutralizer)
  • No False Negatives: The method must not miss true contamination because of product inhibition or operational problems

Analogy: Method validation is like "detective training"

Imagine training a detective to catch criminals (microorganisms). You must first confirm: Do the detective's tools (culture media) work properly? Is anything at the scene getting in the detective's way (the product's inhibitory effect)? In training tests, can the detective find the hidden criminal every time (challenge microorganisms)? If the detective cannot find them even in training, he cannot be sent out on a real case.

CDMO Laboratory Compliance Points

Common audit deficiencies: incomplete media growth promotion records, personnel training records lacking a qualification step, or method validation reports that do not cover bacteriostasis/fungistasis testing. For products containing antimicrobial components (for example, multi-dose preparations containing preservatives), the bacteriostasis/fungistasis test is especially important and is an item customers must confirm on their technology transfer checklist.

Section XI-B — Sampling and Incubation

Original Text

B Sterility tests are limited in their ability to detect contamination because of the small sample size typically used. For example, as described by USP, statistical evaluations indicate that the sterility test sampling plan "only enables the detection of contamination in a lot in which 10% of the units are contaminated about nine times out of ten in making the test."

Statistical Illustration: If a 10,000-unit lot with a 0.1% contamination level was sterility tested using 20 units, there is a 98% chance that the batch would pass the test. This means a lot with 10 contaminated units out of 10,000 would almost certainly be released.

It is important that the samples represent the entire batch and processing conditions. Samples should be taken:

  • at the beginning, middle, and end of the aseptic processing operation
  • in conjunction with processing interventions or excursions

Because of the limited sensitivity of the test, any positive result is considered a serious CGMP issue that should be thoroughly investigated.

Tutorial Commentary

Important Warning: The Sterility Test Is Not a Highly Sensitive Test

This statistic is startling, but it is a fact the industry must face: in a batch of 10,000 units containing 10 contaminated units, a sterility test using 20 samples has a 98% chance of passing.

This is exactly why FDA emphasizes that sterility assurance cannot rest on the final sterility test alone but must be built on systematic control of the entire process, that is, "Sterility Assurance by Process" rather than "Sterility Assurance by Testing".

The Strategic Significance of Representative Sampling

FDA requires that samples represent the conditions of the entire batch process, in particular:

  • Beginning, middle and end of the batch: covering environmental exposure and equipment status at different points in time
  • After interventions: personnel interventions are the highest contamination-risk periods and should be specifically marked and tracked

For a CDMO, the sampling plan must be clearly documented in the batch production record (BPR), including the rationale for sampling timing, to withstand document review by customers and FDA.

CDMO COO Takeaway: A Positive Is a Serious Event

Any sterility positive is a serious CGMP issue and must not be brushed aside with "it may be laboratory contamination". Even if the result is ultimately attributed to laboratory error, the entire investigation process and decision logic must be supported by documentation. For a CDMO, mishandling such an event can affect customer confidence in the entire contract manufacturing platform.

Tutorial Commentary

Golden Rule: Growth Means Positive; a Result Cannot Be "Automatically Invalidated"

FDA's position is very firm: whenever microbial growth appears in the test, the batch is considered non-sterile and an investigation must start immediately. The only circumstance in which the initial test may be invalidated is clear, documented evidence of laboratory error.

Common mistake (audit red flag): the investigation concludes "possible laboratory contamination" without specific evidence and then proceeds directly to a retest. FDA regards this as a procedural non-compliance that may lead to a Warning Letter.

The Logical Structure of the Seven Investigation Elements

These seven elements are not a random list; together they systematically cover all possible sources of contamination:

  • Element 1 (identification of the isolate): confirm which organism it is, so it can be compared with the environmental database to find the source
  • Element 2 (laboratory deviations): rule out or confirm the laboratory as the source of contamination
  • Element 3 (production environmental monitoring): compare environmental data trends around the production day
  • Element 4 (personnel monitoring): microbial monitoring data for the operators
  • Element 5 (pre-sterilization bioburden): the initial microbial load of raw materials or intermediates
  • Element 6 (batch records): whether any abnormal events or deviations occurred during the process
  • Element 7 (manufacturing history): historical data, past deviations, process change records
Investigation Elements — Detail

Elements 1 & 2 Original Text

1. Identification (speciation) of the organism

Sterility test isolates should be identified to the species level. Microbiological monitoring data should be reviewed to determine if the organism is also found in laboratory and production environments, personnel, or product bioburden. Advanced identification methods (e.g., nucleic-acid based) are valuable for investigational purposes. When comparing results from environmental monitoring and sterility positives, both identifications should be performed using the same methodology.

2. Record of laboratory tests and deviations

Review of laboratory deviation and investigation findings can help to eliminate or implicate the laboratory as the source of contamination. For example, if the organism is seldom found in the laboratory environment, product contamination is more likely than laboratory error. If the organism is found in laboratory and production environments, it can still indicate product contamination.

The proper handling of deviations is an essential aspect of laboratory control. When a deviation occurs during sterility testing, it should be documented, investigated, and remedied. If any deviation is considered to have compromised the integrity of the sterility test, the test should be invalidated immediately without incubation.

Tutorial Commentary

Element 1: Technical Depth of Microbial Identification

Reporting only "Gram-positive cocci" is not enough; FDA expects identification to the species level. The reason is that only at species level can the isolate be compared with the environmental monitoring database to confirm whether the same species is present in the production environment, the laboratory or on particular personnel.

Modern molecular methods (such as 16S rRNA sequencing) are more precise than conventional biochemical identification, and FDA specifically mentions the value of nucleic acid sequencing in investigations. Important: the identification method used for environmental monitoring isolates and for the sterility positive must be the same, otherwise the comparison is invalid.

Element 2: The "Immediate Invalidation" Rule for Deviation Records

If a deviation during testing has compromised the integrity of the test (for example, an isolator leak or contaminated culture medium), the test must be invalidated immediately and incubation must not continue. This means the time of invalidation is the moment the deviation is discovered, not afterward during discussion.

Audit red flag: if a firm discusses whether to invalidate only after incubation has finished, FDA will question whether the decision was influenced by the test result (nobody would raise invalidation if there were no growth) rather than based on a genuine deviation; this pattern is itself a CGMP violation.

CDMO COO Application: The Strategic Value of an Environmental Monitoring Database

Maintaining a complete, searchable microbial database with species history is a core strength in sterility positive investigations. When a CDMO serves multiple customers, being able to quickly find from the database where a given species has appeared before greatly improves the efficiency and credibility of the investigation and better protects customer batches from unnecessary rejection.

Elements 3 & 4 Original Text

3. Monitoring of production area environment

Trend analysis of microorganisms in the critical and immediately adjacent areas is especially helpful in determining the source of contamination in a sterility failure investigation. Consideration of environmental microbial data should not be limited to results of monitoring the production environment for the lot, day, or shift associated with the suspect lot. For example, results showing little or no recovery of microorganisms can be misleading, especially when preceded or followed by a finding of an adverse trend or atypically high microbial counts. It is therefore important to look at both short- and long-term environmental trend analyses.

4. Monitoring Personnel

The review of data and associated trends from daily monitoring of personnel can provide important information indicating a route of contamination. The adequacy of personnel practices and training also merit significant review and consideration.

Tutorial Commentary

Element 3: The Time Dimension of Trend Analysis

FDA specifically warns that looking only at environmental monitoring results from the "day of batch production" is not enough and may even mislead the investigation. The correct approach is to review both of the following:

  • Short-term trends: environmental monitoring data from the days before and after production
  • Long-term trends: the environmental monitoring trend for that filling line over the past several months, including any upward trend

"Normal data on the day" does not mean the process is free of problems; if there were excursions on the days before or after, that "normal" result may be only a chance occurrence within an abnormal period.

Element 4: Personnel Are the Highest-Risk Source of Contamination

In aseptic processing, personnel are the main source of contamination (the human body sheds thousands of particles per minute). FDA requires the investigation to review: each operator's daily glove and gowning monitoring data, and whether training records meet requirements.

Special note: if temporary or new personnel took part in operations on the filling day of the batch under investigation, this must be explicitly evaluated in the investigation report.

Elements 5–7 Original Text

5. Product Presterilization Bioburden

We recommend review of trends in product bioburden and consideration of whether adverse bioburden trends have occurred.

6. Production record review

Complete batch and production control records should be reviewed to detect any signs of failures or anomalies that could have a bearing on product sterility. For example, the investigation should include elements such as:

  • Events that could have impacted on the critical zone
  • Batch and trending data that indicate whether utility and/or support systems are functioning properly — e.g., records of air quality monitoring for filling lines could show a time at which there was improper air balance or an unusually high particle count
  • Whether construction or maintenance activities could have had an adverse impact

7. Manufacturing history

The manufacturing history of a product or similar products should be reviewed as part of the investigation. Past deviations, problems, or changes (e.g., process, components, equipment) are among the factors that can provide an indication of the origin of the problem.

Additional FDA Guidance on Lab Trends

A sterility positive result can be viewed as indicative of production or laboratory problems, and the entire manufacturing process should be comprehensively investigated since such problems often can extend beyond a single batch. Where a laboratory has a good track record with respect to errors, this history can lower suspicion of the lab. However, where a laboratory has a poor track record, firms should not assume that the contamination is automatically more attributable to the laboratory and consequently overlook a genuine production problem. Accordingly, it is essential that all sterility positives be thoroughly investigated.

Tutorial Commentary

Element 5: The Significance of Pre-Sterilization Bioburden Trends

Aseptic processing is a process that "cannot be rescued by terminal sterilization", so control of the microbial load before sterilization is critical. If the investigation finds an upward trend in bioburden, controls over raw materials or process intermediates may have broken down, and the investigation needs to trace back to raw material suppliers, the water system, or the cleaning status of process equipment.

Element 6: "Hidden Clues" in the Batch Record

FDA singles out three types of records as especially important:

  • Critical area events: any activity near exposed product (including machine adjustments, dropped containers and so on)
  • Support system records: HVAC airflow, particle counts, WFI TOC and so on; these "peripheral" data are often early warning signals of contamination
  • Construction and maintenance: even work in adjacent areas can affect particles or airflow in the aseptic area

Element 7 Analogy: Manufacturing History Is the "Crime Scene Timeline"

When police investigate a murder, they look beyond the day it happened; they build a complete timeline and identify all potentially related events and people. The logic of the manufacturing history review is exactly the same: recent equipment changes, process deviations, or a change of raw material supplier may be the root of the problem. FDA specifically stresses that "similar products" must also be included in the investigation, because the same filling line, the same operators and the same environment may affect multiple products.

CDMO COO Summary: The Business Implications of the Investigation Report

A high-quality sterility positive investigation report is not only a regulatory requirement; it is also the CDMO's contract of trust with the customer. When a customer's product shows a sterility positive in your facility, the first things they look at are: whether the investigation is complete, objective and scientifically supported, and whether the CAPA is actually effective. The quality of the investigation report directly reflects the CDMO's quality culture and technical capability.

Section XII — Interventions & Line Stoppages Records

Original Text

While interventions and/or stoppages are normally recorded in the batch record, the manner of documenting these occurrences varies. In particular, line stoppages and any unplanned interventions should be sufficiently documented in batch records with the associated time and duration of the event.

In addition to lengthened dwell time of sterile product elements in the critical area, an extensive intervention can increase contamination risk. Sterility failures have often been attributed to atypical or extensive interventions that have occurred as a response to an undesirable event during the aseptic process.

Written procedures describing the need for line clearances in the event of certain interventions, such as machine adjustments and any repairs, should be established. Tutorial Commentary

High-Risk Events: Unplanned Interventions Are the Most Common Root Cause of Sterility Failures

FDA states explicitly that the root cause of many sterility failures is unplanned, prolonged interventions (for example, manual adjustments during an equipment malfunction). Such actions are often performed hastily in an emergency, when aseptic technique is more likely to break down.

Batch record documentation requirements:

  • Type of intervention (planned vs. unplanned)
  • Time of occurrence and duration
  • Identity of the operators
  • Whether a line clearance was triggered

Triggering Criteria for Line Clearance

FDA requires firms to establish written procedures that clearly define which intervention events trigger a partial or full line clearance. Trigger scenarios should include (but are not limited to):

  • Major mechanical repairs near exposed product
  • Continuous interventions exceeding a reasonable exposure time
  • Any event that compromises the sterility of the critical area

For a CDMO, the design quality of this procedure will be evaluated directly by customers during technology transfer review.

Power Interruptions: Zero-Tolerance Batch Record Requirement

Any power interruption that could affect product quality, even a momentary one, must be recorded in the batch record and handled as a manufacturing deviation. The scope of impact may include: interruption of HVAC airflow, stopping of filling pumps, stopping of laminar airflow hood fans, suspension of environmental monitoring equipment, and so on. The extent of impact on each of these must be confirmed one by one in the deviation assessment.

CDMO COO Practical Recommendation: A Tiered Intervention Management System

It is recommended to establish a tiered intervention classification system (for example, Level 1 / 2 / 3), specifying different levels of record detail, QA notification requirements and line clearance decision criteria according to severity. Such a system helps operators make quick decisions on the floor while ensuring the completeness of batch records can withstand scrutiny in FDA and customer audits, and it is also an effective tool for standardized control in a multi-product CDMO environment.

Appendix 1 — Aseptic Processing Isolators: Overview

Original Text

Aseptic processing using isolation systems separates the external cleanroom environment from the aseptic processing line and minimizes its exposure to personnel. A well-designed positive pressure isolator, supported by adequate procedures for its maintenance, monitoring, and control, offers tangible advantages over traditional aseptic processing, including fewer opportunities for microbial contamination during processing.

However, users should remain vigilant to potential sources of operational risk. Manufacturers should also be aware of the need to establish new procedures addressing issues unique to isolators.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Fundamental Advantage of Isolators

The isolator is a major evolution in aseptic processing technology. Its core logic is to physically separate the processing area from the external cleanroom (and from personnel), eliminating the largest source of contamination, people, at the root.

  • Conventional aseptic processing: operators work in an ISO 5 environment wearing sterile gowns, and personnel are the largest source of contamination
  • Isolator: operators work outside the isolator, while the interior maintains a continuous ISO 5 environment
  • Positive pressure design: the isolator interior is kept under positive pressure to prevent outside air from entering

FDA explicitly recognizes this advantage, but also cautions that new technology brings new points of risk and requires dedicated SOPs.

Note: The Isolator Is Not a Cure-All

FDA specifically stresses that users must remain "alert to potential operational risks". Isolator design flaws, glove breaches and incomplete decontamination can all cause contamination events that are harder to detect than in conventional processing. Once an isolator is introduced, a new set of SOPs must be established.

Original Text

A. Maintenance

1. General

Maintenance of isolator systems differs in some significant respects from the traditional, non-isolated aseptic processing operations. Although no isolator forms an absolute seal, very high integrity can be achieved in a well-designed unit. However, a leak in certain components of the system can constitute a significant breach of integrity.

The integrity of gloves, half-suits, and seams should receive daily attention and be addressed by a comprehensive preventative maintenance program. Replacement frequencies should be established in written procedures that ensure parts will be changed before they breakdown or degrade. Transfer systems, gaskets, and seals are among the other parts that should be covered by the maintenance program.

2. Glove Integrity

A faulty glove or sleeve (gauntlet) assembly represents a route of contamination and a critical breach of isolator integrity. A preventative maintenance program should be established. The choice of durable glove materials, coupled with a well-justified replacement frequency, are key aspects of GMP to be addressed.

With every use, gloves should be visually evaluated for any macroscopic physical defect. Physical integrity tests should also be performed routinely. A breach in glove integrity can be of serious consequence.

Due to the potential for microbial migration through microscopic holes in gloves and the lack of a highly sensitive glove integrity test, FDA recommends affording attention to the sanitary quality of the inner surface of the installed glove and to integrating the use of a second pair of thin gloves.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Key Differences in Maintenance Management

Maintenance of isolators differs fundamentally from that of conventional aseptic processing. The key items to maintain include:

  • Gloves/half-suits: daily visual inspection plus periodic physical integrity testing
  • Seams: may deteriorate through frequent use or chemical decontamination
  • Transfer systems, gaskets and seals: all must be included in the preventive maintenance program

Written procedures must require replacement "before parts degrade or fail". This means replacement frequency should be based on material durability studies and actual usage data, not on waiting until a problem is found.

Note: Glove Integrity Is the Number One Risk Point

FDA places particularly high importance on glove integrity, for these reasons:

  • Microscopic holes (invisible to the naked eye) can allow microorganisms to migrate
  • No highly sensitive glove integrity test method is currently available
  • Once a breach occurs, the contamination consequences are serious

FDA's specific recommendation is to maintain hygienic quality on the inner surface of installed gloves and to use double thin gloves as an additional barrier. This is a pragmatic risk mitigation strategy.

Analogy: The Two-Layer Protection of Surgical Gloves

Surgeons routinely wear double gloves in high-risk operations. If the first layer has a small breach, the second layer still protects, and fluid staining between the layers can serve as a visual warning of a breach. FDA's recommendation on isolator gloves rests on the same logic: defense in depth.

Appendix 1-B — Design: Airflow, Pressure & Clean Area Classification

Original Text

B. Design

1. Airflow

There are two types of aseptic processing isolators: open and closed. Closed isolators employ connections with auxiliary equipment for material transfer. Open isolators have openings to the surrounding environment that are carefully engineered to segregate the inner isolator environment from the surrounding room via overpressure.

Turbulent flow can be acceptable within closed isolators, which are normally compact in size and do not house processing lines. Other aseptic processing isolators employ unidirectional airflow that sweeps over and away from exposed sterile materials, avoiding any turbulence or stagnant airflow in the area of exposed sterilized materials, product, and container closures.

2. Materials of Construction

Suitable materials should be chosen based on durability, as well as ease of cleaning and decontamination. Rigid wall construction incorporating stainless steel and glass materials is widely used.

3. Pressure Differential

A positive air pressure differential adequate to achieve physical separation should be employed and supported by qualification studies. Positive air pressure differentials from the isolator to the surrounding environment have largely ranged from approximately 17.5 to 50 Pascals (0.07" to 0.20" water gauge).

The positive pressure differential should be coupled with an appropriately designed opening to the external environment to prevent potential ingress of surrounding room air by induction. Local Class 100 (ISO 5) protection at an opening is an example of a design provision that can provide a further barrier.

4. Clean Area Classifications

The interior of the isolator should meet Class 100 (ISO 5) standards. The classification of the environment surrounding the isolator should be based on the design of its interfaces (e.g., transfer ports), as well as the number of transfers into and out of the isolator. A Class 100,000 (ISO 8) background is commonly used. An aseptic processing isolator should not be located in an unclassified room.

Tutorial Commentary

Note: The Hazard of "Induction"

Induction refers to eddies or pressure waves created by local turbulence that can "draw" external particles into an isolator opening. Even when the positive pressure differential is met, this physical phenomenon can still occur if the opening is poorly designed. Countermeasure: provide local ISO 5 HEPA-filtered protection at the opening.

CDMO Application: Facility Design Decisions

When designing a facility for an isolator, the choice of background environment grade (ISO 8 or higher) should be based on the transfer port design, the number of transfers in and out per batch, and whether the isolator is open or closed. This decision should be confirmed at the URS/DQ stage and supported by data in OQ/PQ.

Appendix 1-C — Transfer of Materials/Supplies

Original Text

C. Transfer of Materials/Supplies

The ability to maintain integrity of a decontaminated isolator can be affected by the design of transfer ports. Various adaptations, of differing capabilities, allow for the transfer of supplies into and out of the isolator.

Multiple material transfers are generally made during the processing of a batch. Frequently, transfers are performed via direct interface with manufacturing equipment. Properly maintained and operated rapid transfer ports (RTPs) are an effective transfer mechanism for aseptic transfer of materials into and out of isolators.

Some transfer ports might have significant limitations, including marginal decontaminating capability (e.g., ultraviolet) or a design that has the potential to compromise isolation by allowing ingress of air from the surrounding room. In the latter case, localized HEPA-filtered unidirectional airflow cover in the area of such a port should be implemented.

Isolators often include a mousehole or other exit port through which product is discharged, opening the isolator to the outside environment. Sufficient overpressure should be supplied and monitored on a continuous basis at this location to ensure that isolation is maintained.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Transfer System Is the Weak Link in Integrity

The transfer system is one of the hardest challenges in maintaining isolator integrity. Materials must move in and out, but every transfer is a potential opportunity for contamination. The main transfer mechanisms FDA recognizes are:

  • Rapid transfer port (RTP): a well-designed, well-maintained RTP is the recommended standard method
  • Direct interface: docking directly with manufacturing equipment (such as a dry heat tunnel)
  • Mousehole: a product exit, which requires continuous monitoring of the positive pressure differential

Note: Limitations of UV Decontamination Ports

FDA clearly points out that some transfer ports have limited decontamination capability, particularly designs that use ultraviolet (UV) light. UV is effective for surface disinfection, but it has poor penetration and pronounced shadowing effects, so it cannot be regarded as equivalent to the systematic decontamination achieved with vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VHP). If such a transfer port is used, local HEPA unidirectional flow protection should be added in its area.

Analogy: Airport Security Gates

An isolator transfer port is like an airport security gate: people and goods must pass through, but security control must be maintained at every passage. The RTP works on a principle similar to a double-door airlock, in which only one door is open at a time, so the internal environment is never contaminated by the external one. Continuously monitoring the pressure differential at the mousehole is like continuously maintaining an air curtain at the exit gate.

Appendix 1-D — Decontamination

Original Text

D. Decontamination

1. Surface Exposure

Decontamination procedures should ensure full exposure of all isolator surfaces to the chemical agent. The capability of a decontaminant to penetrate obstructed or covered surfaces is limited. For example, to facilitate contact with the decontaminant, the glove apparatus should be fully extended with glove fingers separated during the decontamination cycle. It is also important to clean the interior of the isolator per appropriate procedures to allow for a robust decontamination process.

2. Efficacy

The decontamination method should render the inner surfaces of the isolator free of viable microorganisms. An appropriate, quantified Biological Indicator (BI) challenge should be placed on various materials and in many locations throughout the isolator, including difficult to reach areas.

Cycles should be developed with an appropriate margin of extra kill to provide confidence in robustness. Normally, a four- to six-log reduction can be justified depending on the application. The uniform distribution of a defined concentration of decontaminating agent should also be evaluated.

3. Frequency

When an isolator is used for multiple days between decontamination cycles, the frequency adopted should be justified. A breach of isolator integrity should normally lead to a decontamination cycle. Breaches of integrity should be investigated. If it is determined that the environment may have been compromised, any product potentially impacted by the breach should be rejected.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Key Elements of Decontamination Validation

  • Surface exposure: physically ensure that all surfaces can be reached by the decontamination agent, including the inside of gloves and hard-to-reach corners
  • Efficacy: verify the log reduction (4–6 log reduction) with biological indicators (BIs)
  • Frequency: must have a documented rationale; re-decontamination is required after any integrity breach

Note: Limits of the Statistical Method (Fraction Negative)

FDA notes that, because of the characteristics of decontaminating agents such as VHP, the statistical methods generally used for steam sterilization (the fraction negative method) are usually not suitable for confirming the lethality of a decontamination process. A quantitative challenge test should be performed using BIs with a defined spore count.

CDMO Application: Handling an Integrity Breach

Triggers for an integrity breach include power failure, valve failure, insufficient positive pressure, and glove or seam holes. The procedure should specify: (1) immediate segregation of potentially affected product; (2) initiation of an investigation; (3) execution of a decontamination cycle; and (4) a decision on the disposition of the product (rejection) based on the investigation results. This procedure should be tracked in the CAPA system.

Appendix 1-E/F/G — Filling Line Sterilization, Environmental Monitoring & Personnel

Original Text

E. Filling Line Sterilization

To ensure sterility of product contact surfaces from the start of each operation, the entire path of the sterile processing stream should be sterilized. Aseptic processing equipment or ancillary supplies to be used within the isolator should be chosen based on their ability to withstand steam sterilization (or equivalent method).

Where decontamination methods are used to render certain product contact surfaces free of viable organisms, a minimum of a six-log reduction should be demonstrated using a suitable biological indicator.

F. Environmental Monitoring

An environmental monitoring program should be established that routinely ensures acceptable microbiological quality of air, surfaces, and gloves (or half-suits) as well as particle levels, within the isolator. Nutrient media should be cleaned off of surfaces following a contact plate sample.

Air quality should be monitored periodically during each shift. FDA recommends monitoring the exit port for particles to detect any unusual results. Media used for environmental monitoring should not be exposed to decontamination cycle residues, as recovery of microorganisms would be inhibited.

G. Personnel

Although cleanroom apparel considerations are generally reduced in an isolator operation, the contamination risk contributed by manual factors can not be overlooked. Traditional aseptic processing vigilance remains critical, with an understanding that contaminated isolator gloves can lead to product nonsterility. Meticulous aseptic technique standards must be observed (21 CFR 211.113), including appropriate use of sterile tools for manipulations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Two Routes to Filling Line Sterilization

Product-contact surfaces inside the isolator can be sterilized by two routes:

  • Steam sterilization (SIP): the preferred method, which must achieve at least a 6-log kill; the equipment materials must withstand high temperature and pressure
  • Chemical decontamination: used when the equipment cannot withstand SIP; it also requires 6-log BI validation; a decontamination cycle alone must not replace sterilization

Key principle: at the start of every operation, the entire aseptic processing pathway must already have been sterilized.

Note: Special Considerations for Environmental Monitoring

Several details of EM in isolators are easily overlooked:

  • After contact plate sampling, the media must be removed, because residual media will promote microbial growth
  • Environmental monitoring media must not be exposed to decontamination cycle residues (such as VHP residue); otherwise microbial recovery will be suppressed, causing false negatives
  • It is recommended to monitor particle levels at the exit port to detect abnormalities early

Personnel Risk: The Blind Spot of the "Tempered Glass Shell"

Many people mistakenly believe an isolator removes personnel from the process completely. In fact, glove manipulation is still a frequent intervention. FDA specifically reminds us that the aseptic technique standard inside an isolator is exactly the same as in a conventional aseptic room: "changing the environment does not mean aseptic vigilance can be lowered". Contaminated isolator gloves can equally cause product failure.

Appendix 2 — Blow-Fill-Seal Technology: Equipment Design & Air Quality

Original Text

Blow-fill-seal (BFS) technology is an automated process by which containers are formed, filled, and sealed in a continuous operation. This manufacturing technology includes economies in container closure processing and reduced human intervention and is often used for filling and packaging ophthalmics, respiratory care products, and, less frequently, injectables.

A. Equipment Design and Air Quality

Most BFS machines operate using the following steps:

  • Heat a plastic polymer resin
  • Extrude it to form a parison (a tubular form of the hot resin)
  • Cut the parison with a high-temperature knife
  • Move the parison under the blow-fill needle (mandrel)
  • Inflate it to the shape of the mold walls
  • Fill the formed container with the liquid product
  • Remove the mandrel
  • Seal

The three steps with the greatest potential for exposure to particle contamination and/or surrounding air are those in which (1) the parison is cut, (2) the parison is moved under the blow-fill mandrel, and (3) the mandrel is removed (just prior to sealing).

The classified environment surrounding BFS machinery should generally meet Class 100,000 (ISO 8), or better, standards. HEPA-filtered or sterile air provided by membrane filters should be used during the steps when sterile products or materials are exposed. Air in the critical area should meet Class 100 (ISO 5) microbiological standards during operations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Competitive Advantages and Scope of BFS

Three major advantages of BFS technology:

  • Continuous integrated operation: container forming, filling and sealing are done in one machine, reducing material exposure
  • Reduced personnel intervention: highly automated, which greatly reduces operators' contact with the aseptic environment
  • Cost effectiveness: integrating container forming and filling eliminates separate container purchasing, washing and sterilization steps

Typical applications: ophthalmic products (eye drops), respiratory inhalation solutions and some injectables.

Note: The Three Highest-Risk Steps

FDA precisely identifies three critical moments in the BFS process when particulate contamination is most likely:

  1. Parison cutting (a hot knife cutting the plastic tube generates particles)
  2. Parison movement under the filling needle (exposure to the environment)
  3. Mandrel removal (brief exposure after filling and before sealing)

These three steps are the focus of engineering controls and EM design.

Appendix 2-B/C — BFS Validation/Qualification & Batch Monitoring

Original Text

B. Validation/Qualification

Advantages of BFS processing include rapid container closure processing and minimized aseptic interventions. However, only a properly functioning process can realize these advantages. FDA recommends affording special attention to setup, troubleshooting of equipment, and related aseptic personnel procedures.

Equipment sterilization, media fills, polymer extrusion/sterilization, product-plastic compatibility, forming and sealing integrity, and unit weight variation are among the key issues to address in validation and qualification studies.

Data gathered during such studies should ensure that BFS containers are sterile and, if used for parenteral drugs, nonpyrogenic. This can generally be achieved by validating that time temperature conditions of the extrusion process are effective against endotoxin or spore challenges in the polymeric material.

The choice of appropriate polymer material includes assessing if a material is pharmaceutical grade, safe, pure, and passes appropriate criteria for plastics. Polymer suppliers should be qualified and monitored for raw material quality.

C. Batch Monitoring and Control

Various in-process control parameters (e.g., container weight variation, fill weight, leakers, air pressure) provide information to monitor and facilitate ongoing process control. It is essential to monitor the microbial air quality. Samples should be taken according to a comprehensive sampling plan that provides data representative of the entire filling operation.

Container closure defects can be a major problem. It is critical that the operation be designed and set-up to uniformly manufacture integral units. Significant defects due to heat or mechanical problems should be investigated in accordance with §§ 211.100 and 211.192.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Special Validation Points for BFS

Beyond standard equipment qualification, BFS validation has several unique elements:

  • Thermal validation of the extrusion process: the time-temperature conditions during high-temperature plastic extrusion must be validated to show effective removal of endotoxin and spores
  • Polymer material compatibility: drug-plastic compatibility studies (the drug must not be contaminated by plasticizers or adsorbed by the container)
  • Seal integrity: consistency of the container/closure interface is key to batch release
  • Weight variation: consistency of fill weight reflects process stability

Note: Depyrogenation Requirements for Parenteral Use

If BFS is used to produce parenterals, the container must be pyrogen-free, which means the extrusion process must be validated for its ability to remove endotoxin. FDA notes that validating the time-temperature conditions of plastic extrusion for their lethality to endotoxin and spores is an effective way to meet this requirement.

Appendix 3-A — Aseptic Processing from Early Manufacturing Steps

Original Text

The purpose of this appendix is to supplement the guidance with information on products regulated by CBER or CDER that are subject to aseptic processing at points early in the manufacturing process, or that require aseptic processing through the entire manufacturing process because it is impossible to sterile filter the final drug product.

A. Aseptic processing from early manufacturing steps

Some products undergo aseptic processing at some or all manufacturing steps preceding the final product closing step. In other instances, the final drug product cannot be sterile-filtered and, therefore, each component in the formulation would be rendered sterile and mixed aseptically. For example, products containing aluminum adjuvant are formulated aseptically because once they are alum adsorbed, they cannot be sterile-filtered.

Procedures (e.g., aseptic connection) that expose a product or product contact surfaces should be performed under unidirectional airflow in a Class 100 (ISO 5) environment. The environment of the room surrounding the Class 100 (ISO 5) environment should be Class 10,000 (ISO 7) or better.

Process simulation studies covering the steps preceding filling and sealing should be designed to incorporate all conditions, product manipulations, and interventions that could impact on the sterility of the product. The studies should include worst-case conditions such as maximum duration of open operations and maximum number of participating operators.

Process simulation studies for the formulation stage should be performed at least twice per year.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is Aseptic Operation Needed in Early Manufacturing Steps?

The standard aseptic processing logic is to sterilize by 0.22 µm filtration at the end of the process and then fill. But some products simply cannot be filtered, so sterility must be maintained throughout from much earlier steps:

  • Aluminum-adjuvanted vaccines: once adsorbed onto aluminum salts, the particles are too large to pass through a sterilizing-grade filter
  • Large-molecule proteins: some biologics may lose activity or be lost after filtration
  • Cellular therapy products: the living cells are themselves the "product" and cannot be filtered

Note: Scope Requirements for Process Simulation

Process simulation (media fill) in the early manufacturing stage has special requirements:

  • It must cover all aseptic manipulation steps before filling, not just the filling step itself
  • It must include worst-case conditions: the longest open time and the largest number of operators
  • It must simulate storage and transport of bulk tanks/containers
  • Process simulation for the formulation stage should be performed at least twice a year (more often than the standard filling line requirement)
Appendix 3-B — Cellular Therapy Products and Cell-Derived Products

Original Text

B. Aseptic processing of cellular therapy products and cell-derived products

Cellular therapy and some cell-derived products (e.g., lysates, semi-purified extracts) represent a subset of the products that cannot be filter-sterilized and therefore undergo aseptic manipulations throughout the manufacturing process. Where possible, closed systems should be used during manufacturing.

Cellular therapy products often have short processing times at each manufacturing stage, particularly between the harvest, formulation of the final product, and product release. These products are frequently released from the manufacturing facility and administered to patients before final product sterility testing results are available.

In situations where results of final sterility testing are not available before the product is administered, additional controls and testing should be considered. For example, additional sterility tests can be performed at intermediate stages of manufacture, such as after the last manipulation of the product prior to harvest.

Other tests that may indicate microbial contamination, such as microscopic examination, Gram stain (or other bacterial and fungal stain), and endotoxin testing should be performed and meet acceptance criteria prior to product release.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Unique Challenges of Cellular Therapy

Cellular therapy products face fundamental challenges that conventional sterile processes do not:

  • No sterilizing filtration: the living cells are the product itself and cannot be filtered out
  • Very short shelf life: the time from collection to administration may be only hours to days
  • Sterility test lag: the conventional 14-day sterility test cannot be completed before the product is administered
  • Personalized manufacturing: many cellular therapies are "autologous", one patient per batch, so scaled-up QC strategies do not apply

Note: Alternative Control Strategies for "Administer First, Results Later"

When final sterility test results are not available before administration, FDA expects additional microbiological controls:

  • In-process sterility testing: performed after the last manipulation (for example, before harvest)
  • Microscopic examination: rapid morphological examination
  • Gram stain: rapid differentiation of bacteria/fungi
  • Endotoxin testing: rapid LAL testing (can be completed within hours)

All alternative tests must meet predetermined acceptance criteria before release.

CDMO Application: The New Frontier of Cellular Therapy GMP

The quality system of a cellular therapy CDMO must be designed with an "expedited release pathway" that integrates in-process testing, rapid EM data and real-time microbiological monitoring, so that quality decisions can be made within a very short window. Regulatory requirements in this area are still evolving rapidly, so the latest FDA CBER guidance should be followed closely.

An Intuitive Understanding of the D Value

The D value is a "timing indicator" of microbial resistance. If a BI has a D value of 1.5 minutes, 90% of the spores are killed every 1.5 minutes. Achieving a 6-log kill (sterility assurance) requires 6 × 1.5 = 9 minutes of exposure (plus a safety factor). A BI with a higher D value represents a more stringent challenge.

Decontamination vs Disinfection vs Sterilization: Three Levels

  • Disinfection: reduces viable bioburden and may not kill spores; suitable for surface cleaning
  • Decontamination: uses a sporicidal agent to eliminate viable bioburden; suitable for isolator surfaces
  • Sterilization: destroys all forms of microorganisms (including spores) to a SAL ≤ 10⁻⁶; suitable for product-contact surfaces and final containers

The requirements increase in that order, and the choice depends on the level of contact risk.

The Two Types in the Isolator Definition

In its glossary, FDA gives a complete classification of isolators:

  • Closed isolator: material transfer is accomplished through aseptic connection to auxiliary equipment, rather than through openings to the surrounding environment. The whole operation remains sealed.
  • Open isolator: designed to allow continuous or semi-continuous ingress and egress of materials during operations, with engineered openings (such as continuous positive pressure) preventing external contaminants from entering.

Overkill vs Standard Sterilization: A Comparison of Conservatism

The overkill approach is the most conservative steam sterilization design strategy: regardless of the initial bioburden, the cycle is designed to deliver at least a 12-log kill. This is equivalent to the assurance that "even if the load were full of the most resistant microorganisms, they would all be destroyed".

The trade-off is a longer sterilization cycle time, which may put additional stress on heat-sensitive materials. It is therefore not suitable for every product and must be balanced against the product's heat stability.

Unidirectional Flow vs Laminar Flow: FDA's Choice of Wording

In its glossary, FDA gives similar but not identical definitions of these two terms: Laminar flow emphasizes the physical characteristics of the flow (single direction, parallel layers, constant velocity), while unidirectional flow emphasizes its functional purpose in aseptic processing: "sufficient to sweep particles away from the critical area in a reproducible manner". In the main text FDA prefers "unidirectional airflow" because it emphasizes the performance requirement rather than merely describing the flow pattern.
