Part 0: Overview & Scope of Subchapter F

Parts 600–680

21 CFR Subchapter F — Biologics (Parts 600–680) | FDA / CBER | eCFR current as of April 2026

Learning Objectives

  • Understand where 21 CFR Subchapter F sits within the FDA regulatory framework and how it relates to 21 CFR Parts 210/211
  • Identify the scope and principal regulated parties of each Part in Parts 600–680
  • Grasp the legal definition of a "biological product" and its regulatory implications
  • Understand, from a CDMO perspective, which Parts are directly relevant to operations
0.1 Regulatory Architecture of 21 CFR Subchapter F

Original Text

21 CFR Subchapter F (Parts 600–680) is FDA's regulatory framework governing biological products throughout their lifecycle — from licensing through manufacturing, testing, and postmarket reporting.

This applies to vaccines, blood products, plasma-derived therapies, allergenic substances, and certain diagnostic materials. Biological products regulated under section 351 of the Public Health Service Act (PHS Act) must also comply with applicable provisions of 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211 (drug CGMPs).

"Biological products and combination products that include biological product constituent parts must comply with all applicable requirements in parts 600 through 680."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Subchapter F: The subchapter of 21 CFR Title 21 (Food and Drugs), Chapter I (FDA), dedicated to regulating biologics. Its parent statute is Section 351 of the Public Health Service Act (PHS Act), not the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act). This is the legal starting point that distinguishes biological products from conventional drugs.

Dual compliance: A biological product must comply with both Parts 600–680 (biologics-specific requirements) AND Parts 210/211 (drug CGMP). It is not a choice between the two; both apply.

Analogy

Think of the regulatory system as building codes: Parts 210/211 are the "general building code" (every building must comply), while Subchapter F is the "hospital-specific supplement" (an additional layer on top of the general code). To build a hospital, you must follow both codebooks.

Key Notes

★ Parts 600, 601 and 610 are the core of CDMO aseptic filling operations: Part 600 covers facilities and deviation reporting, Part 601 covers licensing changes to the client's BLA, and Part 610 covers the three elements of quality testing (sterility, purity, potency) that every batch must pass before release.

★ Parts 606–680 (blood, diagnostic reagents, allergenics) are almost never applicable to a CDMO handling non-blood products, but the contract should still explicitly exclude them or limit their applicability.

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO takes on filling and packaging of a monoclonal antibody (mAb). Which Parts' requirements must it meet?
  2. Why are BLAs (biologics license applications) for biological products and NDAs (new drug applications) for drugs reviewed by different centers (CBER vs CDER)?
0.2 Definition: What Is a Biological Product?

Original Text

§ 600.3(h) Biological product: "A virus, therapeutic serum, toxin, antitoxin, vaccine, blood, blood component or derivative, allergenic product, protein, or analogous product, or arsphenamine or derivative of arsphenamine (or any other trivalent organic arsenic compound), applicable to the prevention, treatment, or cure of a disease or condition of human beings."

A protein is any alpha amino acid polymer with a specific, defined sequence that is greater than 40 amino acids in size.

"Applicable to the prevention, treatment, or cure" — includes diagnostic use via administration or application.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why the 40-amino-acid threshold matters: The FDA's 2020 final rule expressly defines a "protein" as a polymer of more than 40 amino acids. This means most monoclonal antibodies (mAb, ~1300 AA), insulin (51 AA, just above the threshold), recombinant proteins and similar products fall under Subchapter F.

History of the regulatory transition: Before 2020, many protein drugs (such as insulin) were reviewed by CDER under NDAs. Beginning March 23, 2020, under the BPCIA (Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act), they were automatically transitioned to BLA jurisdiction and required a new biologics license.

Analogy

Think of the 40-amino-acid threshold as a "size limit": just as some vehicles need a commercial driver's license because of their size, protein drugs above 40 AA need a "BLA license" rather than an ordinary NDA.

Key Notes

★ When a CDMO takes on mAb filling, recombinant protein formulations or vaccine filling, the client holds a BLA (not an NDA). The CDMO's contract manufacturing agreement must support BLA-related compliance obligations (45-day deviation reporting, prior approval of process changes, etc.).

★ Combination products whose primary mode of action is that of a biological product are likewise subject to BLA regulation.

Practical Application

Amaran takes on filling of a client's PD-1 monoclonal antibody. The product is a protein (>40 AA) used for treatment, so it is necessarily a biological product, and the client holds the BLA. As the contract manufacturer, Amaran must ensure that its SOPs and Quality Agreement are consistent with the client's BLA commitments on the 600.14 deviation reporting mechanism, the 601.12 process change agreement, and the 610.12 sterility test validation.

Section Summary

  • 21 CFR Subchapter F (Parts 600–680) is the FDA's complete regulatory framework for biological products, with PHS Act § 351 as its parent statute
  • Biological products must comply with both Subchapter F (biologics-specific requirements) AND Parts 210/211 (drug CGMP)
  • For an aseptic filling CDMO, the most critical Parts are 600 (facilities/deviations), 601 (BLA/changes) and 610 (the three elements of batch release)
  • The protein definition (>40 amino acids) places most mAbs and recombinant proteins under BLA jurisdiction, affecting CDMO contractual obligations

1.1 Key Definitions (§ 600.3)

Original Text

Key definitions governing all of Subchapter F:

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Regulatory implications of the definition of a lot: The core of a lot is that the material is "uniformly mixed in a single container." This determines which fills count as the "same lot," and in turn the scope of lot release testing and retention sample requirements. If a CDMO fills the same bulk drug substance in two separate runs and the bulk is not re-mixed, these may technically be different lots.

The trigger point for "Distributed": A product counts as distributed as soon as it leaves the manufacturer's control. This point in time is the key to the 45-day deviation reporting clock, even if the product is still in the client's warehouse, because it is already outside the CDMO's direct control.

Key Notes

★ The Part 600 definitions are the legal vocabulary for the whole of Subchapter F. Understanding them is essential to interpreting the boundaries of the obligations in the later Parts.

★ The regulatory definition of "manufacture" covers filling, testing, labeling, packaging and storage. A CDMO that takes on filling is therefore "manufacturing" and must comply with all relevant GMP requirements.

1.2 Personnel Standards (§ 600.10)

Original Text

Personnel must possess "capabilities commensurate with their assigned functions, a thorough understanding of the manufacturing operations" they perform, and receive proper training. Facilities must include professionally trained persons necessary for competent performance of all manufacturing processes.

Restriction categories:

  • (c)(1) Product safety — Individuals whose presence could "affect adversely the safety and purity of a product" face exclusion from manufacturing areas during active production.
  • (c)(2) Sterile operations — Those performing sterile work must don "clean or sterilized protective clothing and devices" sufficient to prevent product contamination.
  • (c)(3) Pathogenic work — Personnel handling human pathogens or spore-forming microorganisms must either be excluded from other manufacturing areas or change outer garments and footwear before entering such spaces.
  • (c)(4) Live vaccine areas — strictest rules — Personnel cannot enter live vaccine areas after working with other infectious agents the same day. Only essential personnel are permitted during active work. Street clothing must be replaced with laboratory attire. Animal care workers cannot contact other animals during their shift.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Competence vs training records: 600.10 requires not just "completion of training courses" but real understanding and the ability to carry out manufacturing operations. During inspections, FDA questions operators to verify genuine understanding; training records are only the baseline, and actual competence on the floor is what matters.

Analogy

The live vaccine area requirement in (c)(4) is the strictest form of segregation. Imagine a chef who handled raw chicken (a pathogen source) in the morning: in the afternoon he cannot go straight in to prepare sashimi (a sterile area). He must change clothing and shoes completely, and may not even enter certain areas that day.

Key Notes

★ For a biologics CDMO: if the facility makes both viral vaccines and non-viral biological products, the cross-restrictions on personnel ((c)(3) and (c)(4)) must be clearly specified in SOPs, with traceable records. This is a common observation in FDA biologics inspections.

★ "Commensurate with their assigned functions" means that different positions require different levels of competence verification: the training program for a filling operator is not the same as the requirements for a process development scientist.

Practice Questions

  1. How does FDA verify compliance with 600.10 during an inspection? How would you prepare?
  2. Which personnel positions in a CDMO facility are most likely to face the restrictions in (c)(3) or (c)(4)?
1.3 Records & Retention Samples (§§ 600.12–600.13)

Original Text

§ 600.12 Records

"Records shall be made, concurrently with the performance, of each step in the manufacture and distribution of products, in such a manner that at any time successive steps in the manufacture and distribution of any lot may be traced by an inspector."
  • Records must be legible and indelible
  • Must identify the person immediately responsible
  • Include dates of the various steps
  • Be as detailed as necessary for clear understanding by one experienced in product manufacture

§ 600.13 Retention Samples

Retain sufficient quantities from each lot of each product released for distribution to permit reexamination and retesting if needed. Retention samples must be maintained under conditions that will maintain product integrity.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Concurrent recording: "Concurrently with the performance" is the core of data integrity. Records may not be added after the operation is complete, filled in in advance, or have original data changed afterwards without leaving a trace of the change. This requirement is written explicitly into 600.12 and is fully consistent with the ALCOA+ principles.

Traceability: Records must allow an inspector to "follow each step in chronological order." This requires the CDMO's batch record system to have a clear index linking the manufacturing steps together.

Key Notes

★ The storage conditions and retention period for retention samples must be defined in an SOP and comply with the conditions approved in the BLA. For temperature-sensitive biological products, continuous temperature monitoring records must demonstrate the integrity of the samples throughout the retention period.

★ "Legible and indelible" means: no pencil; electronic records must have an audit trail and original entries must not be deletable.

Practice Questions

  1. If an operator completes the batch record 2 hours after filling ends, does this violate 600.12? How would you resolve it in a CAPA?
  2. Is the retention sample obligation borne by the CDMO, or can it be delegated to the client (the BLA holder)?
1.4 Biological Product Deviation Reporting (§ 600.14)

Original Text

Who must report: Licensed manufacturers holding a biological product license who had control over the product when the deviation occurred. Applies even when manufacturing steps are contracted to others.

What must be reported: Events associated with manufacturing, testing, processing, packing, labeling, storage, holding, or distribution if:

  • Represents a deviation from CGMP, applicable regulations, specifications, or established standards
  • Represents an unexpected or unforeseeable event affecting safety, purity, or potency
  • Occurs in your facility or contracted facilities
  • Involves a distributed biological product

Exceptions:

  • In vitro diagnostic products (report under Part 803)
  • Blood and blood components (report under § 606.171)
"You must report at a date not to exceed 45-calendar days from the date you acquire information reasonably suggesting that a reportable event has occurred."

Submission: Form FDA-3486 to CBER or CDER depending on product type.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

600.14 vs 21 CFR 314.81 (NDA deviation reporting): Biological products have their own deviation reporting mechanism (600.14), separate from the Field Alert Report (FAR) for drugs. Reports go to CBER or CDER using Form FDA-3486 (not Form FDA 3331a).

Start of the 45-day clock: The clock runs from the date on which you acquire information reasonably suggesting that a reportable event has occurred. It starts not when the deviation is confirmed but as soon as the initial information is obtained. This means the internal investigation must be completed within the 45-day window.

The CDMO's role: The deviation reporting obligation belongs to the BLA holder (usually the client). But the CDMO must define in the Quality Agreement within how many days it will notify the client (typically 3–5 days), so that the client has enough time to complete the investigation and submit Form FDA-3486 within 45 days.

Analogy

Imagine the CDMO as the "contract manufacturer" and the client (BLA holder) as the "brand owner." If the contract manufacturer has a problem, the brand owner is answerable to the outside world (to FDA). But the brand owner can only report what it knows, so the contract must ensure information flows quickly and never gets "stuck" inside the CDMO.

Key Notes

★ The triggers include "unexpected or unforeseeable events." This means that even where the outcome is good (for example, an investigation confirms the deviation had no effect on the product), if there is a possibility of affecting safety, purity or potency you should still assess whether a report is needed.

★ Deviations involving distributed products need particular attention. Once the product is in patients' hands, a deviation report can trigger a recall decision, with far greater impact than for undistributed lots.

Practical Application

Amaran takes on a mAb filling project, and after filling is complete the client has already collected some lots (distributed). An environmental monitoring (EM) excursion (OOS result) is then discovered. Under 600.14: (1) the CDMO must notify the client (BLA holder) immediately; (2) the client must assess whether this EM OOS constitutes a deviation affecting the safety, purity or potency of the distributed product; (3) if so, the client must submit Form FDA-3486 to CBER within 45 days. The CDMO's Quality Agreement should expressly require the CDMO to notify the client within 48 hours of any event that may trigger 600.14.

1.5 Postmarketing Adverse Experience Reporting (§ 600.80)

Original Text

Biological product license holders must promptly review and report adverse experiences from all sources — commercial marketing, postmarketing studies, scientific literature, and foreign data.

Required information — ICSRs (Individual Case Safety Reports): Patient data, adverse experience details, suspect product information, reporter identification, applicant contact details.

Electronic submission: All safety reports must be submitted electronically per FDA guidance.

Record retention: Adverse experience records must be maintained for 10 years.

Exemptions: Whole blood, blood components, and in vitro diagnostic products.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Serious AND unexpected (dual condition) triggers a 15-day report: "Serious" means life-threatening, requiring hospitalization, resulting in disability, congenital anomaly, or another important medical event; "unexpected" means an adverse reaction not listed in the currently approved labeling. A 15-day alert report is triggered only when both conditions are met.

10-year record retention: Compared with the general retention of drug records (usually 2–3 years), adverse experience records for biological products must be kept for 10 years, reflecting the long-term nature of safety monitoring for biologics.

Key Notes

★ The 600.80 obligations rest with the BLA holder (the client), not the CDMO. But if the CDMO receives an adverse event report through client complaints or other channels, it must forward it to the client immediately. The Quality Agreement should specify the complaint forwarding time limit (usually 1–3 working days).

★ Scientific literature monitoring is an important part of 600.80: the BLA holder must monitor published literature to identify possible new safety signals.

Practice Questions

  1. After a biological product is marketed, a patient reports a possible serious adverse event on Twitter. Does this require starting the 15-day reporting process?
  2. What is the CDMO's role in adverse experience reporting obligations? How should the Quality Agreement define it?

Section Summary

  • The 600.10 personnel requirements cover competence, training and personnel control across manufacturing areas; aseptic operations and live vaccine areas have the strictest segregation requirements
  • The concurrent recording principle in 600.12 is the regulatory foundation of data integrity; records may not be added or original records altered after the fact
  • 600.14 biological product deviation reporting: an obligation of the BLA holder, 45-day clock, Form FDA-3486; the CDMO must define in the Quality Agreement the time limit for notifying the client
  • 600.80 postmarketing adverse experiences: serious and unexpected → 15-day report; periodic reports (quarterly/annual); records retained for 10 years

21 CFR Part 601 §§ 601.2–601.95 | Subparts A, C, D, E, F, G, H

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the basic submission requirements for a BLA (biologics license application) (601.2) and the key differences from an NDA
  • Master the three-tier change management classification in 601.12 (Prior Approval, CBE-30/CBE, Annual Report)
  • Understand special review pathways such as Accelerated Approval and the Animal Rule

2.1 Biologics License Application Requirements (§ 601.2)

Original Text

Manufacturers must submit applications to the Director of CBER or CDER. Applications require:

  • Data from nonclinical laboratory and clinical studies demonstrating product meets safety, purity, and potency requirements
  • Statements on compliance with Parts 58 (nonclinical labs) and 56 (institutional review boards)
  • Manufacturing methods and stability data
  • Product samples and representative lot test results
  • Label specimens and container information
  • All manufacturing location addresses
  • Financial disclosure statements (Part 54)
  • Environmental assessment or categorical exclusion claim

Special categories — exempt from §§ 600.10–600.13, 610.53, 610.62:

  • Therapeutic DNA plasmid products
  • Synthetic peptide products (≤40 amino acids)
  • Monoclonal antibodies for in vivo use
  • Recombinant DNA-derived therapeutic products

§ 601.2(f) Withdrawal reporting: License holders must report product withdrawals within 30 working days, including BLA number, National Drug Code, and expected withdrawal date.

"To obtain a biologics license under section 351 of the Public Health Service Act for any biological product, the manufacturer shall submit an application with data derived from nonclinical laboratory and clinical studies which demonstrate that the manufactured product meets prescribed requirements of safety, purity, and potency."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

BLA vs NDA (biologics license vs new drug application):

  • BLA (Biologics License Application): Under PHS Act § 351, reviewed by CBER or CDER. What is approved is the combination of "product + facility," not just the molecule but also the manufacturing site.
  • NDA (New Drug Application): Under the FD&C Act, reviewed by CDER. What is approved is the "molecule," and the bar for changing the manufacturing site is relatively lower.
  • Key difference: Because a BLA licenses the facility, the CDMO site is named in the BLA. A site change requires a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) and may require an FDA inspection.

Regulatory status of the CDMO site: Once the CDMO site is listed in the BLA, the CDMO must accept an FDA Biologics Product Pre-Approval Inspection (BPIAI) and maintain continuous compliance.

Analogy

A BLA license is like a certification of "a specific chef making a specific dish in a specific kitchen." Changing the kitchen (changing the CDMO) requires re-certification, unlike ordinary drugs, where the contract manufacturer can be changed with relative flexibility.

Key Notes

★ mAbs (monoclonal antibodies) are expressly listed as a "special category" but are still subject to the overall BLA requirements. The exemption "from 600.10–600.13, 610.53 and 610.62" applies only to administrative procedures in specific subsections; the core quality standards still apply.

★ All manufacturing sites must be listed in the BLA. If a CDMO acts as a secondary manufacturer, the client must list the CDMO site in the BLA, which brings the CDMO formally into FDA's regulatory view.

Practice Questions

  1. If the CDMO's site is already listed in the client's BLA but the CDMO replaces filling line equipment, does the client need to report this change to FDA?
  2. What is the essential difference between a BLA and an NDA in the regulatory requirements for changing a manufacturing site?
2.2 Changes to an Approved BLA (§ 601.12)

Original Text

Holders of approved applications must describe and report all changes. Changes are classified by their potential impact:

For changes requiring PAS, FDA must approve the supplement before the changed product may be distributed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Practical meaning of the three-tier change classification:

  • Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): The strictest. FDA must approve before distribution; the wait is typically 6–12 months (standard review) or shorter (priority review). A CDMO introducing new filling equipment or making a major process change will usually require the client to file a PAS.
  • CBE-30: Distribution may begin 30 days after submission (if FDA has not objected). Risk: if FDA raises questions within the 30 days, distribution must be suspended.
  • Annual Report: The lowest-impact, administrative changes, which need only be described in the annual report.

Key Notes

★ Any process change introduced by the CDMO must first be coordinated with the client (BLA holder), who decides the change classification and files the appropriate supplement. The CDMO may not unilaterally implement a process change that could affect the BLA. This is the regulatory basis for the "client approval" step in the change control process.

★ A "new manufacturing site" usually triggers a PAS. This means that if a client wants to transfer production from its current CDMO to another, it may have to wait 6–12 months for FDA review. This is a regulatory risk that must be considered early in the "technology transfer clause" of a CDMO contract.

Change Management Decision Tree

Change to process / equipment / site / labeling?
         ↓
Major risk to product safety / purity / potency?
   Yes → Prior Approval Supplement (PAS)
         Distribute only after FDA approval
   No → Moderate impact (moderate risk)?
         Yes → CBE-30 or CBE
               Distribute 30 days after submission / immediately
         No → Annual Report
               Document in the annual report

Practical Application

Amaran plans to replace a critical filling needle component on the SA25 filler with a new design (to improve accuracy). The evaluation process: (1) the CDMO records the change proposal and assesses its impact on the BLA-approved process; (2) it is sent to the client (BLA holder) for evaluation; (3) the client determines the impact is "moderate" and files a CBE-30; (4) if FDA has no objection after 30 days, the CDMO may ship product filled with the new component; (5) the whole process must be fully documented (CDMO change control + a copy of the client's BLA supplement).

2.3 Special Approval Pathways (§§ 601.40–601.95)

Original Text

Subpart E — Accelerated Approval (§§ 601.40–601.46):

Accelerated approval pathway for biological products for serious or life-threatening illnesses based on a surrogate endpoint or intermediate clinical endpoint reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit.

  • Post-approval studies required to verify actual clinical benefit
  • FDA may withdraw approval if post-approval studies do not verify clinical benefit
  • Applicable to treatments for serious conditions with unmet medical needs

Subpart H — Animal Rule (§§ 601.90–601.95):

Allows approval based on animal model efficacy data when human efficacy trials are not feasible (e.g., chemical or biological threat agents).

  • Requires well-characterized animal model
  • Rigorous manufacturing standards apply — product quality must be rigorously defined
  • Post-approval studies in humans when feasible
  • Applicable to medical countermeasures (MCMs) against CBRN threats

Subpart G — Postmarketing Studies (§ 601.70):

Requirements for postmarketing studies committed to as a condition of approval, including annual status reporting.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The surrogate endpoint strategy of Accelerated Approval: Approval is based on a "surrogate endpoint," such as tumor shrinkage rate (rather than overall survival), so the drug reaches the market sooner. The condition is that a confirmatory trial must be completed after marketing. If the trial fails, FDA can withdraw the approval (a mechanism strengthened by FDORA in 2023).

Manufacturing quality implications of the Animal Rule: Because efficacy cannot be tested in humans, manufacturing quality (including lot-to-lot consistency and analytical methods) must be especially rigorous to ensure that the therapeutic effect of each lot is predictable. A CDMO taking on such products faces particularly high requirements for batch records and analytical method validation.

Key Notes

★ After marketing, an accelerated approval product remains under "conditional approval." The manufacturer must actively pursue the confirmatory trial and report progress in the annual report (§ 601.70). A CDMO taking on commercial batches of an accelerated approval product should understand that the client has an obligation to conduct the confirmatory trial.

★ Animal Rule products are typically products in BARDA/PHEMCE strategic stockpile programs: market demand is uncertain (depending on bioterrorism threats), but manufacturing quality requirements are extremely high.

Practice Questions

  1. A biological product is marketed under accelerated approval, and 2 years later the confirmatory trial fails and FDA withdraws the approval. As a CDMO, how do you handle inventory that has been manufactured but not yet distributed?
  2. Why are the quality standards for a product approved under the Animal Rule stricter than for a product approved on the basis of conventional clinical trials?

Section Summary

  • A BLA licenses the "product + facility" combination; once the CDMO site is listed in the BLA, it comes directly into FDA's regulatory view and must accept inspection
  • The three-tier change classification in 601.12 (PAS/CBE-30/Annual Report) determines the timeline from proposal to implementation for a CDMO process change
  • A CDMO may not unilaterally implement a process change affecting the BLA; it must go through the client's change control, and the client files the corresponding BLA supplement
  • Accelerated Approval and the Animal Rule are special review pathways with higher requirements for manufacturing consistency; the CDMO needs to understand the client's postmarketing study obligations

§§ 610.1–610.18

21 CFR Part 610 Subparts A–B | §§ 610.1–610.18

Learning Objectives

  • Master the four lot release test requirements of Part 610 Subpart B: potency (610.10), sterility (610.12), purity (610.13) and identity (610.14)
  • Understand the specific requirements for sterility test validation under 610.12 and the conditions limiting retesting
  • Understand the special significance of the 610.18 requirements for cultures and cell lines in biological product manufacturing

3.1 Lot Release Framework (§§ 610.1–610.2)

Original Text

§ 610.1 Required Tests: No product may be released by the manufacturer prior to the completion of tests for conformity with standards applicable to such product. The following standards apply to all biological products unless otherwise specified.

§ 610.2 Release of Products — Failure to Meet Standards: Products failing to meet applicable standards may not be released. FDA must be notified and product must be quarantined or destroyed per applicable procedures.

§ 610.9 Equivalent Methods and Processes: Modifications to required test methods or manufacturing processes permitted only when:

  • (a) Evidence submitted through BLA or supplement demonstrating "the modification will provide assurances of the safety, purity, potency, and effectiveness" equal to or greater than the original specified method
  • (b) Written approval obtained from Director of CBER or CDER
"No product may be released by the manufacturer prior to the completion of tests for conformity with standards applicable to such product." (§ 610.1)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Sequence of lot release obligations: Part 610 establishes an absolute "test first, release after" requirement: all required tests must be completed and meet the standards before the product may be distributed. This is consistent in spirit with 21 CFR 211.192 (QA review of drug products), but for biological products it is an explicit regulatory mandate, not an option.

The importance of equivalent methods (610.9): To replace an approved release test method (for example, replacing the traditional culture method with a rapid sterility test), you must file a BLA supplement providing equivalence data and obtain FDA's written approval. A unilateral change is not permitted.

Key Notes

★ 610.2 expressly provides that a product that does not meet the standards must be quarantined and FDA notified. This is not only a basic GMP requirement but also an FDA notification obligation specific to biological products. In some cases a recall procedure must be started.

★ The 610.9 equivalent method application is especially important as Rapid Microbiological Methods (RMM) become more widespread; introducing PAT (process analytical technology) requires this pathway.

Practice Questions

  1. After a lot of biological product has been filled, can it be distributed while awaiting the sterility test result (14 days)?
  2. A CDMO wants to replace the traditional culture method with the BacT/ALERT rapid sterility test. What regulatory pathway is required?
3.2 Potency Testing (§ 610.10)

Original Text

"Tests for potency shall consist of either in vitro or in vivo tests, or both, which have been specifically designed for each product so as to indicate its potency in a manner adequate to satisfy the interpretation of potency given by the definition in § 600.3(s) of this chapter."

§ 600.3(s) Potency definition: "The specific ability or capacity of the product, as indicated by appropriate laboratory tests or by adequately controlled clinical data obtained through the administration of the product in the manner intended, to effect a given result."

Key requirements:

  • Test type options: In vitro (laboratory-based), in vivo (animal-based), or combined testing
  • Product-specific design: Each biological product requires customized testing protocols — no generic methodology
  • Potency limits: Minimum and maximum potency limits established in the BLA for each product
  • Correlation to clinical effect: Tests must indicate potency in a manner that correlates to the clinical therapeutic effect

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Differences between potency, strength and purity:

  • Potency: The "ability" of the product to produce its therapeutic effect, a measure of biological activity correlated with clinical effect. Examples: the binding affinity, neutralizing activity and ADCC effect of a mAb.
  • Strength: The amount of active ingredient per unit dose (usually a concentration).
  • Purity: The state of being relatively free of extraneous matter (§ 610.13).

The potency of a biological product usually cannot be expressed by chemical content alone and requires a biological activity assay (such as a cell-based potency assay or an enzyme-linked immunoassay).

Analogy

A potency test is like testing the "alertness effect" of coffee. Measuring only the caffeine content (strength) is not enough, because different roasting methods affect the bioavailability of caffeine (potency). The same applies to biological products: protein content (strength) is not the same as therapeutic effect (potency).

Key Notes

★ Potency tests are highly product-specific. When a CDMO takes on a new client's product, it must confirm that it is capable of performing the specific potency test approved in the client's BLA, or confirm that the test is performed by the client or a third-party laboratory, and clearly allocate the responsibilities in the Quality Agreement.

★ In vivo potency tests (animal studies) face pressure from the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), and more and more products are moving to in vitro alternatives, but this requires an equivalent method application under 610.9.

Practice Questions

3.3 Sterility Testing (§ 610.12)

Original Text

Manufacturers must perform sterility testing of each lot of each biological product's final container material or other material as approved in the biologics license application.

Test validation requirement:

"The sterility test must be validated to demonstrate that the test is capable of reliably and consistently detecting the presence of viable contaminating microorganisms."

Written procedures must describe:

  • Test method description
  • Sampling methodology
  • Lot acceptance/rejection specifications
  • Sample considerations: lot size, manufacturing duration, container configuration, inhibitor/preservative concentrations, appropriate product dilution factors

Testing methods:

  • Culture-based methods: specified media composition, growth-promotion testing, and incubation conditions required
  • Non-culture-based methods: defined test parameters, acceptance criteria, and controls required

Repeat testing: One repeat test is permitted only if laboratory error or faulty materials caused the positive result. Not permitted due to inconclusive or ambiguous results alone.

Exceptions: Whole blood, vaccines, reagents may be exempt. FDA Director may waive if route of administration precludes sterility concern.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

610.12 vs USP <71> sterility testing: 610.12 is a regulatory requirement specific to biological products, while USP <71> is a methodology standard. The two are complementary: 610.12 provides that "each lot must be tested and the test must be validated," while USP <71> provides the specific culture methods, media and interpretation criteria. The CDMO's sterility testing SOP must comply with both.

Special requirements for test validation: 610.12 expressly requires that the sterility test be validated as able to reliably and consistently detect viable contaminating microorganisms. This means that for products containing bacteriostatic preservatives (such as benzyl alcohol or polysorbate), it must be validated that the dilution or neutralization method eliminates the bacteriostatic interference.

Key Notes

★ The limits on repeat tests are very strict: one repeat test is permitted only where a laboratory error or material defect is confirmed (with evidence). You cannot retest because a result is "uncertain." This is central to the data integrity of sterility testing. If the initial result is positive and is not a confirmed laboratory error, an OOS investigation and deviation report must be initiated.

★ 610.12 applies to "each lot." Skipping lots or using alternative sampling strategies is not permitted unless a 610.9 equivalent method approval has been obtained.

Sterility Test Validation Key Parameters

Media suitability (Growth Promotion):
- Aerobic media (FTM / TSB): at least 7 standard strains
- Growth promotion test: for each lot of media or before each use

Bacteriostasis / fungistasis assessment (B&F):
- First validation for each new product
- Revalidate after a formulation change (preservative/surfactant)
- Target: the recovery of the positive control strain in product-containing medium
  should be no less than 50% of the control without interference

Sampling plan:
- Minimum number of samples determined by batch size per the USP <71> tables
- Biological products in small-volume vials of <1 mL: ≥20 units per lot

Practical Application

Amaran takes on filling of a mAb containing Polysorbate-80. Polysorbate-80 is bacteriostatic, so it must be validated that the dilution ratio is sufficient to eliminate B&F interference. Validation approach: (1) determine the minimum effective dilution factor for PS-80 (usually between 1:10 and 1:100); (2) perform B&F testing under this dilution condition (7 standard strains, one set each for FTM and TSB); (3) confirm recovery ≥50%; (4) have it approved in the BLA or submit the data to the client as the basis for the 610.12 validation. This validation result must be included in the lot release record for each batch.

3.4 Purity (§ 610.13) & Identity (§ 610.14)

Original Text

§ 610.13 Purity: Each lot of biological product must be "free of extraneous material except that which is unavoidable in the manufacturing process" as approved in the BLA.

  • (a) Residual moisture testing: Each dried product lot must undergo testing for residual moisture and meet established limits per approved methods. CBER Director may exempt if unnecessary for product safety, purity, and potency.
  • (b) Pyrogenic substances testing: All final containers intended for injection must be tested for pyrogenic substances via intravenous rabbit injection. Exemptions apply to vaccines, plasma, and diagnostic substances.

§ 610.14 Identity:

"The contents of a final container of each filling of each lot shall be tested for identity after all labeling operations are complete."

The identity test must be "specific for each product in a manner that will adequately identify it as the product designated on final container and package labels and circulars, and distinguish it from any other product being processed in the same laboratory."

Acceptable identity methods:

  • Physical or chemical characteristics
  • Macroscopic or microscopic inspection
  • Specific cultural tests
  • In vitro or in vivo immunological tests

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two dimensions of purity:

  • Residual moisture: Mainly relevant to lyophilized products. Excess moisture affects product stability and is a key CQA for dry powder products.
  • Pyrogenic substances: 610.13(b) still retains the requirement for the Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT), but in practice most firms have switched to the LAL or rFC test (which requires 610.9 equivalent method approval).

The "distinctiveness" requirement for identity testing: 610.14 specifically stresses that the test must "distinguish the product from any other product being processed in the same laboratory." This is especially critical in a multi-product CDMO facility, where tests with sufficient specificity are needed to prevent mix-ups.

Key Notes

★ 610.14 requires the identity test to be performed after all labeling operations are complete. This ensures that verification of consistency between the final label and the contents is the last checkpoint, an important error-prevention measure for a multi-product CDMO.

★ Although the Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT) still appears in the regulatory text, FDA has indicated in several guidances that the LAL/rFC test is preferred and equivalent. A formal replacement still requires the 610.9 pathway.

★ For sterile biological products, "identity" is usually tested by immunological methods (such as ELISA or Western Blot) or electrophoresis (iCE or capillary electrophoresis). These methods are highly specific and can effectively distinguish different antibodies.

Practice Questions

  1. Amaran takes on two different mAbs at the same time (both IgG1 isotype). How would you design identity tests to meet the 610.14 requirement to "distinguish from other products in the same laboratory"?
  2. If the residual moisture of a lyophilized biological product is out of specification (for example, >3% against a specification of ≤2%), how must it be handled under 610.13?
3.5 Cultures & Cell Lines (§ 610.18)

Original Text

§ 610.18(a) Storage and maintenance: Cultures must be "stored in a secure and orderly manner, at a temperature and by a method that will retain the initial characteristics" while ensuring contamination-free conditions.

§ 610.18(b) Identity and verification: Each culture requires clear source identification. New stock preparations need complete strain identification; seed lots must have lot numbers and preparation dates.

"Periodic tests shall be performed as often as necessary to verify the integrity of the strain characteristics and freedom from extraneous organisms."

§ 610.18(c) Cell lines: Cell lines used in manufacturing must be:

  • Identified by history
  • Described regarding cytogenetic characteristics and tumorigenicity
  • Characterized for in vitro growth traits and life potential
  • Tested for detectable microbial agents

Primary cell cultures not subcultivated or subcultivated minimally are exempt.

§ 610.18(d) Records: Appropriate records "shall be prepared and maintained as required" per 21 CFR §§ 211.188 and 211.194.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Seed bank management system: 610.18 is the regulatory basis for the "Seed Lot System" in biological product manufacturing. The typical two-tier structure is:

  • Master Cell Bank (MCB): Highly characterized, stored long-term at low temperature, and used only to establish a Working Cell Bank.
  • Working Cell Bank (WCB): Vials are drawn from the WCB for each production run and, once used up, replenished from the MCB.

610.18(c) requires that the cytogenetic characteristics, tumorigenicity, growth characteristics and microbiological testing of the cell line all be documented. This is the regulatory basis for the MCB/WCB characterization package.

Key Notes

★ For a CDMO, 610.18 is usually the responsibility of the client (BLA holder) or its drug substance (DS) manufacturer. A fill-finish CDMO does not normally hold the cell lines directly, but it must ensure that the DS used comes from a cell line that meets 610.18, which should be verified through the Quality Agreement and supplier audits.

★ "Periodic tests as often as necessary" is a subjective standard. In practice, ICH Q5B and FDA biologics guidances call for full characterization of the WCB periodically (usually every 3–5 years) to confirm that there is no genetic drift.

Practice Questions

  1. If the DS supplied by a CDMO's client comes from a cell line that has not been fully characterized, how should the CDMO respond?
  2. What tests does the characterization package for a Master Cell Bank usually include?

Section Summary

  • The four Part 610 lot release tests: potency (610.10, product-specific, method approved in the BLA), sterility (610.12, every lot, validation includes B&F), purity (610.13, residual moisture + pyrogens), identity (610.14, after labeling is complete, must distinguish from other products in the same facility)
  • The limits on repeat sterility tests (610.12) are very strict: one repeat is allowed only when a laboratory error is confirmed, not because the result is uncertain
  • Replacing any lot release test method (such as switching to a rapid sterility test) requires FDA written approval through the 610.9 pathway
  • The 610.18 requirements for cultures and cell lines are specific to biological product manufacturing; a fill-finish CDMO must confirm the compliance of the client's DS supplier in the Quality Agreement

4.1 Dating Period Limitations (§§ 610.50–610.53)

Original Text

§ 610.50 Selling, barter, or exchange: No product may be sold, bartered, or exchanged after its expiration date.

§ 600.3(l) Dating period definition: "The period beyond which the contents of a package cannot be expected by reasonable assurance to yield its specific result."

§ 600.3(m) Expiration date: "The calendar day, month, and year, or month and year, beyond which the contents cannot be expected by reasonable assurance to yield their specific result."

§ 610.53 Dating periods for specific products: Dating periods (expiration dating) are established for individual biological products through the license application. Products must be labeled with expiration dates and may not be distributed beyond the established dating period unless additional testing demonstrates continued conformance with approved specifications.

Dating period establishment basis:

  • Real-time stability data under approved storage conditions
  • Accelerated stability studies (supporting data)
  • Product-specific stability-indicating assays
  • Container-closure system performance data

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Regulatory meaning of the dating period / expiration date: The dating period of a biological product is not a "manufacturer's guarantee period". It is the time frame during which, under the prescribed storage conditions, the product can reasonably be expected to continue to meet safety, purity and potency standards. It is determined by the stability data approved in the BLA.

Shelf life extension: To extend an approved dating period, you must submit a BLA supplement (usually a CBE-30 or PAS, depending on the level of impact) together with additional stability data supporting the longer dating period. A unilateral extension is not permitted.

Analogy

The dating period is like the coverage period of an insurance contract: during this period the manufacturer "guarantees" that the product meets all the standards approved in the BLA. Once the expiration date has passed, the "guarantee" lapses and the product may not be used or distributed. Extending the coverage requires resubmitting new "underwriting data" (stability data).

Key Notes

★ The dating period of biological products is usually much shorter than that of small-molecule drugs (typically 6–24 months, and shorter for some vaccines). This has a major impact on CDMO batch planning and inventory management: if the dating period after filling is only 12 months, release, distribution and end use must all be completed within that window.

★ Biological products past their expiration date must be destroyed or returned. They may not be sold or used in any form (including for research purposes) unless there is an explicit regulatory exemption.

Practice Questions

  1. A mAb batch needs 4 weeks of post-fill release testing and has a dating period of 18 months from filling. From the CDMO's perspective, how should the filling schedule be planned to ensure the customer has enough time to use the product?
  2. If the QC laboratory finds degradation in the retention samples of a batch that has already been distributed, while still within its dating period, what are the respective obligations of the CDMO and the customer?
4.2 Container Label Requirements (§ 610.60)

Original Text

Biological product containers capable of bearing a complete label must display:

  1. Product name — proper designation
  2. Manufacturer information — name, address, and license number
  3. Lot identification — lot number or equivalent identifier
  4. Expiration date
  5. Dosage — recommended individual dose for multi-dose containers
  6. Prescription designation — "Rx only" for prescription biologicals
  7. Medication Guide — required statement per 21 CFR 208.24(d) when applicable

Alternative label scenarios:

  • (b) Package label: If container lacks packaging, all package label requirements transfer to the container itself.
  • (c) Partial labels: For containers with space limitations, include minimum: product name, lot identification, manufacturer name, and recommended dose. Must be placed within outer packaging containing full package label details.
  • (d) No container label: Unlabelable containers may omit label information if enclosed in properly labeled outer packaging.
  • (e) Visual inspection: A sufficient uncovered container area must remain accessible along the full length or circumference to permit content inspection.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Compliance logic of the seven mandatory label elements:

  • Product name (Proper Name): This must be the name approved in the BLA, not the brand name. For example, "bevacizumab" rather than "Avastin".
  • Manufacturer information + license number: Note that this is the license number of the manufacturer, not the distributor. For a product filled by a CDMO, the license number on the label is that of the BLA holder (the customer), not the CDMO.
  • Lot number: This must be traceable to the complete manufacturing records. The CDMO's lot numbering system must interface with the customer's batch traceability system.

Key Notes

★ 610.60(e) visual inspection window requirement: a vial must have enough area not covered by the label to let inspectors see the liquid. This affects CDMO label design and labeler settings; a wrap-around label that covers the entire vial body would violate this requirement.

★ "Partial labels" are common on small containers (such as 1 mL or 2 mL ampoules): the lot number and product name must appear on the vial itself, with the full information on the outer carton. This requires the CDMO's label management system to ensure that vial and carton labels are matched correctly.

Practical Application

Amaran takes on a 2 mL glass vial filling project. The vial label area is limited and cannot carry all seven elements. Under 610.60(c), the acceptable approach is: (1) the vial label carries the product name, lot number, manufacturer name and dosage; (2) the full information (including expiration date, prescription legend, Medication Guide statement and address) appears on the carton label; (3) ensure the vial has an adequate visual inspection window (610.60(e)). This design must be pre-approved in the BLA, and the CDMO's Label Specification must be consistent with the BLA-approved version.

4.3 Package Label & Labeling Changes (§§ 610.61–610.68)

Original Text

§ 610.61 Package label must include all container label information PLUS:

  • Number of containers in the package (if multiple)
  • Volume of product in container
  • Storage temperature requirements
  • Diluent information if applicable
  • Name and address of the distributor if different from manufacturer
  • Statement of ingredients where required

§ 610.62 Proper name; distinguishing name: The proper name of the product must appear prominently on all labeling.

§ 610.63 Lot number: Must be a unique identifier traceable to manufacturing history of that specific lot.

§ 610.64 Expiration date: Must appear in an unambiguous format, correlated with the dating period established in the license.

§ 610.65 Recommended storage temperature: Label must state the required storage temperature to maintain product safety, purity, and potency throughout the dating period.

§ 610.67 Labeling changes: Changes to labeling must be approved per 21 CFR § 601.12 prior to implementation.

§ 610.68 Exemptions and alternatives: FDA may grant exemptions to specific labeling requirements upon written request with adequate justification.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Compliance layers of storage temperature labeling (610.65): The storage temperature on the label (for example "Store at 2°C–8°C") must be consistent with the storage conditions approved in the BLA. The CDMO's storage facility temperature management and shipping cold chain design must all meet the conditions stated on the label. If the label says 2–8°C but the warehouse occasionally goes out of range, this is both a misleading-labeling issue and a GMP compliance issue.

Regulatory pathway for label changes: Any label change (including minor font adjustments and address updates) must first go through the change classification assessment under 601.12, which determines whether it goes through a PAS, a CBE-30 or the Annual Report. A CDMO may not modify a label on its own without customer authorization and approval of the BLA change.

Key Notes

★ Lot number traceability (610.63) is the anchor of the entire batch record system. When FDA inspects a site, it often starts from the lot number of a marketed product and traces back through all manufacturing records, testing records and raw material records. The CDMO's lot numbering system must ensure that this traceability chain is complete, with no breaks.

★ "Distributor" information on the package label (610.61): if the customer uses a third party for distribution, the distributor's name may appear on the package label, but the name of the manufacturer (the license holder) must still appear. The CDMO needs to reserve this field when designing the label layout.

  1. The storage temperature approved in the customer's BLA is 2–8°C, but the customer now wants the label to say "stable for 3 days at up to 25°C" (to facilitate transport). What regulatory pathway does this label change require?
  2. How does the CDMO's label inspection procedure (Label Reconciliation) ensure compliance with the lot number traceability requirement of 610.63?
4.4 Transfusion-Transmitted Infection Testing (§§ 610.39–610.48) — Blood Products Only

Original Text

Subpart E — Applicability: Testing requirements for transfusion-transmitted infections applicable to blood and blood components. These requirements do NOT apply to non-blood biological products (vaccines, mAbs, recombinant proteins).

Required testing per § 610.40–610.47:

"You must test each donation of Whole Blood or blood components intended for transfusion with a licensed, approved, or cleared test." (§ 610.40)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Scope of Subpart E: The transfusion-transmitted infection testing requirements (§§ 610.39–610.48) apply only to "blood and blood components". For a sterile biologics CDMO (filling mAbs, vaccines, recombinant proteins), this Subpart does not apply directly.

However, some products made from blood-derived starting materials (such as Immune Globulin and Albumin) involve plasma sourcing, and source controls for these may need to follow the spirit of Subpart E, which is the responsibility of the drug substance (DS) manufacturer.

Key Notes

★ For a CDMO handling non-blood products, the main value of Subpart E is to "know it, but not need to comply with it directly". If there is a future opportunity to take on filling of plasma-derived products (such as IVIG and Albumin), the integrated requirements of Parts 606, 630 and 640 and Subpart E will need to be studied in depth.

Practice Questions

  1. A customer wants Amaran to take on filling and packaging of intravenous immune globulin (IVIG). IVIG is a plasma-derived product. When starting the business assessment, which Parts' compliance requirements should be understood first?
  2. In blood safety management, how do the NAT (nucleic acid testing) and antibody testing required by Subpart E complement each other?

Section Summary

  • The dating period is determined by the stability data approved in the BLA; extending it requires submitting a BLA supplement and cannot be done unilaterally
  • Seven mandatory container label elements (610.60): product name, manufacturer + license number, lot number, expiration date, dosage, prescription legend, Medication Guide statement; when space is insufficient, a "partial label + full carton label" may be used
  • Visual inspection window on labels (610.60(e)): enough of the container area must remain uncovered by the label to allow visual inspection of the liquid
  • Transfusion-transmitted infection testing (Subpart E §§ 610.39–610.48) applies only to blood components; CDMOs for non-blood biologics do not need to comply directly

Original Text

Part 606 — Current Good Manufacturing Practice for Blood and Blood Components:

Establishes CGMP requirements specific to blood and blood component manufacturing. Covers:

  • Equipment calibration, maintenance, and qualification
  • Supplies and reagents — licensed tests for infectious disease markers
  • Facilities — donor collection areas, processing labs, storage
