I. Introduction

This guidance describes and explains the final rule on current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements for combination products that FDA issued on January 22, 2013 (final rule). (21 CFR part 4). Prior to issuance of the final rule, although CGMP regulations were in place to establish requirements for drugs, devices, biological products, and Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products (HCT/Ps), there were no regulations to clarify and explain the application of these CGMP requirements to combination products.

The final rule did not establish any new requirements; it was intended to clarify which CGMP requirements apply when drugs, devices, and biological products are combined to create combination products, and to set forth a transparent and streamlined regulatory framework for firms to use when demonstrating compliance with applicable CGMP requirements.

Section II provides definition of "combination product," overview of the final rule, and describes the role of the lead center and other Agency components with respect to combination product CGMP issues. Section III addresses certain general considerations for CGMP compliance for combination products. Section IV presents the purpose and content of specific CGMP requirements addressed in the final rule. Finally, Section V analyzes hypothetical scenarios that illustrate how to comply with certain CGMP requirements for specific types of combination products.

FDA's guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead, this guidance describes the Agency's current thinking on a topic that should be viewed only as recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited.

Introduction  |  Commentary

Core concept: what does this guidance explain?

FDA formally issued 21 CFR Part 4 (the final rule) on January 22, 2013, and this 2017 guidance document is its explanatory manual. The key points are:

  • Part 4 created no new requirements. It only clarifies how the existing drug, device and biologic CGMP requirements apply to combination products.
  • Before Part 4, industry faced a dilemma: it was unclear which set of CGMP to follow for a "drug-device" product.
  • Part 4's core contribution is a transparent and streamlined compliance framework.

Analogy: traffic rules across county lines

Imagine a car driving on a road along the border between two counties. Before Part 4, nobody said clearly which county's rules applied. Part 4 is the "rulebook for border roads": it adds no new speed limits, it only tells you which limit applies and whether there is a way to satisfy both sets of rules at once.

Note on legal status

This guidance document is not legally enforceable; it only represents FDA's current thinking. Part 4 itself (21 CFR part 4), however, is a formal regulation with legal force. Keep the two clearly separate.

Regulatory timeline at a glance

  • 2013-01-22: 21 CFR Part 4 final rule formally issued
  • 2017-01: this guidance issued, giving a detailed explanation of Part 4
  • 2026-02-02: QMSR takes effect (see later in this section)

II. Background

A. Definition of a Combination Product

As set forth in 21 CFR part 3, a combination product is a product composed of two or more different types of medical products (i.e., a combination of a drug, device, and/or biological product with one another). The drugs, devices, and biological products included in combination products are referred to as constituent parts of the combination product.

Under 21 CFR 3.2(e), a combination product includes:

  • Single-entity combination product — A product comprised of two or more regulated components (drug/device, biologic/device, drug/biologic, or drug/device/biologic) that are physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or mixed and produced as a single entity. Examples: a prefilled syringe, a drug-eluting stent.
  • Co-packaged combination product — Two or more separate products packaged together in a single package or as a unit. Examples: a surgical kit, a first-aid kit containing a drug.
  • Cross-labeled combination product — A drug, device, or biological product packaged separately that according to its investigational plan or proposed labeling is intended for use only with an approved, individually specified drug, device, or biological product. Example: a light-emitting device intended for use only with a specific light-activated drug.
  • Investigational cross-labeled combination product — Any investigational drug, device, or biological product packaged separately intended for use only with another individually specified investigational product.

II.A Definition  |  Commentary

The PFS is the classic single-entity product

Take the daily work of an aseptic fill-finish CDMO as an example: the pre-filled syringe (PFS) is a product in which the drug solution (drug) and the syringe (medical device) are physically combined, making it the standard "single-entity combination product".

By contrast, a kit containing an empty syringe and a separate vial of drug is a "co-packaged" product.

The legal meaning of "constituent part"

Constituent parts is an important term: it refers to the individual drug, device or biological product that is combined into the combination product. Each keeps its own original regulatory status (drug, device or biologic) and does not change because it has been combined. This directly affects which CGMP requirements apply.

Practice question

A company manufactures an inhaler pre-loaded with a specific asthma drug and sells it as a single product. What type of combination product is this?

Hint: the whole product is made as one unit, and the drug and device are shipped physically combined.

Answer: a single-entity combination product.

B. Quality and Current Good Manufacturing Practice

One of FDA's goals is to assure the availability of quality drugs, biologics, devices, and combination products that consistently meet applicable requirements and specifications. The drug CGMP and device QS regulations, as well as the CGMPs for biologics and current good tissue practices for HCT/Ps, provide a framework of minimum requirements to help assure product quality.

The core requirements provide for systems that assure proper design, monitoring, and control of manufacturing processes and facilities. This includes:

  • Establishing a strong quality management system
  • Using appropriate quality raw materials
  • Establishing robust manufacturing and control procedures based on sound design principles
  • Detecting and investigating product quality deviations

II.B Quality Foundations  |  Commentary

Analogy: CGMP is a "minimum-standard building code"

CGMP is like a building code: it sets the minimum safety standard but does not stop you from building better. It does not guarantee quality; it is the "threshold" for entering the market. For a combination product the difficulty is that the drug "building code" and the device "building code" have different requirements, and Part 4 tells you how to meet both at the same time.

QS vs. QMSR: an important 2026 update

When this guidance was issued (2017), the device CGMP was called the "Quality System (QS) Regulation" (21 CFR Part 820). Effective February 2, 2026, Part 820 was updated to the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), which is closely harmonized with ISO 13485:2016.

When reading this guidance, any mention of "Part 820" or the "QS regulation" should be understood as referring to the current QMSR.

C. Overview of the Final Rule

1. Summary of the Final Rule

The constituent parts of a combination product retain their regulatory status (as a drug or device, for example) after they are combined. The final rule clarifies that the CGMP requirements that apply to each of the constituent parts apply to the combination product they constitute.

This guidance refers to a "CGMP operating system" to mean the operating system within an establishment that is designed and implemented to address and meet the current good manufacturing practice requirements applicable to the manufacture of a combination product.

For single-entity and co-packaged combination products, part 4 identifies two ways to demonstrate compliance:

II.C Final Rule Overview  |  Commentary

Core principle: constituent parts keep their regulatory status

This is the most fundamental logic of Part 4: combining a drug and a device does not turn the drug into a device, nor the device into a drug. Each constituent part retains its original regulatory identity, so the CGMP requirements for each must still be met.

The question is: how do you satisfy two sets of CGMP at once? That is the problem Part 4 solves.

What is the "CGMP operating system"?

This is an operational concept introduced by the guidance. It refers to the complete set of quality arrangements a facility designs and implements to meet the CGMP requirements for a combination product. It includes:

  • SOPs, records and training system
  • QMS framework (CAPA, design controls, purchasing controls, etc.)
  • Control systems for facilities, equipment and personnel

The facility's quality documentation must clearly identify this operating system.

Analogy: Option 1 vs Option 2

Option 1 is like holding two driver's licenses at once (car + motorcycle): every requirement of each license must be met in full, with nothing omitted. It is the most work, but also the least open to dispute.

Option 2 (the streamlined approach) is like an "integrated license test": you take one license as the base and then take only the key subjects for the other type of vehicle. It greatly reduces duplicated requirements, but you need to know exactly which subjects to add.

Option 2: The Two Streamlined Approaches

A manufacturer may choose either streamlined approach regardless of which constituent part provides the primary mode of action (PMOA).

Drug CGMP-based pathway — the 6 added device QS provisions:

  • 820.20 — Management responsibility
  • 820.30 — Design controls
  • 820.50 — Purchasing controls
  • 820.100 — Corrective and preventive action (CAPA)
  • 820.170 — Installation
  • 820.200 — Servicing

Device QS-based pathway — the 8 added drug CGMP provisions:

  • 211.84 — Testing and approval or rejection of components, drug product containers, and closures
  • 211.103 — Calculation of yield
  • 211.132 — Tamper-evident packaging requirements for OTC human drug products
  • 211.137 — Expiration dating
  • 211.165 — Testing and release for distribution
  • 211.166 — Stability testing
  • 211.167 — Special testing requirements
  • 211.170 — Reserve samples

For cross-labeled combination products manufactured at entirely separate facilities, only the regulations for that constituent part apply (not the streamlined approach).

Streamlined Approaches Explained  |  Commentary

The logic of the two streamlined approaches

Approach A: drug CGMP-based
= 21 CFR 210/211 (in full)
+ 6 key device provisions from Part 820
(management responsibility, design controls, purchasing, CAPA, installation, servicing)

Approach B: device QS-based (now QMSR)
= 21 CFR 820 in full (aligned with ISO 13485)
+ 8 key drug provisions from 21 CFR 211
(component testing, calculation of yield, tamper-evident packaging, expiration dating, release testing, stability testing, special testing requirements, reserve samples)

Why were these 6 / 8 provisions chosen?

FDA chose these provisions because each represents a core element of the other framework that "cannot be overlooked":

  • The 6 device provisions added for drug manufacturers: these mainly address device-specific design controls (820.30) and CAPA (820.100), which have no explicit counterpart in traditional drug CGMP.
  • The 8 drug provisions added for device manufacturers: these mainly address drug-specific expiration dating (211.137), stability testing (211.166) and component testing (211.84), which have no direct counterpart in the device QS.

PMOA determines the lead center, but not the approach chosen

This is the concept most easily confused in this section. The Primary Mode of Action (PMOA) only determines which FDA Center (CDER/CDRH/CBER) acts as lead center; it does not restrict which streamlined approach the manufacturer chooses.

Example: for a PFS whose PMOA is that of a drug, CDER is the lead center, but the manufacturer may choose "device QS-based + 8 drug provisions" and is not obliged to choose the "drug CGMP-based" approach.

The exception for cross-labeled products

If the two constituent parts are manufactured in completely separate facilities, each facility only needs to follow the regulations for its own part (drug or device) and does not need to use a streamlined approach. The streamlined approaches mainly apply where a single facility manufactures or assembles the entire combination product.

2. Documentation of CGMP Approach

Combination product manufacturers should be able to identify all documentation needed to demonstrate compliance with part 4, and make it readily accessible for FDA inspection. The facility's quality system documentation should identify the CGMP operating system for the combination product(s) manufactured at that facility.

  • NDAs, BLAs, ANDAs: CGMP approach described in the Common Technical Document (CTD).
  • PMAs: Documented in the manufacturing section of the PMA.

D. The Role of the Lead Center and Other Agency Components

A combination product is assigned to an Agency center (lead center) based on the primary mode of action (PMOA) — which constituent part provides the most important therapeutic action. The lead center has primary jurisdiction for:

  • Premarket review
  • Ensuring compliance with CGMP requirements

Manufacturers may contact the Office of Combination Products (OCP) for assistance with PMOA determinations and jurisdictional questions.

Note on QMSR (Important for 2026): The Quality System (QS) regulation (21 CFR Part 820) was revised effective February 2, 2026 to become the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), harmonizing with ISO 13485:2016. This guidance was issued prior to that effective date; manufacturers should review the current QMSR when applying the device QS-based streamlined approach.

Documentation Requirements and Lead Center  |  Commentary

Documentation requirements: must be ready at inspection

During an FDA inspection, the manufacturer must be able to clearly identify and promptly provide all documents used to demonstrate Part 4 compliance. This means the quality system documents (such as the quality manual or procedures) must state clearly:

  • Which streamlined approach the facility has adopted (or Option 1, full compliance)
  • How the corresponding CGMP provisions are implemented on site
  • Which SOPs, records and validation documents cover which regulatory provisions

Major 2026 update: QS Reg to QMSR

Effective date: February 2, 2026

21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation) has been formally renamed and revised as the QMSR (Quality Management System Regulation), closely harmonized with ISO 13485:2016.

Impact on combination product manufacturers:

  • Manufacturers that choose the "device QS-based" streamlined approach must now map to the QMSR rather than the old Part 820.
  • The QMSR adopts the clause structure of ISO 13485, which differs from the previous Part 820 numbering.
  • All references to "Part 820" or "820.xx" provisions in this guidance (2017) need to be re-mapped under the QMSR framework.
  • Manufacturers should update their QMS documents to make sure they cite the correct, current version of the regulation.

CDMO practical view: which streamlined approach to choose?

For a CDMO whose main business is aseptic fill-finish, this strategic decision is critical:

  • Drug CGMP-based (Approach A): suited to drug CDMOs that already have a complete 21 CFR 210/211 compliance system. Of the 6 added device provisions, 820.30 (design controls) and 820.100 (CAPA) have the greatest impact on an aseptic fill-finish CDMO and require a dedicated design control documentation system.
  • Device QS-based (Approach B): suited to CDMOs focused mainly on device manufacturing or manufacturers that have already implemented ISO 13485. Of the 8 added drug provisions, 211.137 (expiration dating) and 211.166 (stability testing) are the key new requirements.

Practical recommendation: Most aseptic fill-finish CDMOs have drug CGMP at their core, so Approach A is the most natural choice, but they must seriously build a device design control system and cannot skip 820.30 simply because "this is the client's design".

Relationship between the lead center and PMOA

The OCP (Office of Combination Products) helps determine the PMOA and coordinates across centers. If a manufacturer is unsure which center should be responsible for a product, it can submit an RFD (Request for Designation) to the OCP.

Section Summary

Sections I and II establish the regulatory architecture for combination product CGMP compliance. The key takeaways are:

  • Part 4 clarifies, does not create — existing CGMPs apply to constituent parts; Part 4 explains how.
  • Four types of combination products exist under 21 CFR 3.2(e): single-entity, co-packaged, cross-labeled, investigational cross-labeled.
  • Two compliance options: full compliance with all applicable CGMPs (Option 1), or a streamlined approach (Option 2).
  • Two streamlined pathways: drug CGMP-based (+ 6 device provisions) or device QS/QMSR-based (+ 8 drug provisions).
  • PMOA determines lead center, not which streamlined pathway to use.
  • QMSR in effect Feb 2, 2026 — replaces Part 820 for device QS.

Practice Questions: Self-Assessment

Q1. A CDMO manufactures a pre-filled syringe (PFS) whose drug PMOA is pharmacological action. (a) What type of combination product is this? (b) Which is the lead center? (c) Is the manufacturer limited to the "drug CGMP-based" streamlined approach?

Reference answer: (a) Single-entity combination product; (b) CDER; (c) No, the manufacturer is free to choose either of the two streamlined approaches.

Q2. A manufacturer choosing the "device QS-based" streamlined approach must additionally follow which 8 drug CGMP provisions? Which one is most critical for aseptic fill-finish products?

Reference answer: 211.84, 211.103, 211.132, 211.137, 211.165, 211.166, 211.167, 211.170. The most critical for aseptic fill-finish are 211.166 (stability testing) and 211.137 (expiration dating).

Q3. After February 2, 2026, if a CDMO's QMS documents still cite "21 CFR 820.100 CAPA", how should they be updated?

Reference answer: Part 820 has been updated to the QMSR, so the regulatory references in the QMS documents must be updated, and the QMSR provisions (ISO 13485:2016 structure) must be checked to confirm that the CAPA requirements are fully met.

Introduction

Important reminder: scope of this section

This section addresses CGMP requirements only. A change to product design, intended use or method of manufacture may also trigger premarket submission obligations. The two are parallel obligations and neither replaces the other.

CDMOs should take particular care: when supporting a client with combination product manufacturing, any change must be assessed for both CGMP compliance and regulatory submission.

A

III.A Demonstrating Compliance

How manufacturers must demonstrate compliance under the streamlined approach

What "Demonstrate" Means

The final rule identifies two ways for co-packaged and single-entity combination product manufacturers to demonstrate compliance with applicable CGMP requirements. The Agency intends for the term "demonstrate" to apply for part 4 as it would be for purposes of fulfilling the underlying CGMP regulations listed in 21 CFR 4.3. Manufacturers must demonstrate compliance with each applicable CGMP requirement for constituent parts and combination products.

If a streamlined approach is used, the manufacturer must demonstrate compliance with all of the relevant provisions of either the drug CGMPs or device QS regulation, and the provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b) for the other set(s) of CGMP requirements applicable to the product.

A manufacturer who implements a streamlined approach is not expected to demonstrate compliance with the specific provisions of the non-base set of requirements other than those specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b) (e.g., for a drug CGMP-based streamlined approach, the manufacturer must demonstrate compliance with applicable drug CGMPs plus the six specified provisions from part 820, as applicable, but the manufacturer does not need to demonstrate compliance with the other specific provisions in part 820).

Commentary

The standard for "demonstrate"

The word "demonstrate" in Part 4 carries exactly the same standard as in the underlying CGMP regulations (Parts 210/211, 820, etc.): the manufacturer must be able to prove to an FDA investigator that it actually complies, not merely claim compliance on paper.

Requirement structure of the streamlined approaches

Drug CGMP-based streamlined approach:

  • Must demonstrate: all applicable drug CGMP (Parts 210/211)
  • Additionally demonstrate: the six Part 820 provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2)
  • Need not demonstrate: the other provisions of Part 820

Device QS regulation-based streamlined approach:

  • Must demonstrate: all applicable Part 820 requirements
  • Additionally demonstrate: the Parts 210/211 provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1)
  • Need not demonstrate: the other provisions of Parts 210/211

Conflict resolution principle (21 CFR 4.4(e))

If regulations conflict, the provision that is most specifically applicable to the constituent part in question governs (the specific overrides the general). The purpose of this provision is to resolve regulatory conflicts, not to authorize FDA to require manufacturers to comply with requirements beyond Part 4.

Cross-Referenced Provisions

Some of the provisions specified in part 4 from the drug CGMPs and device QS regulation cross-reference other provisions of parts 211 and 820, respectively. This raises the question of whether manufacturers must specifically demonstrate compliance with these cross-referenced provisions. FDA believes that manufacturers can adequately address the expectations of these cross-referenced provisions through implementation of either a drug CGMP-based or device QS regulation-based CGMP streamlined approach.

For example, 21 CFR 211.170 (Reserve samples) references 21 CFR 211.192 (Production record review). However, 21 CFR 211.192 is not a specified provision in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2) because part 820 encompasses the requirements of that section. In particular, under the CAPA requirements in 21 CFR 820.100, a manufacturer operating under a device QS-based streamlined approach should have the systems in place that would satisfy the requirements of 21 CFR 211.192.

Similarly, 21 CFR 211.103 (Calculation of yield) references 21 CFR 211.68 (Automatic, mechanical, and electronic equipment). Corresponding provisions in 21 CFR 820.70(i), applicable under a QS-regulation based streamlined approach, require that automated data processing systems be validated for their intended uses.

To take another example, the definition of design output in 21 CFR 820.3 makes reference to "device master record." Both part 211 and part 820 have requirements for "master records" (21 CFR 211.186 and 21 CFR 820.181). FDA believes the substantive expectations of these master record provisions are aligned and needed to control product development and manufacture.

Handling of cross-referenced provisions

Analogy: the regulatory "signpost"

Imagine Regulation A says "see also Regulation B". This cross-reference does not automatically require you to also "demonstrate" compliance with Regulation B. FDA's position is that as long as your overall system (drug CGMP or QS) substantively achieves the purpose of Regulation B, you have met the requirement.

The key is "substantive equivalence", not a clause-by-clause match.

Three typical examples

  • 211.170 to 211.192: the reserve sample provision refers to production record review. Under the QS-based approach, 820.100 (CAPA) covers the substance of production record review.
  • 211.103 to 211.68: yield calculation refers to automated equipment. 820.70(i) has a corresponding requirement for validation of automated data processing.
  • 820.3 (design output) to 820.181 DMR: 211.186 (MPCR) and 820.181 (DMR) are substantively aligned; both systems require a master record to control product development and manufacture.

III.B Investigational Products

Phase-dependent CGMP exemptions for drugs and devices in combination products

Scope and Phase Exemptions

Part 4 does not alter the scope of the underlying CGMP regulations for drugs, devices, biological products, and HCT/Ps. In particular, part 4 does not alter the applicability of these CGMP regulations to investigational products.

An investigational drug for use in a phase 1 study is subject to the statutory requirements set forth in 21 U.S.C. 351(a)(2)(B). The production of such a drug is generally exempt from compliance with the regulations in parts 210 and 211 and, therefore, so is an investigational combination product that includes a drug constituent part for use in a phase 1 study. This exemption does not apply to an investigational combination product or drug constituent part, however, once it has been made available for use by or for the sponsor in phase 2 or phase 3 studies, nor does the exemption apply to a drug product that has been lawfully marketed.

Under 21 CFR 812.1, investigational devices are exempt from part 820 except for design control requirements under 21 CFR 820.30. This exemption also applies to the manufacturing of investigational combination products that include device constituent parts. Investigational combination products that include a device constituent part are subject to design controls (except when the device constituent part is exempt from design controls), but not to the other provisions of part 820.

Design controls should start as early as possible, once a company has determined that a product appears to have clinical utility and management has committed to further development.

Overview of CGMP exemptions by investigational phase

Design controls (820.30) are never exempt

Whatever stage the investigational product is at, if it contains a device constituent part, the design controls in 21 CFR 820.30 must apply (unless the device constituent part is itself exempt from design controls under its product classification).

Design controls should start as soon as management decides to proceed with development, not after Phase 1 is complete.

Analogy: design controls are like architectural blueprints

You would not wait until a house is half built to draw the blueprints. Design controls are the design specifications, verification plans and approval processes established before construction begins; the earlier they start, the more you avoid costly design changes later.

FDA states explicitly: "Design controls should begin once management decides to proceed with development." They are not something to be added after clinical success.

CDMO application scenario

A CDMO takes on a client's Phase 1 drug manufacture: although Parts 210/211 are exempt, FDA recommends still using "manufacturing methods, facilities and controls suitable for investigational products" and considering the potential hazards of the manufacturing environment.

Practical recommendation: even during the Phase 1 exemption period, establish an appropriate quality management foundation so that the transition to full CGMP requirements at Phase 2 is smooth.

III.C.1 "Manufacture" and "Manufacturer"

Broad definition — designing is manufacturing too

Broad Definition of Manufacture

The definition of the term "manufacture" in part 4 is intended to include all of the activities considered within the scope of manufacturing for drugs, devices, biological products, and HCT/Ps. Accordingly, the definition of "manufacture" in 21 CFR 4.2 includes, but is not limited to, designing, fabricating, assembling, filling, processing, testing, labeling, packaging, repackaging, holding, and storage.

For example, if a company designs a drug-device combination product, that activity constitutes manufacture of that product, and the facility at which the design work occurs is a manufacturing facility. That company is a manufacturer subject to part 4 for the design activities it performs, even if all other aspects of the combination product's manufacture (e.g., fabricating, labeling, or packaging) are performed by another entity.

Similarly, a facility responsible for the holding and storage of a combination product would be subject to related CGMP requirements such as 21 CFR 211.142 (Warehousing procedures) and/or 21 CFR 820.150 (Storage), depending upon whether the facility applies a streamlined approach and if so, which approach it applies.

The broad definition of "manufacture"

Scope of the definition of "Manufacture" in 21 CFR 4.2

"Manufacture" under Part 4 includes, but is not limited to:

  • Designing: the most important extension; see the explanation below
  • Fabricating and assembling
  • Filling and processing
  • Testing and labeling
  • Packaging and repackaging
  • Holding and storage

Design is manufacture: regulatory implications

This is a highly important regulatory principle: even if you are only responsible for design and never actually produce anything, you are still a "manufacturer" under Part 4 and must comply with the applicable CGMP requirements.

For example: a company that specializes in developing drug-device combination product designs has a design office that is a "manufacturing facility", even if all physical manufacturing is outsourced to other firms.

Analogy: the architect is also a "builder"

An architect only draws plans and does not build, but is still legally responsible for the safety of the building design. "Design is manufacture" under Part 4 works the same way: design decisions determine the safety and effectiveness of the product, so design activities are subject to CGMP.

Key implication for CDMOs

If a CDMO provides design services for a client (for example, helping to design the filling and assembly parameters of a prefilled syringe, or helping the client develop compatibility specifications between a drug and its delivery device), that CDMO becomes a manufacturer under Part 4 and must take on the corresponding CGMP compliance responsibility. It cannot avoid this by saying "we are only a service provider, not a manufacturer".

III.C.2 "Constituent Part" vs. "Component"

Critical terminology distinction — different regulatory purposes

Different Terms, Different Purposes

The term "constituent part" is used by the Agency as a succinct way to identify a drug, device, or biological product included in a combination product.

Under the drug CGMPs, "component" is defined as "any ingredient intended for use in the manufacture of a drug product, including those that may not appear in such drug product" (21 CFR 210.3). Under the device QS regulation, the term "component" is defined as "any raw material, substance, piece, part, software, firmware, labeling, or assembly which is intended to be included as part of the finished, packaged, and labeled device" (21 CFR 820.3(c)). Components can include sub-assemblies that are further processed to make a finished device.

In contrast, a finished device is "suitable for use or capable of functioning, whether or not it is packaged, labeled, or sterilized," as would be the case for a device constituent part as incorporated into a combination product.

A facility that manufactures only device components, including device components used in a combination product, is not made subject to the device QS regulation by part 4. Similarly, a facility that manufactures solely active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) is not subject to part 211 (though it is subject to statutory CGMP requirements under Section 501 of the FD&C Act). Part 4 does not make such a drug component manufacturing facility subject to part 211 if those components are for use in a combination product.

However, a facility that manufactures a drug-device combination product formed from components is subject to parts 211 and 820, and must comply with these two sets of regulations as provided in part 4.

Distinguishing the terms: a three-tier structure

The most important regulatory conclusions

A facility that manufactures only device components is not made subject to Part 820 by Part 4.

A facility that manufactures only API is not made subject to Part 211 by Part 4.

The threshold that triggers the full dual obligation is: are you producing a "complete combination product assembled from components"? If so, both Parts 211 and 820 must be met.

Analogy: car factory vs. parts factory

A factory that makes car engine parts does not need to comply with safety regulations for complete vehicles; only the factory that assembles the final vehicle must be fully compliant.

"Component manufacturer" is not the same as "constituent part manufacturer": the former is responsible only for downstream raw materials, the latter for the entire finished drug or device.

Common points of confusion for CDMOs

Clients often ask: "We only make the syringe piston for you. Is that a constituent part or a component?"

Answer: a syringe piston is a device component, not a finished device. If you only make the piston and do not assemble a finished device, your facility is not subject to Part 820 (but it is still subject to other applicable requirements, such as supplier qualification requirements).

C3

III.C.3 Drug Containers & Closures vs. Delivery Devices

The critical hold-vs-deliver distinction — prefilled syringes are always combination products

The Hold vs. Deliver Distinction

The Agency draws a distinction between mere drug containers and closures and containers and closures that are also devices. The essential difference is generally whether the article is designed to deliver the drug it contains or merely to hold it.

If the article merely holds the drug, it is only subject to drug CGMPs as a container or closure. An article that holds or contains a drug, but also delivers it, may also be a device subject to the device QS regulation in addition to the requirements relating to drug containers and closures.

A container closure system is the sum of packaging components that together contain and protect the drug product. Elements of container closure systems that are device constituent parts include piston syringes, metered dose inhalers (MDIs), and containers for intravenously-administered (IV) fluids (such as IV bags containing saline or anti-coagulants), which both hold and deliver the drugs or biological products they contain. In the case of an IV container, the drug is delivered to the patient via the IV line.

The Agency has exempted some devices from all or certain provisions of the device QS regulations. For example, 21 CFR 880.6430 exempts liquid medication dispensers (cups, droppers, etc.) from all provisions of the device QS regulation with the exception of 820.180 and 820.198. Such exemptions may extend to device constituent parts of combination products.

If the exemptions for a device constituent part cover all of the part 820 provisions included in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1), then the Agency will consider the combination product manufacturer CGMP compliant so long as the CGMP operating system is compliant with part 211 (no demonstration of compliance with part 820 will be necessary).

However, if a device that would ordinarily be exempt from all or certain provisions in part 820 is incorporated into a container closure system (e.g., if a dropper is incorporated into the cap of a bottle), this may be a new use of the device such that the exemptions from part 820 may not be applicable.

Manufacturers should document their evaluation if they believe a container closure system that has delivery attributes is not a device, or if they believe a device constituent part is exempt from any or all provisions under the device QS regulation.

Container-closure vs. delivery device

A prefilled syringe (PFS) is always a combination product

A PFS both "holds" the drug (container/closure) and "delivers" it (delivery device), so it is always a drug-device combination product and must meet the relevant requirements of both Parts 210/211 and Part 820.

This matters greatly for aseptic fill-finish CDMOs: if your client has you fill drug into a PFS, you are producing a combination product and must have a complete dual-track CGMP system.

Analogy: drinking glass vs. water dispenser

A glass only "holds" the water until you drink it yourself. A water dispenser "actively delivers" water to you. The former is a container; the latter is a device.

A PFS is like "a glass with a built-in pushing mechanism": it not only holds the drug but also actively assists the injection, and therefore has a device function.

Two conditions for the device exemption (critical)

Even if a device is exempt from Part 820 under its product classification, integrating it into a container-closure system (for example, building a dropper into a bottle cap) may constitute a "new intended use" and void the exemption.

Any claim of exemption must be supported by a documented assessment. When in doubt, FDA recommends consulting the lead center or the OCP.

Practical exercise

Your CDMO client asks you to fill a biologic into a specially designed "smart injection device" (containing an electronic dosing module).

  • Is this a combination product?
  • Which CGMP requirements must be met?
  • Do design controls (820.30) apply?

III.C.4 Convenience Kits

Narrow definition — any modification removes "convenience kit" status

Definition and Conditions

A kit that includes two or more different types of medical products (e.g., a device and a drug) is a co-packaged combination product and, therefore, part 4 applies to the manufacture of the kit. The CGMP requirements applicable to a kit manufacturer depend on what products are included in the kit.

If the kit includes only products that are 1) legally marketed independently and 2) packaged in the kit as they are when independently marketed (including any labeling used for independent marketing), it is a "convenience kit," and the only manufacturing steps for the combination product would be the assembly, packaging, labeling, any sterilization, and further processing of the kit itself. Accordingly, the kit manufacturer would only have to demonstrate compliance with CGMP requirements with respect to those manufacturing activities.

If a kit includes any products that are repackaged, relabeled, or otherwise modified from the independently marketed product, then the kit is not a convenience kit. Likewise, if the kit manufacturer includes labeling for the kit that modifies the intended use for a constituent part, then the kit is not a convenience kit.

Co-packaged combination product manufacturers, including manufacturers of convenience kits, should carefully consider the impact of any sterilization process on the items in the co-package. Some sterilization methods suitable for devices, such as irradiation, are not suitable for many drugs. Additional verification to confirm no degradation in safety and effectiveness after any re-sterilization may be necessary. Manufacturers should consider whether re-sterilization affects stability, material properties, and expiration dating/shelf-life.

Manufacturers should document their evaluation if they believe a kit qualifies as a convenience kit.

Strict conditions for convenience kits

The two necessary and sufficient conditions for a convenience kit

  1. Independently marketed legally: each product in the kit must already be a legally marketed product sold independently on the market.
  2. Packaged as is: each product must be exactly the same as when marketed independently (including labeling), with no repackaging, relabeling or modification of any kind.

As long as both conditions are met, the kit manufacturer is responsible for CGMP only for "the kitting activities themselves, such as assembly, packaging, labeling and sterilization", and does not assume the full CGMP obligations for each constituent part.

Situations that lose convenience kit status

  • Any constituent is repackaged
  • Any constituent is relabeled
  • Any constituent is modified in any other way
  • The kit labeling changes the intended use of any constituent
  • The kit introduces a new sterilization process that changes the conditions of use of the product

Once convenience kit status is lost, the manufacturer takes on the full CGMP responsibility for all constituents.

Analogy: supermarket gift basket vs. custom gift set

A supermarket puts three bottles of already-marketed soy sauce into a basket as a gift (as is). This is a convenience kit: you only need to make sure the basket is of good quality and do not need to revalidate each bottle of soy sauce.

But if you repackage the soy sauce into small bottles, or print custom labels, it is no longer a convenience kit: you are now responsible for everything about that soy sauce.

Sterilization compatibility considerations (essential for kit manufacturers)

Even for a convenience kit, if the whole kit is sterilized (for example by ethylene oxide or radiation), you must evaluate:

  • Some devices can withstand radiation sterilization, but many drugs cannot
  • The effect of re-sterilization on stability, expiration dating and material properties
  • Cross-effects of the sterilization process on the constituents (for example, volatiles from packaging materials affecting the drug)
  • Sterilization validation must cover every constituent in the kit

CDMO application scenario

A client asks you to do the final packaging and sterilization of its hospital surgical kit. The kit contains one bottle of an already-marketed antiseptic solution and one already-marketed scalpel tray.

Assessment: if both remain in their original packaging with unchanged labeling, you can claim convenience kit status, but you must still confirm that: (1) the sterilization process is safe for both; (2) the sterilization validation covers the whole kit; (3) the entire assessment is documented.

D

III.D What CGMP Requirements Apply?

Product-specific applicability, "where appropriate" language, multi-facility coordination

D.1 Applicability of CGMP Requirements to a Product

The preamble to the proposed rule noted that not all CGMP provisions apply to all combination products. For example, only an over-the-counter (OTC) combination product must comply with the tamper-evident packaging requirements in the drug CGMPs and only combination products that include a type of device constituent part that is installed or serviced must comply with installation and servicing requirements in the device QS regulation.

Many of the requirements for biological products are applicable only to certain types of biological products. For example, blood and blood components are subject to the CGMP requirements for such products under part 606. In addition, a vaccine manufactured using a spore-forming microorganism would be subject to 21 CFR 600.11(e)(3).

Similarly, not all CGMP requirements may apply at a facility that performs only certain aspects of the manufacture of a combination product. As 21 CFR 210.2(b) and 820.1(a)(1) describe, an entity that engages in only some operations subject to the regulations in parts 210, 211, 600 through 680, 820, and 1271, need only comply with the regulations applicable to those operations. For example, a facility that manufactures a co-packaged combination product that includes a finished, already-packaged drug manufactured under the drug CGMPs and received from another facility need not calculate yield for that drug constituent part.

Determining applicability by product and facility activity

Two dimensions of "applicability"

Product-level: some CGMP provisions apply only to specific types of product. For example:

  • Tamper-evident packaging requirements: apply only to OTC combination products
  • Installation and servicing requirements (820.170/820.200): apply only to devices that require installation or servicing
  • Blood CGMP (Part 606): applies only to blood products

Facility-level: a facility that performs only some manufacturing activities need only meet the CGMP provisions relevant to those activities.

Practical inferences

If your CDMO facility receives "finished and packaged" drug constituents from the client and is only responsible for assembling them into a kit:

  • You do not need to recalculate the yield for that drug
  • You need only meet the CGMP relevant to the activities you actually perform, such as assembly, packaging and labeling

"I comply with the CGMP for what I do": the scope of activity determines the scope of compliance.

D.2 The Meaning of "Where Appropriate"

Firms must demonstrate compliance with all regulations applicable to their product and facility under part 4. However, the drug CGMPs, device QS regulation, and CGTPs for HCT/Ps use language such as "where appropriate" or "as necessary" to acknowledge that certain measures may not be necessary or applicable under certain circumstances and that manufacturers must pursue such measures if they are needed.

Such language indicates that firms have the opportunity to document justifications for determining that such a measure or approach is not appropriate or necessary for a particular product or the specific manufacturing activity they are undertaking. Such justifications should generally be maintained within the quality system documentation and may warrant Agency review prior to implementation depending on the product type and relevant premarket submission obligations.

Under both 21 CFR 820.1(a)(3) and 1271.150(e), a requirement that is qualified by "where appropriate," is "appropriate" if non-implementation could reasonably be expected to result in the product not meeting its specified requirements. Under both provisions, a requirement is deemed to be appropriate unless the manufacturer can document justification that it is not.

For example, 21 CFR 820.30(i) stipulates procedures for "the identification, documentation, validation or where appropriate verification, review, and approval of design changes before their implementation." Changes to design, such as changes to properties of the material or dimensional specifications, may not need to be validated if they can be verified through appropriate measurement and test methods. However, changes that could impact user needs, such as changes to the user interface, may require validation.

Correct interpretation of "where appropriate"

Default position: applicable unless you can justify otherwise

"Where appropriate" is not a free pass. FDA's interpretation is:

  • Any provision containing the words "where appropriate" is applicable by default
  • A manufacturer may choose not to implement it, but must have a documented justification
  • The justification must explain "why not implementing it does not affect the product's ability to meet its specified requirements"

In other words: you may leave it out, but you must be able to explain why leaving it out causes no problem.

Analogy: "wear a hard hat where necessary"

A construction site rule says "wear a hard hat where necessary". If you do not wear one, you must be able to explain that "there is no falling-object risk in this area, so it is not necessary", and cannot simply say "the rule says 'where necessary', so I felt it was not necessary".

"Where appropriate" in CGMP works the same way: the burden of proof is on you, not on FDA.

Design change: verification vs. validation (example under 820.30(i))

A change to the dimensional tolerance in a material specification: can usually be handled through measurement-based verification without full validation.

A change to the operating workflow of the user interface: affects user needs and usually requires user studies or usability validation.

Key question: does the change affect user needs or safety and effectiveness? If yes, validation is needed; if not, verification may be used instead, but the rationale must still be documented.

D.3 CGMP Responsibilities Across Facilities

A facility that manufacturers a constituent part of a combination product or a complete combination product must be compliant with the CGMP requirements applicable to each manufacturing process that occurs at that specific facility. In addition, the combination product owner (the holder of the marketing authorization for the product) retains overall responsibility for the product, even if the owner is not directly engaged in its manufacture.

A facility that manufactures only a finished device intended to be a constituent part must comply only with the device QS regulation. Similarly, a facility that manufactures only a drug constituent part must comply only with the drug CGMPs. Even if a facility is manufacturing only one type of constituent part, the CGMP operating system should take into account considerations for the combination product as a whole as appropriate.

Before changes are made to the manufacturing process of a constituent part, the CGMP operating system should ensure consideration of whether such changes could affect performance and/or interaction with the other constituent part(s) and, if so, whether the safety and effectiveness of the combination product could be impacted. Quality agreements with constituent part manufacturer(s) are one way to ensure that changes to a constituent part are transparent to a combination product manufacturer or owner.

Some CGMP requirements concern the product as a whole, such as design controls, and some concern overarching considerations for the manufacturing process as a whole, such as CAPA requirements. Take, for example, a manufacturing facility collecting nonconformance data that are trended as an input to the CAPA system for a combination product. If a problem is detected that requires a product or process design change, the change may require modification of manufacturing activities at another facility or the expertise to develop and implement the change may reside at a different facility.

A CAPA system that is shared between facilities, or facility-specific CAPA systems with established links between them, may facilitate handling of issues requiring multi-facility collaboration. Regardless of the approach, the CAPA system(s) should allow for adequate flow of information between facilities and manufacturers for the combination product and appropriate investigation and resolution of identified CAPA issues.

Manufacturing activities that occur at multiple facilities and associated CGMP operating systems should be coordinated appropriately. Each manufacturing facility for a combination product should have documentation specifying its respective responsibilities, and the manufacturer of the finished combination product should have access to this documentation.

Measures that might be taken to ensure CGMP compliance at all manufacturing facilities include auditing and other oversight activities. When multiple facilities participate in the manufacturing process, the owner may facilitate CGMP compliance by coordinating interactions among the facilities, including by entering into comprehensive quality agreements with the various facilities and suppliers.

Multi-facility manufacturing: allocating responsibility and coordination

The owner's "overall responsibility" does not disappear through outsourcing

Even if the marketing authorization holder outsources all manufacturing, it bears ultimate responsibility for CGMP compliance of the entire combination product. Outsourcing does not transfer responsibility.

The Quality Agreement is the key tool for managing this responsibility. It should state clearly who is responsible for which manufacturing activities, who maintains which documents, how changes are reported, and so on.

CAPA information flow across facilities

If nonconformance data at Facility A shows that a design change is needed, and design controls are the responsibility of Facility B, the CAPA system must allow this information to flow from A to B and ensure that B can start the appropriate design change process.

This can be: (1) a shared CAPA system; or (2) independent CAPA systems at each facility plus an established linking mechanism (such as regular joint reviews or electronic data interfaces).

What FDA requires is an "adequate flow of information between facilities"; it does not require any particular system architecture.

CDMO practice: core content of the Quality Agreement

When a CDMO takes on any manufacturing activity for a combination product, the Quality Agreement should cover:

  • A list of the specific manufacturing activities performed by each party and the allocation of CGMP responsibilities
  • Access to, and custody responsibility for, design control documents
  • Notification mechanism for supplier/raw material changes
  • Cross-facility handling process for nonconformances, complaints and CAPA
  • Audit arrangements (including allowing the client/owner to audit the CDMO)
  • Document access arrangements for contract testing facilities
E

III.E Control of Changes to a Combination Product

Cross-constituent impact assessment — a drug change may trigger a device change

Coordinating Changes Across Manufacturers

While not an issue unique to combination products, coordination of changes among manufacturers participating in the manufacture of a combination product is an important CGMP issue. Appropriate consideration should be given to any implications for the safety or effectiveness of the combination product that might arise from changes to the combination product or its constituent parts.

Single-entity and co-packaged combination product manufacturers must establish arrangements with their suppliers, contractors, and consultants to receive notice of changes in the product or service, where possible, in accordance with 21 CFR 820.50. This notice should address changes the supplier makes to its process or product—including to an API, other drug components, or a container-closure system—that it manufactures, if the change might affect the downstream manufacturing process or the quality of the combination product itself.

These combination product manufacturers should also establish procedures for acceptance of components, containers/closures, and constituent parts to ensure both detection and evaluation of any changes that are critical to the safety or effectiveness of the combination product prior to incorporating them into the finished combination product.

Similarly, if one entity manufactures one constituent part of a cross-labeled combination product and another entity manufactures the other constituent part, both should have procedures in place to inform one another of changes that may affect the safety or effectiveness of the combination product, and to confirm that the specifications for the respective constituent parts remain appropriate or are updated as needed.

For example, a change to the drug constituent part of a cross-labeled combination product might require a design change to the device constituent part for the combination product to remain safe and effective. Accordingly, awareness and assessment of drug changes to determine whether they require a change to the device constituent part(s) may be important to compliance with design control requirements for the device under 21 CFR 820.30.

Similarly, a change to a device constituent part of a cross-labeled combination product may necessitate a change to a drug constituent part, which may require a change in its chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC).

Cross-constituent-part change control: a key risk for CDMOs

Core logic of change control for combination products

What is special about combination products is that a change to one constituent part may affect the safety and effectiveness of another constituent part.

This means change assessment must be "cross-constituent-part" and not only "within this constituent part". It is not enough that your own quality system has passed the change assessment; you must confirm that the other constituent part is also unaffected.

Analogy: interlocking gears

The drug constituent part and the device constituent part are like two meshing gears. If you change the size or pitch of one gear, the other must be adjusted accordingly, or the whole system will jam or spin freely.

Change control for a combination product ensures that the "meshing gear system" can still operate safely as a whole after either side changes.

Supplier change notification mechanism (21 CFR 820.50)

The manufacturer must establish "change notification" arrangements with suppliers, contractors and consultants. The key points are:

  • When a supplier changes its API formulation, excipients or closure system materials, it must notify in advance
  • Incoming inspection procedures must be able to detect and evaluate these changes
  • Before incorporation into the finished product, the effect of the change on the safety and effectiveness of the combination product must be evaluated

Scenario drill

Your CDMO manufactures the device constituent part (an autoinjector) of a cross-labeled combination product. The client (the drug holder) notifies you that their drug manufacturer will raise the viscosity of the drug solution from 2 cP to 8 cP to improve stability.

  • What potential effects does this drug-side change have on your autoinjector design?
  • What design control assessment should you trigger? (See 820.30(i).)
  • How can you build such a notification and assessment mechanism into the Quality Agreement in advance?

CDMO compliance recommendation: SOP for cross-constituent-part change management

A CDMO should establish a standard operating procedure for "cross-constituent-part change impact assessment" in its quality system, covering at least:

  • The process for receiving and recording change notifications from clients or suppliers
  • A decision tree for impact assessment (impact on safety, effectiveness and regulatory compliance)
  • Definition of thresholds for escalation (for example, changes that may require a regulatory submission)
  • A confirmation process with the client to verify that the design specifications remain appropriate
  • Trigger conditions for assessing the effect of the change on expiration dating/stability

Section III Review: Key Takeaways for CDMO Practitioners

Core regulatory principles

  • Demonstrate: the same standard as the underlying CGMP regulations; under the streamlined approaches only specific provisions must be demonstrated, but the quality standard for "demonstrating" is not lowered.
  • Investigational-phase exemptions: drugs are exempt from Parts 210/211 in Phase 1; devices are exempt from Part 820, but design controls (820.30) are never exempt.
  • Constituent part is not a component: component manufacturers are not subject to Part 4; only manufacturers of finished constituent parts are.
  • Hold vs. Deliver: only holding the drug = container/closure only; holding and delivering = combination product, which must meet Part 820.
  • Convenience kit: strict conditions (independently marketed + packaged as is); any modification forfeits eligibility.
  • Owner's overall responsibility: the marketing authorization holder retains ultimate CGMP responsibility even when all manufacturing is outsourced.
  • Cross-constituent-part change assessment: a change to one constituent part must be assessed for its effect on the other constituent parts; the Quality Agreement is the management mechanism.

List of highest CDMO risks

Five major CDMO risk points

  1. Design services are manufacture: if you provide design consulting, you are a manufacturer under Part 4 even if you produce nothing.
  2. A PFS is always a combination product: taking on PFS filling requires a dual-track CGMP system (drug + device).
  3. Misjudging convenience kit eligibility: assuming a product is still a convenience kit after repackaging or relabeling can lead to major compliance deficiencies.
  4. CAPA information silos: unlinked CAPA systems across facilities mean cross-facility problems cannot be handled effectively.
  5. Unnotified supplier changes: changes by API or closure material suppliers not promptly assessed for their effect on the device side.

Section IV.A — Overview: The Six Device QS Provisions

This section provides summary descriptions of the provisions from the device QS regulation with which manufacturers of single-entity and co-packaged combination products that include a device constituent part must demonstrate compliance when using the drug CGMP-based streamlined approach established under 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1).

This discussion is not meant to provide a comprehensive analysis, but rather to help manufacturers — particularly drug and biological product manufacturers who may be less familiar with the device QS regulation — understand the purpose and basic elements of the device QS regulation provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1).

The six required device QS provisions under 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1) are:

  • 820.20 — Management Responsibility
  • 820.30 — Design Controls
  • 820.50 — Purchasing Controls
  • 820.100 — Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA)
  • 820.170 — Installation
  • 820.200 — Servicing
Note: This section does not provide detailed discussion of 820.170 (installation) and 820.200 (servicing) because the Agency anticipates that installed and serviced devices will rarely be constituent parts of single-entity or co-packaged combination products.

Background: Why These Six?

The Logic of the Two Pathways

21 CFR Part 4 offers two CGMP compliance pathways for combination products:

  • Drug CGMP-based approach (4.4(b)(1)): built on 21 CFR Parts 210/211, with these six device QS provisions added. This is the natural pathway for drug manufacturers and CDMOs.
  • Device QS-based approach (4.4(b)(2)): built on 21 CFR Part 820, with specific drug CGMP requirements added. This is the pathway for device manufacturers.

This section covers the former, the route most drug CDMOs take.

Analogy: Native Language Plus Electives

Imagine your company is already fluent in drug CGMP, your "native language". When you take on a combination product, you do not have to switch to speaking the "foreign language" of device QS, but you do need to learn six key "device words": 820.20, 820.30, 820.50, 820.100, 820.170 and 820.200.

FDA chose these six because they fill systemic gaps in drug CGMP in areas such as design controls, the supply chain, closed-loop CAPA and management commitment.

Practical Points for CDMOs

For a drug CDMO focused on sterile filling, the practical impact of these six, from highest to lowest, is as follows:

  1. 820.30 Design Controls: a completely new concept with the greatest impact
  2. 820.100 CAPA: the deviation system must be upgraded to a closed-loop structure
  3. 820.20 Management Responsibility: management review must be formalized
  4. 820.50 Purchasing Controls: the scope of supplier qualification must be expanded
  5. 820.170 / 820.200: not applicable to most sterile filling CDMOs

1. Management Responsibility — 21 CFR 820.20

Requirements under 21 CFR 820.20 ensure that management with executive responsibility (i.e., senior employees of a manufacturer who have the authority to establish or make changes to the quality policy and quality system) are actively engaged in oversight of the quality system, and reflect the expectation that management with executive responsibility demonstrate an active, ongoing commitment to quality system development and implementation.

Statutory CGMP provisions and regulations for drugs establish requirements related to management responsibility, and the Agency has also issued guidance on this topic. However, there are specific requirements in 21 CFR 820.20 that are not explicitly addressed in drug CGMP requirements, and a manufacturer of a combination product that includes a device constituent part must ensure that the elements required under 21 CFR 820.20 are satisfied.

A combination product manufacturer must establish and maintain an adequate organizational structure to ensure that the product is designed and produced in accordance with CGMP requirements. This structure must:

  • Establish the appropriate responsibility, authority, and interrelation of all personnel who manage, perform, and assess work affecting quality;
  • Provide adequate resources for management, performance of work, and assessment activities; and
  • Include an appointed management member who is responsible for ensuring that quality system requirements are effectively established and maintained, and for reporting on the performance of the quality system to management with executive responsibility.

Management with executive responsibility must establish the quality policy and objectives for and commitment to quality. These policies and objectives are reflected in the quality plan and quality system procedures.

The quality plan must define the quality practices, resources, and activities relevant to the combination product that is being designed and manufactured and must establish how the quality requirements will be met. The plan can either be an independent document, or it can reference elements of the manufacturer's quality system.

The quality system procedures (21 CFR 820.20(e)) ensure compliance with each aspect of the CGMPs applicable to the combination product. Drug manufacturers may already have some such procedures in place as part of the requirements for a quality control unit under 21 CFR 211.22 and can reference these procedures and augment them as needed to meet the requirements of 21 CFR 820.20.

Management with executive responsibility must periodically conduct management review of the suitability and effectiveness of the quality system, including to ensure that the quality system satisfies the established quality policy and objectives. These reviews must be conducted at defined intervals and with sufficient frequency. Management review procedures should ensure that management has adequate access to relevant information from facilities contracted to perform manufacturing activities for the combination product.

820.20 Management Responsibility: Interpretation and Application

Core Concept: Management Responsibility Made a Legal Requirement

Drug CGMP (21 CFR 211.22) requires a quality control unit, but is rather vague about the "specific obligations of senior management". 820.20 is explicit:

  • Quality Policy: established by top management personally; it cannot be delegated
  • Management Representative: a member of management appointed to be responsible for the QMS and to report its performance to top management
  • Management Review: a formal, documented review conducted at defined intervals; it is a legal obligation, not an option
  • Quality Plan: planning of the resources and activities for a specific combination product

Analogy: From "Tacit Understanding" to "Contractual Management"

In a pure drug plant, management commitment to quality is often cultural: everyone "knows" that senior management values quality, but it may not be written down. 820.20 requires this commitment to be written as a formal legal obligation: management reviews must have a date, an agenda, conclusions, and a record of follow-up actions.

It is like corporate governance moving from "family understanding" to formal "board meeting procedures".

Gap Analysis for Drug Manufacturers

A drug manufacturer's existing quality control unit (21 CFR 211.22) typically already has: a quality policy, an SOP system, and quality review mechanisms.

Typical gaps:

  • Management review is not formalized (no fixed frequency, no meeting minutes)
  • No clearly designated "Management Representative" role and responsibilities
  • No Quality Plan prepared for the combination product

Recommended approach: add a combination product-specific Quality Plan appendix to the existing QMS, and establish a quarterly management review.

CDMO Practical Scenario

Suppose your sterile filling CDMO accepts a project for a PFS (prefilled syringe) combination product. 820.20 requires you to:

  • Prepare a combination product-specific Quality Plan for this product
  • Appoint a Management Representative (this can be an existing QA Director) and formally document their responsibilities
  • Include this combination product's quality indicators (such as OOS rate, CAPA completion rate and design change records) in every management review
  • Ensure that quality information from contract manufacturing facilities is available to your management review

Thinking Exercise

Your CDMO currently holds one management review a year, and the minutes retain only the agenda. To meet 820.20(c), what do you need to add to the management review? Is the review frequency sufficient?

2. Design Controls — 21 CFR 820.30

Design control activities confirm that there are no negative interactions between constituent parts, and ensure that their combined use results in a combination product that is safe and effective and performs as expected.

Design control requirements apply to activities during product development as well as to postmarket changes to the design or manufacturing process.

The extent and complexity of the design controls process and associated documentation will vary based on the product. For example, consider a drug product that is already legally marketed independently that will have the same formulation, route of administration and intended use when marketed as part of a combination product that also incorporates a delivery device. Design controls would begin when the delivery device configuration is judged to be feasible and appropriate to develop.

While pharmaceutical development focuses on drug considerations, if broadened to take into account the other constituent parts of combination products and how they interrelate, many pharmaceutical development practices (for example, Quality by Design principles) can be leveraged and built upon when demonstrating compliance with design controls for a combination product.

Design and Development Plan

As part of the design controls, a design and development plan must be established to describe or reference the design and development activities and define responsibility for implementation. The plans must identify and describe the interfaces with different groups or activities that provide, or result in, input to the design and development process.

Design Input

Design input requirements for the combination product should include considerations such as performance characteristics, safety and reliability requirements, and expected needs of product users and patients. Design inputs should be considered early in product development to ensure that development efforts are consistent with the product's intended use, including the needs of users, including patients.

The Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP) and Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) in pharmaceutical development may be applied for combination products in a manner consistent with design input and output requirements.

Design Output

Once the design inputs have been established, the design outputs (e.g., specifications and engineering drawings) must be developed based on those inputs.

Design Verification and Validation

Verification means confirmation by examination and provision of objective evidence that specified requirements have been fulfilled. Design verification confirms that the product developed is consistent with the assumptions made by the design team when developing design inputs.

Design validation means establishing by objective evidence that product specifications conform to user needs and intended use(s). Design validation includes testing of production units or their equivalents under actual or simulated use conditions.

Some design verification (e.g., bench and/or pre-clinical testing) and validation (e.g., human factors testing, when appropriate) will typically be completed prior to the initiation of clinical studies of safety and efficacy for the combination product.

Design Review

Design review is required to ensure that formal and documented reviews of the design are planned and conducted at appropriate stages of the product's design development, and all of these activities must be documented in the design history file.

Risk Analysis

Manufacturers must perform risk analysis where appropriate, which should begin early in the design process and continue throughout the lifecycle for the product. Risk analysis should enable identification of unacceptable risks so that they can be mitigated.

Design Transfer

Manufacturers must establish and maintain design transfer procedures to ensure effective translation of design specifications into production methods and procedures. Design transfer is the bridge between the design of the product and the manufacturing process to make the commercial product.

Design Changes

Manufacturers are required to have procedures to ensure that any changes to design requirements are identified, documented, validated or verified where appropriate, reviewed, and approved prior to implementation. Change controls apply once initial design inputs have been approved by the manufacturer.

Design History File (DHF)

The Design History File (DHF) for a combination product captures all design issues relating to the combined use of the constituent parts. The DHF may not need to document design and development planning for established characteristics of the individual constituent parts if that constituent part was previously approved for the same indication.

If a finished device, drug, or biological product is purchased, the combination product manufacturer is not required to retrospectively "design" that constituent part with respect to such previously reviewed characteristics. Rather, the combination product manufacturer should understand the constituent part's existing design specifications thoroughly in order to perform design controls properly for its use in the combination product.

820.30 Design Controls: The Requirement Least Familiar to Drug Manufacturers

Why Is This the Biggest Impact?

A pure drug plant has never needed "design controls", because drug formulation development follows the ICH Q8 pharmaceutical development framework, which focuses on chemical stability and process engineering rather than "hardware design".

But when you manufacture a combination product with a device constituent part (such as a PFS, autoinjector or inhaler), that device part has a physical design, and FDA requires you to run the full design control cycle for it.

Analogy: Architect vs. Chef

Drug development is like a chef: you try out recipes, find the right proportions, then write the recipe down (the batch record). Your focus is whether "the finished dish tastes good" (efficacy, safety).

Device design control is like an architect: you first define user needs (design input), draw the blueprint (design output), confirm the blueprint meets the needs (verification), confirm after construction that people can really live in the building (validation), and file the entire design evolution (DHF).

A combination product requires you to play both chef and architect at the same time.

Key Timing: Start Before Phase 2

FDA guidance is explicit: design verification and some validation activities (such as human factors testing) should be completed before clinical trials begin.

In practice: the design control documentation system must be established in early development, not added just before marketing. A common mistake among drug manufacturers is treating design controls as "pre-launch administrative tidying", whereas FDA expects records from the feasibility assessment onward.

DHF vs. DMR vs. DHR: Three Acronyms Compared

  • DHF (Design History File): all the documents recording "how this product was designed"
  • DMR (Device Master Record): all the current specification documents recording "how this product is manufactured"
  • DHR (Device History Record): the production records showing "how each batch of product was manufactured"

Drug-world equivalents: DHF ≈ development report; DMR ≈ Master Batch Record; DHR ≈ Batch Record.

CDMO Practical Scenario: Upgrading an Existing Drug to a Combination Product

Your client originally contracted you to fill a marketed monoclonal antibody (mAb), and now wants to market it as a PFS autoinjector combination.

The starting point of design controls is the moment you begin "assessing the feasibility of the PFS device", not when the BLA supplement is filed. You need to:

  • Use the existing drug specifications (formulation, dose, route of administration) as the basis of the design inputs
  • Assess the impact of PFS materials (silicone lubricant, rubber plunger) on drug stability; this is a new design input for the combination product
  • Establish a DHF that holds all compatibility testing, human factors studies and clinical bridging data
  • Even if the drug itself does not need redesign, design controls for the device-drug interface are still required

How Do QTPP / CQA Map to Design Controls?

FDA guidance recognizes this bridge: the QTPP (quality target product profile) and CQA (critical quality attribute) concepts familiar to drug manufacturers can serve as the drug-side basis for combination product design inputs.

For example, the CQA "injection dose accuracy ±5%" can be translated directly into a design input specification for the PFS device. This lets drug manufacturers enter the design control framework in familiar language.

3. Purchasing Controls — 21 CFR 820.50

Manufacturers of single-entity and co-packaged combination products that include a device constituent part must control purchased products and services as described in 21 CFR 820.50. They must establish such purchasing controls for products received at their facility for use in the manufacture of the combination product, for all suppliers of these products, and for suppliers of services obtained (such as terminal sterilization conducted by an outside entity).

Facilities that have previously manufactured only drugs, rather than devices or combination products, will likely have relevant procedures in place in accordance with part 211, subpart E. However, if these procedures do not demonstrate compliance with the specific requirements of 21 CFR 820.50, they must be augmented to do so.

Manufacturers must evaluate potential suppliers and define the type and extent of control to be exercised over them based on the evaluation results. Manufacturers may design and conduct such evaluations based on factors such as the risks associated with the supplied product or service and complexity of the specifications for it.

Manufacturers must establish and maintain records of acceptable suppliers for purchased products and services, and establish and maintain data that clearly describe or reference the specified requirements for products and services received (e.g., contracts with relevant terms).

One way to facilitate purchasing control is the careful structuring of purchasing agreements with suppliers. Where possible, such agreements are used to ensure that the combination product manufacturer is notified of changes to the products, the manufacturing process, or the services being provided. Such notice of changes facilitates compliance not only with purchasing control duties, but also, potentially, with other regulatory requirements, including design control obligations to complete additional design verification testing to address the change.

If it is not possible to obtain such notice, the combination product manufacturer should implement additional controls to ensure that changes are identified and appropriate measures are taken.

Note: Regardless of the nature of purchasing controls, combination product manufacturers must comply with the testing requirements under 21 CFR 211.84 with respect to drug components, and product containers and closures.

820.50 Purchasing Controls: The Device Dimension Beyond 211.84

820.50 vs. 211.84: Two Complementary Systems

Under drug CGMP, 21 CFR 211.84 already requires sampling, testing and approval of drug components (raw materials, containers, closures). So why is 820.50 still needed?

  • 211.84: focuses on incoming inspection of the drug side (chemical and microbiological testing)
  • 820.50: focuses on risk assessment and control across the whole supply chain, including qualification and ongoing monitoring of device component suppliers

Key point: even if you already have 211.84 incoming inspection procedures, supplier controls for device constituent parts must still be reinforced under the 820.50 framework.

Three Levels of 820.50 Supplier Control

  • Level 1: Supplier evaluation
    The depth of evaluation is set on a risk basis (criticality of the supplied item, complexity of the specification): document audit → questionnaire → on-site audit
  • Level 2: Approved Supplier List (ASL)
    Establish and maintain a formal Approved Supplier List that records the evaluation conclusions and approval conditions
  • Level 3: Purchasing agreement terms
    The agreement must require the supplier to notify you proactively of material or process changes (Change Notification Clause)

Change Notification Clauses: Why Are They So Important?

The challenge for combination products is that device component suppliers may change their own materials (such as the wall thickness of a PFS glass barrel or the formulation of a rubber plunger), which can directly affect drug stability, yet they may not tell you proactively.

FDA expressly recommends including a supplier change notification obligation in purchasing agreements: when the supplier's process or materials change, they must notify you in advance so that you can perform supplementary design verification testing.

This clause serves both 820.50 (purchasing controls) and 820.30 (design controls), and is a core tool of combination product supply chain management.

Analogy: Car Parts vs. Drug Vials

Buying ordinary drug raw materials is like buying groceries: you only need to confirm that the quality specifications are met. Buying device components for a PFS autoinjector is like buying car parts: you must confirm not only the part specifications but also the supplier's process stability, quality control capability and change management procedures, because a process change in a part can affect the safety of the whole system.

CDMO Practical Scenario

Your CDMO is filling a PFS combination product. The supply chain involves:

  • Glass barrel supplier (device component)
  • Rubber plunger supplier (device component, in direct contact with the drug)
  • Outsourced sterilization service (ETO or gamma sterilization facility)

820.50 requires formal qualification of all three types of supplier, with change notification clauses in the purchasing agreements. Even if these suppliers are already covered by your 211.84 procedures, the device-side risk assessment must still be completed.

4. Corrective and Preventive Action — 21 CFR 820.100

Manufacturers of co-packaged or single-entity combination products that have a device constituent part must establish and maintain procedures for implementing CAPA in accordance with 21 CFR 820.100. Relevant requirements in the drug CGMPs include 21 CFR 211.192 and 21 CFR 211.180(e).

Manufacturers of the combination product must undertake CAPA measures, when required, for issues arising at their facilities. All relevant manufacturers should participate in cross-facility efforts, as appropriate, to determine the root-cause of problems and the appropriate measures to correct such problems and prevent recurrence. Manufacturers of combination products should document these activities.

The CAPA process for combination products also should consider implications of corrective and preventive actions for all constituent parts and for the combination product as a whole.

While combination product manufacturers have flexibility in coordinating CAPA systems across facilities, they maintain responsibility for ensuring that the 21 CFR 820.100 requirements are met. Manufacturers should ensure that an appropriately comprehensive review of activities is undertaken at relevant facilities to determine the cause of existing or potential problems, which could include manufacturing problems, deviations, or nonconformities for a constituent part or the combination product as a whole.

The manufacturer should have appropriate mechanisms in place to ensure that:

  • Issues are identified;
  • Action(s) needed to correct and prevent recurrence are taken; and
  • Necessary changes are implemented.

The manufacturer should take appropriate measures, which may include CAPAs, with regard to all relevant manufacturing steps at all relevant facilities to correct problems and to prevent or mitigate them going forward.

820.100 CAPA: The Core of Closed-Loop Quality Management

What Is "Closed-Loop CAPA"?

Drug CGMP (21 CFR 211.192) requires deviation investigations, but the emphasis is on finding the cause. 820.100 goes further and requires a complete closed-loop system:

  1. Identify: systematically collect quality data and identify problems or potential problems
  2. Investigate: determine the root cause (Root Cause Analysis)
  3. Corrective Action: eliminate the cause of a problem that has occurred
  4. Preventive Action: eliminate the cause of a potential problem, preventing first occurrence
  5. Verify Effectiveness: confirm that the CAPA actually solved the problem
  6. Disseminate Information: feed the CAPA results back to management and relevant departments

The Most Common FDA 483 Observations

For combination product manufacturers, CAPA-related FDA 483 observations most often concern:

  • CAPA procedures lacking an effectiveness check step
  • Preventive actions (PA) that are merely formal and disconnected from the root cause
  • CAPA information from multiple sites/contract manufacturers not effectively integrated
  • CAPA information not regularly fed back into management review (820.20)

Analogy: Hospital Infection Control vs. Fire Prevention

A drug deviation investigation is like "hospital infection control": a patient is infected, so you find the cause and treat it. 820.100 CAPA adds the "fire prevention" function: not only treating the infection, but finding all the potential risks in the hospital that could cause infection and correcting them before they occur.

This is the essential difference between "corrective action" and "preventive action".

CDMO Practical Scenario: Cross-Site CAPA Coordination

Your CDMO does the filling, the device components come from another supplier, and final assembly is done by a third contract manufacturer. When a leakage problem with the autoinjector is found on the market, 820.100 requires that:

  • You (the CDMO) remain responsible for the overall execution and effectiveness of the CAPA
  • You must coordinate all three facilities in a joint root cause investigation
  • CAPA measures must cover all relevant manufacturing steps
  • A mechanism must be established to ensure that the contract manufacturers' CAPA information flows back into your QMS

This means your Quality Agreement must specify obligations for CAPA information sharing and coordination.

5. Installation (820.170) and Servicing (820.200)

21 CFR 4.4(b)(1)(v) and (vi) require manufacturers to demonstrate compliance with installation requirements under 21 CFR 820.170 and servicing requirements under 21 CFR 820.200, respectively. These requirements are included in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1) to ensure manufacturers comply with them when applicable to a single-entity or co-packaged combination product.

However, the Agency anticipates that installed and serviced devices will rarely be constituent parts of such combination products. If they are constituent parts of a combination product, they are more likely to be separately manufactured and marketed as constituent parts of cross-labeled combination products. In such cases, each constituent part manufacturer would be subject to the CGMP requirements associated with the constituent part it manufactures. Accordingly, the device constituent part of such a cross-labeled combination product would be subject to installation and servicing requirements.

820.170 — Installation

820.170 applies when the combination product must be installed at the end-user site and proper installation is necessary to ensure safety and effectiveness. The manufacturer must establish and maintain adequate installation instructions and, where appropriate, inspection procedures. The manufacturer or its representative must perform installation checks and document them.

820.200 — Servicing

820.200 applies when servicing (i.e., maintenance, repair, or modification at the user site) is a specified requirement for the combination product. The manufacturer must establish and maintain instructions and procedures for performing and verifying that the servicing meets specified requirements. Service reports must be documented and analyzed to determine whether they should be addressed as complaints or signals.

820.170 & 820.200: Not Applicable to Most Sterile Filling CDMOs

Key Questions for Determining Applicability

The first question to ask is: does your combination product need to be installed or serviced at the end user's site?

  • If it is a single-use PFS, autoinjector or inhaler: 820.170 and 820.200 are generally not applicable. These products are discarded after use and require no installation or servicing.
  • If it is a drug delivery system that is implanted and serviced periodically (such as an implantable insulin pump): they apply.
  • If it is a large drug delivery device installed in a hospital (such as an IV infusion pump): they apply.

How to Formally Document a "Not Applicable" Determination

Even if you determine that 820.170 and 820.200 do not apply, FDA still expects a written applicability assessment record.

Recommended approach: add a section on "CGMP Applicability Assessment" to the Quality Plan, stating for each 4.4(b)(1) provision whether it applies and why.

This lets an inspector quickly understand your reasoning, rather than questioning whether you "forgot" these two requirements.

Analogy: The Elevator Clause in Building Codes

You build a small two-storey house. The building code has elevator provisions, and you assess that no elevator is needed, but you still need to state in the building plan: "This building has fewer storeys than the threshold requiring an elevator, so this clause does not apply."

820.170 / 820.200 are handled the same way: explicitly document why they do not apply, rather than silently skipping them.

QMSR Update — 21 CFR Part 820 Revised (Effective February 2026)

Note: When this FDA guidance was issued in January 2017, the applicable regulation was 21 CFR Part 820, the Quality System Regulation (QS regulation). FDA has since finalized a major update, replacing Part 820 with the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), effective February 2, 2026.

The QMSR aligns 21 CFR Part 820 with ISO 13485:2016, the international standard for quality management systems for medical devices. This harmonization was designed to reduce the compliance burden for device manufacturers who already comply with ISO 13485.

Under the QMSR, the six provisions referenced in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1) — 820.20, 820.30, 820.50, 820.100, 820.170, and 820.200 — continue to apply as specified requirements for the drug CGMP-based streamlined approach. These provisions have corresponding sections in ISO 13485:2016:

  • 820.20 Management Responsibility → ISO 13485 Section 5
  • 820.30 Design Controls → ISO 13485 Section 7.3
  • 820.50 Purchasing Controls → ISO 13485 Section 7.4
  • 820.100 CAPA → ISO 13485 Section 8.5
  • 820.170 Installation → ISO 13485 Section 7.5.3
  • 820.200 Servicing → ISO 13485 Section 7.5.4

Manufacturers should verify they are applying the current QMSR requirements rather than the legacy 820 text, as the QMSR incorporates additional detail and harmonized terminology from ISO 13485:2016.

QMSR Update Highlights: Effective February 2026

This Is the Latest Regulatory Development in This Section

This FDA guidance was issued in 2017 and cites the old 21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation). But since February 2, 2026, Part 820 has been formally replaced by the new QMSR (Quality Management System Regulation).

The core change in the QMSR: it directly adopts the framework and terminology of ISO 13485:2016, aligning US device regulation with the international standard. For manufacturers holding ISO 13485 certification, the compliance path is more direct.

Practical Impact on Combination Product Manufacturers

  • The substance of the six requirements is unchanged: 4.4(b)(1) still cites these six; only the underlying regulatory text has been updated
  • ISO 13485-certified manufacturers can leverage synergies: if your device component suppliers hold ISO 13485 certification, their QMS structure now aligns more directly with your QMSR obligations
  • Harmonized terminology: the QMSR uses ISO 13485 terminology, which helps integrate compliance activities with EU MDR/IVDR
  • Design control updates: ISO 13485 §7.3 is more detailed than the old 820.30 in some respects; a clause-by-clause comparison of the differences is recommended

Analogy: Upgrading from an Old Version of Windows to a New One

The QMSR is like upgrading an old operating system: the core functions and applications (the six requirements) are still there, but the underlying architecture is more modern and more compatible with international standards. Your existing "applications" (SOPs, procedural documents) are largely usable, but you need to confirm that they run correctly on the new system (meet the new detailed requirements of the QMSR text).

Recommended Actions

If your CDMO is building a combination product quality system:

  1. Map directly against the QMSR (the new Part 820) and ISO 13485:2016, not the old provisions cited in the 2017 guidance
  2. Consider obtaining ISO 13485 certification at the same time, to kill two birds with one stone: QMSR compliance plus EU market access
  3. Confirm with your regulatory affairs consultant how the provisions cited in 4.4(b)(1) map to the new QMSR clause numbers

Context: The Device QS-Based Streamlined Approach

IV.B — Overview: Drug CGMP Provisions Under the Device QS-Based Streamlined Approach

This section provides a brief description of the provisions from the drug CGMPs with which single-entity and co-packaged combination product manufacturers must demonstrate compliance when using the device QS regulation-based streamlined approach established under 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2).

This discussion is not meant to be a comprehensive analysis, but rather to help manufacturers — particularly device manufacturers, who may be less familiar with drug CGMPs — understand the purpose and basic elements of the provisions of the drug CGMPs specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2), as well as to direct such manufacturers to additional guidance.

The specified provisions of the drug CGMPs include requirements for testing and other verification procedures for batches or lots, whether of drug components, drug containers and closures, drug constituent parts, or the whole combination product.

Combination product manufacturers should establish procedures defining a "batch" or "lot" in all phases of production and describe all batch and lot numbering systems used for incoming material, in-process material, and finished products. These procedures allow manufacturers to connect specific batches or lots of constituent parts, components, and in-process material to the specific batches or lots of the combination product for which they are used.

These procedures enable traceability of sampling and testing, packaging, and labeling activities, and can be important when assessing and responding to issues including complaints and adverse events. Master production and control records should be designed to enable this traceability.

Commentary: What is the "device QS-based streamlined approach"?

Core concept: two streamlined approaches

21 CFR Part 4 provides two streamlined approaches for combination products:

  • Drug CGMP-based (4.4(b)(1)): uses 21 CFR 210/211 as the primary framework, with specific device QS requirements added
  • Device QS-based (4.4(b)(2)): uses 21 CFR Part 820 as the primary framework, with 8 specific drug CGMP requirements added

This section (IV.B) covers the second approach: device QS as the main framework, with 8 drug CGMP provisions added.

Analogy: "a set menu plus mandatory add-ons"

Think of the device QS (21 CFR 820) as a complete quality set menu at a restaurant. A manufacturer taking this path must "order" 8 additional dishes. All 8 are requirements unique to drug manufacturing that the device QS does not contain.

The 8 "mandatory dishes" are: 211.84, 211.103, 211.132, 211.137, 211.165, 211.166, 211.167, 211.170.

CDMO scenario: when to choose the device QS approach?

This path is more common for device companies (for example, makers of drug-coated stents or prefilled syringes) adding a drug constituent part. They already have a well-established 21 CFR 820 system and do not want to rebuild a full drug GMP system, so they keep 820 as the main framework and fill in only the 8 drug-specific requirements.

For a traditional sterile drug CDMO, the opposite is more common: 211 as the main framework (the IV.A approach). But as CDMOs take on more drug-device combination products, understanding these 8 requirements is becoming increasingly important.

Why the batch definition matters

Whichever approach is chosen, the definition of a batch/lot is particularly complex for combination products: the drug constituent part, the device constituent part, and the final combination product may each carry a different batch number. A clear traceability procedure must be established so that batches are linked from raw material to finished product.

IV.B.1 - 21 CFR 211.84: Component Testing and Approval/Rejection

1. Testing and Approval or Rejection of Drug Product Components, Containers, and Closures (21 CFR 211.84)

Drug product components, containers, and closures must be tested in accordance with 21 CFR 211.84. A drug component is any ingredient intended for use in the manufacture of a drug product, including those that may not appear in the drug product. A container closure system is the sum of packaging components that together contain and protect the drug product. This includes primary packaging components and also secondary packaging components if the latter are intended to provide additional protection to the drug product. Examples of packaging components are ampules, vials, screw caps, stoppers, and stopper overseals.

Combination product manufacturers do not need to demonstrate compliance with this provision for device constituent parts or materials used in the manufacture of a device constituent part, unless the device constituent part is also the drug container or closure or a part thereof.

21 CFR 211.84 details how to sample, test, examine, and accept or reject drug product components, containers, and closures. In lieu of such testing, 21 CFR 211.84 allows for some reliance on a supplier's report of analysis (COA), provided that certain identity testing is conducted and that the reliability of the supplier's analysis is established by the manufacturer through appropriate validation of the testing results at appropriate intervals.

Each lot of drug components, containers, and closures must be withheld from use until it has been sampled, tested, or examined, as appropriate, and released for use by the quality control unit. The samples collected for each lot must be representative of the entire lot. The number of containers to sample and the amount of material to be taken from each container must be based on appropriate criteria (e.g., component variability, confidence levels, degree of precision desired, past quality history of the supplier, and the quantity needed for analysis).

Commentary: 211.84 - a key reinforcement point for device manufacturers

What does 211.84 require?

  • Identity testing: every lot of drug components must undergo identity testing; reliance on the supplier COA alone is not acceptable
  • Representative sampling: the sampling plan must be statistically justified (sample size, confidence level)
  • Quarantine before release: each lot of material must be quarantined until released by the QCU and must not be used in production
  • Container closure systems: primary packaging (ampoules, vials, stoppers, caps) is brought under control

Typical gaps at device manufacturers

21 CFR 820.80 (receiving acceptance) only requires the manufacturer to ensure that incoming product conforms to specifications. It does not require identity testing, nor verification of COA reliability.

Device manufacturers typically rely on supplier COAs or functional testing and lack identity tests against drug specifications. This is one of the most common compliance gaps.

Analogy: receiving materials at a device plant vs. a drug plant

Device plant practice: receives steel screws, performs visual inspection and dimensional measurement, and releases them if OK.

Drug plant practice under 211.84: receives API, performs identity testing (HPLC, IR), reviews the COA, draws samples under a statistical sampling plan, and the QCU formally releases the lot.

Once a device manufacturer introduces a drug constituent part, it must raise the first practice to the rigor of the second.

CDMO practice: the dual identity of the container closure system

For a prefilled syringe, the syringe barrel is both a device (device constituent part) and the drug container closure system (primary drug packaging). The 211.84 requirements therefore apply to the syringe barrel, including siliconization measurement, extractables testing, and compatibility studies, going far beyond normal incoming acceptance of a device.

Comparison of 211.84 compliance paths

Path A: full in-house testing
Perform all identity, purity, and strength testing in-house, complying with 211.84(d)

Path B: reliance on COA (conditional)
Accept the supplier COA, perform your own identity testing, and periodically validate COA reliability, complying with 211.84(d)(3)

Note: Path B still requires identity testing; it cannot be skipped entirely.

  2. Balloon catheter material (PEBAX, used only in the device constituent part)
  3. The sterilization pouch of the final product (protective outer packaging)
  4. The balloon itself, which contains the drug (the device is also the drug container)

IV.B.2 - 21 CFR 211.103: Calculation of Yield

2. Calculation of Yield (21 CFR 211.103)

Actual yields and percentages of theoretical yield for the drug constituent part(s) of a combination product must be determined as described in 21 CFR 211.103. Excess or low yields may suggest problems in the production process, including equipment failure, that may affect product quality and that may not be discovered, or not be discovered as quickly, using sample-based release testing alone. Actual yield discrepancies outside of established and documented allowable variation from theoretical yield should be investigated.

Yield calculation requirements under 21 CFR 211.103 apply to the drug constituent parts of combination products. Data on the number of device constituent parts and components used and lost during the manufacture of a combination product may be necessary to ensure appropriate control of the manufacturing process, but yield calculation in accordance with 21 CFR 211.103 is not required for device constituent parts.

Yield determinations must be made at the conclusion of each appropriate phase of manufacturing, processing, packaging, and holding for the drug constituent part(s) and for the combination product as a whole. Accordingly, calculation of yield should be determined at each phase at which component, in-process material, or product loss may occur, including during the formulation of the drug prior to incorporation into the combination product, during incorporation into the combination product (e.g., filling or coating), and, possibly, during the packaging process.

These calculations must be performed by one person and independently verified by a second person, unless the yield is calculated by automated equipment in accordance with 21 CFR 211.68, in which case it must be independently verified by one person.

For each appropriate phase of the manufacturing process performed, the formula used and the data generated for the yield calculation for the phase should be documented. For manufacturers operating under a device QS regulation-based system, documentation of yield calculations may be incorporated into existing part 820 documentation (for example, as part of the device history record, 21 CFR 820.184).

If a third party is manufacturing the drug for the combination product, that manufacturer is responsible for complying with the calculation of yield requirement at the appropriate phases of the drug manufacturing process it performs, but the combination product manufacturer is responsible for ensuring that the supplier satisfies these requirements, as a part of the combination product manufacturer's purchasing controls for its product in accordance with 21 CFR 820.50.

Commentary: 211.103 - a brand-new concept for device manufacturers

What are "theoretical yield" and "actual yield"?

Theoretical yield: the number of units expected if there were no losses at all in the manufacturing process (calculated from the drug input).

Actual yield: the number (or weight) of acceptable units actually obtained.

Percentage yield = actual yield / theoretical yield × 100%

Yield must be calculated at each manufacturing phase, with predetermined acceptable limits (upper and lower). A result outside the limits triggers an investigation.

Analogy: baking bread vs. making syringes

Drug plant view: 100 kg API can theoretically make 10,000 injection units, and 9,850 are actually produced, so the yield is 98.5%. It is recorded and confirmed to be within the acceptable range (assume 95-102%).

Traditional device plant view: make 10,000 catheters, 9,850 pass testing, and the yield rate is recorded. But there is no concept of "theoretical yield" and no mandatory investigation trigger.

Both views care about losses, but drug GMP yield calculation has stricter documentation and investigation requirements.

Important: why can yield reveal problems?

Low yield may point to equipment malfunction (underfilling), abnormal raw material density, operator error, or unrecorded waste. These problems may be completely invisible in sample release testing (because the product tested is good product), but yield calculation can catch abnormalities in the batch as a whole.

CDMO practice: yield calculation for prefilled syringes

When manufacturing prefilled syringes (PFS), the key yield calculation points include:

  • Compounding stage: API input vs. volume of drug solution prepared (% of theoretical)
  • Filtration stage: recovery of drug solution after filtration (filter retention must be recorded)
  • Filling stage: total drug solution filled vs. actual number of products after filling × labeled amount
  • Assembly/packaging stage: pass rate of finished product

Each point needs a predetermined acceptable range, and any result outside it must be investigated.

Documentation integration recommendation: yield calculations in the DHR

Device manufacturers already have a DHR (Device History Record, 21 CFR 820.184). FDA recommends integrating drug yield calculation records into the DHR rather than building an entirely new documentation system, but the DHR must be extended to include:

  • Theoretical yield calculation formulas for each manufacturing phase
  • Actual yield records (with second-person verification signature)
  • Predetermined criteria for the acceptable range
  • Links to investigation records for out-of-range results
IV.B.3 - 21 CFR 211.132: Tamper-Evident Packaging (OTC Only)

3. Tamper-Evident Packaging Requirements for Over-the-Counter Human Drug Products (21 CFR 211.132)

Manufacturers of OTC combination products must comply with the requirements of 21 CFR 211.132. The required controls include tamper-evident packaging and labeling alerting customers to these protective features of the product's packaging. These controls are important to help improve the security of OTC combination product packaging and help ensure the safety and effectiveness of OTC combination products.

For single-entity combination products, tamper-evident packaging requirements apply to the packaging for the combination product as a whole. For co-packaged combination products, these requirements can be met through appropriate packaging of the drug constituent part(s) within the larger co-package so long as such an approach is otherwise permissible under the packaging and labeling requirements applicable to that combination product.

Certain combination products may be exempt from OTC tamper-evident packaging requirements (e.g., toothpaste co-packaged with a toothbrush) or from bearing a statement of tamper-evident features on the package (e.g., aerosol saline nasal spray).

An exemption from tamper-evident packaging and labeling requirements for an OTC combination product may be requested in accordance with 21 CFR 211.132(d). If the manufacturer makes changes to packaging and labeling of an approved OTC product to comply with the requirements of 21 CFR 211.132, it must notify the lead center for the combination product before distributing it.

Commentary: 211.132 - applies to OTC products only

Key point: prescription products are not covered!

The tamper-evident packaging requirement of 211.132 applies only to OTC (over-the-counter) combination products. The great majority of sterile injectable combination products (prefilled syringes, drug-coated stents) are prescription (Rx) products and are not subject to this provision.

Of the 8 additional requirements, this is the one with the clearest conditional limitation, and many device manufacturers may find it does not apply at all.

OTC combination products: typical examples

  • Toothpaste + toothbrush (co-packaged): an exemption may apply
  • OTC nasal spray + rinse bottle
  • OTC topical ointment + dressing
  • Smoking cessation patch (single-entity combination product)

Confirm the OTC status of the product and whether any exemption conditions are met.

Overview of 211.132 compliance requirements

Required measures:

  • Adopt a tamper-evident packaging mechanism (such as a sealed film, shrink band, or security label)
  • The outer package label must include a statement describing the tamper-evident feature
  • If the packaging of an approved OTC product is changed, the lead center must be notified before distribution

Possible exemptions (must be requested from FDA):

  • Specific dosage forms (such as aerosols): may be exempt from the labeling statement
  • Specific product categories: an exemption from the packaging requirement may be requested

Analogy: the logic of the safety seal

OTC products are sold on open shelves where anyone can pick them up and examine them, so the opportunity for malicious tampering is higher. The purpose of tamper-evident packaging is to let consumers confirm that "this product has not been opened since it left the factory." Prescription drugs are normally controlled by pharmacists and the distribution chain is more tightly controlled, so the urgency of this requirement is different.

IV.B.4 - 21 CFR 211.137: Expiration Dating

4. Expiration Dating (21 CFR 211.137)

21 CFR 211.137 helps ensure that drug products (drug constituent parts in the case of combination products) meet applicable standards of identity, strength, quality, and purity at the time of use, by requiring that the product labeling bear an expiration date. This date must take into account any storage conditions stated in the labeling and be based on appropriate stability testing as described in 21 CFR 211.166 (see IV.B.6). The expiration dating also should take into account any other applicable shelf-life considerations (e.g., for a product that is to be provided sterile, the length of time that its packaging material can be ensured to retain its integrity and, thereby, maintain a sterile barrier).

When expiration dating is required, the constituent parts of combination products may have individual expiration dates or an expiration date for the entire combination product may be appropriate. Generally, when constituent parts of a co-packaged combination product can be used independently, expiration dating should be addressed separately for each constituent part.

If a single expiration date is listed for a co-packaged combination product, this date should be the earliest expiration date/shortest shelf-life for any constituent part. The expiration date for a combination product may be shorter than the expiration date or shelf life for its constituent part(s) if marketed independently. Reasons for a shorter expiration period could include interactions between the constituent parts when combined, the effects of additional manufacturing steps, or other differences arising from the combination of the constituent parts.

Commentary: 211.137 - a core shift for device manufacturers

Device QS vs. drug GMP: the gap in expiration dating requirements

21 CFR Part 820 (device QS) has no equivalent of the 211.137 expiration dating requirement. Devices may have design validation of a "shelf life", but this is a different concept from drug expiration dating, which must be based on stability data and shown on the label.

Once a drug constituent part is introduced, expiration dating becomes a mandatory obligation and must be supported by stability data.

The "shortest expiry" principle

If a combination product carries a single expiration date, it must use the shortest expiration date of any constituent part. This means:

  • Device constituent part expiry: 5 years
  • Drug constituent part expiry: 2 years (based on stability data)
  • Combination product expiry: 2 years (the shortest)

This can have a major impact on commercial strategy, since drug stability often determines the commercial shelf life of a combination product.

CDMO practice: the expiry challenge of drug-eluting stents

The expiry of a drug-eluting stent involves:

  • Drug stability: degradation rate of the drug (such as everolimus) in the stent coating
  • Device mechanical performance: durability of the metal stent frame (usually longer)
  • Packaging integrity: how long the sterile barrier is maintained
  • Combination interaction: changes over time in compatibility at the device-drug interface

The final expiry is determined by the shortest of these factors and must be supported by stability studies.

Analogy: the shelf life of a combination lunch box

In a combination pack containing a "rice ball (drug, 1 day)" and a "zip bag (device, 1 year)", the overall shelf life is 1 day, determined by the component with the shortest life. Even if the bag lasts 1 year, the lunch box cannot be sold beyond 1 day.

Exercise

A co-packaged combination product contains (A) a glass drug vial (expiry 24 months) and (B) a single-use drug delivery device. If the two can be used independently, how should the expiry be labeled? If they cannot be used independently, how should the expiry be determined?

Answer: If they can be used independently, label each with its own expiry. If they cannot be used independently (single-entity), the shortest expiry applies (24 months), and the interaction after combination must also be considered.

IV.B.5 - 21 CFR 211.165: Testing and Release for Distribution

5. Testing and Release for Distribution (21 CFR 211.165)

Testing and release for distribution are critical in drug product manufacture and quality control. 21 CFR 211.165 requires that an appropriate laboratory determination of satisfactory conformance to final specifications (including the identity and strength of each active ingredient) is made for each batch of drug product prior to release. 21 CFR 211.165 also requires appropriate laboratory testing, as necessary, of each batch of a drug product required to be free of objectionable microorganisms. In addition, sampling and testing plans must be described by written procedures.

Accordingly, manufacturers must test each batch of their combination product to determine satisfactory conformance to final written specifications for the drug constituent part. For single-entity combination products, laboratory testing must be performed on every batch of the combination product; for co-packaged combination products, laboratory testing must be performed on every batch of the drug constituent part(s). This testing ensures that all batches meet appropriate specifications for their approval and release. A detailed listing of all the tests performed and the acceptance criteria used should be maintained and be available for inspection.

In some cases, for single-entity combination products, in lieu of using actual units from a finished combination product batch for testing, manufacturers may wish to use samples that are not finished combination products (but that are representative of the finished combination product with respect to the characteristics and attributes being tested). The Agency does not intend to object to such an approach where the manufacturer can establish, including where appropriate through bridging studies and other quantitative means, that any differences in the manufacturing process for the samples and the finished combination product do not affect the drug constituent part. Such approaches should be supported by appropriate justification and data and discussed with the lead Center, and OCP as needed.

If one facility is manufacturing a drug product as a constituent part to be supplied to another facility for inclusion in a co-packaged combination product, appropriate and adequate laboratory testing should be conducted by the drug product manufacturing facility prior to release in accordance with 21 CFR 211.165. The combination product manufacturer is responsible for ensuring through purchasing controls in accordance with 21 CFR 820.50 that the drug constituent part conforms to specified requirements.

Commentary: 211.165 - the drug logic of "release testing"

Core release testing required by 211.165

  • Identity: confirm that the active ingredient is the correct one
  • Strength: confirm that the active ingredient content meets specification
  • Purity: confirm that impurities are within the acceptable range
  • Quality: other physicochemical and microbiological characteristics
  • Every batch must be tested: batches cannot be skipped
  • Written procedures: the sampling plan and test methods must be covered by SOPs

The biggest gap at device manufacturers: functional testing vs. drug release testing

Device manufacturers usually have well-established functional release tests (burst pressure, tensile strength, seal integrity, and so on). Drug release testing is entirely different: the focus is chemical analysis of the active ingredient.

For manufacturers with 820 as the main framework, 211.165 is the requirement that most often demands new capability, because it usually needs analytical chemistry instruments (HPLC and so on), qualified analytical chemists, and validated analytical methods.

CDMO practice: acceptability of surrogate test samples

FDA expressly allows, in some cases, representative samples of a "non-complete combination product" to be used for drug release testing, provided that:

  • Bridging studies demonstrate that the surrogate sample is equivalent to the final product for the tested attributes
  • Differences in the manufacturing process do not affect the drug constituent part
  • The lead center and OCP agree to the approach

For example, a prefilled syringe manufacturer might use test vials filled with the same drug solution for release testing, provided data support equivalence.

IV.B.6 - 21 CFR 211.166: Stability Testing

6. Stability Testing (21 CFR 211.166)

21 CFR 211.166 requires a testing program designed to assess the stability characteristics of drug products. Furthermore, 21 CFR 211.166 sets forth required elements of the testing program, including elements concerning sample size, storage conditions for samples retained for testing, and other elements related to testing methodology and frequency.

Stability testing is performed to determine appropriate storage conditions and expiration dates (see IV.B.4). Among other considerations, this testing must enable evaluation of any effects on the stability of the drug due to storage in its marketed container closure system, which may be a device constituent part (or component of a device constituent part). For a single-entity combination product, testing must be performed on the drug constituent part as incorporated into the finished combination product.

The Agency does not intend to object to the use of bracketing and matrix approaches for stability studies where the approach has been adequately justified and, where applicable, reviewed by FDA. The Agency also does not intend to object to leveraging stability data for an already marketed combination product during new product development, with adequate justification and, where applicable, review by FDA.

The need for new stability studies should be evaluated when there are changes to any constituent part of a combination product. For example, changes to the manufacturing process, including changes based on the size or shape of the device constituent part that holds the drug or that is coated with the drug, or changes in material of construction of such a device constituent part, may impact drug stability.

If a combination product manufacturer purchases a drug product from another manufacturer for inclusion in its co-packaged combination product, the combination product manufacturer is responsible for ensuring the stability of the drug product as marketed in the co-packaged product through appropriate mechanisms, such as by implementing purchasing controls to ensure the adequacy of the drug product manufacturer's stability testing or by conducting additional stability testing (see 21 CFR 820.50).

If a drug constituent part of a co-packaged combination product has an expiration date, the combination product manufacturer may be able to rely on that labeled expiration date in lieu of conducting new stability studies, if it can be documented that any additional manufacturing operations conducted on the co-packaged product would not be expected to impact the constituent part, including its container-closure system.

Commentary: 211.166 - stability is the core science of combination products

Required elements of a 211.166 stability program

  • Written stability program: required for every product
  • Number of samples: sufficient to perform complete testing at each test time point
  • Storage conditions: consistent with the conditions claimed on the label
  • Testing frequency: at each time point (for example 0, 3, 6, 12, 24 months)
  • On-going stability: continued monitoring of marketed batches
  • Final container testing: single-entity combination products must be tested in the final combined state

Device change = stability reassessment

This is one of the most frequently overlooked requirements. Any change to the device constituent part (material, dimensions, shape, manufacturing process) may affect drug stability, and you must reassess whether a new stability study is needed. For example:

  • Drug-coated balloon changes balloon diameter: coating area/thickness changes, so drug stability may be affected
  • DES polymer material replaced: drug release kinetics change, so new stability data are needed
  • Prefilled syringe rubber stopper supplier changed: compatibility/extractables change, so the impact on stability must be assessed

CDMO practice: stability study strategy

Bracketing: if the stability of the smallest and largest strengths in a product family can represent the intermediate strengths, only the extremes need testing, and full stability studies are not needed for every strength.

Matrixing: a strategic design that reduces the number of test time points, lowering the testing burden while maintaining statistical power.

Borrowing data from marketed products: if a new product is a minor modification of a marketed combination product (for example, a different catheter length with the same drug coating), existing stability data may be leveraged, speeding up the development timeline.

Analogy: drug stability is the "rate-limiting step" of a combination product

Think of a car with an engine (the drug) and a body (the device). Even if the body is designed to last 50 years, it is the engine's service life that determines when the whole car "expires". The life of the device constituent part is usually far longer than the stability of the drug constituent part, so drug stability is often what determines the expiry of the combination product.

Is a new stability study needed? Decision flow

Scenario 1: brand-new combination product → a full stability study is required

Scenario 2: minor change to an existing product (low risk) → a reduced stability study or matrixing may be possible; contact the lead center

Scenario 3: co-packaged, drug already has a labeled expiry → if the combining step does not affect the drug, the existing expiry may be relied on, but this must be justified with documentation

Scenario 4: any change that may affect the drug → must be reassessed; a new study may be needed

IV.B.7 - 21 CFR 211.167: Special Testing Requirements

7. Special Testing Requirements (21 CFR 211.167)

21 CFR 211.167 establishes requirements for batch testing applicable to drug products having particular characteristics. Specifically:

  • 21 CFR 211.167(a): Requires appropriate laboratory testing of drug products purporting to be sterile and/or pyrogen-free to determine conformance with such requirements.
  • 21 CFR 211.167(b): Requires appropriate testing of ophthalmic ointments to determine conformance to specifications regarding the presence of foreign particles and harsh or abrasive substances.
  • 21 CFR 211.167(c): Requires appropriate laboratory testing of controlled-release dosage forms to determine conformance to the specifications for the rate of release of each active ingredient.

A special testing requirement specified in 21 CFR 211.167 applies to a combination product only if the combination product or a drug constituent part of it falls into one or more of the three categories described above. Special testing may be required for the drug constituent part, or the combination product as a whole, depending on the product.

With respect to 21 CFR 211.167(a), batch testing requirements would apply both to the drug constituent part and to the finished combination product for a single-entity combination product (such as a prefilled syringe) to ensure the combination product is sterile and pyrogen-free when distributed. In contrast, if a vial of a vaccine were co-packaged with an empty syringe, the requirements in 21 CFR 211.167(a) would apply only to the vial of vaccine.

For purposes of conducting pyrogen and endotoxin testing, it may be permissible in some circumstances to define a "batch" based on the finished drug product rather than the finished combination product. For example, it may be acceptable to define a batch as a subcomponent of the combination product that incorporates the drug constituent part. However, the viability of such an approach would depend on many factors including: appropriate upstream controls to reduce/limit pyrogens and endotoxins; the chemical or materials-mediated pyrogenicity of the constituent parts; and the risks of pyrogen and endotoxin introduction to the combination product by manufacturing operations that occur after the step at which the batch is defined.

21 CFR 211.167(c) would apply both to a controlled-release drug constituent part of a co-packaged combination product and also to a controlled release single-entity combination product, to confirm rate of release of the active ingredient. For example, a transdermal drug patch or drug-eluting disc or stent would be subject to 21 CFR 211.167(c).

As with drugs and devices, parametric release may be acceptable for some combination products when supported by appropriate data. Such approaches should be discussed with the lead Center, or OCP as needed, prior to implementation and included in premarket submissions.

Commentary: 211.167 - key testing for sterile combination products

Sterile combination products: dual testing requirement

For single-entity sterile combination products (such as prefilled syringes):

  • The drug constituent part (the drug solution) must pass sterility/pyrogen testing
  • The final combination product as a whole must also pass sterility/pyrogen testing

For co-packaged products (such as a vial filled with drug solution + an empty syringe): only the drug-containing vial needs to be tested. The sterility of the empty syringe is controlled by other regulatory requirements (design controls, process controls).

Flexibility in batch definition for endotoxin testing

FDA offers important operational flexibility here: for pyrogen/endotoxin testing, the "batch" may be defined as the drug-containing intermediate component (rather than the final combination product), provided that:

  • There are adequate upstream control measures to reduce pyrogens
  • The pyrogen risk arising from device materials has been assessed
  • Manufacturing steps after the "batch definition point" do not introduce new pyrogen risk

This matters a great deal in practice: testing the whole combination product (such as a DES) may be technically difficult, and allowing the batch to be defined at an earlier manufacturing step for testing greatly simplifies operations.

CDMO practice: dissolution testing of drug-eluting stents

An everolimus-eluting stent must comply with 211.167(c): every batch requires in vitro drug release (IVDR) testing to confirm that the drug is released with the designed kinetics.

This is more complex than dissolution testing for a typical drug product because stent geometry affects release, a test medium simulating physiological conditions is needed, and the test itself may be destructive (the stent cannot be kept for use). All of this must be built into the release testing plan.

Analogy: the insurance logic of parametric release

Traditional sterilization release: a sterility test is done on every batch (taking 2 weeks) before release.
Parametric release: the sterilization process is fully validated and all process parameters (temperature, time, F₀ value) are monitored for every batch. If all parameters pass, the sterility test can be omitted and the batch released directly.

Combination products can likewise apply for parametric release, but this requires prior discussion with FDA and inclusion in the premarket application.

Exercise

A CDMO manufactures the following products. Decide which 211.167 paragraph applies to each:

  1. Prefilled insulin syringe (PFS)
  2. Drug-eluting brain implant (with a sustained-release chemotherapy drug)
  3. OTC ophthalmic antibiotic ointment + eye patch (co-packaged)
  4. Dry powder inhaler (containing antibiotic powder)

Answer: 1 → (a); 2 → (a) + (c) (sterile and controlled-release); 3 → (a) + (b); 4 → (a) (note: if the DPI is not claimed to be sterile, (a) may not apply).

IV.B.8 - 21 CFR 211.170: Reserve Samples

8. Reserve Samples (21 CFR 211.170)

Reserve samples are needed to help ensure the safety and effectiveness of combination products in distribution. They are used, for example, to address certain product complaints, evaluate stability concerns, and assess the causes of adverse events. The reserve sampling requirements of 21 CFR 211.170 must be met for the drug constituent parts of combination products.

Under 21 CFR 211.170, reserve samples must be kept that are representative of both:

  • each lot of the active ingredient (21 CFR 211.170(a)), and
  • each lot or batch of the drug product (21 CFR 211.170(b)).

All reserve samples must consist of at least twice the quantity necessary to perform all required tests, except those for sterility and pyrogenicity. Furthermore, reserve samples of drug products must be retained and stored under conditions consistent with product labeling and stored in the same immediate container-closure system in which the drug product is marketed or in one that has essentially the same characteristics.

For co-packaged combination products, the requirement to keep reserve samples of drug products can be met by maintaining samples of the drug constituent part in its immediate container-closure system, without the need to retain any samples of the device or portions of it. For single-entity combination products, the drug product reserve samples should be of the drug constituent part in or upon the device constituent part or components thereof that come into contact with the drug product as packaged for distribution.

Drug product reserve samples generally must be maintained for 1 year after the expiration date for the drug product; different time periods apply to certain radioactive and OTC products. Active ingredient samples generally must be kept for 1 year after the expiration date for the last lot of the combination product containing the active ingredient.

Manufacturers must examine drug product samples for deterioration, investigate evidence of deterioration, and record and maintain the results of such examination.

Combination product manufacturers may choose to have their reserve samples of the API, drug/biologic constituent part, or finished combination product at a location other than the combination product manufacturing facility. In these cases, the combination product manufacturer should have appropriate controls in place to ensure that requirements under 21 CFR 211.170 continue to be met.

Manufacturers considering use of novel approaches to comply with reserve sampling requirements (such as bracketing and matrixing, or use of surrogate samples for some testing) are advised to discuss them with the lead center for their combination product, or OCP, as needed.

Commentary: 211.170 - a new obligation with no device equivalent

Device QS has no reserve sample requirement

21 CFR Part 820 has no provision equivalent to 211.170 for reserve samples. Device manufacturers usually retain design verification samples and engineering samples, but there is no regulatory obligation to "retain twice the quantity needed for testing, for one year after expiry".

This is one of the new systems a device manufacturer most needs to establish after introducing a drug constituent part.

Single-entity vs. co-packaged: different reserve sample scope

Co-packaged (such as vial + syringe): only the drug constituent part (the vial) must be retained, not the device constituent part (the syringe), but a syringe must be available for use when the reserve sample is tested.

Single-entity (such as prefilled syringe, DES): the whole combination product must be retained, because the device (syringe barrel/stent) is the container/carrier of the drug and cannot be separated for retention.

CDMO practice: practical considerations in reserve sample management

Storage space: prefilled syringe batches run to tens of thousands of units, so twice the testing quantity is still a substantial cold storage requirement, and it must be kept until 1 year after expiry (potentially 3-4 years or more in total).

Off-site storage: FDA allows reserve samples to be stored at a location other than the manufacturing site (such as a contract warehouse or supplier facility), but a written agreement must ensure appropriate storage conditions, access by the manufacturer at any time, adequate testing capability, and sufficient batch quantity.

Periodic visual inspection: drug reserve samples must be visually inspected at least once a year for signs of deterioration, and the results recorded.

Analogy: reserve samples are the product's "witness" after release

Imagine that after each batch ships, some samples are kept in the warehouse to "testify". If a complaint arises later (such as insufficient efficacy or an adverse reaction), the reserve samples can be retested to determine whether the problem lies in the product itself or in storage/transport, or to confirm that no problem exists (preventing unjustified complaints).

Without reserve samples, this kind of retrospective investigation is impossible. That is why FDA requires retention until 1 year past expiry, ensuring samples are available for investigation throughout the product lifecycle.

Exercise

What should the reserve sample plan for a batch of DES (drug-eluting stents, expiry 18 months) include? Until when must the samples be retained? Can only the drug portion on the stent be retained?

Answer: The complete stent must be retained (single-entity), in a quantity twice that needed for all required testing (such as IVDR and sterility testing), excluding sterility/pyrogen testing. Retention period: 18-month expiry + 1 year = 1 year after expiry (disposal is allowed only after month 30). Retaining only the drug portion is not acceptable, because the stent is the container/carrier of the drug.

IV.C - Combination Products Including Biological Products and HCT/Ps

C. Combination Products That Include Biological Products and HCT/Ps

In addition to complying with drug CGMP and device QS regulation requirements as applicable in accordance with part 4, a manufacturer of a combination product that contains a biological product or an HCT/P must comply with the requirements that would apply to the biological product or HCT/P if it were not part of a combination product.

1. Complying with CGMP Requirements for Biological Products

It is important to remember that a biological product regulated under section 351 of the PHS Act is also, by definition, a drug or a device. Accordingly, in addition to the requirements in parts 600 through 680, a biological product is always either subject to the drug CGMPs or subject to the device QS regulation, regardless of whether the biological product is a constituent part of a combination product.

The CGMP requirements for biological products in parts 600 through 680 augment the drug CGMPs and device QS regulation to ensure adequate consideration of issues for biological products. The CGMP requirements for biological products in parts 600 through 680 address the particular challenges biological products pose, including challenges arising from their relative complexity. For biological products, consistency of manufacturing procedures can, in fact, be a primary means by which to ensure the safety, purity, and potency of the product.

The CGMP requirements for biological products in parts 600 through 680 must be satisfied if a combination product includes a biological product constituent part. However, many requirements in parts 600 through 680 are applicable only to certain types of biological products. For example, while part 600 facially addresses biological products in general, only products manufactured using a spore-forming microorganism would be subject to 21 CFR 600.11(e)(3). Similarly, only blood and blood components are subject to the CGMP requirements for such products under part 606.

In short, the specific requirements in parts 600 through 680 that must be met will depend on the type of biological product it includes. The Agency welcomes the opportunity to discuss these requirements with manufacturers. If manufacturers have questions regarding these requirements, they may contact the lead center for the combination product or OCP, as needed, for assistance.

2. Complying with CGMPs for HCT/Ps

An HCT/P (human cell, tissue, and cellular and tissue-based product) that is not regulated solely under section 361 of the PHS Act and Part 1271 is also regulated as a drug, device, or biological product. The drug CGMPs, device QS regulation, and the requirements in parts 600 through 680 may apply to an HCT/P depending on whether the product is regulated as a drug, device, or biological product.

CGMPs and the CGTPs (Current Good Tissue Practice) for HCT/Ps supplement one another and do not supersede each other unless the regulation specifically provides otherwise. In the event that a regulation in part 1271 is in conflict with a requirement in parts 210, 211, 600 through 680, or 820, the regulations more specifically applicable to the product in question will supersede the more general.

The CGTPs, including donor eligibility requirements for the manufacture of HCT/Ps under part 1271, are designed to prevent the introduction, transmission, and spread of communicable diseases, and thereby are essential to protecting the public health. Accordingly, the current CGTPs apply to combination products that include an HCT/P. However, requirements under some sections of part 1271 overlap with the requirements under the drug CGMPs and the device QS regulation. These overlaps are addressed in part 1271 and in the Guidance for Industry on Current Good Tissue Practice (CGTP).

Commentary: additional obligations for biological product and HCT/P combination products

Key principle: "layering", not "substitution"

The 21 CFR Part 4 streamlined approaches (whether drug CGMP-based or device QS-based) do not replace biological product CGMP (21 CFR 600-680) or the CGTP for HCT/Ps (21 CFR 1271).

Combination product containing a biological product = Part 4 streamlined approach + 21 CFR 600-680

Combination product containing an HCT/P = Part 4 streamlined approach + 21 CFR 1271 (+ possibly 600-680)

What is a "biological product"? (21 CFR Parts 600-680)

Under Section 351 of the PHS Act, biological products include:

  • Blood and blood component products (Part 606)
  • Vaccines (Part 610 + specific requirements)
  • Therapeutic proteins (such as monoclonal antibodies and enzyme replacement therapies)
  • Cell and gene therapy products
  • Allergenic products

Each category of biological product has its own specific Part 600-680 requirements, and not all provisions apply to all biological products.

What is an HCT/P? (21 CFR Part 1271)

HCT/P = Human Cells, Tissues, and Cellular and Tissue-Based Products

HCT/Ps regulated solely under Section 361 (minimally manipulated, homologous use, and so on): only need to comply with Part 1271.

HCT/Ps also regulated as drugs/devices/biological products: must comply with Part 1271 (CGTP) + the applicable Part 4 approach + possibly 600-680.

CDMO practice: typical examples of biological product combination products

  • Prefilled monoclonal antibody syringe (such as bevacizumab PFS): Part 4 + Part 211 + Part 600-680 (biologics CGMP)
  • CAR-T cell therapy + infusion device: Part 4 + Part 211 (or 820) + Part 600-680 + Part 1271
  • Allogeneic bone graft + fixation device: Part 4 + Part 820 + Part 1271 (if a Section 351 product)
  • Platelet concentrate + wound care device: Part 4 + Part 606 + Part 1271

Why do biological products need additional CGMP?

FDA states explicitly that for biological products, consistency of manufacturing is itself a core means of assuring safety, purity, and potency. The complexity of biological products means that:

  • Finished product testing cannot fully characterize product quality (batch-to-batch variation is hard to capture in final testing)
  • The manufacturing process must be highly consistent from batch to batch to protect patient safety
  • Parts 600-680 address these biologics-specific challenges (such as viral clearance, potency testing, and special lot release requirements)

Interaction between CGTP (tissue practices) and CGMP

Between Part 1271 (CGTP) and Part 210/211 or Part 820 (CGMP):

  • Complementary, not substitutive: both apply together, each within its own scope
  • Specific provisions prevail: if there is a conflict, the regulation that is more specific to the product applies
  • Donor eligibility: a requirement unique to CGTP with no equivalent in CGMP, intended to prevent the transmission of communicable diseases

Analogy: regulatory layers are like an "onion"

The regulatory requirements for a biological product combination product are like a multi-layered onion:

  • Outermost layer: Part 4 (combination product framework)
  • Middle layer: Part 211 or Part 820 (drug CGMP or device QS)
  • Inner layer: Parts 600-680 (biologics CGMP)
  • Innermost core (if an HCT/P is involved): Part 1271 (CGTP)

No layer can be omitted, and they often stack on one another. The complexity is far greater than for a plain drug or device.

Exercise

A CAR-T cell therapy (genetically modified T cells) is loaded into a single-use infusion device as a combination product and operates under the device QS streamlined approach. List all the major regulatory frameworks that must be followed.

Answer: (1) 21 CFR Part 820 (device QS, the main framework) + (2) the 8 drug CGMP provisions added under 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2) + (3) 21 CFR 600-680 (biologics CGMP, because CAR-T is a biological product) + (4) 21 CFR 1271 (CGTP, because CAR-T is an HCT/P and is not regulated solely under Section 361). This is one of the most complex regulatory scenarios for a combination product.

Section Summary

Key Takeaways — IV.B and IV.C

Section IV.B describes the 8 drug CGMP provisions that must be added to the device QS regulation-based streamlined approach. These provisions address areas where the device QS regulation does not have equivalent requirements: incoming component testing with identity verification, pharmaceutical yield calculation, expiration dating based on stability data, drug product release testing, a formal stability program, special testing for sterile/controlled-release products, and reserve sample retention. Tamper-evident packaging is an additional requirement for OTC products only.

Section IV.C establishes that the streamlined approach under Part 4 does not replace the biological product CGMPs (Parts 600-680) or HCT/P current good tissue practices (Part 1271). Manufacturers of combination products containing biological product or HCT/P constituent parts must comply with all applicable requirements across Part 4, the applicable base regulation (211 or 820), and the biologics/tissue-specific regulations. The specific requirements vary depending on the type of biological product or HCT/P involved, and manufacturers are encouraged to contact the lead center or OCP for product-specific guidance.

Key takeaways

IV.B key points to remember

  • The 8 requirements are the "add-on package" of the device QS approach and are not optional
  • Only 211.132 (tamper-evident packaging) has a conditional limitation (OTC only)
  • The biggest gaps for device manufacturers: 211.84 (identity testing), 211.165 (drug release), 211.166 (stability), 211.170 (reserve samples)
  • Yield calculation (211.103) is a concept entirely unfamiliar to device manufacturers and requires a new documentation system
  • Expiry must be based on stability data, and any change to the device constituent part may trigger a stability reassessment

IV.C key points to remember

  • The Part 4 streamlined approach does not replace 21 CFR 600-680 or 21 CFR 1271
  • Contains a biological product = 600-680 must be added (layering)
  • Contains an HCT/P (not solely Section 361) = 1271 must be added (layering)
  • CGTP and CGMP are complementary, and specific provisions prevail over general ones
  • Specific requirements vary by type of biological product; contact the lead center or OCP for complex cases

  • [ ] Yield calculation SOP and second-person verification process

  • [ ] If a biological product is involved: Part 600-680 compliance assessment
  • [ ] If an HCT/P is involved: Part 1271 CGTP compliance assessment

Section V: CGMP Requirements for Specific Types of Combination Products

V. Application of CGMP Requirements to Specific Types of Combination Products

The hypothetical scenarios addressed in this section focus on three types of combination products. While each of these types of combination products is subject both to the drug CGMPs and to the device QS regulation, each example is used to focus on particular CGMP considerations relating to CGMP provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b). Specifically:

  • Section A — a prefilled syringe example, focuses on how to comply with the device QS regulation provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1) if a manufacturer adopts a drug CGMP-based streamlined approach for its CGMP operating system.
  • Section B — a drug-coated mesh example, focuses on considerations for complying with certain device QS regulations after a drug constituent part is combined with a device.
  • Section C — a drug-eluting stent (DES) example, focuses on how to comply with the drug CGMP provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2) if a manufacturer adopts a device QS regulation-based streamlined approach for its CGMP operating system.

This discussion is intended to highlight only certain issues that a combination product might raise relating to the CGMP provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b). If manufacturers have specific questions relating to their particular products, the Agency recommends that they contact the lead center for the product or OCP, as needed, for assistance.

Commentary: Map of the Three Scenarios

Important Note: Not a Complete Analysis

FDA states explicitly that these scenarios focus only on the specific provisions listed in 21 CFR 4.4(b) and do not represent a complete analysis of all CGMP issues for these types of products.

In other words: an actual product may carry additional compliance requirements, which the manufacturer must identify itself and confirm with the lead center.

CDMO Strategic Perspective

For a sterile fill-finish CDMO, PFS is the most central business scenario. Understanding the logic of Section A means understanding the compliance path that most sterile CDMOs face with combination products.

Core Logic of the Three Hypothetical Scenarios

FDA uses three examples to show how the two streamlined approaches work in practice:

Section V.A: Prefilled Syringe

V.A.1 — Prefilled Syringe: Scenario Description

A drug manufacturer (Manufacturer A) plans to sell a drug in a prefilled syringe presentation. Manufacturer A already has marketing approval for the drug product and intends to apply for marketing approval for the prefilled syringe presentation. No changes to the drug formulation will be made.

Manufacturer A will buy off-the-shelf syringe components from a supplier (Manufacturer B) who also manufactures finished syringes using the same components. Manufacturer A will assemble the syringe components, prefill the syringe at a facility it operates, and then package, label, and distribute the prefilled syringe from this facility.

Manufacturer A's facility has an existing drug CGMP operating system. As the prefilled syringe is a single-entity combination product under 21 CFR 3.2(e)(1), Manufacturer A must demonstrate compliance with both the drug CGMPs and device QS regulation.

To do so, Manufacturer A opts to establish a CGMP operating system using the drug CGMP-based streamlined approach in accordance with 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1). This example focuses on considerations for demonstrating compliance with the provisions from the device QS regulation specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(1).

Commentary: PFS Scenario Background

Legal Classification of a PFS

Prefilled syringe = single-entity combination product (21 CFR 3.2(e)(1))

The drug and the syringe/needle components are physically integrated and cannot be separated, so the product must comply with both Drug CGMP (21 CFR 210/211) and Device QS (21 CFR Part 820).

Analogy: A Restaurant with a Finished Kitchen

Firm A (the drug manufacturer) is like a restaurant that already has a kitchen (the drug CGMP system) and now wants to add a "device menu" (device QS requirements). The whole kitchen does not need to be rebuilt; it only needs 6 device QS provisions added on top of the existing foundation.

Firm B (the syringe component supplier) is the ingredient supplier, whose existing design control data Firm A can leverage.

Drug CGMP-based Approach: The 6 Device QS Provisions to Add

  • 820.20: Management Responsibility
  • 820.30: Design Controls
  • 820.50: Purchasing Controls
  • 820.100: CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action)
  • 820.170: Installation (usually N/A for a PFS)
  • 820.200: Servicing (usually N/A for a single-use PFS)

CDMO Perspective: Why Do Drug Manufacturers Prefer Drug CGMP-based?

The core competence of a sterile fill-finish CDMO is drug manufacturing (21 CFR 211). Adding 6 Device QS provisions is far more feasible than rebuilding an entire Part 820 system. This is also the mainstream choice in the industry.

V.A.2(a) — 820.20: Management Responsibility

While section 501 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 351) and 21 CFR 211.22, 211.25, and 211.180 establish requirements relevant to management responsibility, Manufacturer A must ensure that its CGMP operating system demonstrates compliance with the specific requirements in 21 CFR 820.20.

Manufacturer A should, for example, review its existing CGMP operating system to assess whether changes are needed to comply with this provision. As part of this review, management with executive responsibility for Manufacturer A should review the facility's quality policy and develop and implement appropriate oversight procedures, if such procedures are not already in place, to ensure that both the policy and oversight are adequate.

The oversight procedures should include clear delineation of the personnel to whom management with executive responsibility is delegating responsibility for implementing the quality policy (including translation into methods and procedures) and the CGMP operating system for this product at the facility.

Commentary: 820.20 Management Responsibility

Differences Between the Management Responsibility Requirements of 820.20 and 211

21 CFR 211 already contains management-responsibility-related requirements (211.22 quality control unit, 211.25 personnel qualifications, 211.180 record requirements), but 820.20 has more explicit device-specific requirements, including:

  • Establish and maintain a written Quality Policy
  • Ensure quality objectives are communicated throughout the organization
  • Appoint a Management Representative
  • Conduct periodic Management Review

Practical Points

Drug manufacturers usually already have QMS documents, but the "chain of delegated authority for the quality policy" required by 820.20 must be clearly documented: that is, who is responsible for translating the quality policy into methods and procedures and implementing them at the facility level.

This is a common inspection focus: FDA investigators will ask, "Who is the Management Representative? Where is the document of authority?"

V.A.2(b) — 820.30: Design Controls

Manufacturer A is responsible for establishing and maintaining procedures for design control activities for the combination product. In this scenario, Manufacturer A is the owner and is manufacturing the prefilled syringe. Accordingly, Manufacturer A is responsible for the design control activities for the syringe as part of the combination product, as well as having overall responsibility for the combination product.

However, Manufacturer A is buying the syringe components from Manufacturer B, another manufacturer, which uses the same components to manufacture finished syringes. As a result, Manufacturer A may be able to leverage syringe-specific design control documentation from the design controls Manufacturer B uses for its finished syringes.

Because no changes are to be made to the drug other than being put into the syringe, the drug comes into the design control process as an input to the design of the syringe to ensure that the syringe's design reflects adequate consideration of the drug's characteristics.

i. Design Inputs and Outputs

Commentary: 820.30 Design Controls

Core Logic of PFS Design Controls

For the drug manufacturer (Manufacturer A), "design" is not the drug formulation (already approved) but how the syringe is compatible with the drug and delivers it safely.

Drug = design input; the syringe design must fit the drug's characteristics

Analogy: Leveraging the Supplier's Design Data

Firm B (the syringe manufacturer) already has design control documentation (DHF) for the syringe. Firm A can leverage these data, but must additionally evaluate:

  • Does adding this drug create any new design issues?
  • Container compatibility (drug-syringe compatibility)
  • Leachables/extractables

If Firm B has no design control data, Firm A must perform more extensive design verification itself (e.g., glide force and plunger force testing).

Interpreting Design Inputs and Outputs: Relevance to Sterile Manufacturing

Drug viscosity and needle bore/injection force: High-viscosity protein drugs require a larger-bore needle or a special needle design, which affects the choice of sterilization process (needle pre-attached or assembled separately).

Specifications for drug/syringe contact materials: The amount of siliconization, the elastomer plunger material and the glass coating directly affect stability study design.

V.A.2(b) — Design Verification, Validation & Risk Analysis

ii. Design Verification and Validation

Once Manufacturer A has established design outputs for all design inputs, it must perform design verification and validation activities to ensure that the combination product meets design input requirements, including user needs and intended uses.

Examples of appropriate testing of the prefilled syringe include:

  • Bench testing of the delivery of the drug from the syringe to ensure repeatable and accurate drug delivery
  • Shock and vibration testing of the packaged prefilled syringe to ensure no damage or loss of integrity in shipping
  • Validation that expected users can adequately follow the instructions for use; other human factors studies
  • Biocompatibility testing (ISO 10993-1)
  • Drug and syringe compatibility studies
  • Leachables and extractables testing
  • Verification that the prefilled syringe works with all expected delivery methods (needle, needleless)

These activities would be documented in the DHF (Design History File) pursuant to 21 CFR 820.30(j).

iii. Risk Analysis

Manufacturer A should identify risks associated with the prefilled syringe design, its manufacturing processes, and intended uses, and also reduce or mitigate any unacceptable risks.

Commentary: Design Verification, Validation and Risk Analysis

Verification vs. Validation: The Key Difference

Verification: Do the design outputs meet the design input specifications? "Did we build the product right?"

Validation: Does the product meet the needs of the end user and the intended use? "Did we build the right product?"

Example: needle bore specification testing = Verification; whether clinical users can self-inject correctly = Validation (human factors study)

Key Tests a Sterile PFS Must Have

  • Leachables/extractables (L&E): Silicone oil from the glass syringe and additives from the elastomer plunger may migrate into the drug solution, so testing under stability conditions is required
  • Container closure integrity (CCI): Needle shield and plunger seal affect sterility assurance
  • Compatibility studies: The drug must not alter syringe performance (e.g., corroding the glass); the syringe must not alter drug properties (e.g., protein adsorption)
  • Human factors: Patient self-injection involves force required, grip design and needle guard operation

Risk Analysis in Practice: High-Risk Items for a Sterile PFS

Loss of Sterility is the most severe risk and requires:

  • Package Integrity Validation
  • Container closure integrity (CCI) testing, as required by 21 CFR 211.165
  • Transport validation (ISTA/ASTM standards)

Contamination of the drug by syringe materials: Purchasing controls plus incoming inspection (21 CFR 211.84) provide double protection.

CDMO Operational Implications: DHF Establishment Strategy

As the contract manufacturer, the CDMO needs to clarify "who is responsible for design controls":

  • If the customer (the brand holder) is the Owner, design controls are led by the customer
  • The CDMO is responsible for process validation documents (which can become part of the DHF)
  • The contract must clearly specify access rights to design data (for FDA inspection)

V.A.2(b) — Design Changes & Design History File (DHF)

iv. Design Changes

Manufacturer A must also have procedures in place to ensure that any changes to design requirements are identified, documented, validated and/or verified, reviewed, and approved prior to implementation.

Activities should include review of the original risk analysis, review and approval of the revised design inputs and outputs, and review and approval of the design change.

For example, before changing a material used in the syringe that comes into contact with the drug, Manufacturer A should conduct verification activities to ensure that no degradation of performance characteristics will occur before the expiration date for the combination product as a result of the change of materials. Likewise, any change to the formulation of the drug should include design control activities such as verification to confirm that the new formulation does not degrade performance of the syringe.

v. Design History File

The DHF may include references or point to information residing elsewhere so long as the reference or pointer is precise enough to allow the necessary information to be readily accessed as needed, including for inspections.

For example, elements of the syringe component manufacturer's design documentation may be relied upon to support the combination product DHF. Such information on the syringe may reside at Manufacturer B's (syringe component manufacturer) facility, so long as the necessary documentation can be accessed in a reasonable time during inspection of Manufacturer A.

Manufacturer A should ensure access to such documentation through supplier agreements with Manufacturer B under its purchasing controls (21 CFR 820.50). Documentation of development of the drug product that preceded the design effort to incorporate the drug product into a prefilled syringe may be referenced in the DHF and become an input to the combination product design effort (though such drug-only development is not itself subject to design controls).

Commentary: Design Changes and the DHF

Two-Way Impact of Design Changes

A design change to a PFS must be considered in two directions at once:

  • Syringe material change → drug: Is the new material compatible with the drug? Is stability affected? (This triggers the stability requirements of 21 CFR 211.137 and 211.167.)
  • Drug formulation change → syringe: Does the new formulation affect syringe performance (e.g., corrosiveness, or a viscosity change leading to insufficient injection force)?

For material or formulation changes, FDA allows manufacturers to leverage 211 stability data to help determine the scope of 820.30 verification activities.

DHF: Design Documents Can Be "Pointed To" Elsewhere

The DHF does not need to hold all documents in one place, but they must be retrievable within a reasonable time during an inspection. In practice:

  • Firm B's syringe design data can remain at Firm B, as long as the contract ensures they can be retrieved during an inspection
  • Drug development reports (NDA/ANDA data) can be cross-referenced as DHF inputs
  • However, "drug development itself" is not subject to design controls (because it is drug development, not device design)

CDMO Risk Alert: Supplier Agreement Terms

The CDMO must specify the following in the contract with the syringe component supplier:

  • Obligation to give prior notice of material changes (Change Notification)
  • Right to retrieve design documents during an inspection
  • Collaborative process for change evaluation

Without these terms, the DHF will be incomplete during an FDA inspection, which would result in a Form 483 observation.

V.A.2(c) — 820.50: Purchasing Controls

Manufacturer A is required to control its purchasing activities, including for the syringe components, in accordance with 21 CFR 820.50.

For example, if the syringe barrel and plunger material is critical to ensuring that there is no adverse reaction with the drug, Manufacturer A should structure purchasing agreements with Manufacturer B to ensure that Manufacturer A is notified of any changes to this material prior to implementation of the change.

Similarly, if Manufacturer A uses an outside facility for terminal sterilization of the prefilled syringe, Manufacturer A must also have appropriate controls over that sterilization service provider.

Note: Because the device constituent part (syringe) is also a container/closure for the drug, Manufacturer A must perform acceptance testing of the syringe components in accordance with 21 CFR 211.84.

Commentary: 820.50 Purchasing Controls

The Special Nature of PFS Purchasing Controls: Dual Identity

The syringe components in a PFS play a dual role:

  1. Device constituent part → 820.50 purchasing controls
  2. Container/closure for the drug → 211.84 incoming inspection

This means that the syringe barrel, plunger and needle shield must satisfy both the device purchasing control requirements and the drug component incoming inspection requirements.

Control Requirements for Outsourced Terminal Sterilization

If terminal sterilization is outsourced (e.g., EtO, gamma irradiation, e-beam), the sterilization service provider is also subject to 820.50 controls, which requires:

  • Supplier evaluation and approval procedures
  • Quality Agreement
  • Periodic supplier audits
  • Review and approval of sterilization batch records

211.84 vs. 820.50: The CDMO's Dual-Track Incoming Controls

Both apply to the syringe components of a PFS.

V.A.2(d) — 820.100: CAPA

Manufacturer A is required to establish and maintain CAPA procedures for the combination product. Following are two examples of issues that might arise and exemplary steps for addressing them:

Example 1 — Fill Volume Nonconformity

Manufacturer A has implemented in-process manufacturing verification procedures to confirm that the syringe is being filled with the correct amount of the drug, and the data from this verification are analyzed for potential nonconformities. Manufacturer A notes an increase in nonconformities relating to the volume of drug being put in the syringe and in turn opens a CAPA to investigate the problem. Upon investigation of the cause of the improper fill volume, Manufacturer A determines that maintenance procedures on the filling equipment are the cause of the incorrect fill volume. Manufacturer A updates the maintenance procedures and performs verification/validation testing to confirm that the changes correct the problem and do not cause new ones.

Example 2 — Sterile Package Damage

Manufacturer A begins receiving an increased number of customer complaints related to holes or other damage to the syringe's sterile package and opens a CAPA to investigate the issue. The CAPA reveals that Manufacturer B has made changes to a syringe component such that there are sharp edges that can damage the sterile pouch during shipping.

Manufacturer A works with Manufacturer B to eliminate the sharp edge or finds a new supplier. Manufacturer A also augments purchasing specifications and acceptance test steps to perform visual inspection of the syringe components. Manufacturer A repeats related design verification testing to ensure that the new syringe meets all design requirements and does not result in pouch damage during shipping.

Commentary: CAPA and PFS Combination Products

Special CAPA Requirements for Combination Products

820.100 CAPA is similar in logic to the existing change controls in 211 (e.g., 211.100 written procedures), but the device version of CAPA places more emphasis on:

  • Root cause analysis of systemic problems
  • Verification of CAPA effectiveness
  • Preventive actions across products and systems

Case 1 Analysis: Fill Volume Deviation

Root cause: A problem with the filling equipment maintenance procedure. It was not a failure of the equipment itself, but inadequate design of the maintenance SOP.

CAPA actions: Update the maintenance SOP, and verify/validate the filling accuracy after the equipment improvement.

This case stresses that CAPA is not just "fixing the equipment"; it must trace back to the systemic cause and prevent recurrence.

Case 2 Analysis: Sterile Package Breach

A complaint triggered a CAPA, and the investigation found that an unnotified change by the supplier (Firm B) caused a sharp-edge problem.

Multi-level CAPA actions:

  • Short term: Resolve the sharp-edge problem with Firm B, or replace the supplier
  • Medium term: Strengthen the incoming visual inspection standard
  • Long term: Repeat package design validation testing (the design control loop)

This shows the cross-system link of CAPA → purchasing controls → design controls.

Sterile Package Breach: Regulatory Focus

Sterile package integrity is critical to the Sterile Barrier System. A breach equals a potential loss of sterility assurance (Breach of Sterility Assurance) and may trigger a market recall. The best prevention is a robust CAPA system plus supplier change control.

V.A.2(e) — 820.170 Installation & 820.200 Servicing

Installation and servicing requirements would not apply to the prefilled syringe because the product does not require installation or servicing activities.

Commentary: 820.170 & 820.200 Not Applicable

Why Are Installation and Servicing Not Applicable to a PFS?

820.170 Installation: Applies to equipment-type devices that must be installed at the point of use (e.g., dialysis machines, imaging equipment). A PFS is a single-use product that the user takes out and injects directly, so no installation procedure is needed.

820.200 Servicing: Applies to reusable devices that require post-sale servicing and maintenance. A PFS is a single-use product that is discarded after use, so no servicing is needed.

Although these two provisions must be "listed" under the Drug CGMP-based approach, the manufacturer must document the reason they do not apply (N/A with justification) and may not leave them blank.

Section V.B: Drug-Coated Surgical Mesh

V.B.1 — Drug-Coated Mesh: Scenario Description

Manufacturer A plans to sell a synthetic surgical mesh coated with a drug. Manufacturer A has a marketing authorization to sell the uncoated mesh. Manufacturer A wants to coat the mesh with a drug to treat infection at the site of the product's implantation.

Manufacturer B has an approval to market a drug for local administration to treat infection at the site of implantation of this class of device, but not in a formulation suitable for coating onto the mesh. Manufacturer A has established a business relationship with Manufacturer B to use the drug and develop the data needed to support Manufacturer A's marketing authorization for the coated mesh.

Manufacturer B will manufacture the drug formulation for spraying onto the mesh, and Manufacturer A will manufacture the finished drug-coated mesh combination product.

V.B.2 — Compliance with QS Regulation Requirements

The coated mesh product is a single-entity combination product under 21 CFR 3.2(e)(1). Therefore, Manufacturer A is subject both to the drug CGMPs and the device QS regulation for this combination product.

Because Manufacturer A is already marketing the uncoated mesh, it has an existing device QS regulation-based operating system, and chooses to operate under a device QS regulation-based streamlined approach for the combination product.

This discussion focuses on design control and purchasing control considerations arising from inclusion of the drug constituent part in the combination product.

Commentary: Drug-Coated Mesh Scenario

Regulatory Structure of Scenario B

Firm A = surgical mesh manufacturer (device-led) → already has a Part 820 system → chooses the Device QS-based Streamlined Approach

Firm B = drug supplier (supplies the drug formulation for coating)

The point of Scenario B is not "which approach to choose" but how the Device QS-based approach handles design controls and purchasing controls once a drug constituent part is added.

Analogy: A Chef Adding a Medicinal Ingredient to an Existing Menu

Firm A (the device manufacturer) = a restaurant with a complete kitchen (the Part 820 system). It now wants to add a "medicated coating" to its signature dish (the surgical mesh). The whole kitchen system does not need to be rebuilt, but it requires:

  • Design controls must add input requirements for "drug dose and efficacy"
  • Purchasing controls must control the drug supplier (Firm B)

The 8 Drug CGMP Provisions to Add Under the Device QS-based Approach

In this scenario, Firm A uses the Device QS-based approach and must add:

  • 211.84: Testing of components/containers/closures
  • 211.103: Calculation of yield
  • 211.132: Tamper-evident packaging (applies to OTC; N/A in this case)
  • 211.137: Expiration dating
  • 211.165: Testing and release for distribution
  • 211.166: Stability testing
  • 211.167: Special testing requirements
  • 211.170: Reserve samples

V.B — 820.30: Design Controls for Drug-Coated Mesh

A focus of the design control process for the drug constituent part of the combination product is to ensure that the drug-coated mesh will be safe and effective for treating infection at the site of implantation.

Accordingly, if the necessary dose of the drug for effective treatment of infection is already known, it would be a design input; design outputs and validation and verification would need to be established to ensure that the required dose is provided when the drug elutes from the mesh.

Risk analysis must be conducted to identify any risks associated with the design, manufacturing, and use of the mesh. Design reviews should cover these and other related design considerations for the product, and personnel with expertise in both the drug- and device-specific issues should be included in these reviews.

i. Design Inputs and Outputs

ii. Design Verification and Validation

  • Clinical data must be developed to ensure that the drug, when combined with the mesh, is effective in treating infection and does not raise safety concerns (design validation)
  • Manufacturer A would have to verify that the mechanical properties of the coated mesh are such that the product can withstand the stresses anticipated during the surgical procedure and still perform as intended
  • Stability studies to ensure that the properties of the drug are not degraded over the expected shelf life. Design outputs include the labeled expiration date and storage conditions for the combination product

iii. Risk Analysis and Mitigation

Commentary: Design Controls for Drug-Coated Mesh

Key Challenges of Drug-Device Combined Design

Drug elution rate is the core design parameter:

  • Too fast → drug concentration exceeds limits, toxicity risk
  • Too slow → drug concentration insufficient, infection cannot be treated
  • Coating uniformity is the prerequisite for consistent elution rate

The relationship between these design inputs and outputs: efficacy requirement → coating thickness and uniformity specifications → production process parameters.

Cross-Disciplinary Requirements for Design Reviewers

FDA explicitly requires that design reviews include personnel with both drug and device expertise (as well as an individual who does not have direct responsibility for the design stage being reviewed).

This means a device manufacturer cannot conduct the review with a team of device engineers only; it must bring in drug scientists or hire consultants. Implication for a CDMO: the cross-functional Design Review Board must include clinical, pharmacology and medicinal chemistry representatives.

Dual Considerations in Stability Studies

Stability for a surgical mesh is not just about "drug shelf life"; it also includes:

  • Mesh material stability: Does the drug coating change the mechanical strength of the mesh?
  • Drug stability: Is drug content maintained under storage conditions? Are the degradation products safe?

This is a typical "interaction stability study": you cannot test only the drug or only the mesh; you must test the combined product as a whole.

Relationship Between the DHF and Marketing Application Data

Most of the design control activities (verification/validation data) for a drug-coated mesh also need to be submitted to FDA for premarket review. Firm A can:

  • Incorporate the premarket submission documents into the DHF by cross-reference
  • Use Firm B's existing approved data on the drug as a reference for design inputs (leveraging existing drug data)

V.B — 820.50: Purchasing Controls for Drug-Coated Mesh

Manufacturer A already has established procedures for controlling purchasing/supplier activities pursuant to 21 CFR 820.50. Under this provision, Manufacturer A must ensure that appropriate purchasing controls for Manufacturer B are established and maintained.

Purchasing controls (and acceptance activities under 21 CFR 211.84) with respect to the drug constituent part should focus on ensuring that Manufacturer B can supply the drug that meets the specifications that Manufacturer A has established during the design control process.

Manufacturer A should establish purchasing agreements with Manufacturer B to ensure that Manufacturer A is notified of any changes that may affect the performance of the combination product prior to Manufacturer B's implementation of the changes. The notifications should address issues including:

  • Changes in the drug specification
  • Changes in its components
  • Composition of drug coating materials
  • The drug manufacturing process or facility

Such proposed changes may require that Manufacturer A complete additional design verification and/or validation. For example, verification testing may be necessary to confirm that the purity and stability of the drug is maintained, pursuant to the requirements of 21 CFR 820.30(i).

Commentary: Purchasing Controls & Drug Supplier Management

Challenges for a Device Manufacturer Controlling a Drug Supplier

For a device manufacturer (Firm A), controlling the drug supplier (Firm B) means stepping outside the comfort zone of traditional device supply chain management:

  • Must understand drug specifications (API purity, strength) and the CGMP requirements for drug manufacturing
  • Purchasing agreements must include drug-specific change notification triggers (formulation, process, facility)
  • The 211.84 incoming testing requirements (including identity, purity and potency) must be built into the Part 820 system

Change Notification Obligation: Preventing "Hidden" Combination Product Problems

The quality of a drug-coated mesh depends not only on the manufacturer's own process but also on the drug quality from Firm B. If Firm B fails to give advance notice of a change in drug concentration or process, the elution rate of the combination product may shift silently without Firm A's knowledge.

This is why the "Prior Change Notification obligation" in the contract is important.

Section V.C: Drug-Eluting Stent (DES)

V.C.1 — DES: Scenario Description

In this scenario, Manufacturer A is the owner for a drug-eluting stent (DES) composed of a stent coated with a drug.

  • Manufacturer B — manufactures the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API or bulk drug substance)
  • Manufacturer C — manufactures a polymer with which the bulk drug substance will be combined for coating onto the stent
  • Manufacturer D — manufactures the materials used for the product's primary packaging

Manufacturer A purchases the bulk drug substance from Manufacturer B and the polymer from Manufacturer C, then formulates the drug coating solution and uses it to coat the stent in its own facility. At the same facility, Manufacturer A packages the DES using the primary packaging materials it purchases from Manufacturer D.

V.C.2 — Compliance with Drug CGMP Requirements

The DES is a single-entity combination product as defined in 21 CFR 3.2(e)(1) and, therefore, is subject to both the drug CGMPs and device QS regulation. As a device manufacturer, Manufacturer A already has a CGMP operating system designed to comply with the device QS regulation and has elected to establish a device QS regulation-based streamlined approach for the DES in accordance with 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2).

This example focuses on considerations for complying with the drug CGMP provisions specified in 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2).

Commentary: DES Scenario Structure

Device QS-based Approach: The 8 Drug CGMP Provisions to Add

  • 211.84: Testing of components/containers/closures
  • 211.103: Calculation of yield
  • 211.132: Tamper-evident packaging (N/A in this case; not OTC)
  • 211.137: Expiration dating
  • 211.165: Testing and release for distribution
  • 211.166: Stability testing
  • 211.167: Special testing requirements
  • 211.170: Reserve samples

CDMO Analogy: Challenges for a Device CDMO Taking On a DES

If a device CDMO takes on contract manufacturing of a DES, it must, on top of its existing Part 820 system, build an implementation system for the 8 supplemental drug CGMP provisions. This is a major expansion of a device manufacturer's compliance system and requires bringing in drug QA talent.

V.C — 211.84: Testing and Approval/Rejection of Components

Manufacturer A has controls in place to evaluate suppliers, contractors, and consultants based on their ability to meet quality and other specified requirements and to control incoming materials in accordance with 21 CFR 820.50 and 21 CFR 820.80. Under 21 CFR 4.4(b)(2), Manufacturer A must augment its existing CGMP operating system as needed to satisfy the requirements of 21 CFR 211.84 for the DES.

In accordance with 21 CFR 211.84, the bulk drug substance, polymer, and primary packaging materials supplied by manufacturers B, C, and D must be sampled, tested, or examined, as appropriate, by Manufacturer A to determine whether they should be approved or rejected for use.

  • The primary packaging materials should be visually inspected to ensure that the correct, specified materials are received from Manufacturer D
  • Each lot of bulk drug substance and polymer must be tested to verify conformity with written specifications for purity, quality, and strength
  • Due to the nature and intended use of sterile drug eluting stents, testing must include microbiological testing of the bulk drug substance and polymer

Under certain circumstances, Manufacturer A may rely on a report of analysis from a supplier in lieu of conducting some of its own testing, subject to conditions including: Manufacturer A is still responsible for the performance of at least one specific identity test upon receipt of each lot, and reliance on reports of analyses is contingent on Manufacturer A establishing the reliability of the supplier's analyses through appropriate validation of the test results at appropriate intervals.

V.C — 211.103: Calculation of Yield

Calculation of yield should be conducted during all appropriate steps in the manufacturing process prior to and after application of the coating to the stent, including the formulation of the coating. Manufacturer A is responsible for the calculation of yield at appropriate phases of manufacture at its facility, including application of the coating to the DES, and the packaging of the DES.

Drug-coated stents that are rejected due only to defects in the device constituent part (stent) will result in corresponding loss of drug constituent part from the batch/lot. This loss should be captured as part of the yield calculations for the drug constituent part and investigation of the cause of that loss should identify the manufacturing problem that is leading to these device constituent part nonconformities.

Commentary: 211.84 Incoming Testing & 211.103 Yield

Conditions for Substituting a Supplier CoA for In-House Testing

Cannot be substituted: Identity testing must be performed in-house on every lot, without exception.

Can be substituted (if the following conditions are met):

  • The supplier performed conformance testing before shipment
  • Firm A has validated the reliability of the supplier's test methods
  • The supplier's analytical results are periodically revalidated (at appropriate intervals)

211.103 Yield Calculation: A Unique Challenge for a DES

Yield calculation for a DES is particularly complex because:

  • Drug coating step: Drug content must be calculated before and after coating to confirm yield
  • Rejection of defective stents: If a stent itself is defective and rejected, the drug attached to it is lost as well. This drug loss must be counted in the yield, and the root cause of the stent defect rate must be investigated
  • Multi-step yield: Formulation preparation → coating → curing → packaging; every critical step must be recorded

This concept has no equivalent requirement in pure device manufacturing and is one of the most unfamiliar items for a device manufacturer adopting drug CGMP.

V.C — 211.137, 211.165, 211.166, 211.167, 211.170

211.132 — Tamper-Evident Packaging (OTC)

This regulatory requirement is not applicable to drug-eluting stents, as they are not OTC products.

211.137 — Expiration Dating

As the manufacturer of the combination product, Manufacturer A is responsible for establishing the expiration date on the labeling of the finished combination product. The expiration date must be established based on the data from the stability studies for the finished packaged DES and should also take into account other shelf-life considerations as required under design control. These considerations include the functionality of the stent and polymer and the integrity of the coating and the packaging.

211.165 — Testing and Release for Distribution

Manufacturer A must test each batch of the finished combination product to determine conformance with the final written specifications for the product. A general list of tests for drug-eluting stents:

  • Appearance & Identification
  • Assay & Content Uniformity
  • Impurities and Degradation Products
  • Drug Release Rate (immediate and/or extended release rates)
  • Package Integrity and Sterility Assurance
  • Endotoxins & Particulate Matter
  • Additional Testing (polymer molecular weight, residual monomers, catalysts, and other additives)

211.166 — Stability Testing

Stability testing for the final DES product should address: appearance, assay/drug content, impurities/degradation products, rate of drug release, particulate matter, sterility, and package integrity. Analytical procedures for stability testing should be fully validated as suitable to demonstrate stability.

211.167 — Special Testing Requirements

Manufacturer A must conduct or contract to conduct testing of the DES in accordance with 21 CFR 211.167(a) because this class of product is purported to be sterile and non-pyrogenic. In addition, testing in accordance with 21 CFR 211.167(c) must be conducted because the DES constitutes a controlled-release dosage form.

211.170 — Reserve Samples

Manufacturer A must maintain reserve samples representative of each lot of the active ingredient used in the combination product and of each lot or batch of the finished, packaged DES. Reserve samples must consist of at least twice the quantity necessary to perform all tests required for the active ingredient and the finished DES, excluding sterility and pyrogen testing.

  • Samples of the active ingredient must be kept for 1 year past the expiration date of the last lot of finished DES to use that lot of the active ingredient
  • Samples for each lot of the finished DES must be kept for 1 year after the expiration date for the combination product
  • If Manufacturer A maintains reserve samples in a third-party storage facility, Manufacturer A should ensure, via purchasing controls (21 CFR 820.50), that the facility can meet applicable CGMP requirements

Commentary: DES Drug CGMP Provisions

211.137 Expiration Dating: Multi-Dimensional Stability of a DES

Setting the expiration date for a DES must consider all factors that affect shelf life, not just drug content:

  • Drug assay over time
  • Release rate stability
  • Polymer integrity
  • Coating adhesion on the stent
  • Package seal integrity of the primary packaging

If different DES sizes share a similar design, FDA allows leveraging existing stability data, provided there is sound scientific justification and the data have been reviewed by FDA.

211.165: DES Release Testing List

Drug release rate is the most critical DES-specific test:

  • Immediate release vs. extended release: different designs call for different test methods
  • In vitro dissolution as a surrogate for batch release
  • Testing for polymer residual monomers and catalysts, to ensure polymer processing residues do not exceed limits

211.167 Special Testing: Dual Requirements for Sterility and Controlled Release

211.167(a): Sterile/pyrogen-free: Every DES lot must undergo sterility testing and endotoxin/pyrogen testing (or an equivalent sterility assurance procedure).

211.167(c): Controlled-release dosage forms: A drug-eluting stent is a controlled-release dosage form, so additional dissolution/release rate testing is required to confirm the drug is released at the intended rate.

Commentary: Strategy for Contacting FDA

Lead Center vs. OCP: Whom to Contact First?

FDA recommends contacting the Lead Center (the primary reviewing center) first:

  • CDER: Combination products led by drugs (PMOA is the drug), e.g., PFS, DES
  • CDRH: Combination products led by devices, e.g., surgical mesh, orthopedic implants
  • CBER: Combination products led by biological products, e.g., cell therapy devices

OCP (Office of Combination Products) = the office that handles disputes across centers and PMOA determinations (RFD: Request for Designation).

Analogy: The "Jurisdictional Court" for Combination Products

The Lead Center is like the court with jurisdiction: for general questions, go to that court (the Lead Center). If there is doubt about jurisdiction (PMOA unclear), go to the "supreme court" (OCP) for a ruling (RFD).

A CDMO should proactively submit an RFD in early development to confirm the Lead Center, to avoid heading in the wrong compliance direction later.

Commentary: Key Learning Points on Core Terms

A Memory Aid for the Three Types of Combination Products

Classified by packaging/combination method:

  • Single-entity: Physically/chemically combined → 1 NDC/PMA/510(k)
  • Co-packaged: Independent products in the same box → each has its own separate marketing approval
  • Cross-labeled: Packaged and sold separately, but labeling requires use together → each has its own approval but the labeling is linked

The CGMP requirement logic is the same for all three types, but for cross-labeled products, each manufacturer is responsible for CGMP only for the constituent part it manufactures.

Owner: Outsourcing Does Not Remove Responsibility

The Owner (marketing authorization holder) bears ultimate responsibility for CGMP compliance of the entire combination product, even when manufacturing is outsourced (e.g., to a CDMO for filling).

This means the CDMO's compliance status directly affects the brand holder's regulatory responsibility. The brand holder must ensure the CDMO meets requirements through a Quality Agreement and supplier audits.

Summary of Sections V–VIII

Section Summary — Key Takeaways

PFS (Section V.A): Key Points of the Drug CGMP-based Approach

  • A PFS is a single-entity combination product and must comply with both Drug CGMP and Device QS
  • Drug manufacturer/CDMO chooses Drug CGMP-based → adds 6 Device QS provisions: 820.20, 820.30, 820.50, 820.100, 820.170 (N/A), 820.200 (N/A)
  • Syringe components = device constituent part + drug container/closure → dual requirements of 820.50 + 211.84
  • Design control focus: L&E, CCI, drug-syringe compatibility, human factors
  • 820.170 and 820.200 do not apply to a single-use PFS, but the reason must be documented

Drug-Coated Mesh (Section V.B): Device QS-based Approach

  • The device manufacturer uses Part 820 as its core and adds 8 Drug CGMP provisions
  • Design controls must bring in drug science expertise (cross-disciplinary design review)
  • Drug elution rate and coating uniformity are the core design output specifications
  • Purchasing controls must control the drug supplier, including the prior change notification obligation

DES (Section V.C): Device QS-based + 8 Drug CGMP Provisions

  • The most complex combination product: 4 suppliers, multi-step process
  • 211.84: API, polymer and primary packaging are each tested; identity testing cannot be replaced by a CoA
  • 211.103 yield: drug loss from rejected defective stents must also be counted in the yield
  • 211.165 release testing must include drug elution rate testing
  • 211.170 reserve samples: drug substance (1 year after expiration of the last DES lot) + finished product (1 year after expiration)

CDMO Practical Decision Framework

Sterile Fill-Finish CDMO: PFS Compliance Strategy Choice

Conclusion: Choose the Drug CGMP-based Streamlined Approach.

Reasons:

  • The CDMO's core competence is drug manufacturing (21 CFR 211), and its existing system is Drug CGMP
  • Adding 6 Device QS provisions (mainly 820.20/820.30/820.50/820.100) is far more practical than rebuilding an entire Part 820 system
  • 820.170 and 820.200 do not apply to a PFS, so the actual supplemental workload is smaller
  • Most customers (brand holders) also have a CMC system led by Drug CGMP

CDMO Compliance Checklist for Taking On PFS Business

  • 820.20: Confirm quality policy, management representative appointment and management review procedures
  • 820.30: Establish the DHF for the PFS, including design inputs/outputs, V&V plan and risk analysis
  • 820.50: Syringe component supplier evaluation + purchasing agreement (change notification clause)
  • 211.84: Incoming testing procedures for syringe components (identity, purity, appearance)
  • 820.100: CAPA procedure covering scenarios specific to combination products
  • 820.170/820.200: Document that they do not apply, with justification
  • Quality agreement: Clearly divide design control responsibility with the customer (brand holder / Owner)

Final Summary: The Logic for Choosing Between the Two Approaches

The key to the choice is not "which is easier" but "which system do you already have?"
