0.1

What Are Combination Products?

Statutory definition, four product types, and real-world examples

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

Statutory Definition

Under FD&C Act §3(g) (21 U.S.C. §353(g)), the term "combination product" includes:

  • A product comprising any combination of a drug and a device
  • A product comprising a combination of a biological product and a device
  • A product comprising a combination of a drug and a biological product
  • A product comprising a combination of a drug, device, and a biological product

The product must have two or more regulated article types working together to achieve the product's intended use. The combination may be physical, chemical, or purely functional (cross-labeling).

Four Types of Combination Products

  1. Single-Entity Combination Product — Two or more regulated components physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or mixed and produced as a single entity. The components cannot be separated without affecting the product's function.
  2. Co-Packaged Combination Product — Two or more separate products packaged together in a single container or as a unit, where each component retains its own regulatory identity but is intended to be used together.
  3. Cross-Labeled Combination Product — Two or more separate products manufactured separately, distributed separately, but labeled for use together. The labeling of one product references the other as required for safe and effective use.
  4. Investigational Combination Product — Any of the above types when used under an Investigational New Drug (IND) application or Investigational Device Exemption (IDE). Regulatory requirements apply even in the investigational stage.

Real-World Examples

Commentary

Core Concept: The Four Types of Combination Products

A combination product is a product that combines the characteristics of two or more FDA regulatory categories (drug, device, biological product).

  • Single-entity: the two constituent parts are physically or chemically combined and cannot be separated. Examples: prefilled syringes, drug-eluting stents.
  • Co-packaged: the two constituent parts are manufactured separately but packaged together in one box and sold for use together. Example: a photosensitizer plus a light-delivery device for photodynamic therapy.
  • Cross-labeled: the two products are sold separately, but the labeling (instructions for use) specifies that they must be used with each other. Example: the labeling of some insulin products specifies use with a particular injection pen.
  • Investigational: any of the above types of combination product, when used at the clinical trial stage (IND/IDE), also falls into this category.

Analogy: A Combination Product Is Like a "Multi-Function Swiss Army Knife"

Imagine a Swiss Army knife with a blade (device) and a tube of emergency disinfectant (drug) built into the same handle. This knife:

  • The blade part must meet device safety standards (it must not cut the user)
  • The disinfectant part must meet drug safety standards (it must not be expired or contaminated)
  • The whole combination also needs someone responsible for coordinating the two sets of standards

21 CFR Part 4 is the rulebook that decides "who coordinates."

How to Determine the Primary Mode of Action (PMOA)

Primary Mode of Action (PMOA) is the key to deciding the Lead Center:

  • If the primary therapeutic action comes from the drug constituent part → CDER leads
  • If the primary therapeutic action comes from the biological product constituent part → CBER leads
  • If the primary therapeutic action comes from the device constituent part → CDRH leads

Example: the PMOA of a drug-eluting stent is the stent's physical function of holding the artery open (device); the drug only helps prevent restenosis, so CDRH leads.

The PMOA of a prefilled syringe is the drug itself; the syringe is only a delivery device, so CDER leads.

What If the PMOA Cannot Be Clearly Determined?

When the PMOA is unclear, FDA's OCP (Office of Combination Products) has the authority to assign the lead center. A manufacturer can formally ask FDA through a Request for Designation (RFD). Until OCP issues its determination, a manufacturer should not assume on its own that it only needs to comply with the requirements of a single center. This is one of the common regulatory pitfalls for CDMOs.

0.2

Why Part 4 Was Created

Historical context, regulatory gaps, and the creation of OCP

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

The Problem: Three Centers, One Product

Before Part 4, FDA had three separate centers with distinct regulatory standards, review timelines, and post-market requirements:

  • CDER (Center for Drug Evaluation and Research) — governed drugs under 21 CFR 210/211
  • CDRH (Center for Devices and Radiological Health) — governed medical devices under 21 CFR 820 and the Quality System Regulation
  • CBER (Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research) — governed biologics under 21 CFR 600–680

Combination products fell into regulatory gaps where no center had clear jurisdiction, or they faced duplicative review by multiple centers. This created:

  • Prolonged and unpredictable review timelines
  • Inconsistent CGMP requirements across similar products
  • Manufacturer confusion about which submission pathway to use (NDA, BLA, PMA, 510(k))
  • Post-market surveillance and adverse event reporting ambiguities

Legislative Solution

The Safe Medical Devices Act of 1990 created §3(g) of the FD&C Act, which required FDA to:

  • Designate a lead center (primary regulatory jurisdiction) for each combination product
  • Base the designation on the product's primary mode of action (PMOA)
  • Ensure consistency and predictability in the designation process

21 CFR Part 4 operationalizes §3(g) by establishing the regulatory procedures for designation, including the formal Request for Designation (RFD) process.

Office of Combination Products (OCP)

The Medical Device User Fee and Modernization Act (MDUFMA) of 2002 established the Office of Combination Products (OCP) within the FDA Commissioner's Office. OCP:

  • Manages the RFD process and issues binding jurisdictional determinations
  • Resolves inter-center disputes about jurisdiction
  • Develops and updates guidance on combination product regulation
  • Monitors combination product reviews to ensure timely, consistent decisions
  • Reports annually to Congress on combination product activities
"The purpose of Part 4 is to achieve timely and effective premarket review of combination products and to ensure consistent and appropriate post-market regulation of these products." — 21 CFR Part 4, Preamble
Commentary

Analogy: Three Departments, One Unauthorized Building

Imagine you have built a mixed-use commercial and residential building. The building authority regulates housing, the fire department regulates commercial use, and the health department regulates food service. Your building falls under all three departments, and the result is:

  • Each department says "this is not our responsibility", which creates a regulatory gap
  • Or each department asks you to submit similar information again, which means duplicate review

This is how FDA worked before 1990: combination products fell into the gaps between CDER, CDRH, and CBER. 21 CFR Part 4 came into being to designate the "lead department" and solve this problem.

Legislative Timeline

  • 1990: the Safe Medical Devices Act was passed; FD&C Act §3(g) requires FDA to assign a Lead Center
  • 1991: FDA issued internal assignment procedures (the Intercenter Agreement, ICA), but they lacked legal force
  • 2002: MDUFMA was passed and formally established the Office of Combination Products (OCP)
  • 2004: 21 CFR Part 4 Subpart A was formally published (designation procedures)
  • 2013: 21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B was formally published (CGMP requirements)

The Actual Role of OCP

OCP reports directly to the Office of the Commissioner at FDA rather than to any one product center. This position gives OCP:

  • Decision-making authority: when CDER and CDRH dispute jurisdiction over a product, OCP's determination is binding
  • Oversight authority: monitoring whether the centers' review of combination products is consistent and timely
  • Guidance-development authority: issuing guidance documents on the regulation of combination products

A manufacturer can apply to OCP for a formal jurisdictional center designation through a Request for Designation (RFD). The response period for an RFD is 60 days after receipt of a complete request.

ICA vs. Part 4

The 1991 Intercenter Agreement (ICA) was an internal administrative agreement among the three centers. It was not legally binding, and manufacturers could not rely on it as a definitive regulatory basis. 21 CFR Part 4 is a federal regulation (CFR) with the force of law, under which manufacturers can submit an RFD and obtain a legally binding designation decision.

0.3

Why Part 4 Matters for CDMOs

CGMP overlap, quality agreements, and the mAb autoinjector scenario

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

Four Critical Questions for CDMO Operations

A CDMO manufacturing a sterile injectable drug for a combination product must answer four questions before beginning manufacturing:

  1. Which FDA center leads regulation? — Determines which submission type applies (NDA vs. PMA vs. BLA), which division within FDA reviews the product, and which reviewers have authority over manufacturing-related questions.
  2. Which CGMP applies — or does dual CGMP apply? — Under 21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B (the 2013 CGMP rule), manufacturers of combination products may be subject to both 21 CFR Part 211 (drug CGMP) and 21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation for devices). The CDMO must know which applies to their specific operations.
  3. How do quality agreements allocate compliance responsibility? — The sponsor (drug/device brand owner) and CDMO must explicitly allocate who is responsible for CGMP compliance for each constituent part. Ambiguity in quality agreements is a direct FDA 483 observation risk.
  4. When do manufacturing changes require prior approval vs. notification? — A change to the drug formulation may require a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS), while the same change to device labeling may only require an Annual Report. The CDMO must follow the change control requirements of the lead center.

CDMO Scenario: mAb Autoinjector Sterile Fill-Finish

A CDMO performs sterile fill-finish for a monoclonal antibody (mAb) in a prefilled autoinjector pen. The product is a single-entity combination product: biologic drug (mAb) + device (autoinjector mechanism + prefilled syringe).

Common CDMO Trap

Assuming only drug CGMP (21 CFR 211) applies when manufacturing the drug constituent for a combination product. Under 21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B, if the CDMO also assembles or tests device constituent parts, the quality system must also satisfy relevant provisions of 21 CFR 820. This is frequently flagged in FDA inspections of CDMOs filling combination product presentations.

Commentary

The CDMO Trap: Following Only Drug CGMP

This is the most common regulatory misconception among CDMOs in the combination product field:

A CDMO that specializes in aseptic filling and has long been familiar only with 21 CFR 211 (drug CGMP) takes on a filling contract for a prefilled autoinjector. It often declares compliance with 211 only, overlooking that 21 CFR 820 (the device Quality System Regulation) also applies to the device constituent part.

During an FDA inspection, the investigator will ask directly: "Do you have a Design History File for the device constituent part? How does your QMS meet the design control requirements of Part 820?"

If the CDMO cannot answer, this becomes a Form 483 observation and, in serious cases, may escalate to a Warning Letter.

Practical Application: mAb Autoinjector CDMO Scenario

Scenario: A Taiwanese CDMO takes on a filling contract for an mAb (monoclonal antibody) from a large US pharmaceutical company. The product is a prefilled autoinjector pen, and aseptic filling and assembly must be completed in an ISO Class 5 environment.

Questions the CDMO must clarify:

  • Lead Center: the mAb is the PMOA, so CDER leads (after 2003, most mAbs were moved from CBER to CDER)
  • Applicable regulations: drug filling process → 21 CFR 211; autoinjector assembly and testing → 21 CFR 820
  • Quality agreement: who holds the DHF (Design History File) and the DHR (Device History Record)? Usually the sponsor (pharmaceutical or device company) holds them, but the CDMO provides the process data
  • Change control: a change in needle specification → requires the sponsor's BLA PAS process; the CDMO cannot make the change unilaterally

Practical conclusion: the CDMO's quality agreement must state clearly which party is responsible for each CGMP requirement, as well as the audit trail mechanism for the device constituent part.

Key Clauses of the Quality Agreement

In addition to the standard drug CGMP clauses, a Quality Agreement for a combination product must also include:

  • Clear designation of which party holds the device DHF (Design History File) and DHR (Device History Record)
  • Incoming acceptance criteria for device constituent parts (in line with 21 CFR 820.80)
  • The change control process for device constituent parts, including how to notify FDA and update the BLA/PMA/510(k)
  • Allocation of responsibility for reporting device-related adverse events (MDR)
  • The CDMO's statement of compliance with the requirements of 21 CFR 820 (or an explanation of any exemption from its scope)

Analogy: A Combination Product Is Like a Building Subject to Two Sets of Building Codes

Imagine a building used both as a residence (drug CGMP = 21 CFR 211) and as a commercial kitchen (device QSR = 21 CFR 820).

  • The residential part must meet residential building codes (fire protection, sound insulation, ventilation)
  • The commercial kitchen part must meet food safety regulations (stainless steel countertops, drainage system, temperature control)
  • The whole building needs one lead building authority (= the Lead Center designated by OCP) to coordinate

The CDMO is like the contractor of this building: it cannot know only the residential codes, nor only the commercial codes. It must meet both sets of standards and know clearly which set applies to each space (each process step).

Relationship to Other Regulations

Cross-reference map: Part 4 → Part 3 → CGMP regulations; what Part 4 does NOT govern

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

Cross-Reference Map

21 CFR Part 4 does not operate in isolation. It is part of a regulatory framework that must be read in conjunction with several other parts of the CFR:

What Part 4 Does NOT Govern

Part 4 is frequently misunderstood as covering the entire regulatory lifecycle of combination products. It does not:

  • Govern approval pathways — NDA (21 CFR Part 314), BLA (21 CFR Part 601), PMA (21 CFR Part 814), and 510(k) (21 CFR Part 807) are governed by their respective regulations. Part 4 only determines which center manages those pathways.
  • Establish submission formats — The Common Technical Document (CTD) format and electronic submission requirements are set by the lead center, not Part 4.
  • Define safety standards — Biocompatibility (ISO 10993), sterility, container-closure integrity, and other technical standards are governed by USP, ISO, and center-specific guidance, not Part 4.
  • Override other CFR requirements — Part 4 operates alongside existing requirements; it does not supersede 21 CFR 211, 820, or 600.

21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B (2013 CGMP Rule)

The most operationally significant addition to Part 4 was the 2013 final rule establishing Subpart B: Current Good Manufacturing Practice Requirements for Combination Products. Key provisions:

  • Manufacturers must comply with the CGMP requirements applicable to each constituent part
  • A manufacturer complying with one set of CGMP requirements (e.g., 21 CFR 820) may demonstrate compliance with certain overlapping requirements from another set (e.g., 21 CFR 211) using its own CGMP system
  • The rule provides a streamlined compliance pathway to reduce dual-CGMP burden while maintaining safety and quality
  • Applies to all combination products subject to FDA premarket review

What Part 4 Does Not Cover: Common Misconceptions

Many people mistakenly believe that 21 CFR Part 4 covers all regulatory matters for combination products. The following are what it does not cover:

  • Marketing authorization pathways: NDA (New Drug Application), BLA (Biologics License Application), PMA (Premarket Approval), and 510(k) (Premarket Notification) each have their own regulations; Part 4 only decides which center is responsible for the review
  • Application format: CTD (Common Technical Document) format and eCTD electronic submission requirements → determined by the lead center
  • Technical safety standards: biocompatibility (ISO 10993), sterility assurance, container closure integrity → determined by USP, ISO, and each center's guidance
  • Overriding other regulations: Part 4 operates in parallel with 211, 820, and 600s and does not replace them

2013 Subpart B: The Streamlined Path for Dual CGMP

Subpart B, added in 2013, is the most practically significant provision in Part 4. It solves a real-world problem:

The problem: the same facility, with the same quality management system, must comply with both 21 CFR 211 (drugs) and 21 CFR 820 (devices), and the two sets of regulations have many overlapping requirements worded differently. Do you have to write two sets of SOPs?

Subpart B's answer: no. If your QMS already complies with one set of CGMP and you can demonstrate how the corresponding requirements of the other set are satisfied within your existing system, you are considered compliant.

For example: a CAPA system that covers both 21 CFR 820.100 (CAPA) and 21 CFR 211.192 (production record review) can satisfy both with a single SOP, as long as the correspondence is clearly explained.

Summary

1. Definition and Types

A combination product as defined in FD&C Act §3(g) is any product that combines two or more regulatory categories: drug, device, and biological product. There are four operational types: single-entity, co-packaged, cross-labeled, and investigational.

2. Origin of Part 4

Part 4 assigns jurisdiction; it does not set marketing authorization pathways (NDA/BLA/PMA) or technical standards, nor does it replace other CFR requirements. It is a set of jurisdictional and procedural rules, not a manufacturing or submission standard.

5. Practical Takeaways for CDMOs

Before taking on a combination product contract, a CDMO must confirm:

  • The lead center designation status (or confirmation through an RFD)
  • The applicable CGMP regulations (211, 820, or both?)
  • A clear allocation of CGMP responsibilities in the quality agreement
  • The change control routing mechanism for device constituent parts

These four confirmations are the most basic defense against FDA Form 483 observations.

1.1 §4.1 — What Does This Part Cover?

Original Text

§4.1 — Scope.

(a) This subpart applies to combination products as defined in §3.2(e) of this chapter. It establishes, for purposes of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and the Public Health Service Act, which current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements in parts 4, 210, 211, 600 through 680, and 820 of this chapter apply to combination products, and provides for the assignment of combination products to a component of FDA.

(b) This subpart does not cover biological products that are regulated solely under the Public Health Service Act and that do not have a drug or device constituent part.

(c) Agreements and decisions made under this part regarding which CGMP requirements apply to a specific combination product are intended to promote the efficient and timely review of applications and the timely compliance of manufacturers with applicable CGMP requirements.

"This subpart applies to combination products as defined in §3.2(e) of this chapter."
— 21 CFR §4.1(a)

Key cross-references:

  • §3.2(e) — Definition of "combination product" (the operative definition)
  • Parts 210, 211 — Drug CGMPs (manufacturing and finished product)
  • Parts 600–680 — Biological product regulations
  • Part 820 — Device Quality System Regulation / QMSR
  • 21 CFR Part 3 — Product Jurisdiction (designation authority)

Tutorial Commentary

The Three Core Functions of §4.1

Part 4 does only three things, but each one is critical:

  1. Assignment of the lead center: determines which center (CDER, CDRH, or CBER) leads the review
  2. Clarification of CGMP applicability: confirms which CGMP (or which several together) a manufacturer must follow
  3. A framework for compliance efficiency: allows manufacturers to negotiate an integrated CGMP compliance path (see §4.4)

If your product is only a drug, only a device, or only a biological product, Part 4 does not apply to you. Part 4 exists specifically to resolve the regulatory dilemma of "cross-category" products.

Key Exclusion: Biological Products Regulated Solely Under the PHS Act

§4.1(b) provides that if a biological product is subject only to the PHS Act and contains no drug or device constituent part, Part 4 does not apply.

Practical meaning: most vaccines and blood products have drug characteristics themselves and are usually still subject to Part 4. Only a very small number of "purely biological" products (such as certain blood components) benefit from this exclusion.

Quick Check

In which of the following situations does Part 4 not apply?

  1. A cardiovascular catheter with a drug coating
  2. A prefilled insulin pen (the pen body is a device, the insulin is a drug)
  3. A blood product regulated only under the PHS Act and containing no drug or device constituent part
  4. A monoclonal antibody (mAb) used with an injection device

Answer: (3). The other three are typical combination products and are all subject to Part 4.

1.2 §4.2 — Key Definitions

Original Text

§4.2 — Definitions.

The following definitions apply to this subpart:

Combination product means a product comprised of any combination of a drug and a device; a biological product and a device; a drug and a biological product; or a drug, a device, and a biological product. A combination product includes: (1) A product comprised of two or more regulated components that are physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or mixed and produced as a single entity (single-entity combination product); (2) two or more separate products packaged together in a single package or as a unit and comprised of drug and device products, device and biological products, or biological and drug products (co-packaged combination product); (3) a drug, device, or biological product packaged separately that according to its investigational plan or proposed labeling is intended for use only with an approved individually specified drug, device, or biological product where both are required to achieve the intended use, indication, or effect and where upon approval of the proposed product the labeling of the approved product would need to be changed, e.g., to reflect a change in intended use, dosage form, strength, route of administration, or significant change in dose (cross-labeled combination product); (4) any investigational drug, device, or biological product packaged separately that according to its proposed labeling is for use only with another individually specified investigational drug, device, or biological product where both are required to achieve the intended use, indication, or effect. (See §3.2(e) of this chapter.)

Constituent part means a drug, device, or biological product that is part of a combination product.

Primary mode of action (PMOA) means the single mode of action of a combination product that provides the most important therapeutic action of the combination product. The most important therapeutic action is the mode of action expected to make the greatest contribution to the overall intended therapeutic effects of the combination product.

Lead center (also referred to as designated center) means the center within FDA that has primary jurisdiction for the premarket review and regulation of a combination product, as determined under §3.4 of this chapter.

Requesting party means any person who submits a request for designation (RFD) under §3.7 of this chapter or an agreement/determination request under this part.

Office of Combination Products (OCP) means the office within the Office of the Commissioner that administers the combination product designation provisions of section 503(g) of the act.

Tutorial Commentary

The Four Types of Combination Products (§3.2(e))

The most important thing in understanding combination products is knowing "which situations constitute a combination product":

  • Type 1: Single-entity: the drug and device are physically or chemically combined into one and cannot be used separately. Examples: drug-eluting stents (DES), antimicrobial-coated catheters
  • Type 2: Co-packaged: a separate drug and device are placed in the same package. Examples: an insulin kit that includes a syringe, a prefilled syringe set that includes needles
  • Type 3: Cross-labeled: packaged separately and sold separately, but the labeling specifies that they must be used with a particular other product, and the intended use cannot be achieved without the other product. Example: a targeted therapy that can be used only with a specific genetic test
  • Type 4: Investigational: in a clinical trial, separately packaged investigational drugs/devices/biological products that, under the trial protocol, must be used with another investigational product

Constituent Part vs. Component

Constituent part is a term specific to Part 4. It refers to the drug, device, or biological product "itself" that makes up the combination product.

Note: do not confuse it with "component" (a part or raw material) in 21 CFR Part 820.

  • The "constituent parts" of a prefilled syringe = the drug (API and drug product) + the syringe (device)
  • The "components" of the syringe = needle, plunger, glass barrel, and other parts

This distinction is very important during inspections: FDA investigators assess the CGMP compliance of each constituent part separately.

Primary Mode of Action (PMOA): The Key to Determining the Lead Center

PMOA determines which FDA center takes the lead (Lead Center):

  • PMOA = pharmacological action → CDER leads
  • PMOA = mechanical/physical action of a device → CDRH leads
  • PMOA = biological/immunological action → CBER leads

Difficulty: if the PMOA cannot be determined scientifically, OCP assigns the lead center under §3.4(b) using the "most stringent regulation" principle.

Analogy: PMOA Is Like the Main Function of a Vehicle

Imagine an "electric car with GPS navigation":

  • GPS is one function, but it is not the most important one: you buy a car for transportation, not for navigation
  • The most important function (PMOA) = the electric propulsion system
  • → So regulation should be led by the "transportation authority" (equivalent to CDRH or CDER), not by the "satellite communications authority"

Likewise for the PMOA of a drug-eluting stent: although it contains a drug, the most important therapeutic effect comes from physically propping the artery open (a device action) → CDRH leads.

Office of Combination Products (OCP)

OCP is located in the Office of the FDA Commissioner (not under CDER/CDRH/CBER) and is the arbitration center for combination product regulation:

  • Receives Request for Designation (RFD) submissions
  • Issues a designation decision within 60 days
  • Resolves disputes among the centers over jurisdiction
  • Oversees regulatory consistency across centers

Practical advice: if you are unsure whether a product is a combination product or which center should review it, submit an RFD to OCP first rather than sending the submission directly to the centers.

1.3 §4.3 — Which CGMP Regulations Apply?

Original Text

§4.3 — Applicable CGMP requirements.

(a) Combination products with a drug constituent part. The CGMP requirements of parts 210 and 211 of this chapter apply to combination products that include a drug constituent part.

(b) Combination products with a device constituent part. The CGMP requirements of part 820 of this chapter (quality system regulation) apply to combination products that include a device constituent part.

(c) Combination products with a biological product constituent part. The CGMP requirements of parts 600 through 680 of this chapter apply to combination products that include a biological product constituent part.

(d) Combination products with an HCT/P constituent part. The requirements of part 1271 of this chapter apply to combination products that include a human cell, tissue, or cellular or tissue-based product (HCT/P) constituent part.

(e) Single-entity and co-packaged combination products. For single-entity or co-packaged combination products, manufacturers must comply with the CGMP requirements applicable to each constituent part. The combination product is subject to all of those requirements unless a streamlined approach is agreed upon under §4.4.

Note on Streamlined Compliance (§4.4): A manufacturer of a single-entity or co-packaged combination product containing a device and either a drug or biological product may petition the lead center to demonstrate compliance using the primary constituent part's CGMP framework, provided the manufacturer can show that the other applicable CGMP requirements are also satisfied through that framework.

"For single-entity or co-packaged combination products, manufacturers must comply with the CGMP requirements applicable to each constituent part."
— 21 CFR §4.3(e)

Cross-labeled combination products: Cross-labeled combination products are subject to the CGMP requirements applicable to their individual constituent parts as regulated independently. Each constituent part must comply with its own applicable CGMP regime.

Tutorial Commentary

The Core Logic of §4.3: Constituent Parts Determine the Regulations

The logic of §4.3 is very straightforward:

  • Contains a drug constituent part → 21 CFR Parts 210 & 211 (drug CGMP)
  • Contains a device constituent part → 21 CFR Part 820 (QSR/QMSR)
  • Contains a biological product constituent part → 21 CFR Parts 600-680
  • Contains an HCT/P constituent part → 21 CFR Part 1271
  • Contains multiple constituent parts → all relevant CGMP apply at the same time

The most important thing to recognize: "the primary mode of action is the device" does not mean you only need to follow Part 820. As long as there is a drug constituent part, 21 CFR 211 must also be followed.

Common Pitfall: Is a Prefilled Syringe "Just a Drug"?

This is one of the most common misconceptions in the industry.

Constituent parts of a prefilled syringe:

  • Drug (API + formulation) → 21 CFR 211 must apply
  • The syringe itself (device) → 21 CFR 820 must also apply

Many QA staff at pharmaceutical companies are familiar only with Part 211 and mistakenly believe that complying with drug CGMP is enough. During an FDA inspection, investigators also assess Part 820 compliance for the device constituent part, including design controls, needle safety features (if applicable), and functional testing.

Summary of compliance requirements: a prefilled syringe manufacturer must hold both the drug NDA/ANDA and device QS/QMSR compliance documentation, or apply for an integrated compliance path under §4.4.

§4.4 Integrated CGMP Path (Streamlined Compliance)

§4.4 provides a "burden-reducing" compliance option: if a manufacturer can demonstrate that, by complying with the "primary" CGMP system, it has also substantially satisfied the requirements of the "secondary" CGMP system, it may use the integrated path.

Example: drug-eluting stent (PMOA = device) →

  • Primary: Part 820 (QMSR)
  • Applying for integration: under the Part 820 framework, demonstrate that the quality controls for the drug constituent part are equivalent to the Part 211 requirements

Note: the integrated path requires prior agreement from the Lead Center; it is not something the manufacturer decides unilaterally.

Application Scenario: CDMO Manufacturing a Prefilled mAb Autoinjector

Scenario: You are a CDMO commissioned by a client to produce an autoinjector containing an mAb (monoclonal antibody). The client holds the BLA, and you are responsible for filling, assembly, and final QC.

Constituent part analysis:

  • mAb = biological product → 21 CFR Parts 600s + 211
  • Autoinjector mechanism = device → 21 CFR Part 820

The CDMO's CGMP obligations (all three sets apply at the same time):

  • Part 211: filling environment control, batch records, release testing
  • Part 600s: requirements specific to biological products (potency testing, viral safety, etc.)
  • Part 820: syringe design verification, device functional testing, Device History Record (DHR)

Key contract points: the CDMO contract must state clearly which party (the CDMO or the pharmaceutical client) is responsible for the compliance documentation of each CGMP set, and each party's obligation to cooperate during an FDA inspection.

Decision Logic

Three Steps to Determine CGMP Applicability

  1. List all constituent parts: identify each drug, device, and biological product constituent part one by one
  2. Look up the corresponding CGMP in the table: map each constituent part to its CGMP regulation (drug → 211, device → 820, biological → 600s)

Analogy: CGMP Is Like Building Codes

Imagine a "mixed-use residential and commercial building":

  • Residential part → must meet residential building codes
  • Commercial part → must meet commercial building codes
  • Restaurant part → food hygiene regulations also apply

You cannot say "it is mainly residential, so the commercial codes do not matter"; every use has its own mandatory requirements. CGMP for combination products works the same way: each constituent part brings its own regulatory obligations.

Special Complexity of Gene Therapy

A viral vector for gene therapy (such as AAV) combined with a delivery device is the most complex type of combination product:

  • Viral vector = biological product (Parts 600s) + drug characteristics involving genetic manipulation (Parts 210/211)
  • Delivery device (such as an intracranial injection device) = Part 820
  • All three sets of CGMP apply

CBER is usually the lead center (the PMOA is the biological/gene therapy mechanism), but CDRH and CDER will both take part in the review. For this type of product, seeking guidance from OCP early is almost essential.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Cultural Differences Among the Three Centers

Understanding the "mindset" of each of the three centers helps explain why combination product regulation is so complex:

  • CDER's drug mindset: focuses on "chemical consistency of the composition": chemical purity, content, and impurities of each batch must be within specification, and the batch record is the core document
  • CDRH's device mindset: focuses on "safety and effectiveness of the design": has the product design been adequately validated? Is the manufacturing history of each device traceable? Is the CAPA system sound?
  • CBER's biological product mindset: focuses on "reliability and safety of biological activity": is potency consistent? How is viral safety ensured? Is immunogenicity as expected?

All three mindsets must coexist in a combination product, which is exactly what makes combination product regulation the most challenging.

MDR vs. FAERS vs. VAERS: Different Postmarket Reporting Systems

Postmarket adverse event reporting for a combination product depends on the Lead Center:

  • FAERS (FDA Adverse Event Reporting System): drug adverse events, submitted by healthcare professionals and manufacturers, using MedWatch Forms 3500/3500A
  • MDR (Medical Device Reporting): device adverse events, mandatory reporting, covering deaths (30 days), serious injuries (30 days), and malfunctions (30 days); the MAUDE database is publicly searchable
  • VAERS (Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System): vaccine adverse events, administered by CBER; it also accepts reports for some other biological products

For a combination product, the Lead Center determines the primary reporting system, but in practice reports may need to be submitted to several systems.

510(k) vs. PMA vs. NDA vs. BLA: Approval Pathways at a Glance

Device side (CDRH):

  • 510(k): Substantial Equivalence: Class II devices, citing a marketed "predicate device", typically 90-180 days
  • PMA: Premarket Approval: Class III high-risk devices, requiring full clinical data, typically 180 days or more
  • De Novo: novel moderate-risk devices with no predicate, establishing a new classification

Drug side (CDER):

  • NDA: New Drug Application: new drugs, requiring full Phase I/II/III data
  • ANDA: Abbreviated New Drug Application (generic drugs): cites the RLD and demonstrates bioequivalence
  • 505(b)(2): a "hybrid" NDA that relies on existing data, often used for combination products

Biological product side (CBER):

  • BLA: Biologics License Application: application for approval of a biological product; 351(a) is for the originator product and 351(k) is for biosimilars

Analogy: The Three Centers Are Like Three Different Government Departments

Imagine you have opened a "robotic pharmacy that dispenses pills from a vending machine":

  • The drug authority (CDER) wants to see: whether the pill ingredients are pure, the expiry dating, and the manufacturing records
  • The device authority (CDRH) wants to see: whether the robot's design is safe, whether the dispensing mechanism has been validated, and whether there is any risk of injury
  • The biological product authority (CBER), if the pill contains a protein biological product, also wants to see: potency and viral safety

The three departments have different concerns, but you must satisfy all of them. This is why combination product manufacturers must be fluent in all three CGMP "languages".

2.1 §4.5 + §3.4 — How Do You Know Which Center Regulates?

Original Text

§4.5 — Agreement, Determination, and Designation Procedures.

Under 21 CFR §3.4 and §4.5, a manufacturer or sponsor seeking to determine which FDA center has primary jurisdiction over a combination product has three procedural options:

Option 1 — Informal OCP Consultation (Non-binding)
A sponsor may contact OCP informally to discuss product classification questions. OCP staff will provide informal guidance based on precedent products and existing regulatory determinations. This path is fastest and costs no application time, but the response is not a formal designation and does not bind FDA.

Option 2 — Pre-RFD Meeting
Before filing a formal RFD, a sponsor may request a pre-RFD meeting with OCP to discuss the product's constituent parts, proposed PMOA, and likely center assignment. This meeting clarifies whether a formal RFD is warranted and helps the sponsor prepare a stronger submission. Pre-RFD meetings are especially useful when the PMOA is scientifically ambiguous.

Option 3 — Formal Request for Designation (RFD)
A sponsor files a written RFD with OCP under §3.7. FDA must respond within 60 calendar days. The designation issued is binding on FDA and establishes the lead center for the product's lifecycle. Filing an RFD is strongly recommended before submitting the first marketing application (IND, NDA, BLA, or PMA).

When to file formal RFD vs. proceed directly:

  • File formal RFD when: PMOA is unclear; no clear precedent product exists; the product spans two or more center jurisdictions; sponsor needs binding confirmation for regulatory strategy
  • Proceed directly to lead center when: prior OCP informal guidance is consistent; the product is nearly identical to a previously designated combination product; the primary constituent part classification is unambiguous

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: The Logic of Customs Import Classification

Imagine you want to import a "special pharmaceutical storage box with electronic temperature control" into the United States. Customs has to decide whether it is "electronic equipment" (3% duty), a "medical device" (duty-free) or "drug auxiliary packaging" (governed by yet another set of rules).

You have three routes:

  1. Informal consultation: You call a customs officer, who says, "I think it should be a medical device." But this is not a formal ruling, and a different officer tomorrow might give a different answer.
  2. Pre-application meeting: You ask a customs supervisor for a meeting to discuss the classification basis and confirm that the direction of your application documents is right.
  3. Formal ruling request: You submit a complete package, and customs issues a formal written ruling within 60 days. This is the legal basis you can actually rely on.

The RFD process follows the same logic: FDA decides which regulatory "bucket" your combination product falls into, and tells you in a formal written document.

Choosing Among the Three Routes

In practice, most experienced RA teams take a "step-up" approach:

  1. Start with an informal consultation to learn OCP's preliminary view
  2. If questions remain, request a Pre-RFD meeting to clarify how to prepare the documentation
  3. Finally, submit a formal RFD to obtain a legally binding designation

Key principle: once a formal RFD designation is obtained, it is binding throughout the product lifecycle, unless there is a significant change to the product's constituent parts or intended use.

Key Point: When an RFD Is a Must

A formal RFD is strongly recommended in the following situations; informal consultation alone is not enough:

  • The drug and device contributions of the combination product are nearly "evenly matched," making the PMOA hard to judge
  • CBER jurisdiction is involved (cell therapy, gene therapy); these products have the most complex regulatory requirements
  • A CDMO needs written evidence of the applicable CGMP scope before accepting a contract manufacturing assignment
  • The company plans to file in multiple markets at the same time and needs the FDA designation as a reference for other regulators
2.2 §3.7 — Request for Designation (RFD)

Original Text

§3.7 — Request for Designation.

(a) Any person may submit a request for designation (RFD) to the Office of Combination Products (OCP) to obtain a formal designation of the center that will have primary jurisdiction over a combination product.

When to File: An RFD should be filed early in product development — ideally before initiation of the first clinical study or before submission of the first marketing application. Filing early ensures that the correct center's requirements are applied throughout development and manufacturing scale-up.

FDA 60-Day Response Clock: Upon receipt of a complete RFD, FDA has 60 calendar days to issue a designation. If FDA fails to act within 60 days, the product is automatically assigned to the center that has primary jurisdiction over the most significant mode of action (§3.9(c)). The designation issued by OCP is binding on FDA.

Binding Effect: Once a designation is issued, the lead center identified in the designation is responsible for the premarket review and regulation of the combination product. All FDA centers must honor the designation. If the sponsor later seeks to change the designation, it must demonstrate a substantial change in the product's intended use, constituent parts, or mode of action.

Contact: combination@fda.hhs.gov
Submissions: Office of Combination Products, WO32-2340, 10903 New Hampshire Ave, Silver Spring, MD 20993

Tutorial Commentary

The Essence of an RFD: A "Birth Certificate" for Regulatory Identity

An RFD is like asking FDA to confirm your product's "regulatory identity." Once the designation is obtained:

  • The lead center is established: you know to whom to submit the IND/NDA/BLA/PMA
  • The applicable CGMP is established: you know which set of requirements the manufacturing site must meet
  • The review pathway is established: you know which review division's guidance documents are most relevant

Settling these three points early in development avoids costly rework and regulatory delays later caused by an unclear designation.

Key Point: The 60-Day Clock Cuts Both Ways

The 60-day clock has meaning for both sides:

  • For the requester: After a complete RFD is submitted, you do not have to wait indefinitely. If FDA has not responded after 60 days, the automatic designation rule applies.
  • For FDA: It is a mandatory response obligation, ensuring that OCP cannot delay its decision indefinitely.

Common misconception: The 60 days run from FDA's receipt of a "complete" RFD, not from the submission date. If FDA considers the RFD incomplete, it may issue a refuse-to-review notice and the clock stops.

Submitting a high-quality, complete RFD is therefore the key to getting the 60-day clock started on time.

Scope of an RFD's Binding Effect

An FDA designation is binding, but it has limits:

  • What it binds: which FDA center takes the lead, and which CGMP baseline applies
  • What it does not bind: the specific review decision (approval or rejection), or the technical conclusions of any individual review
  • Grounds for re-filing: significant changes to the constituent parts; a fundamental change in intended use; new scientific evidence showing that the PMOA determination was wrong
2.3 §3.7(a) — Required RFD Content

Original Text

§3.7(a) — Content of an RFD.

A complete RFD must contain the following seven elements. Failure to include any element may result in a Refuse to Review action, restarting the 60-day clock.

Element 1 — Product Description and Constituent Parts: A complete description of the combination product, including identification of each constituent part (drug, device, biological product), how they are physically or functionally combined, and the overall product configuration.

Element 2 — Regulatory Status of Each Constituent: For each constituent part, a statement of its current regulatory status — whether it is currently approved/cleared, under investigation, or not yet regulated — and any existing FDA regulatory history.

Element 3 — Proposed PMOA with Scientific Rationale: The sponsor's proposed primary mode of action, supported by a detailed scientific rationale explaining why the proposed PMOA provides the most important therapeutic action. This is the most technically demanding element.

Element 4 — Intended Use, Patient Population, Route of Administration: The intended clinical indication, target patient population, and route of administration (e.g., intravascular, subcutaneous, topical), as this directly affects which center has most relevant expertise.

Element 5 — Manufacturing Process Description: A description of the manufacturing process sufficient to understand how the constituent parts are combined, the order of manufacturing steps, and any relevant in-process controls. Not required to be a full batch record, but must be substantive enough to assess process complexity.

Element 6 — Packaging Description (for co-packaged products): If the combination product is co-packaged, a description of the packaging configuration, how the drug and device components are arranged, and how they are intended to be used together by the end user.

Element 7 — Prior FDA Interactions: A summary of any prior communications with FDA regarding this product, including meeting minutes, prior submissions, informal feedback from any FDA center, and any written FDA correspondence relevant to product classification or regulatory status.

Tutorial Commentary

Ranking the Seven Elements by Practical Importance

From FDA's review perspective, the seven elements do not carry equal weight:

  • Most critical (Element 3): The scientific rationale for the PMOA. This is the core basis for OCP's decision. The stronger the rationale, the more likely FDA is to accept your proposed designation.
  • High importance (Elements 1 + 2): The product description and the regulatory status of each constituent part. Without these, OCP cannot build a basic understanding of the product.
  • Medium importance (Elements 4 + 5): The description of intended use and the manufacturing process, which help OCP judge which center has the most relevant review expertise.
  • Situational elements (Elements 6 + 7): The packaging description (needed only for co-packaged products) and prior interactions (must be disclosed if any).

The Quality of Element 3 Decides Everything

Almost every RFD succeeds or fails on the persuasiveness of Element 3:

  • Cite published scientific literature to support the PMOA argument
  • Explain why the other modes of action are not primary
  • If both MOAs are strong, address the tie-breaker analysis up front (which center has more relevant review experience)
  • Both nonclinical and clinical data (if available) can be used to support the PMOA argument

A strong Element 3 can lead FDA to accept your recommendation directly; a weak Element 3 makes OCP spend time re-analyzing and may even overturn your recommendation.

Exercise: Matching RFD Elements

For an "orthopaedic fixation plate with antibiotic coating," try writing the key content points for each element:

  1. Element 1: Describe the product form: titanium plate (device) + vancomycin coating (drug)
  2. Element 2: Titanium plate: similar devices already have 510(k)s; vancomycin: approved drug (ANDA)
  3. Element 3 (critical): Fracture fixation is the primary therapeutic effect (mechanical support), and infection prevention by the antibiotic is a secondary effect → propose PMOA = mechanical (Device), Lead Center = CDRH
  4. Element 4: Intended use: fixation in fracture surgery; patient population: adult trauma patients; route: surgical implantation
  5. Element 5: Process: titanium plate machining → surface treatment → vancomycin dip coating → sterilization
  6. Element 6: Not co-packaged, so N/A (the bone plate and the coating are a single entity)
  7. Element 7: Attended a CDRH Pre-Sub meeting, where CDRH verbally indicated it was inclined to accept this product
2.4 §3.7(b) + §4.3 — PMOA Determination

Original Text

§3.7(b) — Primary Mode of Action (PMOA) Determination.

The primary mode of action (PMOA) is the single mode of action of a combination product that provides the most important therapeutic action of the combination product. The most important therapeutic action is the mode of action expected to make the greatest contribution to the overall intended therapeutic effects of the combination product. (21 CFR §3.2(m))

Five Mode of Action Categories:

  • Pharmacological — the product achieves its effect through chemical interaction with biological receptors or biochemical pathways → leads to CDER jurisdiction
  • Immunological — the product achieves its effect through stimulation, suppression, or modulation of the immune system → leads to CBER jurisdiction
  • Metabolic — the product achieves its effect by altering metabolic processes (e.g., gene therapy affecting cellular metabolism) → typically leads to CBER
  • Mechanical — the product achieves its effect through physical force, structural support, or mechanical function (e.g., stents, scaffolds, delivery devices) → leads to CDRH
  • Physical — the product achieves its effect through physical means other than mechanical force (e.g., thermal, electrical, photochemical) → leads to CDRH or CDER depending on specifics

3-Step Analytical Framework:

  1. Step 1 — List all MOAs: Enumerate every mode of action present in the combination product. Be comprehensive; include both primary and secondary effects of each constituent part.
  2. Step 2 — Determine which MOA provides the primary therapeutic effect: Which single MOA makes the greatest contribution to achieving the intended clinical outcome? Consider clinical evidence, mechanism studies, and the product's labeling claims.
  3. Step 3 — Apply tie-breaker if MOA is unclear (§3.8/§4.8(b)): If two MOAs are scientifically indistinguishable in their contribution to therapeutic effect, OCP applies a tie-breaker: (a) assign to the center with the most expertise in the predominant safety/effectiveness questions raised by the combination product; (b) if still tied, assign to the center with the most relevant regulatory experience with the most similar products.

PMOA Case Studies:

Tutorial Commentary

The PMOA Definition, Parsed Precisely

FDA's definition of PMOA has two core requirements, and both must be met:

  1. "Single": There is only one PMOA; you cannot say there are "two primary MOAs." The regulatory logic requires this, because a single lead center must be selected.
  2. "Most important therapeutic action": The yardstick is the "greatest contribution to the overall intended therapeutic effect," not "which MOA is more complex" or "which constituent part is more expensive."

Common mistake: inferring the PMOA from "the drug accounts for 80% of the cost" or "the device has the higher technical barrier." Neither is a correct criterion.

PMOA Analysis Logic for Four Cases

1. Drug-coated balloon:
Q: "Without the paclitaxel, would the balloon alone still have a therapeutic effect?" → A: Yes (mechanical dilation)
Q: "Without the balloon, would paclitaxel alone still have a therapeutic effect?" → A: No (it cannot reach the lesion)
Conclusion: Mechanical dilation is the most important effect → PMOA = Mechanical → CDRH

2. Prefilled syringe (small-molecule drug):
Q: "If the syringe is replaced with an ordinary syringe, does the drug's effect change?" → A: No
Conclusion: The drug is the therapeutic core and the syringe is only a delivery tool → PMOA = Pharmacological → CDER

3. Cell-therapy scaffold:
Q: "What is the scaffold's main function?" → A: To provide three-dimensional structural support for cell growth
Q: "Does the therapeutic effect come from the cells or the scaffold?" → A: The cells (biological function)
Conclusion: The living cells are the therapeutic core → PMOA = Immunological/Biological → CBER

4. Photodynamic therapy:
Q: "Can the light kill the tumor (without the drug)?" → A: No
Q: "Can the drug kill the tumor without the light?" → A: No
Difficulty: Neither works without the other; this is a classic tie-breaker situation
Practical conclusion: The light is a tool for activating the drug, and the main safety concern is drug toxicity → CDER usually takes the lead

What the Tie-Breaker Means in Practice

The tie-breaker rule (§3.8/§4.8(b)) is not used often in practice, but it is important to understand:

  • It is based on "who has the most relevant review expertise," not on a subjective sense of "which looks more like a drug"
  • OCP will look at how many similar products the center has reviewed in the past, and whether the center has accumulated deeper knowledge of this type of safety issue
  • What it means for the requester: if your PMOA argument is not strong enough, OCP will apply the tie-breaker, and the outcome may differ from what you expected. Strengthening the scientific rationale in Element 3 is therefore the best strategy.
2.5 §4.9 / §3.9 — Center Assignment & Consequences

Original Text

§3.9 / §4.9 — Designation of Center with Primary Jurisdiction.

Lead Center (Designated Center): The center identified in the RFD designation has primary jurisdiction over the combination product. The lead center's responsibilities include:

  • Primary reviewer of the first marketing application (IND/NDA for CDER; PMA/510(k) for CDRH; BLA for CBER)
  • Primary point of contact for all regulatory correspondence
  • Coordinates with consulting center(s) on cross-jurisdictional issues
  • Issues final decisions on approval, labeling, and post-market obligations

Consulting Center: The center(s) not designated as lead retain authority to review aspects of the combination product within their area of expertise. The consulting center:

  • Reviews its specific constituent part (e.g., CDRH reviews device constituent even if CDER leads)
  • Provides binding comments to lead center on safety/effectiveness questions within its expertise
  • May request additional data specific to its regulatory area
  • Does not issue independent approvals — all decisions flow through lead center

CGMP Trigger — Direct Link to §4.3: The center assignment directly determines which CGMP obligations apply:

  • CDER leads → 21 CFR Parts 210/211 are the primary CGMP; 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) applies to device constituent
  • CDRH leads → 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) is primary; 21 CFR Parts 210/211 apply to drug constituent
  • CBER leads → 21 CFR Parts 600–680 plus either 210/211 or 820 depending on constituent parts

Manufacturers may seek a streamlined CGMP approach under §4.4, but the center assignment established by RFD always sets the baseline obligations.

Tutorial Commentary

Lead Center vs. Consulting Center: The Division of Roles Explained

A common misunderstanding is: "I only need to follow the lead center's requirements; the consulting center's requirements can be ignored." This is wrong.

The correct understanding:

  • Lead Center = the main communication contact and final decision-maker, but this does not mean you only need to comply with its CGMP
  • Consulting Center = its input is binding, and the Lead Center must give it serious consideration
  • In practice, if CDER leads a combination product that contains a device constituent part, CDRH still has a say in the quality system requirements for the device constituent part

This is why §4.3 sets out dual CGMP obligations: not the Lead Center's CGMP, but the CGMP applicable to each constituent part.

CDMO Scenario: Confirming the Center Assignment Before Signing the Contract

Case: A client commissions a CDMO to manufacture a "drug-eluting stent (DES)."

Before signing the manufacturing services agreement (MSA), the CDMO must confirm:

  1. Center assignment status: Has the client obtained a formal RFD designation from OCP? (DES are usually led by CDRH)
  2. Applicable CGMP: CDRH leads → 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) is the primary CGMP; 21 CFR 210/211 apply to the drug component (sirolimus/paclitaxel coating)
  3. Quality agreement allocation: Which constituent part's CGMP compliance is the CDMO responsible for? The whole combination product, or only the device component?
  4. Consulting center obligations: CDER, as consulting center, has requirements for the drug coating. Does the CDMO's drug manufacturing facility comply with 21 CFR 211?

If the CDMO signs without confirming the above, it may face: a misjudged compliance scope → compliance costs exceeding budget; and Form FDA 483 observations during an FDA inspection because its systems do not meet the applicable CGMP.

2.6 RFD Timeline & Dispute Resolution

Original Text

RFD Timeline Overview:

Dispute Resolution — §3.10:

If a sponsor disagrees with OCP's designation, the following dispute resolution pathway applies:

  • Step 1 — Request for Reconsideration: Sponsor may submit a written request for reconsideration to OCP within 15 calendar days of receiving the designation. The request must identify specifically what is disputed and provide new scientific or regulatory arguments.
  • Step 2 — OCP Review: OCP reviews the reconsideration request and responds within 15 calendar days of receipt.
  • Step 3 — Appeal to Office of the Commissioner: If the sponsor remains dissatisfied, it may appeal to the Office of the Commissioner within 30 days of the OCP reconsideration decision. This is the final internal FDA level of review.

Note: While a dispute is pending, the original designation remains in effect unless OCP explicitly issues a stay. Sponsors may proceed with development under the original designation while pursuing dispute resolution.

Tutorial Commentary

The Practical Meaning of the RFD Timeline

Within the overall product development timeline, the 60-day RFD is relatively quick:

  • Compared with a full PMA review (180 days, usually extended), the certainty of 60 days is "cheap"
  • But to use the 60 days effectively, you need sufficient scientific data supporting the PMOA at the time of RFD submission
  • Submitting too early (no data supporting the PMOA) → OCP may request additional information → the clock stops → it ends up causing delay
  • Submitting too late (already close to the marketing application) → if the center assignment comes out unexpectedly, the entire application strategy may need to be readjusted

Recommended timing: after proof-of-concept (PoC) studies are complete and before entering Phase 1/First-in-Human.

Key Time Points in Dispute Resolution

There are three key time points, and missing any one of them forfeits a specific avenue of relief:

  • 15 days: Deadline for filing a Request for Reconsideration, counted from receipt of the designation
  • 15 days: Deadline for OCP to respond to the reconsideration request
  • 30 days: Deadline for appealing to the Office of the Commissioner, counted from receipt of OCP's reconsideration decision

Practical tip: while the dispute is pending, the original designation remains in effect. This means you cannot suspend compliance work on the grounds that "the matter is in dispute." Advance on both fronts: pursue the dispute and also carry out development work consistent with the original designation.

Comprehensive Exercise: Four PMOA Classification Exercises

For each of the following four products, determine the PMOA category and the recommended Lead Center:

  1. Heparin-coated vascular catheter
    Drug MOA: Heparin anticoagulation (pharmacological action)
    Device MOA: The catheter enters the vein and provides venous access (mechanical action)
    Q: What is the primary therapeutic purpose, venous access or anticoagulation?
    Analysis: Venous access is the primary clinical purpose, and the heparin coating is a secondary design feature to prevent catheter-related thrombosis → PMOA = mechanical → CDRH
  2. Insulin auto-injector pen
    Drug MOA: Insulin lowers blood glucose (pharmacological action, primary)
    Device MOA: Automatic injection mechanism (mechanical, delivery tool)
    Analysis: Changing the injection method does not affect the effect of insulin; the pen is only a delivery tool → PMOA = pharmacological → CDER
  3. Growth factor-eluting bone matrix
    Drug/biologic MOA: BMP-2 (bone morphogenetic protein) stimulates bone regeneration (biological action)
    Device MOA: The bone matrix provides structural support and osteoconduction (mechanical action)
    Q: Without BMP-2, could the bone matrix stimulate new bone formation? → Only osteoconduction; it cannot actively stimulate bone regeneration
    Analysis: The biological action of BMP-2 is the most important therapeutic effect → PMOA = biological/metabolic → CBER
  4. Contrast agent-impregnated absorbable suture
    Drug MOA: The contrast agent (iodine) makes the suture visible under imaging (radiographic action, secondary)
    Device MOA: The suture closes the tissue wound (mechanical action, primary)
    Analysis: The therapeutic purpose is wound closure; the imaging function is only an auxiliary diagnostic aid → PMOA = mechanical → CDRH
CDMO Scenario: Confirmation Checklist Before Taking On Drug-Eluting Stent Manufacturing

CDMO Scenario: Client Asks CDMO to Manufacture Drug-Eluting Stent — What Must CDMO Verify Before Signing?

Scenario: A CDMO's sales department receives a request for quotation. The client wants to outsource manufacturing of a drug-eluting stent (DES, a coronary stent with a sirolimus coating) to the CDMO. The sales manager asks: "This is similar to our existing device manufacturing capability. Can we just sign the contract?"

The answer is: no, not directly. Five things must be confirmed first:

  1. Confirm the RFD designation status
    Ask the client to provide the formal OCP designation document. If no RFD has been obtained yet, the CDMO needs to learn when the client plans to submit it, and should set a precondition in the contract that "manufacturing may start only after the RFD is obtained."
    Why: Without a formal designation, the CDMO cannot determine the lead center or the applicable CGMP scope.
  2. Confirm the Lead Center and the CGMP obligation matrix
    The PMOA of a DES is usually "Mechanical" → Lead Center = CDRH → primary CGMP = 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR). But the sirolimus coating is a drug component → 21 CFR Parts 210/211 also apply to the manufacture of the drug constituent part.
    The CDMO needs to confirm: does the site hold both a QMS that meets Part 820 and drug manufacturing capability that meets Part 211? Or only one of the two?
  3. Confirm the allocation of CGMP responsibilities in the quality agreement
    If the CDMO is responsible only for the device component (the metal stent scaffold) and the sirolimus coating is supplied by another supplier, the quality agreement must clearly define: who is responsible for Part 211 compliance for the drug constituent part?
    During an FDA inspection, CGMP compliance is assessed separately for each constituent part. Without a clear allocation, the CDMO may be held jointly responsible for matters outside its capability.
  4. Confirm the specific requirements of the Consulting Center
    Even if CDRH is the Lead Center, CDER, as Consulting Center, will still set requirements for the sirolimus coating's formulation, stability and dose uniformity. The CDMO needs to know whether these CDER requirements are already reflected in the client's IND/NDA plan, and whether any unresolved CDER comments could affect the manufacturing specifications.
  5. Confirm whether the CDMO facility is listed in the client's FDA submissions
    If the CDMO's site has not yet been registered in the client's PMA (Premarket Approval) or IDE (Investigational Device Exemption) application, any manufacturing change will require a PMA Supplement or an IDE amendment. The CDMO should specify in the contract which party is responsible for registering the manufacturing site with FDA, and who bears the regulatory fees for adding the site.

Conclusion: A DES is a typical product with "dual CGMP obligations." If the CDMO does not complete the above confirmations before the contract, the best case is compliance cost overruns; the worst case is a Warning Letter in an FDA inspection because the CGMP systems do not meet the applicable requirements, which in turn affects the client's marketing authorization. These five confirmations are the most basic due diligence for a CDMO's combination product business.

3.1 §4.4 — Combination Product CGMP Requirements: The Streamlined Approach

Regulatory Framework

§ 4.4 Current good manufacturing practice requirements.
21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B establishes CGMP requirements for combination products. The 2013 Final Rule (effective December 22, 2013) clarified how manufacturers demonstrate compliance when a combination product includes both drug and device constituents.

§4.4(a) — Default Dual Compliance Rule

The default rule is straightforward: a combination product must comply with the CGMP regulations applicable to each constituent part.

  • If the combination product includes a drug constituent part → must comply with 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211 (drug CGMP)
  • If the combination product includes a device constituent part → must comply with 21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation / QSR)
  • If the combination product includes a biological product constituent part → must also comply with 21 CFR Parts 600–680
  • If a combination product includes all three types → all three sets of CGMP requirements apply

§4.4(b) — The Streamlined Approach

The streamlined approach is the practical pathway for most manufacturers. Under §4.4(b), a manufacturer may comply with only one set of CGMP regulations — either Part 211 or Part 820 as the primary framework — provided the manufacturer demonstrates that the controls established under the primary CGMP framework satisfy the requirements of the other applicable CGMP regulation.

Key operational requirement: The manufacturer must document how each applicable requirement from the non-primary CGMP set is satisfied by the primary system. This documentation is the gap analysis crosswalk — FDA will review it during inspection.

§4.4(b) — What "Streamlined" Does NOT Mean

Critically, "streamlined" does not mean the manufacturer can ignore requirements that exist only in one regulation and not the other. If a requirement is unique to one regulation with no functional equivalent in the other, that unique requirement must still be implemented. The streamlined approach only allows avoiding literal duplication of procedures when the same outcome is already achieved through an existing system.

"A person who manufactures or processes a combination product may satisfy the current good manufacturing practice requirements applicable to the combination product by complying with either the drug current good manufacturing practice regulations in parts 210 and 211 of this chapter or the device quality system regulation in part 820 of this chapter, provided the person demonstrates that compliance with that regulation satisfies the requirements of the other applicable regulation(s)."
— 21 CFR §4.4(b), the streamlined compliance option

2013 Final Rule — FDA's Operational Guidance

The 2013 Final Rule clarified several key implementation questions:

  • FDA confirmed that the lead center's CGMP framework should typically serve as the primary CGMP system
  • The crosswalk documentation does not need to be a separate standalone document — it may be embedded in SOPs, quality agreements, or the Quality Manual
  • FDA's "Guidance for Industry and FDA Staff: Current Good Manufacturing Practice Requirements for Combination Products" (January 2017) provides detailed guidance on implementing the streamlined approach
  • FDA confirmed that both CDRH and CDER/CBER may conduct inspections of a single site manufacturing combination products

Tutorial Commentary

Dual CGMP: Three Scenarios

The best way to understand §4.4 is to distinguish three scenarios:

  1. Scenario A (most common): The lead center's CGMP framework is the primary system, with streamlined compliance: documentation shows that the other set of requirements is already satisfied by the existing system. Example: for a drug-led autoinjector, the primary system is Part 211, and a cross-reference document shows that the Part 820 requirements are met.
  2. Scenario B: Full dual systems: complete procedures for both Part 211 and Part 820 are maintained, with no integration. This is permissible but time-consuming, and suits sites whose lines make both pure drug products and pure device products.
  3. Scenario C (non-compliant): Only one system is run, with no cross-reference document and no assessment of whether the other set of requirements is met. FDA will issue a 483 directly during an inspection.

The vast majority of CDMOs choose Scenario A. The key is not "which set you use" but "whether you have the documentation."

Analogy: The Dual-Standard Factory

Imagine an automobile plant that must meet both crash safety standards (NHTSA) and emissions standards (EPA). The plant does not need two separate production lines: it has the same line, the same workers and the same quality system, but it must be able to show NHTSA how it meets the crash safety requirements and show EPA how it meets the emissions requirements.

The dual CGMP logic for combination products is exactly the same: a single quality system must be documented as meeting both Part 211 (the drug regulator's "crash safety standard") and Part 820 (the device regulator's "emissions standard").

Documentation is the heart of this analogy. The plant must be able to produce a clear written explanation, not merely claim verbally that "we do all of it."

Four-Step Framework for Executing the Streamlined Approach

  1. Step 1 — Determine the governing regulatory framework: Select the primary CGMP system based on the combination product's lead center (CDER/CBER → Part 211; CDRH → Part 820).
  2. Step 2 — Identify the "unique requirements": Compare the primary and secondary systems requirement by requirement, and find the requirements in the secondary system that the primary system lacks (e.g., CAPA under §820.100 of Part 820 is an explicit requirement; Part 211 has no corresponding provision, although the spirit of CAPA is implicit in it).
  3. Step 3 — Document the cross-reference (Gap Analysis Crosswalk): For each secondary-system requirement, explain which procedure, record or control in the existing primary system satisfies it. If it cannot be satisfied, add the corresponding control.
  4. Step 4 — Keep it current: Any change to a primary-system procedure must also be assessed for its impact on the secondary-system requirements, and the cross-reference document must be updated.

Common Misconception

Misconception: "We only need to comply with the lead center's requirements; the other set does not apply."

This is wrong. The default rule in §4.4(a) is that both sets must be met. The streamlined approach in §4.4(b) is an alternative that allows one system to fulfill both sets of requirements, but only if equivalence is documented. It does not mean the other set "does not apply"; it means "my system already satisfies it."

During an inspection, an FDA investigator will ask: "How does your system meet the §820.100 CAPA requirement?" If the site answers, "That is a device requirement; we are a drug manufacturer, so it does not apply," that alone constitutes a 483 observation.

Practical Meaning of the Table: CAPA Is the Integration Focus

For most CDMOs with a pharmaceutical background, the item in this table that needs the most attention is CAPA.

3.3 Post-Market Reporting Obligations — Dual Reporting Pathways

Post-Market Regulatory Framework

Adverse Event Reporting: Which System?

Post-market reporting for combination products follows the lead center assignment, but manufacturers must understand when both reporting systems apply simultaneously.

Drug-Primary Combination Products (CDER/CBER lead):

  • MedWatch / FAERS reporting under 21 CFR §310.305 (OTC drugs) and §314.81 (NDA holders) for adverse drug experiences (ADE)
  • 15-day expedited reports for serious unexpected adverse events; periodic reports for expected events
  • NDA holder bears primary reporting responsibility; CDMO must promptly notify NDA holder of any complaint or adverse event received

Device-Primary Combination Products (CDRH lead):

  • Medical Device Reporting (MDR) under 21 CFR Part 803 — mandatory reporting for device malfunctions, serious injuries, or deaths
  • 30-day reports for most MDR events; 5-day reports for events requiring remedial action to prevent unreasonable risk to public health
  • Device manufacturer (which may include the CDMO) is independently responsible for MDR reporting regardless of NDA/BLA holder reporting

When BOTH Systems Apply:

For certain combination products where the drug AND device aspects each contribute to an adverse event, reporting under BOTH MedWatch/FAERS and MDR Part 803 may be required. FDA's 2019 guidance "Submission of Quality Metrics Data" and the combination products post-market guidance address this dual reporting scenario.

Example: An autoinjector (drug-primary) fails to deliver the correct dose due to a device malfunction that also caused an injection site reaction classified as an adverse drug experience. The NDA holder must file an ADE report; the device manufacturer must evaluate MDR reportability independently.

Recalls

Recall procedures under 21 CFR Part 7 apply regardless of product type. The lead center coordinates the recall, but the recall strategy must address both the drug and device aspects of the combination product. Both CDER (or CBER) and CDRH may be involved in recall classification and strategy approval for combination products.

Post-Market Studies

Lead center requirements drive post-market study obligations:

  • Drug-primary combo: Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) may be required; post-marketing commitments under 21 CFR §314.81(b)(2)(vii) for NDA holders
  • Device-primary combo: Post-approval study (PAS) conditions may be imposed; post-market surveillance study under §522 of the FD&C Act for certain class II/III devices

Labeling Changes

Labeling changes follow the lead center's submission pathway: NDA supplement (Prior Approval Supplement or Changes Being Effected) for drug-primary; PMA supplement or 510(k) for device-primary. Changes affecting the device constituent of a drug-primary combo product may require CDRH consultation even if filed through CDER.

Tutorial Commentary

Adverse Event Clauses in the Quality Agreement

A combination product quality agreement must clearly specify:

  • Notification timeline: Within how many hours of becoming aware of a complaint or adverse event the CDMO must notify the NDA/PMA holder (typically 24–48 hours for serious events and 5 working days for general events)
  • Who assesses MDR eligibility: Does the CDMO or the NDA holder perform the initial MDR reportability assessment?
  • Who submits the MDR: Does the CDMO submit directly to CDRH, or does the NDA holder submit on a consolidated basis?
  • Information sharing: Is the NDA holder's ADE database shared with the CDMO? How does the CDMO learn of safety signals for marketed products?
  • FSCA (Field Safety Corrective Action) trigger: Who has the authority to decide to initiate a recall or an FSCA?

Post-Market Studies: Hidden CDMO Obligations

An NDA holder or PMA holder may include manufacturer-related obligations in its post-marketing commitments (PMCs), for example:

  • Providing specific batches for post-market stability studies (which involves the CDMO's sample retention obligations)
  • Supporting data submission for a PAS (Post-Approval Study) (which involves the CDMO's manufacturing data recording requirements)
  • §522 surveillance studies may require the CDMO to provide manufacturing records to support real-world data analysis

The quality agreement must clearly allocate these obligations; otherwise the CDMO may breach a PMC commitment without knowing it.

3.4 CDMO Responsibilities in Combination Product Manufacturing

CDMO Regulatory Scope

Scenario 1: CDMO as Drug Constituent Manufacturer

When a CDMO manufactures only the drug constituent part (e.g., API, drug formulation, or filled drug vials that will later be assembled with a device by another party):

  • 21 CFR Parts 210/211 apply to the drug manufacturing operations
  • If the CDMO's operations also involve contact with the device constituent (e.g., filling drug into a prefilled syringe barrel that is also a primary container/device component) → Part 820 requirements also apply to those operations
  • The CDMO is responsible for its portion of the BPR/DHR

Scenario 2: CDMO as Device Constituent Manufacturer

CDMOs providing contract sterilization, contract device assembly, or device testing services:

  • 21 CFR Part 820 applies to device manufacturing operations
  • If the device constituent has drug contact (e.g., drug-eluting stent assembly, drug-coated device), Part 211 also applies
  • Contract sterilization specifically: §820.75 process validation requirements for sterilization, plus Part 211 sterilization records if drug product is sterilized

Scenario 3: Split-Site Manufacturing

The most complex scenario for combination products involves multiple manufacturing sites:

  • Site A (Drug Constituent): Drug formulation and fill-finish → Part 211 applies
  • Site B (Device Constituent): Device component fabrication/sterilization → Part 820 applies
  • Site C (Final Assembly/Labeling): Assembly of drug + device constituent → BOTH Part 211 and Part 820 apply; this site bears dual CGMP compliance responsibility for the integrated product

The NDA/PMA/510(k) holder must ensure all three sites are in compliance; the registration and listing obligations under 21 CFR Part 807 and Part 207 must be fulfilled by each site.

Quality Agreement — Essential Content for Combination Products

Quality agreements for combination product manufacturing must address the following elements that are unique to the combination product context:

  1. Constituent part scope: Which constituent parts does each party manufacture? What are the exact physical boundaries of each party's manufacturing responsibility?
  2. Record ownership: Who maintains which portions of the BPR/DHR? How are records consolidated before batch release?
  3. Lead center interface: Which party holds the regulatory submissions? Who is the primary contact with CDER vs. CDRH?
  4. Adverse event reporting: Who reports to which center? Within what timeframe must CDMO notify the client?
  5. Change management authority: Which party holds approval authority for manufacturing changes? Which changes require prior FDA notification (and to which center)?
  6. CGMP compliance scope: Which set(s) of CGMP regulations does the CDMO implement for this product? Who maintains the crosswalk documentation?
  7. Inspection support: If FDA inspects the CDMO related to a combination product, who provides regulatory support? How are inspection findings communicated to the client?
  8. Right to audit: Frequency and scope of client audits of CDMO's combination product manufacturing operations

Tutorial Commentary

The CDMO Trap — "We Only Do Fill-Finish"

The most common CDMO compliance misunderstanding:

"We are only responsible for drug fill-finish; device CGMP does not apply to us."

This is wrong, and especially dangerous in the following situations:

  • Prefilled syringe filling: The syringe barrel is both a drug container and a device component. What the CDMO handles during filling is a device part, so the Part 820 requirements (especially §820.60 identification, §820.80 acceptance activities and §820.86 acceptance status) apply to the management of the syringe barrel.
  • Autoinjector assembly: Even if the drug has already been filled by another manufacturer, if the CDMO is responsible for final assembly (assembling the drug vial + autoinjector body), its operations have crossed into Part 820 jurisdiction.
  • Drug-eluting patch systems: The backing of a drug-eluting patch is a device constituent part. If the CDMO takes part in the process that integrates the backing with the drug, Part 820 applies.

Test: As long as the CDMO's manufacturing operation "touches, manipulates or integrates" a device constituent part, the relevant Part 820 requirements apply to that operation, regardless of whether its primary business is positioned as a pharmaceutical or non-pharmaceutical manufacturer.

Quality Agreement: The Importance of Change Management Clauses

The item most easily overlooked in a combination product quality agreement is the allocation of change management. A common dispute scenario:

Scenario: The CDMO decides on its own to change the material of a product-contact part of its filling equipment from 316L stainless steel to Hastelloy C-276 (for supply chain reasons). The CDMO considers this only an equipment material change, within its own manufacturing discretion, and does not notify the client.

Question: For a drug-led combination product, this material change:

  • May trigger an equipment change assessment under §211.68
  • May require reassessment of the E&L study (extractables of container/equipment contact materials)
  • May trigger NDA CMC reporting (depending on the level of detail of the equipment description originally submitted)
  • If a device constituent part is also involved (such as a syringe), may also require a §820.30 design change assessment

The quality agreement should clearly specify: all changes to contact materials, however "minor," must be reported to the NDA/PMA holder, who then assesses whether a regulatory pathway is triggered.

Multi-Site Compliance Coordination

The biggest challenge of split-site manufacturing is the integrity of batch records.

FDA requires batch records that can trace each constituent part of the combination product to its manufacturing site. When the drug (Site A), the device (Site B) and final assembly (Site C) belong to different companies, the responsibility for collecting, reconciling and retaining batch records must be clearly specified in the quality agreement:

  • When, and in what format, are copies of Site A's BPR sent to Site C?
  • When, and in what format, are copies of Site B's DHR sent to Site C?
  • Is Site C's quality head qualified (and authorized) to review the records of Site A and Site B?
  • Who is responsible for batch release: Site C's QP/QU, or the NDA holder's QU?

3.5 FDA Inspection Implications for Combination Products

Inspection Framework

Lead Center Coordination

For combination product manufacturing sites, the lead center assigned to the combination product typically coordinates inspection scheduling. However, the FDA's Office of Combination Products (OCP) facilitates cross-center inspections when needed.

Dual-Center Inspections

Both CDER and CDRH may inspect a single combination product manufacturing site:

  • CDER investigators focus on drug CGMP compliance (Part 211): batch records, laboratory controls, process validation, complaint handling (ADE focus)
  • CDRH investigators focus on device QSR compliance (Part 820): design controls, DHR, §820.100 CAPA, MDR procedures
  • Both center investigators may be present simultaneously, or may conduct separate inspections
  • FDA expects the manufacturer to demonstrate compliance with both sets of requirements regardless of which investigator is present

OAI — Official Action Indicated

A critical consequence unique to combination product sites: an Official Action Indicated (OAI) classification by either center can block product approval or trigger enforcement action for the combination product as a whole.

  • OAI from CDER → may result in application hold or Warning Letter citing Part 211 deficiencies → blocks drug approval pathway
  • OAI from CDRH → may result in PMA hold or 510(k) refuse-to-accept → blocks device approval pathway
  • For the combination product: either OAI alone may be sufficient to prevent market authorization

Inspection Focus — What FDA Checks for Combination Products

Based on FDA Warning Letters and 483 observations specific to combination products, inspectors focus on:

  1. Is dual CGMP compliance documented? The crosswalk between Part 211 and Part 820 — does it exist? Is it current? Does it address actual operations?
  2. Are complaint handling procedures dual-capable? Does the SOP include both ADE and MDR evaluation steps?
  3. Is CAPA implemented per §820.100? Even for drug-primary combo products, FDA expects a formal CAPA system.
  4. Are DHR and BPR properly integrated? Can the investigator trace a specific unit of combination product through both sets of records?
  5. Are all manufacturing sites registered? Site A, B, and C must each comply with applicable registration/listing requirements.
  6. Are design controls applied? For device constituents: §820.30 design history file (DHF) must be maintained even if the device constituent is purchased.

Tutorial Commentary

Preparing for Dual-Center Inspection

The biggest inspection challenge for combination product manufacturers is: one issue, two ways of asking.

Example: on change management, a CDER investigator will ask, "Was an NDA supplement submitted for this manufacturing change?" A CDRH investigator will ask, "Did this design change go through design verification/validation under §820.30(i), with design review records?"

QA staff must be able to answer the same operational decision from both angles. This needs to be emphasized in internal communication and training: drug QA staff need to understand the language of the device QSR, and device QA staff need to understand the language of drug CGMP.

The OAI Cascade Effect

For a CDMO, the cascade effect of an OAI is more serious than for a single drug or device manufacturer:

  • A CDMO's own OAI does not affect just one client; it may affect every client whose combination products are made at that site
  • If an OAI leads to the site being placed on Import Alert, all products made at the site (including drug or device products that are not combination products) may be affected
  • The client's (NDA/PMA holder's) approval process may be put fully on hold because of the CDMO's OAI
  • The CDMO must specify in the quality agreement that if it receives a Form 483 or Warning Letter from any center, it must notify the client within a set number of working days and provide the full text of the observations

High-Frequency 483 Observations for Combo Products

Based on publicly available FDA 483 observations and warning letters, the issues most frequently cited for combination product manufacturers are:

  • The complaint system does not include an MDR reportability assessment step (§820.198 + Part 803)
  • The BPR does not integrate the required DHR elements (§820.184), leaving device constituent part traceability records missing
  • No explicit §820.100 CAPA procedure (relying on deviation management as an implicit substitute for CAPA)
  • Process validation does not consider the §820.75 requirements for device constituent parts
  • The dual CGMP cross-reference document does not exist or is out of date
  • Supplier management does not include the §820.50 approval assessment for device constituent part suppliers (only §211.84 incoming testing records)

Full CDMO Application Scenario

Scenario: mAb autoinjector, with the CDMO responsible for filling and visual inspection

Product: A drug-led combination product (CDER lead). The mAb drug solution is filled into prefilled syringes, which are then assembled into the autoinjector body.

CDMO scope of work: Receive pre-sterilized syringe barrels (device component, supplied by Device CDMO B) → fill the mAb drug solution → perform container closure integrity testing (CCIT) → visual inspection (syringe + drug solution) → refrigerated storage pending handover to the client for autoinjector assembly.

Applicable CGMP analysis:

  • Part 211 applies: mAb drug solution filling (§211.113 aseptic filling), environmental monitoring (§211.113(b)), BPR (§211.188), complaints (§211.198, ADE assessment), incoming testing (§211.84, for the API and the syringe barrels)
  • Part 820 applies (because device constituent parts are handled): receiving inspection of syringe barrels (§820.80 acceptance activities), identification of syringe barrels (§820.60), CCIT as finished product testing (§820.80(d)), CAPA (§820.100)
  • The cross-reference must explain: §820.184 DHR → the CDMO's BPR should include the syringe barrel lot number (device component lot) as a DHR element; §820.198 complaints → the CDMO's complaint SOP needs an added MDR assessment step

Key quality agreement clauses: the CoA and DHF for the syringe barrels are provided by Device CDMO B; how the DHR elements in the CDMO's BPR are passed to the final assembler; the mAb CDMO's responsibility for MDR assessment when any CCIT failure is found; any 483 observation must be notified to the NDA holder within 3 working days.

Section 3 Summary

Key Takeaways

  • The essence of dual CGMP is documented equivalence, not double the workload. The streamlined approach in §4.4(b) allows one system to satisfy both sets of requirements, but only with a clear cross-reference document (Gap Analysis Crosswalk), which is the core exhibit during an FDA inspection.
  • Each of the six core areas has its own integration points. CAPA (§820.100) is the explicit requirement that CDMOs from a pharmaceutical background most need to add; BPR/DHR integration is the key challenge in record management; change control must assess the triggers under both Part 211 and Part 820.
  • Post-market reporting is a dual-track system. ADE reports follow the FAERS/CDER pathway; MDRs follow the CDRH/Part 803 pathway. As a manufacturer, the CDMO has an independent obligation to assess MDRs and cannot rely on the NDA holder's ADE report as a substitute. The adverse event reporting allocation clauses in the quality agreement must not be omitted.
  • A CDMO's responsibility boundary is determined by actual operations, not by self-positioning. Whenever an operation touches a device constituent part, Part 820 applies to that operation. "We only do drug filling" is not a reason to be exempt from device CGMP, especially in prefilled syringe, drug-eluting patch or autoinjector assembly scenarios.
  • Split-site manufacturing requires an explicit batch record integration agreement. The biggest compliance risk of multi-site combination product manufacturing is fragmented batch records. The quality agreement must specify each site's record format, transfer timelines, integration responsibility and the party authorized to release the final batch.
  • An OAI from either center affects market access for the entire combination product. For a CDMO, this means maintaining dual-center compliance is as commercially important as it is a regulatory matter: an OAI at one site can interrupt product supply for multiple clients, with serious commercial consequences.
