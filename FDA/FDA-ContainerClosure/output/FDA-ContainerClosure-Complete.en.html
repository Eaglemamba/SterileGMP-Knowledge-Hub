FDA Guidance — Container Closure Systems for Packaging Human Drugs and Biologics

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the regulatory status and scope of this FDA guidance and how it differs from the 1987 guidance it replaced
  • Master the core definition of the container closure system (CCS), including primary, secondary and associated components
  • Become familiar with the regulatory framework for containers and closures under CGMP (21 CFR 211), USP and CPSC
  • Understand how packaging submission requirements differ across the IND phases, and for contract packagers and repackagers
I. Introduction

Original Text

This document is intended to provide guidance on general principles for submitting information on packaging materials used for human drugs and biologics. This guidance supersedes the FDA Guideline for Submitting Documentation for Packaging for Human Drugs and Biologics, issued in February 1987 and the packaging policy statement issued in a letter to industry dated June 30, 1995 from the Office of Generic Drugs. This guidance is not intended to describe the information that should be provided about packaging operations associated with drug product manufacture.

Approaches which differ from those described in this guidance may be followed, but the applicant is encouraged to discuss significant variations in advance with the appropriate CDER chemistry review staff or CBER review staff. This is to prevent applicants or sponsors from spending unnecessary time and effort in preparing a submission that the FDA may later determine to be unacceptable.

"This guidance document represents the Agency's current thinking on container closure systems for the packaging of human drugs and biological products. It does not create or confer any rights for or on any person and does not operate to bind FDA or the public."

In general, this guidance does not suggest specific test methods and acceptance criteria (except for references to The United States Pharmacopeia methods), nor does it suggest comprehensive lists of tests. These details should be determined based on good scientific principles for each specific container closure system for particular drug product formulations, dosage forms, and routes of administration. Acceptance criteria should be based on actual data for particular packaging components and container closure systems, and they should be set to ensure batch-to-batch uniformity of packaging components.

As used in this guidance, the terms drug and drug product include biologics unless otherwise noted.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Container Closure System (CCS): The sum of all packaging components that together contain and protect the dosage form. It is the core unit FDA reviews when assessing packaging information: not the bottle or the cap alone, but the whole system.

FDA Guidance: Its regulatory status is that of a non-binding guidance document, representing FDA's current thinking. Companies may use alternative approaches, provided they satisfy the regulatory requirements.

Analogy

Think of the container closure system as choosing tableware for a restaurant. It is not enough to ask whether the plate is good; you also need to know whether the lid seals tightly, whether the material could react with the food, and whether the spoon could release harmful substances. It is the whole set that determines food safety. In the same way, FDA looks at the impact of the entire packaging system on drug product quality, not at any single part.

Key Notes

This guidance replaces the 1987 guidance and the 1995 OGD packaging policy statement. For a CDMO, this means every packaging submission strategy should be based on this document rather than citing the outdated earlier version.

FDA states explicitly that the guidance does "not recommend specific test methods and acceptance criteria". These are left to the applicant to establish on scientific principles, but this also gives reviewers considerable discretion.

Practical Application

As a sterile fill-finish CDMO, when a client submits the packaging plan for a new product, always confirm that the CCS information meets the requirements of this guidance. If the client follows the format of the 1987 guidance, proactively remind them to update to the 1999 standard. At the pre-submission meeting, discuss with CDER/CBER any approach that deviates from this guidance, to avoid review delays later.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does FDA take the "container closure system" rather than the individual component as its unit of review? How does this affect the way a CDMO prepares its submission documents?
  2. This guidance does not recommend specific test methods. Is that an advantage or a challenge for a CDMO's quality unit, and why?
II. Background

Original Text

The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (the Act) mandates the need for adequate information related to packaging materials. Section 501(a)(3) of the Act states that a drug is deemed to be adulterated "if its container is composed, in whole or in part, of any poisonous or deleterious substance which may render the contents injurious to health...." In addition, section 502 of the Act states that a drug is considered misbranded if there are packaging omissions. Also, section 505 of the Act requires a full description of the methods used in, and the facilities and controls used for, the packaging of drugs (see Attachment A).

Section 505(b)(1)(D) of the Act states that an application shall include a full description of the methods used in, the manufacturing, processing and packing of such drug. This includes facilities and controls used in the packaging a drug product.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Adulterated: If the container is composed of any poisonous or deleterious substance that may render the contents injurious to health, the drug is adulterated (FD&C Act Section 501(a)(3)).

Misbranded: If the packaging is omitted or misleading in design, the drug is misbranded (FD&C Act Section 502).

Key Notes

The FD&C Act provides three statutory bases for packaging requirements: Section 501 (adulteration), Section 502 (misbranding) and Section 505 (new drug applications). This means packaging is not only a quality issue but is directly tied to regulatory compliance. If a container leaches harmful substances, the product can be deemed adulterated and removed from the market.

II.A. Definitions

Original Text

Materials of construction refer to the substances (e.g., glass, high density polyethylene (HDPE) resin, metal) used to manufacture a packaging component.

A packaging component means any single part of a container closure system. Typical components are containers (e.g., ampules, vials, bottles), container liners (e.g., tube liners), closures (e.g., screw caps, stoppers), closure liners, stopper overseals, container inner seals, administration ports (e.g., on large-volume parenterals (LVPs)), overwraps, administration accessories, and container labels. A primary packaging component means a packaging component that is or may be in direct contact with the dosage form. A secondary packaging component means a packaging component that is not and will not be in direct contact with the dosage form.

A container closure system refers to the sum of packaging components that together contain and protect the dosage form. This includes primary packaging components and secondary packaging components, if the latter are intended to provide additional protection to the drug product. A packaging system is equivalent to a container closure system.

A package or market package refers to the container closure system and labeling, associated components (e.g., dosing cups, droppers, spoons), and external packaging (e.g., cartons or shrink wrap). A market package is the article provided to a pharmacist or retail customer upon purchase and does not include packaging used solely for the purpose of shipping such articles.

Quality refers to the physical, chemical, microbiological, biological, bioavailability, and stability attributes that a drug product should maintain if it is to be deemed suitable for therapeutic or diagnostic use. In this guidance, the term is also understood to convey the properties of safety, identity, strength, quality, and purity (see 21 CFR 211.94(a)).

An extraction profile refers to the analysis (usually by chromatographic means) of extracts obtained from a packaging component. A quantitative extraction profile is one in which the amount of each detected substance is determined.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Materials of Construction: The basic substances used to manufacture packaging components, for example Type I glass, HDPE resin and stainless steel. They must be defined by their specific chemical composition, not merely by a generic name.

Primary Packaging Component: A packaging component that is in direct contact or may come into contact with the dosage form. For example, for an injectable: the vial, stopper and syringe barrel.

Secondary Packaging Component: A packaging component that is not in direct contact with the dosage form. For example, the carton or overwrap. However, if it provides additional protection (such as moisture or light protection), more detailed information must be submitted.

Extraction Profile: The analytical results obtained by chromatographic analysis of extracts from a packaging component. A quantitative extraction profile quantifies the amount of each substance detected. This is the foundation of extractables and leachables (E&L) studies.

Analogy

Imagine a lunch box system: the box body (container) and the lid (closure) are primary components because they touch the food directly; the carrying bag is a secondary component, which does not touch the food but provides protection; the chopsticks and spoon that come with it are associated components, which touch the food only during use. The whole set is the container closure system. An extraction profile is like testing whether the lunch box material releases plasticizers when heated: every extractable has to be found and quantified.

Key Notes

Note that FDA's definition of quality covers the "safety, identity, strength, quality, and purity (SISPQ)" in 21 CFR 211.94(a). This links directly to the CGMP requirement of 21 CFR 211.94 for containers and closures: they must not be reactive, additive or absorptive to the extent that SISPQ is affected.

Container closure system and packaging system are synonyms, but the market package has a broader scope, including labeling and outer packaging. A CDMO must distinguish between these levels in its submission documents.

Practical Application

When establishing packaging material specifications, a CDMO should ensure that the materials of construction of every primary packaging component have a clear chemical composition record, including the polymer type and all additives. When a supplier changes, reassess whether the composition remains consistent. Establish baseline extraction profile data to serve as the basis for monitoring batch-to-batch consistency.

Practice Questions

  1. Is a stopper overseal a primary or a secondary packaging component, and why?
  2. If an LDPE overwrap is designed to prevent solvent loss from an injectable bag, it should be regarded as a secondary component. In that case, what additional information does FDA expect to be provided?
  3. How does an extraction profile differ from a leachables study? Why does FDA specifically stress the quantitative extraction profile in its definitions?
II.B. CGMP, CPSC and USP Requirements on Containers and Closures

Original Text

Current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements for the control of drug product containers and closures are included in 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211. A listing of the relevant sections is provided in Attachment A. In addition, a listing of Compliance Policy Guides that deal with packaging issues is provided in Attachment B. References in this guidance to CGMP regulations are provided for completeness. For additional information, refer to the FDA Compliance Program Guidance Manual for Pre-Approval Inspections/Investigations (7346.832) which describes specific responsibilities for CDER scientists and for field investigators.

The FDA requirement for tamper-resistant closures is included in 21 CFR 211.132 and the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) requirements for child-resistant closures are included in 16 CFR 1700. An outline of these and other applicable regulatory requirements is provided in Attachment A.

The United States Pharmacopeial Convention has established requirements for containers which are described in many of the drug product monographs in The United States Pharmacopeia/National Formulary (USP/NF). For capsules and tablets, these requirements generally relate to the design characteristics of the container (e.g., tight, well-closed or light-resistant). For injectable products, materials of construction are also addressed (e.g., "Preserve in single-dose or in multiple-dose containers, preferably of Type I glass, protected from light"). These requirements are defined in the "General Notices and Requirements" (Preservation, Packaging, Storage, and Labeling) section of the USP. The requirements for materials of construction are defined in the "General Chapters" of the USP (see Attachment A).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

21 CFR 211.94 (Drug Product Containers and Closures): The core CGMP provision on packaging. It requires that container closures not be reactive, additive or absorptive, and that they provide adequate protection against external factors. This is the regulatory basis on which FDA inspectors examine packaging controls.

USP General Chapters: USP <661> (Containers), USP <381> (Elastomeric Closures for Injections) and USP <87>/<88> (Biological Reactivity Tests), among others, form the system of standard methods for testing packaging materials.

Analogy

Think of the packaging regulatory framework as a building: the FD&C Act is the foundation (statutory level); 21 CFR 211 is the building code (CGMP level); USP is the engineering specification (test standard level); and this FDA guidance is the architectural design guide, telling you how to prepare submission documents within these frameworks.

Key Notes

Note that three different regulatory bodies each have their own packaging requirements: FDA (CGMP + tamper-resistant), CPSC (child-resistant packaging, 16 CFR 1700) and USP (material testing standards). A CDMO must satisfy all three sets of requirements at the same time.

USP requirements for injectable packaging are far stricter than for solid oral dosage forms: they specify not only the design characteristics of the container but also the materials of construction (such as Type I glass). This reflects the high-risk nature of sterile products.

Practice Questions

  1. 21 CFR 211.94 requires that containers not be reactive, additive or absorptive. Give an example of how each of these three problems could arise in a sterile injectable.
  2. Why does USP specify Type I glass for injectables? How do the differences among Type I, Type II and Type III glass relate to drug product stability?
II.C. Additional Considerations

Original Text

1. Submissions of INDs

The packaging information in the chemistry, manufacturing, and controls section of an IND usually includes a brief description of the components, the assembled packaging system and any precautions needed to ensure the protection and preservation of the drug substance and drug product during their use in the clinical trials.

For general guidance regarding the container closure system information to be submitted for phase 1 studies, refer to the FDA guidance for industry Content and Format of Investigational New Drug Applications (INDs) for Phase 1 Studies of Drugs, Including Well-Characterized, Therapeutic, Biotechnology-derived Products (November 1995).

General guidance regarding the container closure system information to be submitted for phase 2 or phase 3 studies will be provided in the FDA guidance for industry INDs for Phase 2 and 3 Studies of Drugs, Including Specified Therapeutic Biotechnology-Derived Products, Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Content and Format, when finalized (draft guidance published April 21, 1999).

2. Submissions on Packaging of a Drug Product by Another Firm

a. Contract Packager

A contract packager is a firm retained by the applicant to package a drug product. The applicant remains responsible for the quality of the drug product during shipping, storage, and packaging.

The information regarding the container closure system used by a contract packager that should be submitted in the CMC section of an application (NDA, ANDA, or BLA), or in a DMF which is referenced in the application, is no different from that which would be submitted if the applicant performed its own packaging operations. If the information is provided in a DMF, then a copy of the letter of authorization (LOA) for the DMF should be provided in the application (see section V.A).

b. Repackager

A repackager is a firm that buys drug product from the drug product manufacturer or distributor and repackages it for sale under a label different from that of the manufacturer. The repackager is responsible for ensuring the quality and stability of the repackaged drug product. The repackaging operation is required to be in compliance with CGMPs (21 CFR Part 211), and there are limits to the expiration period that may be used with the repackaged product unless the repackager conducts stability studies. Packaging qualification information is not required if the repackager uses the same container closure system approved in the original application.

All significant phases of the manufacturing and processing of a drug product (including packaging) should be described as part of the CMC section of an application (NDA, ANDA or BLA), or in a DMF referenced in the application. The only exception is the repackaging of solid oral drug products for which an approved application already exists. For biologics, repackaging is considered a step in the manufacturing process for which licensing is required (21 CFR 600.3(u) and 601).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Packaging information at the IND stage: Phase 1 requires only a brief description; Phase 2/3 requires more detailed CCS information. This reflects FDA's phase-appropriate approach: as clinical development advances, the packaging qualification information required becomes progressively more detailed.

Contract Packager: A firm that performs packaging operations on behalf of the applicant. CCS submission requirements are exactly the same as for self-packaging.

Repackager: A firm that buys drug product from the manufacturer and repackages it under a different label. If the same approved CCS is used, no additional packaging qualification information is required.

Letter of Authorization (LOA): A letter from the DMF holder to the applicant authorizing FDA to refer to the proprietary information in the DMF when reviewing a specific application.

Analogy

The phase-appropriate approach is like "growing from student to professional": in the first year (Phase 1) you only need to master the basics; by the fourth year (Phase 3) the expectations for professional competence rise sharply. Likewise, early in the IND only basic packaging information is needed, but at NDA/BLA submission full CCS qualification data is required. A CDMO acting as a contract packager is like a contract manufacturer: quality responsibility still rests with the client (the applicant), but the standard of manufacturing and documentation cannot be compromised.

Key Notes

The most important sentence for a CDMO: "The information regarding the container closure system used by a contract packager... is no different from that which would be submitted if the applicant performed its own packaging operations." This means that when a CDMO performs packaging operations, the quality and completeness of the CCS-related information must be exactly the same as if the client did the work itself; the standard cannot be lowered because the work is outsourced.

For biologics, repackaging is considered a manufacturing step and requires licensing (21 CFR 600.3(u)). This is especially important for biologics CDMOs.

Practical Application

As a sterile CDMO, when accepting a packaging job from a client: (1) ensure that all CCS information (material composition, suitability data, QC methods) is documented in the client's NDA/BLA or in your DMF; (2) issue an LOA authorizing FDA to refer to it; (3) remind the client that the applicant retains ultimate responsibility for quality, but the CDMO must cooperate by providing complete and accurate technical information.

Practice Questions

  1. If a CDMO acting as a contract packager uses a proprietary elastomeric stopper formulation in its CCS, how can it use the DMF mechanism to protect the formulation while still meeting FDA's review needs?
  2. CCS information requirements rise progressively from Phase 1 to Phase 3. What does this mean for the business of a CDMO's early development support services?

III.A. Introduction - Qualification of Packaging Components

Original Text

CDER and CBER approve a container closure system to be used in the packaging of a human drug or biologic as part of the application (NDA, ANDA or BLA) for the drug or biologic. A packaging system found acceptable for one drug product is not automatically assumed to be appropriate for another. Each application should contain enough information to show that each proposed container closure system and its components are suitable for its intended use.

The type and extent of information that should be provided in an application will depend on the dosage form and the route of administration. For example, the kind of information that should be provided about a packaging system for an injectable dosage form or a drug product for inhalation is often more detailed than that which should be provided about a packaging system for a solid oral dosage form. More detailed information usually should be provided for a liquid-based dosage form than for a powder or a solid, since a liquid-based dosage form is more likely to interact with the packaging components.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Product-Specific Qualification: A CCS approved for product A is not automatically suitable for product B. Each application needs its own CCS suitability data. For a CDMO this means that even if the same vial-stopper system is already used for several clients' products, every new product still needs its own qualification.

Risk-Based Approach: Through the matrix in Table 1, FDA established a tiered framework with two axes: (1) the risk level of the route of administration; (2) the likelihood of packaging-dosage form interaction. The intersection determines how detailed the CCS information must be.

Analogy

The idea behind Table 1 is like tiered airport security. International flights (highest concern = injections/inhalation products) get the strictest screening; domestic flights (low concern = oral solid dosage forms) get simpler screening. Likewise, liquids (high interaction = liquid dosage forms) need more checking than solids (low interaction = tablets). An injectable is like "carrying liquids on an international flight": the highest level of screening.

Key Notes

Key takeaway for CDMOs: The main products of a sterile fill-finish CDMO (injections, injectable suspensions) sit in the top-left corner of Table 1: highest concern + high likelihood of interaction. This is the category for which FDA requires the most CCS information, meaning the most complete extraction/toxicological evaluation, the most detailed disclosure of material composition and the strictest QC monitoring.

Table Analysis

How to read Table 1:

  • Vertical axis (rows) = risk level of the route of administration: Highest (direct entry into the circulatory/respiratory system) > High (ophthalmic/transdermal) > Low (oral/topical)
  • Horizontal axis (columns) = likelihood of packaging interaction: High (liquids) > Medium (powders) > Low (solids)
  • Top-left corner = the most detailed CCS information required; bottom-right corner = relatively simplified information is sufficient

Practical Application

In a CDMO's commercial proposal, the workload and cost of CCS qualification should be tied to the Table 1 risk classification. The CCS data package for an injectable client (extraction studies, toxicological evaluation, USP tests, compatibility studies) is far more complex than for an oral solid dosage form. This should be reflected in quotations and timeline planning.

III.B. General Considerations - Suitability Overview

Original Text

Suitability refers to the tests and studies used and accepted for the initial qualification of a component or a container closure system for its intended use. Quality control (QC) refers to the tests typically used and accepted to establish that, after the application is approved, the components and the container closure system continue to possess the characteristics established in the suitability studies. The subsections on associated components and secondary components describe the tests and studies for establishing suitability and quality control for these types of components. However, the ultimate proof of the suitability of the container closure system and the packaging process is established by full shelf life stability studies.

1. Suitability for the Intended Use

Every proposed packaging system should be shown to be suitable for its intended use: it should adequately protect the dosage form; it should be compatible with the dosage form; and it should be composed of materials that are considered safe for use with the dosage form and the route of administration. If the packaging system has a performance feature in addition to containing the product, the assembled container closure system should be shown to function properly.

Information intended to establish suitability may be generated by the applicant, by the supplier of the material of construction or the component, or by a laboratory under contract to either the applicant or the firm. An adequately detailed description of the tests, methods, acceptance criteria, reference standards, and validation information for the studies should be provided. The information may be submitted directly in the application or indirectly by reference to a DMF. If a DMF is used, a letter authorizing reference (i.e., letter of authorization (LOA)) to the DMF must be included in the application (see section V.A).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four pillars of suitability:

1. Protection: Protects against external factors (light, temperature, oxygen, moisture, microorganisms)

2. Compatibility: The packaging and the dosage form do not undergo harmful interactions

3. Safety: The materials do not release harmful substances to a level to which the patient will be exposed

4. Performance: The CCS performs its designed function correctly (such as drug delivery from a prefilled syringe)

Shelf Life Stability Studies: FDA states explicitly that the ultimate proof of CCS suitability is the full shelf life stability study: not accelerated studies, but real-time, real-condition data.

Analogy

The four pillars of CCS suitability are like the criteria for choosing a home: Protection = the house withstands wind and rain (moisture and light protection); Compatibility = the building materials do not cause adverse reactions in the occupants (such as formaldehyde release); Safety = the building materials are themselves non-toxic (no asbestos); Performance = the door lock locks and the windows open and close (everything functions properly). The stability study is like actually living in the house for the whole "warranty period" to verify all of this.

Key Notes

Suitability data can be generated by three parties: the applicant, the material supplier, or a contracted third-party laboratory. This gives the CDMO flexibility: it can use the supplier's DMF data, test in-house, or commission a CRO. But whatever the source of the data, sufficient detail must be provided on "tests, methods, acceptance criteria, reference standards, and validation information".

  1. Why does FDA position full shelf life stability studies as the "ultimate proof" of CCS suitability? Why are accelerated stability studies not enough?
III.B.1.a. Protection

Original Text

A container closure system should provide the dosage form with adequate protection from factors (e.g., temperature, light) that can cause a degradation in the quality of that dosage form over its shelf life. Common causes of such degradation are: exposure to light, loss of solvent, exposure to reactive gases (e.g., oxygen), absorption of water vapor, and microbial contamination. A drug product can also suffer an unacceptable loss in quality if it is contaminated by filth.

Not every drug product is susceptible to degradation by all of these factors. Not all drug products are light sensitive. Not all tablets are subject to loss of quality due to absorption of moisture. Sensitivity to oxygen is most commonly found with liquid-based dosage forms. Laboratory studies can be used to determine which of these factors actually have an influence on a particular drug product.

Light protection is typically provided by an opaque or amber-colored container or by an opaque secondary packaging component (e.g., cartons or overwrap). The USP test for light transmission (USP <661>) is an accepted standard for evaluating the light transmission properties of a container. Situations exist in which solid and liquid-based oral drug products have been exposed to light during storage because the opaque secondary packaging component was removed, contrary to the approved labeling and the USP monograph recommendation. A firm, therefore, may want to consider using additional or alternate measures to provide light protection to these drug products when necessary.

Loss of solvent can occur through a permeable barrier (e.g., a polyethylene container wall), through an inadequate seal, or through leakage. Leaks can develop through rough handling or from inadequate contact between the container and the closure (e.g., due to the buildup of pressure during storage). Leaks can also occur in tubes due to a failure of the crimp seal.

Water vapor or reactive gases (e.g., oxygen) may penetrate a container closure system either by passing through a permeable container surface (e.g., the wall of a low density polyethylene (LDPE) bottle) or by diffusing past a seal. Plastic containers are susceptible to both routes. Although glass containers would seem to offer better protection, because glass is relatively impermeable, glass containers are more effective only if there is a good seal between the container and the closure.

Protection from microbial contamination is provided by maintaining adequate container integrity after the packaging system has been sealed. An adequate and validated procedure should be used for drug product manufacture and packaging.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The five degradation factors addressed by protection:

1. Light: USP <661> testing; amber containers or opaque outer packaging

2. Solvent loss: Through the container wall, a poor seal or leakage

3. Reactive gases - O₂: Through wall permeation or gaps in the seal

4. Water vapor: Through the wall or the seal; LDPE is a poor barrier

5. Microbial contamination: Maintenance of seal integrity, the most critical factor for sterile products

Analogy

The protective function of a CCS is like a space suit: it must simultaneously guard against radiation (light), vacuum (solvent loss), manage oxygen supply (gas exchange), keep out water (water vapor) and keep out microorganisms. But not every astronaut faces the same threats: some products are not light sensitive, and some tablets do not fear moisture. The key is to first carry out a "vulnerability assessment" (laboratory studies) to determine what your product is sensitive to, and then design the protection accordingly.

Key Notes

The glass trap: FDA points out that although glass itself is almost impermeable to gas, the protection provided by a glass container depends on how well the container and closure fit together. Even with the best Type I glass vial, if the stopper seal is poor, oxygen and water vapor can still enter through gaps in the seal.

Practical issues in light protection: FDA has noted the problem of light-sensitive drugs being exposed to light once the secondary packaging (such as the carton) is removed. It recommends considering light protection in the primary packaging itself (such as an amber vial) rather than relying on the carton alone.

Practical Application

When selecting packaging for a light-sensitive injectable, a CDMO should consider a dual light-protection strategy: primary container (amber Type I glass vial) + secondary packaging (opaque carton with overwrap). For products in plastic containers (such as cyclic olefin polymer), the WVTR (water vapor transmission rate) and OTR (oxygen transmission rate) must be evaluated with particular care, and their actual impact monitored in stability studies.

Practice Questions

  1. Why are LDPE bottles unsuitable for injectables containing highly volatile solvents? Explain from the standpoint of polymer structure.
  2. Why is container closure integrity testing (CCIT) the last line of defense for protection in sterile injectables? What new requirements does PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) introduce on this?
III.B.1.b. Compatibility

Original Text

Packaging components that are compatible with a dosage form will not interact sufficiently to cause unacceptable changes in the quality of either the dosage form or the packaging component.

Examples of interactions include loss of potency due to absorption or adsorption of the active drug substance, or degradation of the active drug substance induced by a chemical entity leached from a packaging component; reduction in the concentration of an excipient due to absorption, adsorption or leachable-induced degradation; precipitation; changes in drug product pH; discoloration of either the dosage form or the packaging component; or increase in brittleness of the packaging component.

Some interactions between a packaging component and dosage form will be detected during qualification studies on the container closure system and its components. Others may not show up except in the stability studies. Therefore, any change noted during a stability study that may be attributable to interaction between the dosage form and a packaging component should be investigated and appropriate action taken, regardless of whether the stability study is being conducted for an original application, a supplemental application, or as fulfillment of a commitment to conduct postapproval stability studies.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Absorption: Drug molecules are "taken up" into the packaging material (for example, a protein absorbed by a stopper)

Adsorption: Drug molecules adhere to the surface of the packaging material (for example, insulin adsorbed onto a glass surface)

Leachable-induced degradation: Chemicals leaching from the packaging material trigger degradation of the drug (for example, metal ions catalyzing oxidation reactions)

Analogy

The difference between absorption and adsorption is like that between a sponge and a magnet: a sponge takes water into its interior (absorption), while a magnet holds iron filings on its surface (adsorption). Both reduce the concentration of the active ingredient. Leachable-induced degradation is like a rusty water pipe contaminating the tap water: the problem lies in the pipe itself, and it degrades the water quality.

Key Notes

The stability study as a "sentinel": Some compatibility problems do not show up at initial qualification and are only discovered in long-term stability studies. FDA requires that any change observed in a stability study that may be related to a packaging interaction be investigated, whether in the initial submission or in postapproval stability.

This is a two-way problem: not only can the quality of the dosage form change (loss of potency, pH shift, precipitation), but the packaging component itself can also change (embrittlement, discoloration).

Practical Application

CDMOs handling protein drugs (mAbs) should pay particular attention to adsorption: proteins adsorb onto glass and polymer surfaces, reducing recovery. Solutions include siliconized vials/syringes, formulations containing surfactants (such as polysorbate 80), or low-adsorption materials (such as COC/COP containers). A container compatibility study should be performed before each new product goes live.

  1. If a stability study shows that the pH of an injectable has dropped by 0.3 units after 12 months, how would you investigate whether this is related to the packaging material?
  2. Polysorbate 80 can prevent protein adsorption in protein formulations, but it may itself react with antioxidants in the stopper. How is this conflict balanced in CCS design?
III.B.1.c. Safety

Original Text

Packaging components should be constructed of materials that will not leach harmful or undesirable amounts of substances to which a patient will be exposed when being treated with the drug product. This consideration is especially important for those packaging components which may be in direct contact with the dosage form, but it is also applicable to any component from which substances may migrate into the dosage form (e.g., an ink or adhesive).

Making the determination that a material of construction used in the manufacture of a packaging component is safe for its intended use is not a simple process, and a standardized approach has not been established. There is, however, a body of experience which supports the use of certain approaches that depend on the route of administration and the likelihood of interactions between the component and the dosage form (see Table 1).

For a drug product such as an injection, inhalation, ophthalmic, or transdermal, a comprehensive study is appropriate. This involves two parts: first, an extraction study on the packaging component to determine which chemical species may migrate into the dosage form (and at what concentration); and, second, a toxicological evaluation of those substances which are extracted to determine the safe level of exposure via the label specified route of administration. This technique is used by the Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN) to evaluate the safety of substances that are proposed as indirect food additives (e.g., polymers or additives that may be used for packaging foods).

The approach for toxicological evaluation of the safety of extractables should be based on good scientific principles and take into account the specific container closure system, drug product formulation, dosage form, route of administration, and dose regimen (chronic or short-term dosing).

For many injectable and ophthalmic drug products (see sections III.E and III.F), data from the USP Biological Reactivity Tests and USP Elastomeric Closures for Injections tests will typically be considered sufficient evidence of material safety.

For many solid and liquid oral drug products, an appropriate reference to the indirect food additive regulations (21 CFR 174-186) promulgated by CFSAN for the materials of construction used in the packaging component will typically be considered sufficient. Although these regulations do not specifically apply to materials for packaging drug products, they include purity criteria and limitations pertaining to the use of specific materials for packaging foods that may be acceptable for the evaluation of drug product packaging components. Applicants are cautioned that this approach may not be acceptable for liquid oral dosage forms intended for chronic use (see section III.F.1).

For drug products that undergo clinical trials, the absence of adverse reactions traceable to the packaging components is considered supporting evidence of material safety. Safety assessments for specific dosage forms are discussed further in section III of this guidance.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two-Part Safety Evaluation:

Step 1: Extraction Study → identify which chemical species may migrate into the dosage form (and at what concentrations)

Step 2: Toxicological Evaluation → assess the safe exposure level of these extractables via the specified route of administration

This approach applies to high-risk products: injections, inhalation, ophthalmic and transdermal.

The three-level structure of safety evidence:

Level 1 (most stringent): Extraction study + toxicological evaluation (injections, inhalation products, etc.)

Level 2 (intermediate): USP Biological Reactivity Tests + USP Elastomeric Closures tests (injections/ophthalmic)

Level 3 (simplest): Reference to indirect food additive regulations, 21 CFR 174-186 (oral solids)

Analogy

Safety assessment is like tiered food safety testing: an injectable that goes straight into the blood (such as an intravenous injection) is like "raw food", where every possible contaminant must be tested down to the lowest limit; an oral solid is like "cooked food", where the first-pass effect of the digestive tract allows a wider safety margin. The food packaging regulations (21 CFR 174-186) were originally standards for "food containers"; FDA allows them to be used for oral drug packaging because the routes of exposure and the risks are similar.

Key Notes

Cross-disciplinary borrowing from the CFSAN approach: FDA states explicitly that the safety assessment of packaging borrows the indirect food additive evaluation approach of food safety (CFSAN). But note that the food additive regulations "do not directly apply" to drug packaging: FDA merely accepts them as "sufficient evidence" in certain situations.

Warning for long-term use liquid oral dosage forms: FDA specifically warns that for liquid oral dosage forms used chronically, citing the food additive regulations alone may not be enough, because long-term use means a higher cumulative exposure, beyond the assumptions of food contact.

Supporting value of clinical trials: That a drug was used in a specific package in clinical trials without adverse reactions is "supporting evidence" of safety: not sufficient evidence, but strong corroboration.

Practical Application

When budgeting for CCS qualification, a CDMO will find that the safety assessment of injectables is the biggest cost item. Recommended strategy: (1) give priority to packaging material suppliers that have a well-established Type III DMF, to leverage their existing extraction/toxicological data; (2) for elastomeric stoppers, make sure the supplier's DMF includes USP <381> and <87>/<88> data; (3) for non-standard formulations (products containing cosolvents or surfactants), be prepared to conduct a drug product-specific extraction study, because generic aqueous extraction data may be insufficient.

  1. Why can the results of an extraction study not be equated directly with the results of a leachables study? What role does each play in CCS qualification?
  2. How do the PQRI Safety Concern Threshold (SCT = 0.15 μg/day) and Qualification Threshold (QT = 5 μg/day) connect with the toxicological evaluation requirements in this guidance?
III.B.1.d. Performance

Original Text

Performance of the container closure system refers to its ability to function in the manner for which it was designed. A container closure system is often called upon to do more than simply contain the dosage form. When evaluating performance, two major considerations are container closure system functionality and drug delivery.

i. Container Closure System Functionality

The container closure system may be designed to improve patient compliance (e.g., a cap that contains a counter), minimize waste (e.g., a two-chamber vial or IV bag), improve ease of use (e.g., a prefilled syringe), or have other functions.

ii. Drug Delivery

Drug delivery refers to the ability of the packaging system to deliver the dosage form in the amount or at the rate described in the package insert. Some examples of a packaging system for which drug delivery aspects are relevant are a prefilled syringe, a transdermal patch, a metered tube, a dropper or spray bottle, a dry powder inhaler, and a metered dose inhaler.

Container closure system functionality and/or drug delivery are compromised when the packaging system fails to operate as designed. Failure can result from misuse, faulty design, manufacturing defect, improper assembly, or wear and tear during use. Tests and acceptance criteria regarding dosage form delivery and container closure system functionality should be appropriate to the particular dosage form, route of administration, and design features.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Functionality: Additional functions of the CCS, such as a counting cap, a dual-chamber vial or the ease of use of a prefilled syringe. These go beyond the basic function of "containment".

Drug Delivery: The ability of the CCS to accurately deliver the labeled dose. Essential for prefilled syringes, MDIs, DPIs and transdermal patches.

Analogy

Performance is like a car that must not only carry people (the containment function) but also drive safely to the destination (the delivery function). A prefilled syringe is like a self-driving car: it not only holds the drug but must also precisely control the injected volume and the plunger force. A plunger that will not move, or an MDI that delivers inaccurately, is like a car that will not start: a complete functional failure.

Key Notes

The five main causes of performance failure: misuse, faulty design, manufacturing defect, improper assembly and wear and tear. In packaging process validation, a CDMO should design tests for each possible failure mode.

The performance tests most relevant to a sterile CDMO: for prefilled syringes, break-loose force, glide force and deliverable volume; for vial-stopper systems, coring resistance and resealability.

  2. What items should the performance testing of a two-chamber vial include? How is the accuracy of drug delivery after reconstitution verified?

III.B.2. Quality Control of Packaging Components

Original Text

In addition to providing data to show that a proposed container closure system is suitable for its intended use, an application should also describe the quality control measures that will be used to ensure consistency in the packaging components (see section III.C.3). These controls are intended to limit unintended postapproval variations in the manufacturing procedures or materials of construction for a packaging component and to prevent adverse affects on the quality of a dosage form.

Principal consideration is usually given to consistency in physical characteristics and chemical composition.

a. Physical Characteristics

The physical characteristics of interest include dimensional criteria (e.g., shape, neck finish, wall thickness, design tolerances), physical parameters critical to the consistent manufacture of a packaging component (e.g., unit weight), and performance characteristics (e.g., metering valve delivery volume, or the ease of movement of syringe plungers). Unintended variations in dimensional parameters, if undetected, may affect package permeability, drug delivery performance, or the adequacy of the seal between the container and the closure. Variation in any physical parameter is considered important if it can affect the quality of a dosage form.

b. Chemical Composition

The chemical composition of the materials of construction may affect the safety of a packaging component. New materials may result in new substances being extracted into the dosage form or a change in the amount of known extractables. Chemical composition may also affect the compatibility, functional characteristics or protective properties of packaging components by changing rheological or other physical properties (e.g., elasticity, resistance to solvents, or gas permeability).

A composition change may occur as a result of a change in formulation or in a processing aid (e.g., using a different mold release agent) or through the use of a new supplier of a raw material. A change in the supplier of a polymeric material or a substance of biological origin is more likely to bring with it an unexpected composition change than a change in the supplier of a pure chemical compound, because polymeric and natural materials are often complex mixtures. A composition change may also occur with a change in the manufacturing process, such as the use of different operating conditions (e.g., a significantly different curing temperature), different equipment, or both.

A change in formulation is considered a change in the specifications for the packaging component. This change in the formulation of a packaging component by its manufacturer should be reported to the firm that purchases that component and to any appropriate DMF. The firm that purchases the component should, in turn, report the change to its application as required under 21 CFR 314.70(a) or 601.12. Manufacturers who supply a raw material or an intermediate packaging component should inform their customers of any intended changes to formulations or manufacturing procedures and update the DMF in advance of implementing such a change. Changes which seem innocuous may have unintended consequences on the dosage form marketed in the affected packaging system.

The use of stability studies for monitoring the consistency of a container closure system in terms of compatibility with the dosage form and the degree of protection provided to the dosage form is accepted. Currently there is no general policy concerning the monitoring of a packaging system and components with regard to safety. One exception involves inhalation drug products for which batch-to-batch monitoring of the extraction profile for the polymeric and elastomeric components is routine.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The two focus areas of QC:

1. Physical Characteristics: Dimensions (shape, neck finish, wall thickness, tolerances), weight, and performance (metering valve delivery, plunger movement)

2. Chemical Composition: Material formulation, processing aids and supplier source: any change may affect safety, compatibility and protection

Analogy

QC monitoring is like incoming inspection of car parts: you check not only the appearance and dimensions (physical) but also the material composition (chemical). Imagine buying a batch of tires that look identical, but the rubber formulation has been quietly changed: the appearance is unchanged, but the wear resistance and grip may be completely different. The same applies to packaging materials: the stopper looks the same, but if the vulcanizing agent in the formulation has been replaced, new extractables may appear.

Key Notes

The knock-on effect of supply chain changes: FDA describes a complete chain of change notification: raw material supplier → packaging component manufacturer → drug product manufacturer → regulatory application. Every link has a notification obligation. "Changes which seem innocuous may have unintended consequences": this sentence is FDA's core warning.

The special risk of changing polymer suppliers: FDA states explicitly that changing the supplier of a polymer or a raw material of biological origin carries a higher risk than changing the supplier of a pure chemical compound, because polymers and natural materials are "complex mixtures".

The gap in routine safety monitoring: Apart from inhalation products, there is currently no general policy on routine monitoring of packaging safety. For an injectable CDMO, however, establishing a periodic extraction profile comparison is best practice.

Practical Application

A CDMO's supplier quality management (SQM) program should include: (1) a Quality Agreement with the packaging material supplier that explicitly requires prior notification of any formulation or process change; (2) an incoming QC procedure for packaging components that includes dimensional checks and at least one chemical composition identification test; (3) periodic (at least annual) verification that the supplier's extraction profile is consistent with the initial qualification data; (4) an approved supplier list and a controlled approval process for supplier changes.

  1. What could the consequences be if a stopper supplier changes the mold release agent without notifying the CDMO? Would the CDMO's incoming QC procedure be able to detect this change?
  2. Why does FDA specifically mention that changing suppliers of polymers and materials of biological origin carries a higher risk than for pure chemical compounds? How should a CDMO control this risk in the quality agreement?
III.B.3. Associated Components

Original Text

Associated components are packaging components that are typically intended to deliver the dosage form to the patient but are not stored in contact with the dosage form for its entire shelf life. These components are packaged separately in the market package and are either attached to the container upon opening or used only when a dose is to be administered. Measuring spoons, dosing cups, measuring syringes, and vaginal delivery tubes are examples of associated components that typically contact the dosage form only during administration. A hand pump or dropper combined into a closure are examples of an associated component that would contact the dosage form from the time the packaging system is opened until the dosing regimen is completed.

The complete and assembled component and its parts should meet suitability criteria appropriate for the drug product and the actual use of the component (see sections III.B.1 and III.B.2). Safety and functionality are the most common factors to be established for suitability. The length of time that the associated component and the dosage form are in direct contact should also be taken into consideration when assessing the suitability of an associated component.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Associated Components: Designed to deliver the dosage form to the patient but not in contact with the dosage form throughout its shelf life. There are two modes of contact:

1. Contact only at the time of administration (measuring spoon, dosing cup): very short contact time

2. Continuous contact after opening until the product is used up (hand pump, dropper): contact time can be several weeks

Analogy

Associated components are like disposable chopsticks in a restaurant: they are not immersed in the food all the time, but they do touch the food when used. If the bamboo used for the chopsticks contains bleach residues, even brief contact could cause a problem. Likewise, if the plastic of a dosing cup contains a high level of extractables, its safety needs to be assessed even though it touches the liquid drug only briefly at the time of administration.

Key Notes

Safety and functionality are the two main focuses of suitability for associated components. Contact time is the key consideration: a hand pump or dropper stays in contact with the liquid drug after opening (possibly for weeks), so its safety assessment should be stricter than for a single-use measuring spoon.

III.B.4. Secondary Packaging Components

Original Text

Unlike primary and associated packaging components, secondary packaging components are not intended to make contact with the dosage form. Examples are cartons, which are generally constructed of paper or plastic, and overwraps, which may be fabricated from a single layer of plastic or from a laminate made of metal foil, plastic, and/or paper.

A secondary packaging component generally serves one or more of the following additional functions:

  1. Provides protection from excessive transmission of moisture or solvents into or out of the packaging system
  2. Provides protection from excessive transmission of reactive gases (atmospheric oxygen, inert headspace filler gas, or other organic vapors) into or out of the packaging system
  3. Provides light protection for the packaging system
  4. Provides protection for a packaging system that is flexible or needs extra protection from rough handling
  5. Provides an additional measure of microbiological protection (i.e., by maintaining sterility or by protecting the packaging system from microbial intrusion)

When information on a container closure system is submitted in an application, the emphasis would normally be on the primary packaging components. For a secondary packaging component, a brief description will usually suffice unless the component is intended to provide some additional measure of protection to the drug product. In this case, more complete information should be provided, along with data showing that the secondary packaging component actually provides the additional protection.

Because secondary packaging components are not intended to make contact with the dosage form, there is usually less concern regarding the materials from which they are constructed. However, if the packaging system is relatively permeable, the possibility increases that the dosage form could be contaminated by the migration of an ink or adhesive component, or from a volatile substance present in the secondary packaging component. (For example, a solution packaged in a LDPE container was found to be contaminated by a volatile constituent of the secondary packaging components that enclosed it.). In such a case, the secondary packaging component should be considered a potential source of contamination and the safety of its materials of construction should be taken into consideration.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The dual role of secondary components:

1. Usually only a brief description is needed: they do not contact the dosage form directly, so there is less concern about material safety

2. When they provide an additional protective function (against moisture, light or microorganisms): complete information and protection performance data must be provided

Analogy

Secondary components are like the outer walls and roof of a house: they do not touch your food directly (primary = bowls and plates) but they protect the whole living environment. You normally would not run food safety tests on the outer walls, but if the wall paint contains volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and the house is poorly ventilated, these chemicals could get indoors and affect health. Likewise, if the ink or adhesive of secondary packaging migrates into a permeable primary container, it becomes a safety problem.

Key Notes

The LDPE container cross-contamination case: FDA specifically cites a real case in which a liquid drug in an LDPE container was contaminated by volatile components from the secondary packaging. This is a reminder to CDMOs: for products in highly permeable primary containers (such as LDPE or EVA bags), the material safety of the secondary packaging cannot be ignored.

Of the five protective functions (moisture, gases, light, handling, microbial), if a secondary component provides any one of them, additional qualification data must be provided.

Practical Application

A sterile CDMO handling IV bag products must take particular care: the overwrap of a flexible bag (usually multilayer plastic) provides protection against solvent loss and against microorganisms. This overwrap needs complete qualification data and cannot be covered by a "brief description". The printing ink and adhesive of the overwrap should also be assessed for possible migration through the bag wall into the drug solution.

  1. The carton for an injectable vial normally needs only a brief description. But if the carton is labeled "protect from light", how do FDA's expectations differ?
  2. Under what circumstances should the overwrap of an IV bag be regarded as a primary rather than a secondary packaging component?

A general description of the entire container closure system should be provided in the CMC section of the application. In addition, the following information should be provided by the applicant for each individual component of the packaging system:

Key Concepts

The three elements of the description:

Original Text

2. Information About Suitability

  1. To establish safety and to ensure consistency, the complete chemical composition should be provided for every material used in the manufacture of a packaging component.
  2. Test results from appropriate qualification and characterization tests should be provided. Adequate information regarding the tests, methods, acceptance criteria, reference standards, and validation information should be provided.

To address protection, use of USP tests (see Attachment A) for light transmission, moisture permeation, microbial limits, and sterility are generally considered sufficient. Testing for properties other than those described in USP (e.g., gas transmission, solvent leakage, container integrity) may also be necessary.

To address safety and compatibility, the results of extraction/toxicological evaluation studies should be provided for drug products that are likely to interact with the packaging components and introduce extracted substances into the patient (see Table 1). For drug products less likely to interact, other tests (e.g., USP Biological Reactivity Test) or information (e.g., appropriate reference to the indirect food additive regulations at 21 CFR 174-186) could be used to address the issue of safety and compatibility.

To address performance, the results of USP and non-USP functionality tests are considered sufficient if the test and acceptance criteria are appropriate for the intended purpose.

Testing on an assembled container closure system is usually performed by the applicant (or a testing laboratory commissioned by the applicant) and the test results provided in the application. Such tests may include vacuum leak testing, moisture permeation, and weight loss or media fill.

Testing on an individual packaging component is typically performed by the manufacturer of the component and reported via a DMF (see section V).

3. Information About Quality Control

The fabricator/manufacturer of a packaging component and the drug product manufacturer who uses this firm share the responsibility for ensuring the quality of packaging components. These firms should have a quality control program in place so that consistent components are produced. The drug product manufacturer must have an inspection program for incoming packaging components and materials (21 CFR 211.22, 211.84 and 211.122). For most drug products, a drug product manufacturer may accept a packaging component lot based on receiving a Certificate of Analysis (COA) or Certificate of Certification (COC) from the component supplier and the performance of an appropriate identification test, provided the supplier's test data are periodically validated (21 CFR 211.84(d)(3)). Acceptance of a packaging component lot based on a supplier's COA or COC may not be appropriate in all cases (e.g., some packaging components for certain inhalation drug products).

a. Applicants

The tests and methods used by the applicant for acceptance of each batch of a packaging component that they receive should be described. If a batch is to be accepted based on a supplier's COA or COC, then the procedure for supplier validation should be described. The data from the supplier's COA or COC should clearly indicate that the lot meets the applicant's acceptance criteria. Acceptance criteria for extractables should also be included, if appropriate.

Dimensional and performance criteria should be provided. Dimensional information is frequently provided via a detailed schematic drawing complete with target dimensions and tolerances and may be provided via the packaging component manufacturer's DMF. A separate drawing may not be necessary if the packaging component is part of a larger unit for which a drawing is provided or if the component is uncomplicated in design (e.g., a cap liner).

b. Manufacturers of Packaging Components Sold to Drug Product Manufacturers

Each manufacturer of a packaging component sold to a drug product manufacturer should provide a description of the quality control measures used to maintain consistency in the physical and chemical characteristics of the component. These generally include release criteria (and test methods, if appropriate) and a description of the manufacturing procedure. If the release of the packaging component is based on statistical process control,18 a complete description of the process (including control criteria) and its validation should be provided.

The description of the manufacturing process is generally brief and should include any operations performed on the packaging component after manufacture but prior to shipping (e.g., washing, coating, and/or sterilization). In some cases it may be desirable for the description to be more detailed and to include in-process controls.

This information may be provided via a DMF (see section V).

18 Statistical process control is defined as "[t]he application of statistical techniques for measuring and analyzing the variation in processes." (Juran, J.M., ed., 1988, Quality Control Handbook, 4th ed., McGraw-Hill, p. 24.2.)

c. Manufacturers of Materials of Construction or of Packaging Components Used to Make Other Packaging Components

The quality control procedures of the manufacturer of a packaging component may sometimes rely in whole or in part on the quality control procedures of a manufacturer who makes an intermediate packaging component that is used to create the component. If so, each contributor to the final packaging system should provide a description of the quality control measures used to maintain consistency in the physical and chemical characteristics of the separate components and of the assembled packaging system that they provide.

The manufacturer of each material of construction should be prepared to describe the quality control measures used to maintain consistency in the chemical characteristics of their product.

This information may be provided via a DMF (see section V).

4. Stability Data (Packaging Concerns)

Stability testing of the drug product should be conducted using the container closure systems proposed in the application. The packaging system used in each stability study should be clearly identified.

The container closure system should be monitored for signs of instability. When appropriate, an evaluation of the packaging system should be included in the stability protocol. Even when a formal test for quality of the packaging system is not performed, the applicant should investigate any observed change in the packaging system used in the stability studies. The observations, results of the investigation, and corrective actions should be included in the stability report. If the corrective action requires a change in an approved container closure system, a supplemental application should be submitted.

Key Concepts

Division of labor in suitability testing:

1. Assembled CCS tests → Applicant/CDMO: vacuum leak testing, moisture permeation, media fill, weight loss

2. Individual component tests → Supplier (via DMF): material characterization, extraction studies, USP tests

This makes clear the respective areas of responsibility of the CDMO and the supplier.

Analogy

The division of labor in CCS qualification is like building a house: the building material supplier (packaging component manufacturer) is responsible for proving the quality of every brick and every steel bar (via DMF); the architect/contractor (applicant/CDMO) is responsible for proving that the assembled house is structurally sound as a whole (assembled system testing). Neither can be dispensed with.

Key Notes

Limits on accepting by COA/COC: 21 CFR 211.84(d)(3) permits acceptance of packaging components on the basis of the supplier's COA/COC + an identification test. But FDA cautions that this "may not be appropriate in all cases", particularly for certain components of inhalation products. For an injectable CDMO, it is advisable to add periodic confirmatory full testing on top of COA-based acceptance.

CCS monitoring in stability studies: A stability study should monitor not only the drug product itself but also any change in the packaging system. Even where there is no formal packaging test item, any change observed in the packaging must be investigated.

III.C.3.a/b/c - The QC Chain of Responsibility Key Concepts

Three-tier QC responsibility structure:

1. Applicant (III.C.3.a): Defines the incoming test methods, the COA/COC verification procedure, the dimensional/performance standards and the extractables acceptance criteria. A CDMO, as the applicant's contractor, usually has to carry out these duties.

2. Component Manufacturer (III.C.3.b): Provides release criteria, a process description and statistical process control (SPC) validation. This information is usually provided through a Type III DMF.

3. Materials/Intermediate Manufacturer (III.C.3.c): Upstream QC in the supply chain: the quality control of the manufacturers of raw materials or intermediate components. When the packaging component manufacturer's QC depends on upstream suppliers, every contributor along the chain must describe its QC measures.

Supplier Change Notification Obligations Key Notes

Change Notification Chain: raw material supplier → packaging component manufacturer → drug product manufacturer → FDA (via 21 CFR 314.70(a) or 601.12). Any change in the formulation or process of a raw material or intermediate component should be notified to downstream customers and the relevant DMF updated. A change that seems innocuous may have unintended consequences for the finished dosage form.

Special requirements for SPC: If the release of a packaging component is based on statistical process control rather than batch-by-batch testing, a complete description of the SPC process (including control criteria) and its validation must be provided. This is common for high-volume components (such as vial caps and stoppers).

Practical Application

A CDMO's stability study protocol should include CCS monitoring items: (1) visual inspection (discoloration, embrittlement and deposits on the container/closure); (2) container closure integrity testing (CCIT); (3) extractables/leachables trending; (4) headspace gas analysis (if applicable). These data not only support the stability report but also serve as evidence of the continued suitability of the CCS.

Supplier management SOP requirements: A CDMO should establish an SOP for packaging component supplier change notification, explicitly requiring suppliers to notify the CDMO before changing the formulation or manufacturing process. This requirement should be written into the Quality Agreement to ensure traceability across the supply chain.

III.E. Drug Products for Injection and Ophthalmic Drug Products

Original Text

These dosage forms share the common attributes that they are generally solutions, emulsions, or suspensions, and are all required to be sterile. Injectable dosage forms represent one of the highest risk drug products (see Table 1). Any contaminants present (as a result of contact with a packaging component or due to the packaging system's failure to provide adequate protection) can be rapidly and completely introduced into the patient's general circulation. Although the risk factors associated with ophthalmics are generally considered to be lower than for injectables, any potential for causing harm to the eyes demands caution.

1. Injectable Drug Products

Injectable drug products may be liquids in the form of solutions, emulsions, suspensions, or dry solids that are to be combined with an appropriate vehicle to yield a solution or suspension. Injections are classified as small-volume parenterals (SVPs), if they have a solution volume of 100 mL or less, or as large-volume parenterals (LVPs), if the solution volume exceeds 100 mL.

An SVP may be packaged in a disposable cartridge, a disposable syringe, a vial, an ampule or a flexible bag. An LVP may be packaged in a vial, a flexible bag, a glass bottle or, in some cases, as a disposable syringe.

Cartridges, syringes, vials, and ampules are usually composed of Type I or II glass, or polypropylene. Flexible bags are typically constructed with multilayered plastic. Stoppers and septa in cartridges, syringes, and vials are typically composed of elastomeric materials. The input (medication) and output (administration) ports for flexible bags may be plastic and/or elastomeric materials. An overwrap may be used with flexible bags to retard solvent loss and to protect the flexible packaging system from rough handling.

The potential effects of packaging component/dosage form interactions are numerous. Hemolytic effects may result from a decrease in tonicity and pyrogenic effects may result from the presence of impurities. The potency of the drug product or concentration of the antimicrobial preservatives may decrease due to adsorption or absorption. A cosolvent system essential to the solubilization of a poorly soluble drug can also serve as a potent extractant of plastic additives. A disposable syringe may be made of plastic, glass, rubber, and metal components, and such multicomponent construction provides a potential for interaction that is greater than when a container consists of a single material.

Injectable drug products require protection from microbial contamination (loss of sterility or added bioburden) and may also need to be protected from light or exposure to gases (e.g., oxygen). Liquid-based injectables may need to be protected from solvent loss, while sterile powders or powders for injection may need to be protected from exposure to water vapor.

For elastomeric components, data showing that a component meets the requirements of USP Elastomeric Closures for Injections will typically be considered sufficient evidence of safety. For plastic components, data from USP Biological Reactivity Tests will typically be considered sufficient evidence of safety. Whenever possible, the extraction studies should be performed using the drug product. If the extraction properties of the drug product vehicle may reasonably be expected to differ from that of water (e.g., due to high or low pH or due to a solubilizing excipient), then drug product should be used as the extracting medium. If the drug substance significantly affects extraction characteristics, it may be necessary to perform the extractions using the drug product vehicle. If the total of extracts significantly exceeds the amount obtained from water extraction, then an extraction profile should be obtained. It may be advisable to obtain a quantitative extraction profile of an elastomeric or plastic packaging component and to compare this periodically to the profile from a new batch of the packaging component. Extractables should be identified whenever possible.

For a glass packaging component, data from USP Containers: Chemical Resistance — Glass Containers will typically be considered sufficient evidence of safety and compatibility. In some cases (e.g., for some chelating agents), a glass packaging component may need to meet additional criteria to ensure the absence of significant interactions between the packaging component and the dosage form.

Performance of a syringe is usually addressed by establishing the force to initiate and maintain plunger movement down the barrel, and the capability of the syringe to deliver the labeled amount of the drug product.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The highest-risk nature of injectables: Any contaminant introduced by the packaging can enter the systemic circulation "rapidly and completely". This is why injectables occupy the highest-risk position in Table 1.

SVP vs LVP: Small-volume parenteral (≤100 mL) vs large-volume parenteral (>100 mL). SVP packaging options: cartridge, syringe, vial, ampule, flexible bag; LVP options: vial, flexible bag, glass bottle, syringe.

The four main interaction risks:

  1. Hemolytic effects: due to a change in osmolality (solvent loss alters tonicity)
  2. Pyrogenic effects: due to impurities introduced by packaging components
  3. Potency loss: due to adsorption/absorption
  4. Extractable contamination: cosolvent systems can act as powerful extractants

Analogy

The packaging risk of an injectable is like injecting food straight into the bloodstream: with oral administration there is at least the "first line of defense" of the gastrointestinal tract (first-pass effect), but an injectable bypasses all natural barriers. Trace substances leached from the packaging might be harmless if taken orally but could cause hemolysis or fever if injected directly. The multi-material construction of a prefilled syringe (plastic + glass + rubber + metal) is like a multi-material "chemical minefield": every material can be a source of extractables.

Key Notes

Solvent strategy for extraction studies (extremely important):

  • First choice: use the drug product itself as the extraction medium
  • If the extraction characteristics of the drug product may differ from those of water (high/low pH, solubilizing excipients) → the drug product must be used
  • If the drug substance significantly affects extraction → the drug product vehicle (placebo without API) may be used
  • If total extractables significantly exceed the amount extracted by water → an extraction profile must be obtained

This "escalation gradient" is FDA's core strategy for extraction studies on injectables. A CDMO must follow it when designing E&L studies.

The special case of glass: The USP Glass Containers test is usually sufficient. But drug products containing chelating agents may extract metal ions from the glass, so an additional compatibility assessment is needed.

Practical Application

A CDMO's extraction study design checklist:

  1. Confirm the pH and cosolvent composition of the product formulation to determine whether extraction with the drug product/vehicle is needed
  2. For elastomeric stoppers: USP <381> + extraction profile (drug product vehicle recommended)
  3. For plastic components: USP <87>/<88> + an extraction profile where necessary
  4. For glass vials: USP <660> Chemical Resistance + additional assessment if chelating agents are present
  5. Establish an extraction profile baseline and compare new batches against it periodically (at least annually)

III.F.1. Liquid-Based Oral Drug Products

Original Text

Typical liquid-based oral dosage forms are elixirs, emulsions, extracts, fluid extracts, solutions, gels, syrups, spirits, tinctures, aromatic waters, and suspensions. These products are usually nonsterile but may be monitored for changes in bioburden or for the presence of specific microbes.

These dosage forms are generally marketed in multiple-unit bottles or in unit-dose or single-use pouches or cups. The dosage form may be used as is or admixed first with a compatible diluent or dispersant. A bottle is usually glass or plastic, often with a screw cap with a liner, and possibly with a tamper-resistant seal or an overcap that is welded to the bottle. The same cap liners and inner seals are sometimes used with solid oral dosage forms. A pouch may be a single-layer plastic or a laminated material. Both bottles and pouches may use an overwrap, which is usually a laminated material. A single-dose cup may be metal or plastic with a heat-sealed lid made of a laminated material.

A liquid-based oral drug product typically needs to be protected from solvent loss, microbial contamination, and sometimes from exposure to light or reactive gases (e.g., oxygen).

For glass components, data showing that a component meets the requirements of USP Containers: Glass Containers are accepted as sufficient evidence of safety and compatibility. For LDPE components, data from USP Containers tests are typically considered sufficient evidence of compatibility. The USP General Chapters do not specifically address safety for polyethylene (HDPE or LDPE), polypropylene (PP), or laminate components. A patient's exposure to substances extracted from a plastic packaging component (e.g., HDPE, LDPE, PP, laminated components) into a liquid-based oral dosage form is expected to be comparable to a patient's exposure to the same substances through the use of the same material when used to package food. Based on this assumption, an appropriate reference to the indirect food additive regulations (21 CFR 174-186) is typically considered sufficient to establish safety of the material of construction, provided any limitations specified in the regulations are taken into consideration. This assumption is considered valid for liquid-based oral dosage forms which the patient will take only for a relatively short time (acute dosing regimen).

For liquid-based oral drug products which the patient will continue to take for an extended period (i.e., months or years (chronic drug regimen)), a material of construction that meets the requirements for indirect food additives will be considered safe — on that basis alone — only if the patient's exposure to extractables can be expected to be no greater than the exposure through foods, or the length of exposure is supported by toxicological information. For example, if the dosage form is aqueous-based and contains little or no cosolvent (or other substance, including the active drug substance, liable to cause greater extraction of substances from plastic packaging components than would be extracted by water), meeting the requirements of the indirect food additive regulations will usually satisfy the issue of safety.

If the dosage form contains cosolvents (or if, for any reason, it may be expected to extract greater amounts of substances from plastic packaging components than water), then additional extractable information may be needed to address safety issues.

Performance is typically not a factor for liquid-based oral drug products.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Differences in safety assessment for acute vs. chronic dosing:

Acute (short-term): Citing the indirect food additive regulations (21 CFR 174-186) is usually sufficient, on the assumption that exposure from drug packaging is comparable to that from food packaging.

Chronic (months to years): Citing only the food additive regulations is sufficient only if: (1) patient exposure to extractables does not exceed exposure from food; or (2) toxicological data support the duration of exposure. Formulations containing cosolvents need additional extractables information.

Analogy

The acute vs. chronic distinction is like eating out occasionally vs. eating a packed lunch every day. If the plastic lunch box leaches a trace of material now and then, one occasional meal makes little difference (acute). But if you reheat and eat from the same lunch box every day, the accumulated exposure deserves attention (chronic). FDA's logic is that the food packaging regulations assume a general dietary pattern, while the exposure pattern of long-term medication may exceed that assumption.

Key Notes

The "magnifying glass effect" of cosolvents: Formulations containing cosolvents such as alcohol, glycol or polysorbate extract more from plastic packaging than plain water does. FDA states explicitly that in this case "additional extractable information may be needed", even for an oral product.

The gap in USP for PE/PP/laminate safety: The USP General Chapters have no safety tests specifically for HDPE, LDPE, PP or laminate materials. This gap is filled by the indirect food additive regulations, subject to the usage limitations described above.

Practical Application

Sterile CDMOs seldom handle non-sterile oral liquids, but understanding this tiering logic helps build overall CCS knowledge. When a CDMO handles the accompanying diluent for an oral solution for injection reconstitution, the packaging safety assessment for the diluent should consider: (1) whether it contains cosolvents; (2) whether it is for long-term use; (3) whether citing only the food additive regulations is acceptable.

III.F.2. Topical Drug Products

Original Text

Topical dosage forms include aerosols, creams, emulsions, gels, lotions, ointments, pastes, powders, solutions, and suspensions. These dosage forms are generally intended for local (not systemic) effect and are generally applied to the skin or oral mucosal surfaces. Topical products also include some nasal and otic preparations as well as some ophthalmic drug products. Ophthalmic drug products are discussed in section III.E.2. Vaginal and rectal drug products may be considered to be topical if they are intended to have a local effect. Some topical drug products are sterile or may be subject to microbial limits. In these cases, additional evaluation may be necessary when determining the appropriate packaging.

A liquid-based topical product typically has a fluid or semi-solid consistency and is marketed in a single- or multiple-unit container (e.g., a rigid bottle or jar, a collapsible tube, or a flexible pouch). A powder product may be marketed in a sifter-top container. An antibacterial product may be marketed as part of a sterile dressing. There are also a number of products marketed as a pressurized aerosol or a hand-pumped spray.

A collapsible tube is usually constructed from metal or is metal-lined, from LDPE or from a laminated material. Tubes are identified as either blind-end or open-end. In the former, there is no product contact with the cap on storage. Usually, the size of the tube is controlled by trimming it to an appropriate length for the target fill volume. Fill volume is commonly determined as an in-process measurement using bulk density. Usually there is no cap liner, although the tube may have a liner. Aluminum tubes usually include a liner. A tube liner is frequently a lacquer or shellac whose composition should be stated. A tube is closed by folding or crimping the open end. The type of fold (roll or saddle) should be described, as well as the type and composition of any sealant. If the tube material is self-sealing through the application of heat alone, this should be stated. If the market package includes a separate applicator device, this should be described. Product contact is possible if the applicator is part of the closure, and therefore an applicator's compatibility with the drug product should be established, as appropriate.

The packaging system for a liquid-based topical product should deter solvent loss and should provide protection from light when appropriate. Because these dosage forms may be placed in contact with mucosal membranes or with skin that has been broken or otherwise compromised, the safety of the materials of construction for the packaging components should be evaluated. For liquid and semisolid dosage forms, the same information as described in section III.F.1 is accepted for establishing safety and compatibility. For solid dosage forms, an appropriate reference to the indirect food additive regulations is typically considered sufficient to establish safety.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Technical details of tube packaging:

1. Blind-end vs. open-end: With a blind-end tube, the product does not contact the cap during storage; an open-end tube needs to be folded/crimped to seal.

2. Tube liner: Usually a lacquer or shellac, which prevents direct contact between the metal tube and the product. Aluminum tubes usually have a liner.

3. Closure method: Roll fold or saddle fold, and the type and composition of the sealant.

Analogy

A collapsible tube is like a toothpaste tube: it looks simple, but there is a lot of science in the internal structure (tube wall material, liner, sealing method, fill-volume control). An aluminum tube needs a liner to keep the metal from reacting with the ointment, much as a frying pan needs a non-stick coating to keep food from sticking. The blind-end design means the ointment touches only the tube wall and liner during storage, not the cap, which removes one contact surface and its compatibility risk.

Key Notes

Higher safety considerations for topical products: Although applied externally, FDA specifically notes that these products may contact "mucosal membranes or skin that has been broken or otherwise compromised", and the permeability of broken skin approaches that of intravenous administration. The material safety of topical products therefore cannot be treated as entirely equivalent to that of solid oral dosage forms.

Sterile topical products: Some topical products must be sterile or meet microbial limits, such as sterile dressings and ophthalmic products. These need additional packaging evaluation.

III.F.3. Topical Delivery Systems

Original Text

Topical delivery systems are self-contained, discrete dosage forms that are designed to deliver drug via intact skin or body surface. USP Pharmaceutical Dosage Forms defines three types of topical delivery systems: transdermal, ocular, and intrauterine.

Transdermal systems are usually applied to the skin with an adhesive and may be in place for an extended period. Ocular systems are inserted under the lower eyelid, typically for seven days. Intrauterine systems are held in place without adhesive and may stay in place for a year.

A transdermal system is usually comprised of an outer barrier, a drug reservoir (with or without a rate-controlling membrane), a contact adhesive, and a protective liner. An ocular system usually consists of the drug formulation contained in a rate-controlling membrane. An intrauterine system may be constructed of a plastic material impregnated with active ingredients or a coated metal. It is shaped to remain in place after being inserted in the uterus.

Each of these systems is generally marketed in a single-unit soft blister pack or a preformed tray with a preformed cover or overwrap.

Compatibility and safety for topical delivery systems are addressed in the same manner as for topical drug products. Performance and quality control should be addressed for the rate-controlling membrane. Appropriate microbial limits should be established and justified for each delivery system. Microbiological standards are under development; therefore the review division for a specific application should be consulted.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three types of topical delivery systems:

1. Transdermal: Adhered to the skin, possibly for several days.

2. Ocular: Inserted under the lower eyelid, usually for 7 days.

3. Intrauterine: May remain in place for up to one year.

Rate-controlling membrane: The key component that controls the rate of drug release; its performance and QC are a focus of the submission.

Analogy

A rate-controlling membrane is like the flow regulator on a faucet: it determines how fast the drug is released onto the skin, into the eye or into the uterus. If membrane quality is inconsistent (thickness varying between batches), it is like every faucet delivering a different flow, so some patients get too much drug and others too little. This is why performance QC is critical for delivery systems.

Key Notes

The CCS of a transdermal patch is very special: the patch itself is both the drug product and the delivery device, while the blister pack that holds it is the CCS in the traditional sense. FDA's material safety requirements for a patch are the same as for topical products, but its performance (drug delivery rate) requirements are closer to those for precise dose-delivery devices such as MDIs and DPIs.

An intrauterine system may remain in the body for up to one year, which places very high demands on long-term material compatibility and safety.

III.G. Solid Oral Dosage Forms and Powders for Reconstitution

Original Text

The most common solid oral dosage forms are capsules and tablets. For the purpose of this guidance, oral powders and granules for reconstitution are also included in this group.

The risk of interaction between packaging components and a solid oral dosage form is generally recognized to be small. Powders that are reconstituted in their market container, however, have an additional possibility of an interaction between the packaging components and the reconstituting fluid. Although the contact time will be relatively short when compared to the component/dosage form contact time for liquid-based oral dosage forms, it should still be taken into consideration when the compatibility and safety of the container closure system is being evaluated.

A typical container closure system is a plastic (usually HDPE) bottle with a screw-on or snap-off closure and a flexible packaging system, such as a pouch or a blister package. A typical closure consists of a cap, often with a liner, and frequently with an inner seal. If used, fillers, desiccants, and other absorbent materials are considered primary packaging components.

The most common forms of flexible packaging are the blister package and the pouch. A blister package usually consists of a lidding material and a forming film. The lidding material is usually a laminate which includes a barrier layer (e.g., aluminum foil) with a print primer on one side and a sealing agent (e.g., a heat-sealing lacquer) on the other side. The sealing agent contacts the dosage form and the forming film. The forming film may be a single film, a coated film, or a laminate. A pouch typically consists of film or laminate which is sealed at the edges by heat or adhesive. Leak testing is usually performed on flexible packages as part of the in-process controls.

Solid oral dosage forms generally need to be protected from the potential adverse effects of water vapor. Protection from light and reactive gases may also be needed. For example, the presence of moisture may affect the decomposition rate of the active drug substance or the dissolution rate of the dosage form. The container should have an intrinsically low rate of water vapor permeation, and the container closure system should establish a seal to protect the drug product.

Three USP Standards for Water Vapor Permeation:

  1. Polyethylene Containers (USP <661>): Conducted on containers heat-sealed with foil laminate; evaluates container only. Acceptance criteria per USP <671>.
  2. Single-Unit and Unit-Dose Containers (USP <671>): Measures WVPR of a single-unit or unit-dose CCS. Establishes acceptance criteria for five standards (Class A–E containers).
  3. Multiple-Unit Containers (USP <671>): Intended for prescription drugs. Inner seal removed before testing. Results reflect contributions through container and through seal. Two standards (tight and well-closed containers).

For solid oral dosage forms, a reference to the appropriate indirect food additive regulation for each material of construction is typically considered sufficient evidence of safety. However, for a powder for reconstitution dosage form, reference only to the indirect food additive regulations as evidence of safety for the materials of construction is not recommended.

The USP monographs for Purified Cotton and Purified Rayon will typically be considered sufficient standards to establish the safety of these materials as fillers in the packaging of tablets or capsules, with the following caveats: cotton need not meet the monograph requirements for sterility, fiber length, or absorbency; and rayon need not meet the monograph requirements for fiber length or absorbency. Appropriate tests and acceptance criteria for identification and for moisture content should be provided for both cotton and rayon filler. Rayon has been found to be a potential source of dissolution problems for gelatin capsules and gelatin-coated tablets and this characteristic should be considered when choosing a filler.

If a desiccant or other absorbent material is used, the composition should be provided (or an appropriate DMF referenced). The component should differ in shape and/or size from the tablets or capsules with which it is packaged. This will help distinguish between the component and the dosage form.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three main USP water vapor permeation tests:

1. USP <661> (PE Containers): Tests only the container itself (sealed with foil).

2. USP <671> Single-Unit: Tests the whole CCS, with 5 classes (Class A-E).

3. USP <671> Multiple-Unit: Tests the CCS after the inner seal is removed, with 2 classes (tight/well-closed).

Blister package structure: Lidding material (usually an aluminum foil barrier layer + print primer + heat-sealing lacquer) + forming film (single film, coated, or laminate). The sealing agent contacts the dosage form and the forming film.

Analogy

Water vapor permeation testing is like a "moisture-proofing test" for a house. USP <661> tests only the moisture resistance of the walls themselves (with perfectly sealed doors and windows); the USP <671> multiple-unit test tests the whole house with the doors and windows open, including leakage at the wall and window/door joints. Testing after removal of the inner seal reflects the actual conditions of use once the patient has opened the pack.

A blister package is like an egg carton: the forming film makes the cavities that hold the eggs (tablets), and the lidding foil covers and seals them. The aluminum foil layer is the key moisture barrier.

Key Notes

Higher safety requirements for powder for reconstitution: FDA states explicitly that for powders for reconstitution, "reference only to the indirect food additive regulations is not recommended", because the reconstituted liquid contacts the packaging and the safety requirements should therefore be higher than for solids.

Dissolution problems with rayon filler: Rayon has been found to cause dissolution problems with gelatin capsules and gelatin-coated tablets, so this must be considered when selecting a filler.

Physical distinction of desiccants: The shape and size of a desiccant should differ from the tablets/capsules to prevent patients from swallowing it by mistake. This is a simple but important safety design principle.

Practical Application

Solid oral dosage forms are not core business for a sterile CDMO, but powders for injection (lyophilized powders for reconstitution) are extremely common in sterile CDMOs. CCS considerations for such products: (1) water vapor protection of the lyophilized powder is critical, and the stopper's MVTR (moisture vapor transmission rate) is a key parameter; (2) the reconstituted liquid contacts the container, so the safety requirements fall between those for solid oral products and injectables; (3) evaluation should follow Case 2c (compatibility) and Case 2s (safety) in Table 2.

Key Concepts

A first-principles approach to "other dosage forms": For dosage forms not covered by the preceding sections, FDA provides three basic considerations:

1. The compatibility/safety concerns determined by the route of administration and the nature of the dosage form.

2. The type of protection the CCS should provide.

FDA's guidance on "other dosage forms" is like the "improvisation guide" at the end of a cookbook: there is no specific recipe, but it gives you three basic principles (balancing flavors, controlling heat, pairing ingredients) so you can handle any new dish. Original Text

For an approved application (NDA, ANDA or BLA), a change to a container closure system, to a component of the container closure system, to a material of construction for a component, or to a process involving one of the above must be reported to the application. The filing requirements are specified under 21 CFR 314.70 (supplements and other changes to an approved application) for an NDA or ANDA, and under 21 CFR 601.12 (changes to an approved application) for a BLA. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Postapproval change: Any change to the CCS, its components, material composition or related processes in an approved application. All changes must be reported to FDA.

21 CFR 314.70: Governs the reporting requirements for supplements and changes to an NDA/ANDA.

21 CFR 601.12: Governs the reporting requirements for postapproval changes to a BLA (Biologics License Application).

Analogy

Think of an approved packaging system as a building permit: once approved, any structural change (changing materials, altering the design, adjusting the process) must be reported back to the building authority. V. Type III Drug Master Files

Original Text

A. General Comments

The responsibility for providing information about packaging components rests foremost with the applicant of an NDA, ANDA or BLA, or the sponsor of an IND. This information may be provided to the applicant by the manufacturer of a packaging component or material of construction and may be included directly in the application. Any information that a manufacturer does not wish to share with the applicant or sponsor (i.e., because it is considered proprietary) may be placed in a Type III DMF and incorporated into the application by a letter from the manufacturer to the applicant which authorizes reference to the DMF. The letter of authorization should specify the firm to whom authorization is granted, the component or material of construction being described, and where the information and/or data is located in the file by page number and/or date of submission. This last item is especially important for files that contain information on multiple components or have several volumes.

Information in a Type III DMF is not restricted to data of a proprietary nature. DMF holders may include in their files as much or as little information as they choose. In addition, a manufacturer of a packaging component is not required to maintain a Type III DMF. Without a DMF there is no procedure for the Agency to review proprietary information except by submission to the application.

The Agency ordinarily reviews a DMF only in connection with an application (IND, NDA, ANDA, or BLA). If the combined information from the application and the DMF is not adequate to support approval of the application or safety for the IND, then the Agency may request additional information from the applicant and/or the DMF holder, as appropriate.

In the event of a change in the DMF, the holder of a DMF must notify the holder of each application supported by the DMF (21 CFR 314.420(c)). Notice should be provided well before the change is implemented to allow the applicant or sponsor enough time to file a supplement or an amendment to the affected application.

General information on format and content of a DMF and a LOA may be found in the CDER Guideline for Drug Master Files (September 1989).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Type III DMF: The DMF type dedicated to packaging materials. It allows packaging material manufacturers to submit proprietary information to FDA without disclosing it directly to the drug applicant.

Letter of Authorization (LOA): A formal letter from the DMF holder to the applicant, which must state: (1) the authorized company; (2) the components or materials concerned; (3) the location of the information in the DMF (page number/submission date).

Analogy

A Type III DMF is like a bank safe deposit box: the packaging material supplier locks its confidential formulation inside and gives the customer an "authorization key" (LOA). The FDA reviewer opens it only when reviewing a specific application. This protects trade secrets while still giving FDA the complete information it needs to make an approval decision.

Key Notes

DMF change notification obligation (21 CFR 314.420(c)): The DMF holder must notify all affected applicants "well in advance" of implementing a change. This is extremely important for a CDMO: if your packaging material supplier updates the DMF content, you must receive timely notice and assess whether it needs to be reported to your own clients and the relevant applications.

FDA usually reviews a DMF only when reviewing a specific application. A DMF is not "approved" on its own; it is simply a repository for supporting documents.

Practical Application

A CDMO's supplier management procedure should include: (1) maintaining a list of all referenced DMFs; (2) signing a Quality Agreement with the DMF holder that clearly defines change notification obligations and timelines; (3) periodically confirming the validity of the LOA and the accuracy of the information locations; (4) when DMF content changes, assessing the impact on all affected products and notifying clients.

VI. Bulk Containers

Original Text

A. Containers for Bulk Drug Substances

Drug substances are generally solids, but some are liquids or gases.

The container closure system for storage or shipment of a bulk solid drug substance is typically a drum with double LDPE liners that are usually heat sealed or closed with a twist tie. A desiccant may be placed between the bags.

The drum provides protection from light and mechanical strength to protect the liner during shipment and handling. The majority of the protection from air and moisture is provided by the liner. Because LDPE is not a particularly good moisture barrier, a drug substance that is moisture sensitive may need additional protection. An alternative to a LDPE bag is a heat-sealable laminate bag with a comparatively low rate of water vapor transmission.

Qualification of the packaging system is usually based on establishing compatibility and safety of the liner but may also include characterization for solvent or gas transmission (see section III.B).

The container closure system for the storage or shipment of a bulk liquid drug substance is typically plastic, stainless steel, a glass-lined metal container, or an epoxy-lined metal container with a rugged, tamper-resistant closure. Qualification of the container closure system may include characterization for solvent and gas permeation, light transmittance, closure integrity, ruggedness in shipment, protection against microbial contamination through the closure, and compatibility and safety of the packaging components as appropriate (see section III.B).

The application (or Type II DMF) should include a detailed description of the complete container closure system for the bulk drug substance as well as a description of the specific container, closure, all liners, inner seal, and desiccant (if any), and the composition of each component. A reference to the appropriate indirect food additive regulation is typically considered sufficient to establish the safety of the materials of construction (also note the discussion on this subject in section III). The tests, methods, and criteria for the acceptance and release of each packaging component should be provided.

Stability studies to establish a retest period for bulk drug substance in the proposed container closure system should be conducted with fillers or desiccant packs in place (if used). Smaller versions which simulate the actual container closure system may be used. Stability recommendations for container closure systems of different types are described in the Guideline for Submitting Documentation for the Stability of Human Drugs and Biologics (February 1987).

Container closure systems for compressed medical gases are discussed in section III.H.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Bulk Drug Substance Container: A container system used to store or ship drug substance. For solids this is usually a drum with double LDPE liners; for liquids it is usually stainless steel, glass-lined metal or plastic containers.

Retest Period: The period during which the drug substance remains stable in the specified container, after which it must be retested before use. Stability studies must be conducted with the desiccant in place.

Analogy

Packaging of bulk drug substance is like the storage of food ingredients: dry goods (solids) go in large drums with sealed inner bags; sauces (liquids) go in stainless steel drums. The moisture barrier of an LDPE inner bag is like an ordinary plastic bag for biscuits: it holds up for a while, but long-term storage needs a laminated foil bag. A stability study is like testing how long an ingredient keeps in a given package without spoiling.

Key Notes

LDPE has a relatively high water vapor transmission rate (WVTR). For moisture-sensitive drug substances (such as the API of many lyophilized products), an LDPE bag may not provide sufficient protection. In this case, consider a laminate bag (usually with an aluminum foil barrier layer).

Stability studies may use "a smaller version simulating the actual CCS". This is a pragmatic approach, because running stability studies in the actual large drum (such as a 200 L drum) is very inconvenient in practice. However, the simulated version must be representative of the protective properties of the actual system.

Practical Application

When receiving a client's API, a CDMO should verify: (1) that the CCS description of the shipping container matches what is recorded in the DMF/application; (2) that the desiccant is in place and effective; (3) that the material is within its retest period. If the container type does not match or the retest period has been exceeded, a deviation investigation should be initiated and the client notified.

B. Containers for Bulk Drug Products

A container closure system for bulk drug products may be used for storage prior to packaging or for shipment to repackagers or contract packagers. In all cases, the container closure system should adequately protect the dosage form and should be constructed of materials that are compatible and safe.

Container closure systems for on-site storage have generally been considered a CGMP issue under 21 CFR 211.65. However, if a firm plans to hold bulk drug products in storage, then the container closure system and the maximum storage time should be described and justified in the application. In addition, stability data should be provided to demonstrate that extended storage in the described containers does not adversely affect the dosage form. Even when the storage time before packaging will be short, a firm should use a container closure system that provides adequate protection and that is manufactured from materials that are compatible and safe for the intended use (see section III.B).

A container closure system for the transportation of bulk drug products to contract packagers (section II.C.3) should be described in the application. The container closure system should be adequate to protect the dosage form, be constructed with materials that are compatible with product being stored, and be safe for the intended use. The protective properties of the shipping container are verified by the practice of including annual batches of the packaged product in postapproval stability studies.

A container closure system specifically intended for the transportation of a large volume of drug product to a repackager (section II.C.3), whether for a solid or liquid dosage form, is considered a market package. The package should meet the same requirements for protection, compatibility, and safety as a smaller market package; should be included in the stability studies for application approval and in the long term stability protocol; and should be fully described in the application. The length of time that the dosage form will spend in the bulk container may be a factor in determining the level of detail of the supporting information. Two examples of a large-volume shipping package are a 10,000-tablet HDPE pail with tamper-evident closure, and a 10-liter polyethylene terephthalate (PET) container with a screw cap closure with dispenser attachment for a liquid drug product. Both are intended for sale to a mass distribution pharmacy. A special case is the pharmacy bulk package which is described in USP <1>.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

On-site Storage Container: Generally treated as a CGMP issue (21 CFR 211.65), but if long-term storage is planned, the CCS and the maximum storage time must be described in the application and supported by stability data.

Large-Volume Shipping Package → Market Package: A key concept: when a bulk package is used to ship drug product to a repackager for sale, FDA treats it as a market package, which must meet all the same requirements as a small marketed package.

Analogy

An on-site bulk holding tank is like a temporary container in a restaurant kitchen: short-term holding is routine operation (controlled under CGMP), but if you will hold the food for several days, you must make sure the container material does not react with the food and can maintain temperature and hygiene. A drum of 10,000 tablets shipped to a retail pharmacy, by contrast, is equivalent to the packaging of a shelf product and must meet exactly the same quality and stability standards as a small bottle.

Key Notes

A critical distinction: For bulk drug product shipped to a contract packager, the container only needs to be described in the application; but a bulk container shipped to a repackager is treated as a market package and must be included in the stability testing program and the long-term stability protocol. The regulatory requirements for the two are at completely different levels.

Even for short-term storage, a CCS that provides adequate protection and is compatible and safe must still be used. "Short-term" is not a reason to lower the standard.

Practical Application

A common CDMO scenario: after sterile filling, the finished product may need to be held temporarily in a bulk container awaiting labeling and secondary packaging. In this case: (1) define the bulk holding CCS and the maximum holding time in the BPR/application; (2) support the holding time with stability data; (3) ensure the material compatibility of the holding container has been validated. If the CDMO also acts as a contract packager receiving a client's bulk product, the shipping container must also be brought within the scope of control.

  1. Why does FDA treat a bulk container shipped to a repackager as a market package, rather than as a contract packager's shipping container? What is the patient safety logic behind this?
  2. What test items should a CDMO's bulk holding time validation cover? Answer with reference to the characteristics of sterile injectables.

Section Summary

  • Any postapproval CCS change (system, component, material, process) must be reported under 21 CFR 314.70 (NDA/ANDA) or 21 CFR 601.12 (BLA).
  • A Type III DMF is a mechanism for protecting proprietary information on packaging materials; FDA reviews it only when reviewing a specific application; the LOA must specify precisely the scope of authorization and the location of the information.
  • DMF holders are obliged to notify all affected applicants before a change is implemented (21 CFR 314.420(c)).
  • Bulk drug substance containers: drum + LDPE liners for solids, stainless steel / glass-lined containers for liquids; stability studies may use a simulated version.
  • Bulk drug product containers: on-site storage is a CGMP issue but must be supported by stability data; a bulk container shipped to a repackager is treated as a market package, with the highest regulatory requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four core requirements of 21 CFR 211.94:

(a) Non-reactive / non-additive / non-absorptive: Does not alter SISPQ.

(b) Adequate protection: Resists deterioration or contamination from external factors.

(c) Clean / sterilized / depyrogenated: Suitable for its intended use.

(d) Written standards: There must be written standards, acceptance criteria and test methods.

These four points form the regulatory basis of CGMP control of packaging materials and are also the main focus of FDA inspections.

Analogy

21 CFR 211.94 is like a restaurant's four requirements for tableware: (a) the tableware must not react chemically with the food (the non-stick coating must not flake off); (b) the tableware must protect the food from dust and pests (containers with lids); (c) the tableware must be clean and, where necessary, sanitized (heat-sterilized tableware); (d) the cleaning and sanitizing procedures must have a written SOP.

Key Notes

Limitations of the Indirect Food Additive Regulations (21 CFR 174-186): These regulations were originally written for food packaging and do not apply directly to drug packaging. However, FDA accepts citing them as safety evidence for packaging materials of solid oral dosage forms. Note that for oral liquids (especially those used long-term) and injectables, citing only the food additive regulations is usually not enough; additional extraction/toxicological evaluation is needed.

Child-resistant packaging exemptions: Use within institutions (hospitals/nursing homes) and physician samples can be exempted. However, prescriptions dispensed to patients on discharge must still comply with the PPPA requirements.

Attachment C: Extraction Studies

Original Text

An extraction study of a packaging component typically involves exposing a sample of the component, often subdivided into small pieces to increase surface area, to an appropriate solvent system at elevated temperatures, followed by chemical analysis. The purpose of elevated temperature is to increase the rate of extraction, so that a short experimental time may simulate a longer exposure time at room temperature, or to maximize the amount of extractables obtained from a sample.

The methods employed to analyze the resulting extracts vary, depending on the purpose of the extraction study and the nature of the packaging component. The extraction solvent may be evaporated to concentrate the extracts or to determine the total weight of nonvolatile extractables. Appropriate methods, such as HPLC or gas chromatography, may be used to obtain qualitative or quantitative extraction profiles of volatile or nonvolatile extractables.

Extraction studies may be conducted during the qualification of packaging components for any of the following purposes:

  • To perform USP characterization tests on plastics (USP <661>) or elastomers (USP <381>)
  • To perform USP Biological Reactivity Tests (USP <87> and <88>) on plastics or elastomers
  • To obtain qualitative extraction profiles of plastics or elastomers
  • To obtain quantitative extraction profiles of plastics or elastomers
  • To evaluate whether the FDA indirect food additive regulations provide an adequate indicator of safety

Extraction studies may also be conducted on a routine basis as a quality control measure to monitor the chemical compositions of elastomeric or other packaging components.

The solvent that should be used in an extraction study depends on the purpose of the study. The ideal situation is for the extracting solvent to have the same propensity to extract substances as the dosage form, thus obtaining the same quantitative extraction profile. For this study, the preferred solvent would be the drug product or placebo vehicle. When feasible, the dosage form itself would be used. A stronger extracting solvent than the drug product would be used to obtain a qualitative extraction profile that would be used to establish quality control criteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Extraction study: A sample of the packaging component (usually cut into pieces to increase surface area) is exposed to a suitable solvent and extracted at elevated temperature, and the extract is then analyzed. This is the core experimental method of Extractables & Leachables (E&L) studies.

Extractables: Chemicals extracted from the packaging component under accelerated conditions (high temperature, strong solvents), representing everything that could migrate into the drug product under "worst-case" conditions.

Leachables: Substances that actually migrate into the drug product under normal conditions of use; a subset of the extractables.

Analogy

An extraction study is like brewing tea with hot water: raising the temperature accelerates extraction, just as boiling water brews tea far faster than cold brewing. All the substances that can be dissolved out of the tea leaves (extractables) are like everything obtained by vigorous extraction with boiling water, while the substances that actually dissolve at everyday drinking temperature (leachables) are a small part of that. FDA requires first using "boiling water" to find all possible extractables, then assessing how much patients would be exposed to under normal conditions.

Key Notes

Solvent selection logic (extremely important):

  • Quantitative profile: Use the drug product itself or a placebo vehicle as the solvent; this best reflects actual leaching.
  • Qualitative profile: Use an extraction solvent stronger than the drug product to find all possible extractables, for use in establishing QC criteria.

This solvent selection logic directly affects the design of E&L studies and is a focus of FDA review.

Extraction studies are used not only for initial qualification but also as a routine QC tool to monitor the consistency of the chemical composition of packaging components. This is especially important for elastomeric stoppers.

Formula

The five main purposes of an extraction study:
1. USP <661> plastics characterization tests
2. USP <381> elastomers characterization tests
3. USP <87>/<88> biological reactivity tests
4. Qualitative extraction profile → QC criteria
5. Quantitative extraction profile → Safety evaluation

Practical Application

When qualifying a new packaging component, a CDMO should consider the following in designing the extraction study: (1) whether the product is aqueous or contains cosolvents (such as polysorbate 80 or ethanol), since cosolvents enhance extraction capacity; (2) the sample surface area/volume ratio should simulate actual conditions of use; (3) the elevated temperature should accelerate extraction reasonably without altering the chemical nature of the material. Running both a qualitative study (strong solvent) and a quantitative study (drug product vehicle) is recommended. The results can be compared against the PQRI Safety Concern Threshold (SCT) and Qualification Threshold (QT) to assess safety.
