0.1 Regulatory Basis & FDCA Link

Original Text

Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FDCA) — Adulteration Provision:

"A drug shall be deemed to be adulterated… if the methods used in, or the facilities or controls used for, its manufacture, processing, packing, or holding do not conform to or are not operated or administered in conformity with current good manufacturing practice to assure that such drug meets the requirements of this chapter as to safety and has the identity and strength, and meets the quality and purity characteristics, which it purports or is represented to possess."
— FDCA §501(a)(2)(B), 21 U.S.C. §351(a)(2)(B)

This provision is the ultimate enforcement anchor for all CGMP requirements. Any drug product that deviates from CGMP is deemed adulterated under federal law — regardless of whether it actually caused patient harm.

21 CFR 210.1 — Status of Current Good Manufacturing Practice Regulations:

"(a) The regulations set forth in this part and in parts 211 through 226 and parts 600 through 680 of this chapter contain the minimum current good manufacturing practice for methods to be used in, and the facilities or controls to be used for, the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of a drug to assure that such drug meets the requirements of the act as to safety, and has the identity and strength, and meets the quality and purity characteristics that it purports or is represented to possess."

"(b) The failure to comply with any regulation set forth in this part and in parts 211 through 226 and parts 600 through 680 of this chapter in the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of a drug shall render such drug to be adulterated under section 501(a)(2)(B) of the act and such drug, as well as the person who is responsible for the failure to comply, shall be subject to regulatory action."

21 CFR 210.2 — Applicability of Current Good Manufacturing Practice Regulations:

"(a) The regulations in this part and in parts 211 through 226 and parts 600 through 680 of this chapter as they may pertain to a drug; (1) apply to all persons who are manufacturers, packers, or holders of such drug marketed in the United States; (2) apply to all drugs…"

"(b) Where a person engages in only some operations subject to the regulations in this part, parts 211 through 226 and parts 600 through 680 of this chapter, such person shall comply with the regulations governing those operations."

"(c) An investigational drug for use in a Phase 1 study… is subject to legal requirements of the act relating to the safety of the drug product… [The regulations in part 211 do not apply to the manufacture of such investigational drug for human use, except where the investigational drug has been made available for a Phase 2 or Phase 3 investigation…]"

21 CFR 210.3 — Definitions:

The following definitions apply to Parts 210 through 226 and Parts 600 through 680 unless the context otherwise requires:

Batch
A specific quantity of a drug or other material that is intended to have uniform character and quality, within specified limits, and is produced according to a single manufacturing order during the same cycle of manufacture. (§210.3(b)(2))
Lot
A batch, or a specific identified portion of a batch, having uniform character and quality within specified limits; or, in the case of a drug product produced by continuous process, a specific identified amount produced in a unit of time or quantity in a manner that assures its having uniform character and quality within specified limits. (§210.3(b)(10))
Lot number, control number, or batch number
Any distinctive combination of letters, numbers, or symbols, or any combination of them, from which the complete history of the manufacture, processing, packing, holding, and distribution of a batch or lot of drug product or other material can be determined. (§210.3(b)(11))
Component
Any ingredient intended for use in the manufacture of a drug product, including those that may not appear in such drug product. (§210.3(b)(3))
Drug product
A finished dosage form, for example, tablet, capsule, solution, etc., that contains an active drug ingredient generally, but not necessarily, in association with inactive ingredients. The term also includes a finished dosage form that does not contain an active ingredient but is intended to be used as a placebo. (§210.3(b)(4))
Drug substance
An active ingredient that is intended to furnish pharmacological activity or other direct effect in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease or to affect the structure or any function of the body of humans or other animals. The term includes those parts of the molecule that impart the activity and may be modified to form salts, esters, or other noncovalent derivatives used as formulated intermediates. Drug substance does not include intermediates used in the synthesis of such ingredient. (§210.3(b)(7))
Fiber
Any particulate contaminant with a length at least three times greater than its width. (§210.3(b)(8))
Non-fiber releasing filter
Any filter, which after any appropriate pretreatment such as washing or flushing, will not release fibers into the component or drug product that is being filtered. All filters composed of asbestos are deemed to be fiber releasing filters. (§210.3(b)(13))
In-process material
Any material fabricated, compounded, blended, or derived by chemical reaction that is produced for, and used in, the preparation of the drug product. (§210.3(b)(9))
Strength
(1) The concentration of the drug substance (for example, weight/weight, weight/volume, or unit dose/volume basis), and/or (2) The potency, that is, the therapeutic activity of the drug product as indicated by appropriate laboratory tests or by adequately developed and controlled clinical data (expressed, for example, in terms of units by reference to a standard). (§210.3(b)(16))
Theoretical yield
The quantity that would be produced at any appropriate phase of manufacture, processing, or packing of a particular drug product, based upon the quantity of components to be used, in the absence of any loss or error in actual production. (§210.3(b)(17))
Actual yield
The quantity that is actually produced at any appropriate phase of manufacture, processing, or packing of a particular drug product. (§210.3(b)(1))
Percentage of theoretical yield
The ratio of the actual yield (at any appropriate phase of manufacture, processing, or packing of a particular drug product) to the theoretical yield (at the same phase), stated as a percentage. (§210.3(b)(14))
Active ingredient
Any component that is intended to furnish pharmacological activity or other direct effect in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or to affect the structure or any function of the body of humans or other animals. The term includes those components that may undergo chemical change in the manufacture of the drug product and be present in the drug product in a modified form intended to furnish the specified activity or effect. (§210.3(b)(7) — see also drug substance definition)
Inactive ingredient
Any component other than an active ingredient. (§210.3(b)(8) cross-reference)
Acceptance criteria
Numerical limits, ranges, or other criteria for acceptance of the results of analytical procedures. While this specific term is defined in ICH Q6A and referenced throughout Part 211 (particularly §§211.160, 211.165, 211.194), Part 210.3 provides the foundational framework under which specification criteria are understood. Acceptance criteria must be established, documented, and consistently applied. (see §211.160(b))
Expiration date / Expiry
The date placed on the label of a drug product designating the time during which a batch of the product is expected to remain within the approved shelf life specifications if stored under defined conditions, and after which it must not be used. Part 211 §211.137 requires expiration dating for drug products. Expiration dating is a prerequisite for certain yield calculations and retention period determinations under §211.180(b). (§211.137)
Representative sample
A sample that consists of a number of units that are drawn based on rational criteria, such as random sampling and intended to assure that the sample accurately portrays the material being sampled. (§210.3(b)(15))

Additional Definitions Cross-Referenced in Part 211 (not in §210.3, but important for operational context):

  • Quality control unit (QU) — defined by function in §211.22: "a unit that has the responsibility and authority to approve or reject all components, drug product containers, closures, in-process materials, packaging material, labeling, and drug products…"
  • Out-of-specification (OOS) result — not in Part 211 text, but addressed in FDA's 2006 OOS Guidance; test result falling outside established specifications (§211.165) or acceptance criteria triggers investigation under §211.192 / §211.194
  • Process validation — not defined in Part 211, but the concept underlies §§211.100, 211.110, 211.113; defined in FDA's 2011 Process Validation Guidance as collecting and evaluating data from process design through commercial production to establish scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering a quality product

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: FDCA 501(a)(2)(B) - The Foundation of Regulatory Enforcement

FDCA §501(a)(2)(B) is the legal pillar of the entire CGMP system. You need to understand this provision to follow FDA's enforcement logic.

Key point: no harm is required for a violation
This provision uses a "deemed adulterated" mechanism. If the methods used in manufacture or the facility controls do not comply with CGMP, the drug is "deemed adulterated", regardless of whether that batch actually turns out to have a quality problem. This means:

  • FDA does not have to wait until patients are harmed before it acts
  • Even if all finished-product test results pass, a process that violates CGMP is still a violation
  • Both the responsible person and the drug itself can be the target of regulatory action

CGMP is a "minimum standard"
21 CFR 210.1(a) states explicitly minimum current good manufacturing practice. This wording is very important: the regulation says "you must not fall below this standard", not "this is enough". The ICH Q10 pharmaceutical quality system (PQS) and lifecycle management approach are voluntary enhancements built on top of the CGMP minimum.

Practical Use of the Definitions: Why Do They Matter on the Shop Floor?

Batch vs. lot
Many people treat batch and lot as synonyms, but FDA defines them differently: one batch can be divided into several lots (for example, in aseptic filling, a single bulk solution is filled in two runs and each run constitutes a lot). In the batch record, the batch number and batch size must correspond clearly to these definitions.

Regulatory significance of the lot number
The lot number must allow the "complete history" to be traced, covering every step of manufacture, processing, packing, holding and distribution. This is the regulatory basis of traceability and the starting point of a deviation investigation. If you cannot trace from the lot number to all of the records, that is a violation of the §211.188 batch record requirements.

Component vs. drug substance
A component is broadly any "raw material/ingredient", including the active ingredient (API) and excipients; a drug substance refers specifically to the pharmacologically active API. Telling the two apart matters a great deal for incoming material control (§211.80-211.99) and labeling control.

Theoretical yield / actual yield / percentage of theoretical yield
§211.192 requires that, during batch record review, yield be evaluated against the approved range (§211.188(b)(11)). Yield deviations outside the range must be investigated. In CDMO plants, yield control is one of the most common Form 483 observations: yield limits not established, or yield outside the range released without investigation.

Common Deficiency Warnings

Exception for Phase 1 investigational drugs
§210.2(c) states that Phase 1 investigational drugs are not subject to all of the Part 211 requirements, although the safety-related provisions of the FDCA still apply. When a CDMO accepts Phase 1 work, it must confirm which version of the requirements applies and whether the client has a specific Phase 1 Quality Agreement. Once a product moves into Phase 2/3, Part 211 applies in full.

Limits of the Phase 1 exception: what still applies?
Even though most Part 211 provisions are not mandatory for Phase 1, FDA and clients still expect the following in practice:

  • Identity testing: Even for Phase 1, every batch of API and critical excipients should still be identity tested. This is a basic safety requirement, not a CGMP requirement
  • Basic batch records: There must be sufficient manufacturing records to trace the batch history during an adverse event investigation
  • Sterility assurance (where applicable): Injectable Phase 1 products must still be sterile; otherwise this is a direct violation of the FDCA safety requirements
  • Labeling accuracy: Errors in the labeling of clinical trial drugs (such as dose or storage conditions) can directly endanger subjects, so labeling is outside the Phase 1 exception

CDMO practical advice: Even though the Phase 1 regulatory requirements are looser, most CDMOs choose to manufacture Phase 1 drugs to a standard close to Part 211, because: (1) clients usually require it; (2) complete records help later clinical work and commercial filings; (3) it avoids a gap in quality standards at the Phase 1 → Phase 2 transition.

0.2 Dual Compliance Architecture

Original Text

21 CFR 211.1 — Scope:

"(a) The regulations in this part contain the minimum current good manufacturing practice for preparation of drug products for administration to humans or animals."

"(b) Any production facility where drugs are produced which are not subject to part 211 may be subject to other regulations in this chapter."

"(c) The regulations in §§211.80 through 211.94 apply to drug substances used as components of drug products subject to this part and the components shall also comply with applicable requirements of subpart E of part 211."

21 CFR 211.3 — Definitions:

"The definitions set forth in §210.3 of this chapter apply in interpreting the regulations in this part."

Relationship to 21 CFR Part 600 (Biologics):

Biological products licensed under the Public Health Service Act (42 U.S.C. §262) are subject to CGMP requirements under both 21 CFR Part 211 AND 21 CFR Parts 600–680 (biological product standards). Key provisions from Part 600:

  • 21 CFR 600.10 — Personnel qualifications for biologics manufacturing
  • 21 CFR 600.11 — Physical establishment requirements
  • 21 CFR 600.12 — Records requirements (supplement to §211.180–211.198)
  • 21 CFR 601.12 — Changes to an approved BLA (supplements and annual reports)
  • 21 CFR 606.xx — Blood and blood components (own CGMP framework within Part 600)

Conflict resolution rule (21 CFR 210.2):
Where any regulation applicable to a specific type of product (e.g., Part 600 for biologics) is in conflict with Part 211, the more specific regulation takes precedence. The general principle: specific supersedes general.

Tutorial Commentary

Dual Compliance Architecture: Pharmaceutical CDMO vs. Biologics CDMO

Understanding how Part 210, Part 211 and Part 600 relate to one another is the foundation of CDMO compliance management.

General pharmaceutical CDMO (small molecules, non-biologics):

  • Main regulatory framework: 21 CFR Part 211
  • General definitions: 21 CFR Part 210
  • Basis for penalties: FDCA §501(a)(2)(B)

Biologics CDMO (mAbs, vaccines, cell therapy, gene therapy):

  • Must comply with both: 21 CFR Part 211 AND 21 CFR Parts 600-680
  • In case of conflict: the Part 600 series (more specific) takes precedence
  • For example, Part 600.12 has additional record retention requirements; even though Part 211.180 already has basic requirements, both sets must be followed, and where they differ, the stricter one applies

Practical impact:
A CDMO that takes on both small-molecule and antibody programs must have a QMS (Quality Management System) that can distinguish the applicable regulations for each product line. Client audit and FDA inspection checklists must also reflect this difference. A common audit finding is a biologics CDMO whose SOPs cite only Part 211 and do not incorporate the Part 600 requirements.

Part 211 §211.1(c) - When does Part 211 apply to a drug substance?

§211.1(c) states that §§211.80-211.94 (the component control subpart) apply to drug substances used as components of drug products. This means:

  • If a CDMO only synthesizes API (drug substance) and the API does not go directly into a drug product made at the same site, the site is mainly governed by ICH Q7 and 21 CFR Part 211 Subpart E, rather than the whole of Part 211
  • If a CDMO makes both API and finished dosage forms, the API operations are governed by both Part 211 Subpart E and the overall Part 211 framework
  • This distinction directly affects inspection scope: when FDA inspects an "API + finished drug" CDMO, it will review the whole of Part 211, including Subpart E

Common Compliance Gaps at Biologics CDMOs

The following are common observations made at biologics CDMOs in FDA Warning Letters, arising from a failure to integrate the dual requirements of Part 211 and Part 600:

  • Insufficient record retention period: Part 600.12 requires some biologics (such as blood products) to be retained for longer than the basic period in Part 211.180
  • BLA changes not reported: Part 601.12 requires the BLA holder to report manufacturing process changes to FDA (Prior Approval Supplement / CBE-30 / Annual Report), but the Quality Agreement between the CDMO and the client does not clearly divide responsibilities, so the client is unaware that a change must be reported
  • Insufficient evidence of personnel qualifications: Part 600.10 sets specific qualification requirements for personnel engaged in biologics manufacture, which are more specific than Part 211.25

0.3 Regulatory Architecture: How FDA Uses 210/211

Original Text

FDA Enforcement Mechanism — The Inspection Pyramid:

FDA enforces 21 CFR Parts 210/211 through a graduated series of actions, each with increasing legal consequences:

CGMP as a "Floor, Not a Ceiling":

21 CFR 210.1(a) explicitly states these regulations contain the minimum CGMP. This "floor, not a ceiling" principle has significant practical implications:

  • Companies may — and often must — implement controls exceeding CGMP minimums to ensure product quality and patient safety
  • FDA expects that state-of-the-art manufacturing science and quality systems (e.g., process analytical technology, continuous manufacturing, risk-based approaches) will be employed even when not explicitly required by the CFR text
  • FDA Guidance documents (not legally binding) describe the agency's current thinking on how to meet or exceed CGMP standards

ICH Q10 Alignment with 21 CFR 210/211:

FDA endorsed ICH Q10 (Pharmaceutical Quality System) in its 2009 guidance. ICH Q10 defines a lifecycle approach to pharmaceutical quality that supplements and builds upon CGMP requirements. Key connections:

  • ICH Q10 §1.6: "An effective pharmaceutical quality system for the manufacture of pharmaceutical products should incorporate GMP as described in relevant GMP guidance from ICH…"
  • ICH Q10 encourages companies to go beyond minimum CGMP (e.g., through KASA — Knowledge-Aided Assessment & Structured Application, product and process understanding, continual improvement)
  • FDA inspection programs such as the Pharmaceutical Quality Assessment System (PQAS) and the CGMP Operational Model reflect ICH Q10 lifecycle concepts

Tutorial Commentary

Inspection Logic: How Does FDA Use 210/211 to Issue a Form 483?

Understanding FDA's inspection mindset helps a CDMO QA team prepare effectively before an inspection.

The legal nature of FDA Form 483
Form 483 is not a "notice of violation"; it is a "list of possible violations observed during the inspection". Each 483 observation usually contains:

  • The CFR provision cited (such as 21 CFR 211.192)
  • The specific condition observed (such as "In the batch records, the operator initials for step X are missing, in 17 batches")
  • Sometimes supporting data (such as the proportion of batches with incomplete OOS investigations)

Key concept: FDA cites specific provisions of Part 211, not Part 210. Part 210 is the background framework; Part 211 is the basis for 483 citations.

Escalation logic from 483 to Warning Letter
Not every 483 observation escalates to a Warning Letter. Common reasons for escalation:

  1. Repeat findings (a 483 was issued at the last inspection and the same problem appears again)
  2. Systemic failure (not a single event but a loss of control across the whole system)
  3. Data integrity problems (FDA regards these as among the most serious CGMP failures)
  4. A dishonest or inadequate CAPA response

The Practical Meaning of "Floor, Not Ceiling"

CGMP minimum standard ≠ target standard

FDA has stressed repeatedly, in its 2014 CGMP white paper and in successive guidances, that the CGMP regulations are the floor that guards against the worst case (unsafe or ineffective drugs), not the target line for quality work. For a CDMO, this has several important practical implications:

  • Client contract requirements are often higher than the regulations: The Technical Agreements and Quality Agreements of large pharmaceutical clients (Big Pharma originators) usually require more than the CGMP standard, for example shorter deviation closure times and stricter environmental monitoring action limits
  • Advanced approaches are an expectation, not a bonus: For late-stage (Phase 3 / commercial) products, FDA inspectors expect to see ICH Q10 lifecycle management elements, such as Continued Process Verification (CPV) and the Annual Product Review / Product Quality Review (APR/PQR)
  • PAT and RTRT are not in the body of Part 211, but FDA supports them: Process analytical technology (PAT) and real-time release testing (RTRT) are not expressly required by Part 211, but FDA guidance (the 2004 PAT Guidance, ICH Q8/Q10/Q11/Q12) encourages their adoption, and they fit the spirit of going beyond the CGMP minimum

The CDMO Inspection Readiness "High-Frequency 483 Defense List"

Based on the FDA 483 database and Warning Letter statistics, the following are the Part 211 observations most common at CDMOs, with the corresponding preparation priorities:

1. §211.192 - Batch Record Review
Preparation priorities: Ensure all batch records are fully reviewed by the quality unit (QU) before release; all deviations and anomalies are recorded with an assessment; yield calculations are documented and within the approved range. Common deficiencies: incomplete electronic signature records in the eBR system; operators making entries after the fact.

2. §211.194 - Laboratory Records
Preparation priorities: Raw data are fully retained; analysts record contemporaneously; LIMS audit trails are not disabled. Common deficiencies: audit trails in LIMS/CDS not enabled or able to be disabled by an administrator (a Part 11 issue); OOS investigations not completed within 30 days.

3. §211.100 - Written Procedures & Deviations
Preparation priorities: All SOPs are effective and controlled; 100% of deviations are recorded; CAPAs have an assigned owner and completion date; deviation closure rate and timeliness are tracked regularly. Common deficiencies: deviations communicated only verbally at shift handover are not recorded; deviation investigations only describe the phenomenon without analyzing the root cause.

4. §211.68 - Computerized Systems
Preparation priorities: All systems subject to Part 11 have validation records; audit trails are generated automatically by the computer and cannot be disabled; access control is documented. Common deficiencies: shared "User IDs" on a system; audit trail logs never reviewed periodically.

5. §211.22 - Quality Unit Responsibilities
Preparation priorities: The QU is organizationally independent of production; the QU has real authority for final batch release, free from commercial pressure; QU headcount is commensurate with the size of the plant. Common deficiencies: the QU Director also holds production-related duties; the QU "rubber-stamps" releases because of understaffing.

Data Integrity - The Highest-Risk Topic in CGMP

In recent years, the most common and most serious category of observations in FDA Warning Letters has been data integrity failures. Although Part 211 has no express "data integrity" clause, FDA builds its enforcement basis from the following combination of provisions:

  • §211.68 (computerized systems) - systems must reproduce data accurately and have audit trails
  • §211.194 (laboratory records) - records must be timely, accurate and complete
  • §211.192 (batch record review) - batch records must be complete, and deviations must be investigated
  • §211.100 (written procedures) - procedures must be followed, and deviations must be recorded

Special CDMO risks: CDMOs often face the complexity of "multiple clients, multiple systems, multiple sites", together with commercial pressure (delivery dates, capacity). This makes the following data integrity risks particularly likely:

  • Rejecting an OOS result and retesting without recording the initial test (commonly called "cherry picking")
  • Backdating or pre-signing original records
  • Inconsistent clock settings across LIMS/CDS systems, so audit trail timestamps cannot be trusted
  • Shared User IDs (actions cannot be attributed to an individual)

Once FDA finds a data integrity problem during an inspection, it usually escalates directly to a Warning Letter and may trigger an Import Alert, preventing products made at the facility from entering the U.S. market.

0.4 The Full CGMP Regulatory Universe: Where 210/211 Fit

Original Text

The CFR Title 21 Drug CGMP Framework — At a Glance:

21 CFR Parts 210 and 211 are the core general CGMP requirements for finished pharmaceuticals. They exist within a larger framework of Title 21 regulations that together govern drug manufacturing from API synthesis through to commercial distribution.

FDA's Integrated CGMP Inspection Approach:

FDA conducts drug manufacturing inspections through two primary programs:

  • Domestic Surveillance Inspections: Routine inspections of domestic manufacturers; typically every 2–3 years for high-risk sites. Inspectors use the Compliance Program Guidance Manual (CPGM) 7356.002 as their inspection framework, which organizes the inspection around Part 211 subparts.
  • Foreign Inspections (GDUFA/PDUFA-funded): FDA inspects foreign API and finished drug manufacturers that supply the U.S. market. Same Part 211 standards apply regardless of geographic location.
  • Pre-Approval Inspections (PAI): Triggered by NDA/ANDA/BLA filing; FDA inspects manufacturing sites before approving the application to verify CGMP compliance and process validation.
  • For-Cause Inspections: Triggered by complaints, recalls, adverse events, or data integrity tips; highest intensity; most likely to result in Warning Letter or import alert.

The Establishment Inspection Report (EIR) and OAI/VAI/NAI Designations:

  • NAI (No Action Indicated): No significant CGMP objections; routine surveillance finding
  • VAI (Voluntary Action Indicated): Minor CGMP deficiencies; voluntary corrections expected; typically 483 issued but no Warning Letter
  • OAI (Official Action Indicated): Significant CGMP violations; regulatory action (Warning Letter, import alert, consent decree) likely

Tutorial Commentary

The Regulatory Map: A CDMO's "Sense of Terrain"

For CDMO QA, RA and compliance professionals, being able to quickly locate "which CFR Parts apply to the product in hand" is a core professional skill.

Decision flow (decision tree):

  1. Is it a drug product? → Yes → Part 210/211 baseline applies
  2. Is it a biologic (with a BLA)? → Yes → add Parts 600-680
  3. Is it a combination product? → Yes → check Part 4; Part 820 may also apply
  4. Is it at the IND stage? Phase 1? → Yes → §210.2(c) exception, but the safety requirements still apply
  5. Are there electronic records? → Yes → Part 11 applies (predicate rule = the relevant provisions of Part 211)
  6. Is it an API (drug substance) synthesis facility? → Yes → mainly ICH Q7 applies (adopted by FDA), rather than the whole of Part 211

A complex CDMO (producing API + finished product + biologics + Phase 1/2 clinical drugs at the same time) may face overlapping application of Part 4, Part 11, Part 210/211, Part 600 and Part 312. This calls for a clear "Regulatory Applicability Matrix" as a regulatory management tool.

Pre-Approval Inspection (PAI): The CDMO's "Big Exam"

For many CDMOs, the PAI is the most important type of FDA inspection, because it directly determines whether the client's new drug application is approved.

When a PAI is triggered:

  • The client submits an NDA (505(b)(1) or 505(b)(2)) or an ANDA (generic drug)
  • The client submits a BLA (biologic)
  • A major manufacturing process change to an approved product (Prior Approval Supplement)

Key PAI inspection areas (directly mapped to Part 211):

  • Batch records of the process validation batches (§211.188)
  • Analytical method validation data (§211.160, §211.194)
  • Whether the APR/PQR has been established (§211.180(e))
  • Batch release procedures (§211.22, §211.192)
  • Change control records (§211.100, §211.68)

Common reason CDMOs fail a PAI:
The validation batches are inconsistent with the process described in the application (the process was "quietly modified" after validation, but the application documents were not updated and no revalidation was performed). This deficiency violates both §211.100 (not following written procedures) and the Application Integrity Policy (21 CFR 314).

1.1 §211.22 — Quality Control Unit (QCU): The Independence Requirement

Original Text

§ 211.22(a) — There shall be a quality control unit that shall have the responsibility and authority to approve or reject all components, drug product containers, closures, in-process materials, packaging materials, labeling, and drug products, and the authority to review production records to assure that no errors have occurred or, if errors have occurred, that they have been fully investigated. The quality control unit shall be responsible for approving or rejecting drug products manufactured, processed, packed, or held under contract by another company.

§ 211.22(b) — Adequate laboratory facilities for the testing and approval (or rejection) of components, drug product containers, closures, packaging materials, in-process materials, and drug products shall be available to the quality control unit.

§ 211.22(c) — The quality control unit shall have the responsibility for approving or rejecting all procedures or specifications impacting on the identity, strength, quality, and purity of the drug product.

§ 211.22(d) — Adequate laboratory facilities for the testing and approval (or rejection) of drug products shall be available to the quality control unit. The responsibilities and procedures applicable to the quality control unit shall be in writing; such written procedures shall be followed.

Operational meaning of "written procedures shall be followed" (§211.22(d)): This clause is deceptively important. It is not enough to have written QCU SOPs — those SOPs must actually be followed in practice. FDA inspectors look for consistency between stated procedure and actual practice. Examples of noncompliance: a written procedure says QCU must review all batch records within 48 hours of completion, but batch records are routinely reviewed weeks later; a SOP mandates QCU signature on all change control approvals, but unsigned change controls are found in files. Gaps between written procedure and practice frequently become Warning Letter content, not merely 483 observations.

"The quality control unit shall be responsible for approving or rejecting drug products manufactured, processed, packed, or held under contract by another company."
— 21 CFR §211.22(a), contract manufacturing clause

Subpart A General Scope — §211.1(a): The regulations in this part contain the minimum current good manufacturing practice for preparation of drug products (excluding positron emission tomography drugs) for administration to humans or animals. The failure to comply with any regulation in 21 CFR Part 211 causes a drug product to be adulterated under section 501(a)(2)(B) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

§211.3 — Definitions: Key terms include: batch (a specific quantity of a drug or other material that is intended to have uniform character and quality); lot (a batch or a specific identified portion of a batch); component (any ingredient intended for use in the manufacture of a drug product); drug product container (any object that holds the drug product); quality control unit (any person or organizational element designated by the firm to be responsible for the duties relating to quality control).

Adulteration consequence of §211.1(a): The link between CGMP failure and drug adulteration is not merely administrative. Under Section 501(a)(2)(B) of the FD&C Act, a drug product manufactured, processed, packed, or held in conditions that do not conform to CGMP is deemed adulterated — regardless of whether the product itself has any measurable quality defect. This means a drug can be removed from the market, subjected to a recall, and trigger criminal liability for responsible persons, even if laboratory testing shows it meets all specifications. CGMP compliance is not optional because it produces good products; it is legally mandatory because its absence makes the product legally adulterated.

§210 vs §211 — the regulatory relationship: 21 CFR Part 210 establishes the general CGMP framework and the consequence of non-compliance (adulteration). 21 CFR Part 211 provides the detailed operational requirements. Part 210 §210.2(a) states that the failure to comply with Part 211 constitutes a failure to comply with Part 210 as well — so both parts are triggered together in any enforcement action. Understanding this dual-part structure is important when reading FDA Warning Letters, which may cite both §210 and §211 violations arising from the same observation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is QCU Independence So Important?

§211.22 is the provision of 21 CFR Part 211 most often cited by FDA investigators on Form 483. The core question is not "do you have a QA department," but "can the QA department exercise its reject authority without being influenced by production pressure."

Three tests of independence:

  • Organizational independence: The head of the QCU (QA Director / VP of Quality) should not report to the VP of Production, but directly to the company's top management.
  • Decision independence: The QCU can reject a batch without the agreement of the production department.
  • Resource independence: The QCU must have adequate laboratory facilities (§211.22(b)) and must not depend on production in order to carry out testing.

Most Common 483 Observations: QCU-Related

Frequent FDA 483 deficiency patterns:

  • The QCU released a batch without adequately investigating an out-of-specification (OOS) result
  • Errors in production records were not fully investigated by the QCU (second half of §211.22(a))
  • QCU written procedures do not exist or are not followed (§211.22(d))
  • The QCU did not review new procedures or specification changes that affect product quality (§211.22(c))
  • Product from a contract manufacturer was released by someone other than the QCU of the contract giver (the originator)

CDMO Context: Allocation of QCU Responsibility in Contract Manufacturing

The last sentence of §211.22(a) is extremely important in CDMO business: "For drugs manufactured under contract, release responsibility of the QCU rests with the contract acceptor (the CDMO)."

Common misunderstanding: the client (contract giver) says, "We have our own QA that will review," and this does not relieve the CDMO's QCU of its release responsibility. Both companies must have a QCU exercising its duties: the CDMO's QCU releases the "manufactured batch," and the client's QCU releases the "final commercial product."

The Quality Agreement must clearly define which QCU activities are performed by whom. FDA will ask to see this agreement during an inspection.

QCU Design Challenges for Small Companies / Start-Up Drug Makers

§211.22 allows "any person or organizational element" to serve as the QCU, including an outsourced or one-person QCU. This is critical for small pharmaceutical companies or start-up CDMOs with limited resources:

  • Outsourced QCU (Contract QA Services): This is legal, but the "responsibility and authority" in §211.22(a) must actually be granted in the outsourcing contract. The written contract must give the outsourced QCU real authority to veto production decisions, not merely an advisory role of "review and recommend." Outsourced QA services without veto authority do not satisfy §211.22.
  • One-person QCU: One person can legally serve as the entire QCU, provided that the person is adequately qualified (§211.25) and organizationally independent of production. In a very small facility, having the owner also act as head of the QCU may technically comply, but FDA will scrutinize the actual independence of such an arrangement very closely.
  • Matrix QCU: Some companies have staff from several departments share QCU duties (for example, the production supervisor releases in-process material and a QA specialist performs final batch release). Such an arrangement must define each person's QCU responsibility boundaries in a written procedure and ensure that critical release decisions are made by people with adequate independence.

The key FDA question: "Is your QCU truly technically capable of, and actually authorized to, reject a batch, even if that means the company suffers a financial loss?" The answer to this question is found not on the organization chart but in the record of actual historical cases.

QCU Scope of Authority at a Glance: §211.22(a)-(c) Cross-Reference

Analogy

The role of the QCU is like that of the "supervising structural engineer" on a construction project: production (the contractor) does the building, but every critical milestone (foundation, structure, completion) must be signed off by an independent structural engineer before work can continue. If the contractor could sign off its own work, the whole safety system would collapse.

The logic of §211.22(a) is exactly the same: the production department cannot approve batches it made itself. An organizationally independent QCU must review and approve them before they can reach the market.

"Right-First-Time" Culture vs. Inspection-Driven Compliance

The design philosophy of §211.22 implies an important cultural question: many companies see the QCU as the "last line of defense," on the idea that production may make mistakes as long as the QCU catches them at the end. This "inspection-driven compliance" model is the low-maturity quality culture that FDA criticizes.

FDA's 2011 Process Validation Guidance and broader quality systems guidance state clearly that quality should be "built into the process" rather than assured only by end-product testing. The purpose of the broad authority §211.22 gives the QCU is to let it intervene proactively and prevent problems, not merely to release or reject passively.

Signs of a QCU in a "Right-First-Time" culture:

  • The QCU leads root cause analysis (RCA) and CAPA, rather than merely reviewing batch records
  • The QCU takes part in design reviews of new products or processes and provides quality input before problems appear
  • The QCU regularly reports quality metrics to top management, including OOS rate, deviation rate and CAPA completion rate
  • Deviation and reprocessing rates are low and stable (rather than "many deviations, all investigated and closed by the QCU")

The harshest language in FDA Warning Letters is usually aimed at facilities with "a large number of deviations and investigations, all closed quickly without effective CAPA," which is the hallmark of a "compliant but unhealthy" quality culture.

Cross-Section Extensions of the §211.22 QCU Responsibilities

Organizational Links Between §211.22 and §211.42 (Buildings and Personnel Flow)

Although the full content of §211.42 (facility requirements) is covered in Section 2 of this document, QCU responsibilities are closely tied to facility design:

  • Cleanroom classification, personnel flow and equipment segregation design must all be defined in written procedures approved by the QCU under §211.22(c)
  • The QCU's right of access to production areas (§211.22(a): the QCU may review production records) means QCU staff must be able to enter the relevant production areas to conduct on-site audits
  • Qualification reports (IQ/OQ/PQ) for sterile facilities must be approved by the QCU to ensure the facility design meets quality requirements

1.2 §211.25 — Personnel Qualifications

Original Text

§ 211.25(a) — Each person engaged in the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of a drug product shall have education, training, and experience, or any combination thereof, to enable that person to perform the assigned functions. Training shall be in the particular operations that the employee performs and in current good manufacturing practice (including the current good manufacturing practice regulations in this chapter and written procedures required by these regulations) as they relate to the employee's functions. Training in current good manufacturing practice shall be conducted by qualified individuals on a continuing basis and with sufficient frequency to assure that employees remain familiar with CGMP requirements applicable to them.

§ 211.25(b) — Each person responsible for supervising the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of a drug product shall have the education, training, and experience, or any combination thereof, to perform assigned functions in such a manner as to provide assurance that the drug product has the quality and purity it purports or is represented to possess.

§ 211.25(c) — There shall be an adequate number of qualified personnel to perform and supervise the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of each drug product.

"Training shall be conducted by qualified individuals on a continuing basis and with sufficient frequency to assure that employees remain familiar with CGMP requirements applicable to them."
— 21 CFR §211.25(a), continuing GMP training requirement

Interpreting "continuing basis" and "sufficient frequency": FDA does not specify a numerical frequency (e.g., annual) for continuing GMP training. This is deliberate — it is a risk-based standard. Factors that affect required frequency include: turnover rates (high turnover = more frequent refresher training), complexity of operations, history of deviation events linked to human error, and regulatory changes. A facility with frequent CGMP deviations attributable to human error and no increased training frequency is a strong 483 candidate. Industry convention is a minimum of annual GMP refresher training, but the legal standard is "sufficient to assure familiarity" — if annual is not enough for a particular workforce, more frequent training is required.

§211.25(c) — Adequate Number of Qualified Personnel: This clause is sometimes called the "staffing adequacy" requirement. FDA has cited facilities where understaffing caused CGMP failures — situations where one QC analyst was required to perform simultaneous laboratory testing on multiple batch releases, or where a single QA reviewer was responsible for reviewing hundreds of batch records with inadequate time. Inadequate staffing is a systemic CGMP violation, not merely an operational challenge.

Accountability for supervisors under §211.25(b): The supervisor qualification standard is more demanding than the general worker standard. A supervisor must perform functions "in such a manner as to provide assurance that the drug product has the quality and purity it purports or is represented to possess." This is an outcomes-based standard: the supervisor is not just responsible for following procedures, but for the quality outcome of the activities under their supervision. Practically, this means supervisors must: understand the quality implications of deviations from procedure; know when to escalate issues to QCU; and be able to identify whether an operation has been performed correctly even without direct personal observation of every step. A supervisor who rubber-stamps documentation without substantive review may satisfy the letter of the training requirement but fails the supervisory accountability standard of §211.25(b).

Tutorial Commentary

Regulatory Difference Between "Qualified" and "Trained"

This is the most commonly misunderstood point of §211.25. The regulation requires a combination of education, training and experience, not merely "completing a training course."

Trained: The employee has read the SOP and signed to confirm it. This is only the minimum threshold for compliance.

Qualified: The employee has adequate education, training hours and hands-on experience to perform the GMP functions of the position independently. Qualification should be confirmed through a competency assessment, not just a training signature.

During an inspection, FDA may ask to see: (1) the qualification requirements in the job description; (2) documentation of the employee's educational background; (3) training records; (4) competency assessment results.

Systemic Significance of §211.25 in FDA Enforcement

Although §211.25 itself is not a frequently cited Warning Letter provision (compared with §211.22 or §211.192), inadequate personnel qualification is often the root cause of serious CGMP failures rather than the provision that is directly cited. The logic of an FDA Warning Letter usually runs as follows:

"Your facility had many OOS results and deviations → the investigation found the cause was operator error → tracing back showed the operator's training records were missing or incomplete → root cause: the training and qualification requirements of §211.25 were not met → QCU oversight under §211.22 therefore also failed."

This "chain of violations" pattern is very common in Warning Letters. An apparently isolated operating error, if its root cause traces back to a systemic training gap, often leads to the QCU independence provision (§211.22) and CAPA effectiveness (§211.192, written investigations) being cited together.

Lesson for CDMOs: When a production deviation occurs, the first question is not only "what went wrong in this operation," but "is there a training and qualification system adequate to support this operation and prevent the same problem from recurring." The answer determines whether the CAPA is an "immediate correction" or a "systemic improvement."

Training Matrix Design Principles

A training program that complies with §211.25 should include the following elements:

  • Initial Training: Completed when new staff join, covering CGMP basics plus position-specific SOPs
  • Continuing Training: Periodic refresher training (usually annual); the regulation requires "sufficient frequency"
  • Change-Driven Training: Triggered by SOP revisions, equipment changes and the issuance of new regulations
  • Corrective Training: Triggered when a CAPA or investigation finds a training gap

Trainer qualification (last sentence of §211.25(a)): Training must be given by "qualified individuals." Trainers themselves also need documented trainer qualification.

Comparison of Training Record Elements

Analogy

The "qualified" standard of §211.25 is like the pilot licensing system: a pilot must pass written exams (education), meet flight-hour requirements (experience) and pass simulator assessments (competency testing) to obtain a license. Merely "reading the flight manual and signing" is nowhere near enough.

The same logic applies to qualifying pharmaceutical operators. An aseptic filling operator must have: (1) adequate education in the life sciences; (2) complete training records on the relevant SOPs; (3) an on-the-job training (OJT) assessment of actual operations; and (4) a competency assessment (usually including observed simulated filling or media fill) before being considered a "qualified aseptic filling operator."

Additional requirements for supervisors under §211.25(b): Supervisors must not only be qualified themselves but must also perform their functions "in such a manner as to provide assurance that the drug product has the safety, identity, strength, quality, and purity that it purports or is represented to possess." This means supervisors must understand how their supervisory activities affect product quality, including an understanding of the quality system and the judgment to apply it, not just the execution of operations.

Frequent 483 Observations on Personnel Training Under §211.25

The most common 483 observations on training records:

  • Incomplete training records: The SOP version the employee used for an operation is not in the training record (for example, the SOP has been updated to Rev. 3 but the employee has training records only for Rev. 1)
  • No annual CGMP training: The training system has only onboarding training and lacks the continuing training program the regulation requires
  • Trainers without qualification documents: The trainers responsible for training other employees have no trainer qualification records of their own
  • Training effectiveness not confirmed: Employees "read" the SOP but the company cannot demonstrate that they "understood" it (no competency assessment)
  • Missing training after CAPA: The CAPA actions in an investigation report mention "retraining," but no corresponding training record can be found in the training system
  • Insufficient personnel (§211.25(c)): Batch records show the same QA reviewer reviewed an unrealistic number of batches in one day, suggesting a serious shortage of resources

1.3 §211.28 — Personnel Responsibilities: Health, Apparel & Hygiene

Original Text

§ 211.28(a) — Any person shown at any time (either by medical examination or supervisory observation) to have an apparent illness or open lesions that may adversely affect the safety or quality of drug products shall be excluded from direct contact with components, drug product containers, closures, in-process materials, and drug products until the condition is corrected or determined by competent medical personnel not to jeopardize the safety or quality of drug products. All personnel shall be instructed to report to supervisory personnel any conditions that may have such an adverse effect.

§ 211.28(b) — Personnel shall wear clothing appropriate for the duties they perform. Protective apparel, such as head coverings, face masks, gowns, and gloves, shall be worn as necessary to protect drug products from contamination.

§ 211.28(c) — Personnel shall practice good sanitation and health habits.

§ 211.28(d) — Only personnel authorized by supervisory personnel shall enter those areas of the buildings and facilities designated as limited-access areas.

"Any person shown at any time to have an apparent illness or open lesions that may adversely affect the safety or quality of drug products shall be excluded from direct contact."
— 21 CFR §211.28(a), health exclusion clause

Scope of "direct contact" under §211.28(a): The exclusion applies to direct contact with components, drug product containers, closures, in-process materials, and drug products — essentially the same scope as QCU oversight under §211.22(a). An ill person is excluded from any activity where physical contact with product-contact items could occur. This includes assembly operations, sampling, laboratory analysis handling open samples, and visual inspection. Personnel who perform only documentation or computer work, and who do not physically contact product or product-contact surfaces, are not within the direct exclusion clause — though supervisory judgment and good hygiene practice (§211.28(c)) still apply.

§211.28(c) — Good sanitation and health habits: This deceptively broad clause is the legal basis for requiring handwashing procedures, prohibition of eating and drinking in manufacturing areas, no use of personal cosmetics (nail polish, jewelry) in clean areas, and general personal hygiene standards. The specifics of what constitutes "good sanitation and health habits" are not enumerated in the regulation — they are defined by the firm's written procedures (required by §211.22(d) and cross-referenced throughout Part 211). FDA interprets violations of detailed gowning or hygiene SOPs as simultaneously violating §211.28(c).

Medical examination under §211.28(a): The regulation allows health conditions to be identified by either "medical examination or supervisory observation." This creates a dual detection system. Supervisory observation is practical for day-to-day management — a supervisor noticing a coughing, visibly ill employee. Medical examination may be required for pre-employment health screening or for return-to-work assessment after illness. The firm's written procedure should define when each mechanism applies and what documentation is required in each case. Most pharmaceutical facilities use an occupational health program that interfaces with the GMP health exclusion SOP.

Tutorial Commentary

§211.28 Is the CGMP "Floor," Not the "Ceiling"

§211.28 sets the baseline minimum requirements (baseline floor) that apply to all drug manufacturing. For non-sterile manufacturing (such as oral solid dosage forms), strict compliance with §211.28 is usually sufficient.

For sterile manufacturing, however, §211.28 is only the starting point. Higher expectations come from:

  • FDA aseptic processing guidance (2004 / 2022 draft): Gowning qualification of aseptic filling personnel, assessment of aseptic technique, and personnel investigations of environmental monitoring failures
  • EU GMP Annex 1 (2022): Detailed gowning requirements for each cleanroom grade (A/B/C/D), gowning procedures, hand disinfection frequency, and colony count monitoring for each person on each entry
  • PIC/S: Requirements corresponding to Annex 1

In an inspection, FDA investigators may cite different levels of requirements depending on the facility type. Understanding this "hierarchy" helps in responding correctly to observations.

Role of §211.28 in FDA Enforcement Cases

§211.28 is relatively rarely cited alone as the main 483 observation; it more often appears as part of a group of systemic deficiencies. Typical enforcement patterns include:

Pattern 1: Inadequately documented gowning procedures. The facility has gowning requirements but no written SOP, or an SOP exists but actual practice does not match it. FDA will cite both §211.28(b) (lack of appropriate protective apparel) and §211.22(d) (procedures not followed).

Pattern 2: No system for reporting personnel health conditions. §211.28(a) requires that "all personnel" be instructed to report to supervisors any health conditions that may adversely affect drug products. If the facility has no written health reporting procedure (which symptoms must be reported, how to report, who receives the report, how it is handled), it lacks the system needed to meet the second half of §211.28(a).

Pattern 3: Inconsistent gowning observed in the aseptic core. When FDA investigators observe behavior on site that does not meet gowning requirements (such as uncovered stray hair or continuing to work with a damaged glove), they will cite §211.28(b) even if a written procedure exists, because the regulation requires actual practice, not just the existence of documents.

Pattern 4: Unauthorized persons entering restricted areas. The access authorization requirement of §211.28(d), where there is no written authorization matrix or access control records, is often observed during FDA inspections, particularly in the management of visitors and maintenance personnel.

Designing the Health Exclusion Procedure for §211.28(a)

The regulation requires a written procedure to address the following:

  • A list of reportable health conditions such as fever, diarrhea, rash, open wounds and respiratory infections
  • Steps for when a supervisor observes an abnormal health condition
  • Medical evaluation requirements before return to work ("confirmed by competent medical personnel")
  • Record requirements: should health exclusion events be recorded? (Recommended, so that they are available for review)

Additional considerations for sterile facilities: Some companies require aseptic filling personnel to stop entering grade A/B areas for operations while they have cold symptoms, even if the symptoms are mild. This is stricter than the "apparent illness" standard of §211.28(a) and is consistent with the spirit of Annex 1.

Analogy: The "Minimum Standard" Logic of §211.28

The position of §211.28 in the regulatory hierarchy can be understood through a food safety analogy: §211.28 is like a "basic food hygiene law" that says cooks may not work with a fever, must wear hairnets and must wash their hands often. This is the baseline requirement for every food handling environment, applying from street-food stalls to Michelin-starred restaurants.

But the hygiene requirements of an operating room (sterile surgical gowns, double gloves, pre-operative disinfection procedures) go far beyond basic food hygiene rules. Likewise, the personnel requirements of a sterile drug manufacturing environment (gowning qualification, environmental monitoring, behavioral controls in grade A/B areas) go far beyond the text of §211.28. These higher requirements come from regulatory guidance (FDA aseptic processing guidance, EU Annex 1) and facility-specific SOPs, not from §211.28 itself.

Compliance strategy takeaway: When writing personnel hygiene and gowning SOPs for a sterile facility, do not aim to "comply with §211.28." Aim to "meet the requirements of Section X of the FDA aseptic processing guidance and Section Y of EU Annex 1," and the minimum requirements of §211.28 will naturally be included.

Compliant Design for Restricted-Area Access Under §211.28(d)

This provision requires a written authorization system controlling personnel entry into specific areas. Practical design:

  • Access Authorization Matrix: lists the areas each position may enter
  • Access rights in electronic access control systems combined with the data integrity requirements of §211.68
  • Visitor / maintenance personnel entry records: date, time, escort, purpose of entry
  • GMP training as a prerequisite for access authorization

Cross-Cutting Issue of Personal Electronic Devices (PEDs) with §211.28(c)(d)

In recent years, the entry of personal electronic devices (PEDs, including mobile phones and smart watches) into manufacturing areas has become a significant GMP compliance issue, spanning §211.28 and §211.68:

  • §211.28(b)(c): Apparel and hygiene. Personal devices can be vectors of cross-contamination (surface microorganisms). In sterile manufacturing, bringing in a personal device violates gowning procedures and runs against the requirement for "good sanitation habits." Many facilities explicitly prohibit personal devices in cleanrooms of grade C/D and above.
  • §211.28(d): Access authorization. The access authorization procedure should cover items carried in (including electronic devices), not just the people themselves.
  • §211.68: Data integrity. Photographing GMP records or equipment screens with a personal phone can create uncontrolled electronic copies, a data integrity risk (unauthorized copies of GMP records). Written procedures should clearly prohibit, or strictly limit, the use of personal devices for photography in manufacturing areas.

A written SOP should address all three aspects together, establishing an "Electronic Device Management Procedure for Manufacturing Areas" and communicating it to all relevant personnel as part of entry training.

1.4 §211.34 — Consultants

Original Text

§ 211.34 — Consultants advising on the manufacture, processing, packing, or holding of drug products shall have sufficient education, training, and experience, or any combination thereof, to advise on the subject for which they are retained. Records shall be maintained stating the name, address, and qualifications of any consultants and the type of service they provide.

"Records shall be maintained stating the name, address, and qualifications of any consultants and the type of service they provide."
— 21 CFR §211.34, consultant records requirement

Regulatory interpretation: A "consultant" under §211.34 is any external individual retained specifically to provide expert advice on manufacturing, processing, packing, or holding activities. This is broader than a formal consulting contract — it includes subject matter experts (SMEs) retained for specific projects.

Record retention: §211.68 and 21 CFR Part 211 Subpart J (§211.180) require that records be retained for the shelf life of the product plus one year, or two years after the batch distribution, whichever is longer. Consultant records should be maintained with the same retention discipline as other GMP records.

Qualification standard — "any combination thereof": Like §211.25, the consultant qualification standard explicitly accepts a combination of education, training, and experience. An industry expert with 30 years of hands-on sterile manufacturing experience but no formal degree may satisfy §211.34 if their track record demonstrates sufficient expertise for the specific subject matter. The key is documentation: the on-file record must present enough biographical detail (work history, publications, prior consulting engagements) to allow an FDA inspector to independently assess whether the consultant was adequately qualified for the advisory role performed.

Scope specificity matters: The "type of service" field in the consultant record is not merely administrative — it defines the boundary of the consultant's advisory mandate. If a consultant was retained to advise on environmental monitoring program design but also verbally advised on sterilization validation, the expanded scope should be documented. Undocumented scope creep creates a compliance gap: services were rendered without a qualifying record in place.

Tutorial Commentary

Who Is a "Consultant" Under §211.34?

This question is more complex in a CDMO setting than it appears. FDA defines "consultant" by functional interpretation, not by job title or contract form. The test is: does this outside person advise on the manufacture, processing, packing or holding of drugs?

Cases that are generally §211.34 consultants:

  • An external GMP consultant (helping to prepare for an FDA inspection)
  • A process validation expert engaged specially (performing a process validation assessment)
  • An aseptic manufacturing technical consultant (advising on improvements to a filling line)
  • An external laboratory analyst (giving interpretive opinions on a specific technical issue)

Cases that are generally not §211.34 consultants:

  • Vendor technicians who only supply equipment (installation, calibration)
  • Legal counsel (providing legal advice, not GMP technical advice)
  • IT vendors (unless they are advising on GMP system validation)

Regulatory Analysis of the "Consultant" Qualification Requirements

§211.34 uses almost the same qualification language as §211.25(a): "sufficient education, training, and experience, or any combination thereof." This is no coincidence: FDA's position is that outside experts who advise on pharmaceutical manufacturing should meet the same qualification standard as internal staff.

The regulatory trigger for a "consultant": The core element is "advising on." Whenever an outside person advises on the manufacture, processing, packing or holding of drugs (whether through a written report, verbal opinion or on-site guidance), §211.34 is triggered. The following common borderline cases deserve special attention:

  • Equipment vendor technical support: A vendor engineer who comes on site to calibrate or maintain equipment → generally not a §211.34 consultant. But if the vendor engineer also advises on "how to set equipment parameters to optimize product quality" → may be a consultant.
  • Academic partners: A university professor in a collaborative research program who gives specific technical advice on process development or improvement → in most cases a §211.34 consultant, even without a formal consulting contract.
  • Client technology transfer staff: Technical staff the client stations at the site during technology transfer, if they give guidance on the CDMO's manufacturing operations → under the CDMO's §211.34 compliance framework, the qualifications of these people should be documented.

How to Set Up Consultant Records in Practice

Minimum record elements required by §211.34:

  • Name — full legal name
  • Address — business contact address
  • Qualifications — educational degrees, years of relevant work experience, professional certifications (if any)
  • Type of Service — the specific scope of GMP activities on which advice is given

Recommended best practice: Set up a "Consultant Qualification Log" that collects the above information in a standard format, with copies of the CV or qualification documents attached. Update it each time a consultant is engaged. It can then be produced immediately during an FDA inspection.

GMP clauses in service contracts: State the scope of services clearly in the consulting contract. This makes it easier to confirm whether §211.34 requirements are triggered and to cite the "type of service" in the records later.

Analogy

§211.34 is like the rule for a hospital engaging a surgical consultant: you may bring in an outside expert, but you must confirm that the person is licensed and qualified for this kind of surgery, and keep a record of it. The same logic applies to a drug maker engaging a consultant. FDA's position is: "The advice of the expert you bring in affects the quality of your drug, so I need to know whether this person is sufficiently qualified."

Common Questions in CDMO Inspections

Typical FDA questions on §211.34 during an inspection:

  • "Have you used any GMP consultants in the past two years? Please show me the consultant records."
  • If consultant records are missing or incomplete → 483 observation (violation of §211.34)
  • If a consultant's qualification documents are insufficient to support the scope of advice given → a more serious observation

Special CDMO situation: When a client sends its own QA staff to the CDMO to provide "technical guidance," are those people §211.34 consultants? It depends on the nature of the service. If client QA staff give GMP advice on the CDMO's manufacturing operations on site → they may need to be included in the consultant records. The Quality Agreement should clarify this.

Warning Letter-level risk: If a consultant is not adequately qualified and the CDMO nonetheless follows the advice, and that advice leads to a quality problem or CGMP deficiency, FDA may cite §211.34 in a Warning Letter as part of a systemic deficiency. Consultant record problems alone are usually at the 483 level; combined with "advice from an unqualified consultant led to quality system deficiencies," they may escalate to a Warning Letter.

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO hires a former FDA investigator as a consultant to prepare for FDA inspections. Are §211.34 records required? Which qualification documents need to be recorded?
  2. A university professor is invited to give one-time technical advice on a specific sterilization process issue (unpaid). Is this a §211.34 consultant? Is "whether payment is made" the criterion?
  3. An FDA investigator asks to see "the records of all consultants for the past two years." Your consultant log has only three people, but you are sure the company has used other external technical support. What is your inspection response strategy?
1.5 §211.68 — Automatic, Mechanical, and Electronic Equipment

Original Text

§ 211.68(a) — Automatic, mechanical, or electronic equipment or other types of equipment, including computers, or related systems that will perform a function satisfying a requirement of this part, shall be routinely calibrated, inspected, or checked according to a written program designed to assure proper performance. Written records of those calibration checks and inspections shall be maintained.

§ 211.68(b) — Appropriate controls shall be exercised over computer or related systems to assure that changes in master production and control records or other records are instituted only by authorized personnel. Input to and output from the computer or related system of formulas or other records or data shall be checked for accuracy. The degree and frequency of input/output verification shall be based on the complexity and reliability of the computer or related system. A backup file of data entered into the computer system shall be maintained except where certain data, such as calculations performed in connection with laboratory analysis, are eliminated by computerization or otherwise have become unnecessary. Hard copy or alternative systems, such as duplicates, tapes, or microfilm, shall be maintained.

"Appropriate controls shall be exercised over computer or related systems to assure that changes in master production and control records or other records are instituted only by authorized personnel."
— 21 CFR §211.68(b), computer system access control requirement

Key requirements summary for §211.68:

  • Written calibration/inspection program for all qualifying equipment
  • Maintenance of calibration and inspection records
  • Access controls: only authorized personnel can modify master records
  • Input/output accuracy verification (frequency based on complexity)
  • Backup data files (except where computerization makes redundant data unnecessary)
  • Hard copy or equivalent backup system maintenance

Scope of "automatic, mechanical, or electronic equipment": §211.68 applies to any equipment that "performs a function satisfying a requirement" of Part 211. This encompasses a very wide range of equipment, including: analytical instruments (HPLC, UV spectrophotometers, pH meters, balances), environmental monitoring systems (particle counters, temperature sensors), manufacturing equipment with programmable controllers (tablet presses, lyophilizers, autoclaves with digital recorders), computerized systems (MES, LIMS, SCADA, ERP systems used for GMP records), and data loggers and building management systems (BMS) used to monitor critical parameters.

Calibration program design requirements (§211.68(a)): The written calibration program must specify: which equipment is in scope; calibration intervals (based on manufacturer specifications, historical performance, and risk assessment); calibration standards used (traceable to NIST or equivalent national standard); acceptance criteria; what happens when calibration fails (out-of-calibration investigation — impact on product made since last passing calibration); labeling requirements (calibration status label on equipment); and who is authorized to perform calibration (in-house metrology lab or qualified external calibration service).

Out-of-calibration impact assessment: One of the most underappreciated aspects of §211.68(a) is the requirement to assess the impact on products when equipment is found out of calibration during a routine check. If a temperature sensor used to monitor a critical process step was found to be reading 2°C high for the past three months, all batches manufactured during that period may need retrospective evaluation by the QCU — potentially triggering recalls if the true process temperature was below specification. Written procedures for out-of-calibration investigations must define how far back the impact assessment reaches and what product disposition criteria apply.

Tutorial Commentary

§211.68 Is the Entry Point for Electronic Systems into CGMP Compliance

§211.68 is the provision of Part 211 that connects most directly to modern pharmaceutical IT infrastructure. It establishes the basic CGMP requirements for electronic equipment (including computer systems), while 21 CFR Part 11 builds on it to provide a fuller framework for electronic records and electronic signatures.

Three-tier compliance framework:

  • Tier 1, §211.68: Basic CGMP requirements for calibration, maintenance, access control and backup (applicable to all drug manufacturers)
  • Tier 2, 21 CFR Part 11: Detailed requirements for electronic records and electronic signatures (applicable to GMP records maintained in electronic form)
  • Tier 3, CSV (Computer System Validation): FDA guidance expects validation (IQ/OQ/PQ) of computerized systems that affect product quality

§211.68(b) and the ALCOA+ Data Integrity Framework

The access control requirements for computer systems in §211.68(b) map directly onto the ALCOA+ data integrity principles:

  • A: Attributable. Every change to a record must be traceable to a specific authorized person
  • L: Legible. Records must be clearly readable, and electronic records need appropriate display and print functions
  • C: Contemporaneous. Record at the time the activity is performed; back-filling after the fact is not permitted
  • O: Original. Original data must not be overwritten; the backup requirement of §211.68(b) directly supports this principle
  • A: Accurate. Input/output verification (§211.68(b)) ensures the data are accurate
  • + Additional: Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available

Frequent 483 Observations on §211.68

The following are the deficiencies FDA cites most often for computer systems and automated equipment:

  • Audit trail missing or not reviewed: The system has audit trail functionality but it is not enabled, or it is enabled but no procedure exists for periodic review
  • Shared accounts: Several people use the same login account → cannot satisfy the attributability requirement that "only authorized personnel may change records"
  • Poor administrator account management: The system administrator account is used for routine operations, or accounts of departed employees are not disabled promptly
  • No backup procedure: There is no written data backup procedure, or backups are not periodically tested for restoration
  • Incomplete calibration records: Equipment bears a calibration label but the written calibration record is missing or incomplete

Practical Meaning of "Input/Output Verification" Under §211.68(b)

§211.68(b) requires that the degree and frequency of input/output verification be based on the "complexity and reliability" of the computer system. In practice this requirement is often simplified to "completing system validation (CSV) is enough." But CSV is a one-time validation, whereas I/O verification under §211.68(b) is routine quality control during ongoing operation.

Practical examples of I/O verification:

  • LIMS calculation formula verification: Each time the LIMS is upgraded or a calculation formula is modified, re-verify output accuracy using samples with known results
  • Batch record system data transfer verification: For production parameters transferred from the MES into the batch record, sample and check that the original SCADA data agree with the MES record
  • Verification of digital outputs from analytical instruments: For results calculated from an instrument's digital output (such as concentration from HPLC peak area integration), periodically check the accuracy of the algorithm against manual calculation
  • ERP inventory quantity reconciliation: Periodic cycle counts of material inventory in the system against actual stock

The frequency does not need to be daily; §211.68(b) explicitly allows it to be adjusted according to complexity and reliability. A highly stable LIMS with a validated history of many years may need only a semi-annual I/O spot check, while a newly deployed system may need monthly verification. The key point is that the frequency decision must have a documented basis (risk assessment) and must not be arbitrary.

The Relationship Between CSV (Computerized System Validation) and §211.68

§211.68(a) requires written procedures to "assure proper performance" of equipment. In several guidance documents FDA interprets this requirement as including validation of computerized systems:

  • 1983 FDA software policy: Requires validation of computerized systems that affect product quality
  • 2003 21 CFR Part 11 scope guidance: Confirms that CSV is a predicate rule requirement (§211.68) and that Part 11 addresses "how to record"
  • GAMP 5 (2022): ISPE's best practice guide for computerized system validation, widely referenced by FDA

Practical implication: System validation documents (URS, risk assessment, IQ/OQ/PQ, CVR) are the core written evidence of §211.68 compliance, not an optional best practice.

Risk-Tiered Design of the §211.68(a) Calibration Program

Not all equipment needs an equally rigorous calibration program. Risk-based equipment classification (Equipment Criticality Classification) is industry best practice and is consistent with the design intent of §211.68(a):

  • Critical equipment (Direct Impact): Equipment that directly affects product quality or safety (such as autoclave temperature sensors and aseptic filling machine weighing systems). Highest calibration frequency, usually every 3-6 months; a failure immediately triggers an impact assessment; NIST-traceable calibration standards are required.
  • Indirect-impact equipment (Indirect Impact): Equipment that affects quality indirectly (such as equipment that monitors the environment but not process parameters). Calibration frequency can reasonably be extended (every 6-12 months); a failure triggers an investigation but not necessarily an immediate stop to production.
  • No-impact equipment (No Impact): Equipment with no effect on product quality (such as office computers). May be excluded from the GMP calibration program, but the basis for the classification must be documented.

The equipment classification decision should have a documented basis (risk assessment) and be approved by the QCU under §211.22(c). The calibration program is part of the Master Validation Plan, and it is often one of the first documents requested during an FDA inspection.

Analogy: The Three-Tier Compliance Framework

Imagine the safety system of a building:

  • §211.68 = the Building Code: The minimum safety standard every building must meet. It says you must have fire-fighting equipment (calibration), emergency exits (backup) and door locks (access control).
  • 21 CFR Part 11 = the security system specification: If you decide to replace traditional keys with electronic access control, your electronic system must meet this more detailed security specification (audit trail, electronic signatures and so on).
  • CSV / GAMP 5 = the security system installation and testing standard: Before your electronic access control is put into use, it must be correctly installed (IQ), functionally tested (OQ) and verified under actual use conditions (PQ).

If a drug maker says, "My LIMS has no audit trail because I only need to comply with §211.68, not Part 11," the argument may hold, but only if the LIMS data are not "GMP records maintained in electronic form." In practice, almost every modern LIMS is a Part 11 record system, and this exception is extremely hard to sustain.

Emerging Technology Challenge: §211.68 Compliance for AI / Machine Learning Systems

As AI systems enter pharmaceutical manufacturing (for example automated anomaly detection, predictive maintenance and AI-assisted release review), the "calibration, maintenance and access control" framework of §211.68 faces new interpretive challenges:

  • What is "calibration" for an AI model? Traditional calibration addresses the accuracy of measuring instruments. "Calibration" of an AI model may mean periodic retraining and performance verification to ensure the model is still accurate on new production data.
  • Audit trail of AI decisions: If an AI system recommends releasing or rejecting a batch, the access control requirement of §211.68(b) means the recommendation must be confirmed by an authorized person and not executed automatically by the AI. The layer of human oversight cannot be omitted.
  • FDA's 2024 AI/ML draft guidance: FDA is developing a regulatory framework for AI in drug manufacturing, and it is expected to add AI-specific requirements (model risk assessment, continuous performance monitoring) on top of the existing §211.68 and CSV framework.

For now, §211.68 remains the foundational regulatory entry point for bringing AI systems into the GMP compliance framework.

Comparison of §211.68 vs. 21 CFR Part 11 Requirements

The "Backup Exception" Clause of §211.68(b)

§211.68(b) contains an important exception clause: "except those components of the data that are not necessary because of computerization." This "backup exception" is often misunderstood.

Meaning of the exception: In the early era of computerization, a LIMS would automatically calculate raw values entered manually by the analyst (such as absorbance). If the LIMS stored the calculated result and the analyst also recorded the same calculation on paper, the paper copy was a "redundant backup made unnecessary by computerization" and could be omitted. The exception targets redundant copies, not primary raw data.

Where the exception does not apply: If the computer is the sole source of the raw data (such as an HPLC chromatogram), there is no "redundancy," so the electronic raw data must be backed up. In that case both the backup requirement of §211.68(b) and the record protection requirements of Part 11 are necessary, and there is no exception to speak of.

Position of FDA's 2018 data integrity guidance: It states explicitly that original electronic data should not be left without a backup on the grounds of the "exception." The exception applies only to redundant paper copies that genuinely disappear (are no longer generated) because of computerization, and does not apply to any original data.

Data Integrity Enforcement History: From §211.68(b) to the Wave of Data Integrity Warning Letters

After 2013, FDA's enforcement on data integrity of electronic systems increased sharply, and the root of it lies in the long neglect of §211.68(b). Main enforcement patterns:

  • 2013-2017 wave of India/China CDMOs: In many overseas inspections FDA found HPLC software used without an audit trail (such as unauthorized versions of Empower or ChemStation), failing test results deleted, and analyses run under shared accounts. These practices directly violate the access control and record integrity requirements of §211.68(b).
  • FDA's 2018 "Data Integrity and Compliance With Drug CGMP" guidance: Provides an integrated interpretation of §211.68(b) and 21 CFR Part 11, stating clearly that an audit trail is an "expected" element of compliance even though §211.68(b) does not use the term "audit trail."
  • Typical Warning Letter language: "Your firm failed to exercise appropriate controls over computer-related systems... Specifically, your HPLC data system did not have audit trails enabled, allowing analysts to delete and overwrite raw data without creating a permanent record of these actions."

Lesson for CDMOs: The "access control" requirement of §211.68(b) is the regulatory foundation of data integrity compliance. Any electronic system that allows unauthorized personnel to modify GMP records is a direct violation of §211.68(b), and a 483 or Warning Letter can be issued without citing Part 11.

Integration of Subpart B Provisions: How the Five Core Requirements Support Each Other

Subpart B: A Systems View

§§211.22-211.68 are not five isolated compliance requirements but an interlocking organizational quality system:

  • §211.22 (QCU) is the hub of the whole system: the execution and oversight of all other requirements come back to the QCU's approval and review responsibilities
  • §211.25 (personnel qualifications) is the foundation for the QCU to operate effectively: the QCU's own staff must be qualified in order to review production activities effectively
  • §211.28 (personnel health and hygiene) is the minimum protective layer for all personnel requirements, preventing people from becoming a source of product contamination
  • §211.34 (consultants) is the qualification control applied when outside knowledge is brought into the organization, ensuring the quality of external advice
  • §211.68 (equipment) ensures that the tools the organization relies on (electronic systems and automated equipment) provide trustworthy data and records that support the QCU's review decisions

When FDA finds systemic CGMP problems at a facility, it can usually find several mutually reinforcing deficiencies across these five provisions, not just one isolated violation. This is also why Subpart B is regarded as a key window for assessing the maturity of a drug maker's quality culture.

Quick Reference of Key Regulatory Terms

  • Quality Control Unit (QCU) — the person or organizational element responsible for quality control duties (§211.22)
  • Batch Record Review — QCU review of batch production and control records to confirm they are error-free (§211.22(a))
  • Continuing GMP Training — ongoing CGMP education conducted with sufficient frequency (§211.25(a))
  • Competency Assessment — an assessment confirming that an employee has the ability needed to perform the job (best practice under §211.25)
  • Health Exclusion — removing personnel with apparent illness from work that involves direct contact with drug products (§211.28(a))
  • Gowning Qualification — an assessment confirming that personnel perform the gowning procedure correctly (best practice under §211.28(b))
  • Consultant Qualification Record — the written record of consultant name, address, qualifications and type of service required by §211.34
  • Calibration Program — the written procedures and record system that ensure equipment calibration accuracy (§211.68(a))
  • Audit Trail — a computer system-generated record of operations that tracks who made what change and when (§211.68(b))
  • ALCOA+ — a data integrity framework: Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate, plus Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available
Combined Application of Subparts A-B: Organizational Design Decision Matrix

Compliance Considerations in Organizational Design

When a new drug maker or CDMO designs its organization, the requirements of Subpart B (§§211.22-211.68) should drive the following key organizational design decisions:

Decision 1, QCU reporting line: The head of the QCU should report to the CEO or board level (not to production or commercial functions), so that the independence requirement of §211.22(a) is reflected in the organizational structure. In large multinational companies with matrix management, the head of the QCU typically has a dual reporting line to both the local president and the global Chief Quality Officer.

Decision 2, QCU resource planning: The "adequate laboratory facilities" requirement of §211.22(b) means laboratory space, equipment and staff (§211.25(c)) must all be allocated early in business planning. In new site investment planning, the QC laboratory footprint and equipment list should be fixed elements of facility design, not the first items cut when the budget is squeezed.

Decision 3, timing of training system setup: §211.25 requires personnel to meet the qualification standard before they begin operations. The training system (learning management system or LMS, training matrix, OJT assessment protocols) must therefore be established and validated before commercial manufacturing starts, not built retroactively after production begins.

Decision 4, electronic system procurement strategy: The requirements of §211.68 and 21 CFR Part 11 for electronic systems should be included as key requirements in the URS (User Requirements Specification) at the purchasing decision, not after implementation. "Retrofit compliance" is one of the most expensive mistakes in the industry.

Subpart A-B Inspection Readiness Self-Check List

Before an FDA inspection, confirm that the answers to all of the following are "yes":

  • Does the QCU organization chart clearly show that the QCU head's reporting line does not pass through production?
  • Do the written QCU SOPs (§211.22(d)) cover all release and review activities, and are they version-controlled?
  • Do all employees' training records include the latest version of the SOPs they currently use?
  • Do the continuing CGMP training records show a history of "sufficient frequency" (usually ≥ annually)?
  • Do all GMP consultants from the past two years have written qualification records (name, address, qualifications, type of service)?
  • Is there a written SOP for health exclusion, with records of its execution (even if never invoked, there should at least be training records)?
  • Do cleanroom / restricted areas have a written access authorization matrix and a visitor record procedure?
  • Does all GMP-related electronic equipment have current calibration records and calibration status labels?
  • Do computer systems have audit trails enabled, with a periodic review procedure and records?
  • Is the data backup procedure documented, with backup restoration tests performed periodically and recorded?

Key Takeaways

  • QCU independence is at the core of CGMP: §211.22 requires the QCU to be organizationally independent of production, with unimpeded authority to release or reject batches. The QCU's authority spans all product types (raw materials, in-process material, finished product), approval of all procedures and specifications, and the power to review all production records. In contract manufacturing, the contract acceptor's (CDMO's) QCU bears independent release responsibility for all contracted batches and cannot shift it onto the client on the grounds that the client has its own QA. The Quality Agreement must clearly define the division of QCU responsibilities.
  • A "Right-First-Time" culture goes beyond mere compliance: The purpose of the QCU is not only to be the last line of defense but to intervene actively and prevent quality problems. Facilities with high deviation rates whose investigations are closed quickly are often the targets of FDA Warning Letters, which reflects a quality culture problem, not a technical one.
  • Training ≠ qualification: §211.25 requires a combination of education, training and experience. The training matrix must cover four types of training: initial, continuing ("sufficient frequency"), change-driven and corrective. Trainers themselves also need qualification documents. The staffing adequacy requirement of §211.25(c) means that a serious shortage of personnel is in itself a CGMP violation.
  • §211.28 is the floor for sterile manufacturing, not the ceiling: The regulation requires only the exclusion of personnel with "apparent illness" and protective apparel "as necessary." Sterile manufacturing facilities must build on this with stricter gowning qualification procedures (FDA aseptic processing guidance) and detailed gowning specifications for each cleanroom grade (EU Annex 1) to meet the dual regulatory expectations of global markets.
  • Consultant records are a preventable 483 deficiency: The consultant qualification records of §211.34 follow a functional test: the nature of the service decides whether it applies, not the form of the contract or whether payment is made. Setting up a consultant qualification log (with name, address, qualification documents and type of service) is the simplest compliance measure, but many companies overlook the duty to update records as "the scope of service expands over time."
  • §211.68 is the legal entry point for electronic systems into CGMP: The calibration procedures (§211.68(a)) and access control / backup (§211.68(b)) are foundational obligations for the electronic systems of every drug maker. On top of §211.68(b), 21 CFR Part 11 establishes the electronic records / electronic signatures framework, and CSV / GAMP 5 provides system validation guidance. Impact assessment of calibration failures (out-of-calibration investigation) is a high-risk compliance blind spot and can trigger batch recalls. The wave of data integrity enforcement after 2013 is rooted in the long neglect of the access control requirements of §211.68(b).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: §211.42 Is the Regulatory Cornerstone of a Sterile Facility

§211.42 is the CGMP legal foundation for the design of the entire sterile drug manufacturing facility. It has a three-tier structure:

Tier 1 (a): Overall suitability of the facility - size, construction and location must support cleaning, maintenance and proper operations.

Tier 2 (b): Flow design - the flow of materials, containers and labeling must be designed to prevent contamination and mix-ups. This is the regulatory source of the "unidirectional flow" design principle.

Tier 3 (c): Separation of operations - the most detailed requirement, listing 10 types of operations that must have separate or defined areas; (c)(10) specifically addresses aseptic processing operations.

High-Frequency 483 Observation: "Inadequate Separation"

The most common §211.42 483 deficiencies:

  • Quarantined material and released material are not physically separated and are distinguished by labeling only
  • Returned goods and unopened stock are held in the same area
  • Insufficient airlock design between the aseptic filling area and lower-grade preparation areas
  • Waste routes cross material entry routes
  • The laboratory (control operations) is not actually separated from the production area

§211.42(d) Penicillin cross-contamination: The facility separation requirement for penicillin is absolute - penicillin and non-penicillin human drug products may not be manufactured in the same facility, even at different times. This is one of the strictest separation requirements in the industry, because even extremely low residue levels can cause severe allergic reactions.

§211.42(c)(10) is a mandatory CGMP requirement (regulation), whereas ISO 14644 and the FDA 2004 Aseptic Guidance are technical standards (not mandatory, but representing industry best practice).

  • Compressed air quality: Compressed air that contacts the drug solution must meet the oil, water and particulate standards of ISO 8573 (typically Class 1.2.1 for critical contact points)

§211.67(b)(4)–(6): Removal of Batch Identification and Protection After Cleaning

  • (b)(4) Removal of batch identification: The identification of the previous batch must be completely removed before or during cleaning (including handwritten marks, stickers and tags on the equipment).   • (b)(5) Protection against contamination after cleaning: Cleaned equipment must be protected before use to prevent recontamination. Example: after a compounding tank is cleaned, it is sealed with a lid and not exposed to the environment beyond the CHT limit.

  • Cleaning method number (SOP number) and the signature and date of the person who performed the cleaning

Equipment logs are among the documents FDA most often asks to review during inspections, and missing or incomplete logs are a high-frequency 483 observation.

Microbiological Monitoring Link: USP <1116> and Cross-Contamination Risk

§211.67(a) requires that equipment be cleaned to prevent "contamination" - for aseptic manufacturing equipment, microbial contamination and chemical cross-contamination are the two principal risks.

Link to USP <1116> (Microbiological Control and Monitoring of Aseptic Processing Environments):

  • Recommended Action Limits and Alert Limits for ISO 5/7/8 environments
  • Guidance on equipment surface sampling with contact plates (Replicate Organism Detection and Counting, RODAC) and swabs
  • Methods for assessing the bioburden of equipment after cleaning

Cross-contamination (carryover contamination) risk management: The greatest GMP risk of shared equipment is residue of the previous product contaminating the next. High-risk scenarios include:

  • Highly potent active pharmaceutical ingredients (HPAPI): very low PDE (ng/day level), very small MACO values, and extremely stringent cleaning validation requirements
  • Sensitizing substances (penicillin, β-lactams): dedicated equipment is required and sharing is not permitted
  • Biologics (alkaloids, proteins): TOC analysis is often the main residue detection method

When equipment cleaning SOP validation data (§211.67) is analyzed together with environmental monitoring trend data (USP <1116>), the state of microbial and chemical contamination control in aseptic operations can be assessed comprehensively.

  • Cleaning SOP lacks sufficient detail ("rinse with water" without specifying temperature, flow rate or time)
  • Cleaning validation does not cover all shared equipment, or validation data do not support commercial production scale
  • Equipment is used after the maximum clean hold time (CHT) has been exceeded, with no record of re-cleaning
  • Cleaning verification records in the equipment log are missing or incomplete
  • Cleaning method not revalidated after a change of cleaning agent supplier or formulation
  • Missing identification of dedicated equipment - shared equipment is mistaken for dedicated equipment and cleaning validation is skipped

2.5 §211.72 — Filters: The Sterilizing Filter Framework

Original Text

§ 211.72 — Filters
Filters for liquid filtration used in the manufacture, processing, or packing of injectable drug products intended for human use shall not release fibers into such products. "Filters for liquid filtration used in the manufacture, processing, or packing of injectable drug products intended for human use shall not release fibers into such products."
— 21 CFR §211.72, the non-fiber-releasing filter requirement for injectables

Key provisions of §211.72:

  • Non-fiber-releasing requirement: All filters for injectable products must be non-fiber-releasing
  • Exception: If fiber-releasing filters must be used, they must be followed by a 0.22 µm (or 0.45 µm where manufacturing conditions dictate) non-fiber-releasing filter
  • Absolute prohibition: Asbestos-containing filters are prohibited without exception
  • Pore size specification: §211.72 specifies 0.22 µm only as the required secondary filter when fiber-releasing primary filters are unavoidable — it does not independently mandate 0.22 µm for all injectable filtration

Connection to §211.42(c)(10)(iii): HEPA (High-Efficiency Particulate Air) filters for aseptic areas are separately required by §211.42(c)(10)(iii). §211.72 addresses liquid filtration; §211.42(c)(10)(iii) addresses air filtration. HEPA filter integrity (DOP/PAO leak testing per ISO 14644-3) is the air-side analogue of liquid filter integrity testing under §211.72.

Tutorial Commentary

Post-Use Integrity Test Failure: GMP Deviation Handling Process

A post-use integrity test failure is a serious GMP event for an aseptic batch. The standard handling process is:

  1. Immediate actions: Place the batch on hold (Product Hold) and start a deviation investigation (Deviation Investigation)
  2. Root cause investigation (RCA) directions:
    • Did the pre-use test pass? (If so, the damage occurred during filtration)
    • Did the filtration pressure exceed the validated maximum pressure? (Review the batch record / pressure records)
    • Did the filter exceed the validated maximum use time?
    • Are the product pH and solvent composition consistent with the validated conditions? (Chemical compatibility)
    • Was the installation procedure performed correctly? (Personnel qualification check)
  3. Sterility risk assessment: The batch release decision must be based on an overall risk assessment - is filtration the only sterilization step, or a preliminary step before terminal sterilization? What is the status of other sterility assurance measures (nitrogen overlay, environmental control)?
  4. CAPA: Establish preventive actions based on the root cause identified in the investigation, and assess whether revalidation is needed

HEPA Integrity Test (DOP/PAO Leak Test): The Air-Side Analogue of §211.72

§211.72 addresses liquid filters, while §211.42(c)(10)(iii) addresses HEPA air filtration. The HEPA integrity test (commonly called the DOP test or PAO test) is the air-side analogue:

  • Method: Inject a PAO (polyalphaolefin) aerosol upstream of the HEPA filter and scan downstream with a photometer to detect leaks
  • Acceptance criterion: Downstream concentration ≤ 0.01% of upstream concentration (per ISO 14644-3)
  • Test frequency: Initial verification after installation (IQ/OQ); thereafter retested periodically based on risk assessment (typically every 6–12 months)
  • Failure handling: After a leak is located, it may be repaired (with a sealant) or the HEPA filter replaced; after repair, the filter must pass the integrity test again

High-Frequency 483 Observations: §211.72-Related Deficiencies

  • Post-use integrity test failure without an adequate deviation investigation, with only "replaced filter" recorded
  • Filter use time or filtered volume exceeds the validated maximum
  • Filter validation does not include worst-case drug solution concentration, pH or temperature conditions
  • The integrity test instrument (filter tester) is not calibrated as required by §211.68(a)
  • The batch record has no filtration entries (filter lot number, pre-/post-use test results, tester signature)
  • Filter used directly without a pre-use integrity test (relying on the post-use test alone)

Tutorial Commentary

Component Qualification Program

§§211.80–211.89 establish a complete "component life cycle control" framework, which can be understood as six stages:

  1. Receipt — incoming records and verification of the supplier CoA
  2. Quarantine — no use before testing is complete; physical segregation or status labeling
  3. Sampling & Testing — identity, purity, strength and quality testing, using the statistical sampling principles of §211.84(b)
  4. Approval/Rejection — the QU decides on release or rejection
  5. Storage (FEFO) — the first-expired, first-out (FEFO) principle of §211.86
  6. Retesting — §211.87 requires re-confirmation after long-term storage or exposure to special conditions

A procedural gap at any one of these stages can become the source of a 483 observation.

Supplier CoA Reliance: §211.84(c)(2)

A site may "rely on the supplier's Certificate of Analysis (CoA)" in place of performing full testing itself, but two conditions must both be met:

Condition 1 (no exemption): The site must still perform at least one "specific identity test" on each component. This is a mandatory requirement that cannot be outsourced or waived. For an API used in sterile manufacturing, this is usually an IR spectrum comparison or HPLC identification.

Condition 2 (validation obligation): The site must validate the reliability of the supplier's test methods at appropriate intervals. This means you cannot rely on the CoA indefinitely; you need systematic supplier audits and method comparison/verification.

Common 483 Pattern

"Failure to conduct at least one specific identity test on each component" — even when a supplier CoA is available, if the site skips the identity test (for example, it only reviews the CoA document and performs no laboratory identification), FDA will cite §211.84(c)(1) directly in a 483.

Another common pattern: relying on the supplier CoA but never performing a method validation comparison — this violates the "reliability validation" obligation of §211.84(c)(2).

100% Identity Testing Requirement

The "each component" requirement of §211.84(c)(1) applies to each lot, while the sampling plan under §211.84(b) determines how many containers are sampled.

FDA's interpretation: for APIs used in sterile injectable drugs, the identity test must cover every container of each incoming lot; it is not acceptable to test only the sampled containers and skip the others. This position appears in FDA's Compliance Policy Guide (CPG) Section 430.100 on CGMP for Finished Pharmaceuticals (2003) and in FDA EIR citation patterns.

Practical advice: establish a "container-by-container identity confirmation" procedure for high-risk components (principal APIs, sterile raw materials); for excipients, statistical sampling may be used based on risk assessment.

Rejected Material Control

The "quarantine system" required by §211.89 is more than attaching a label. FDA investigators will check the following:

  • Is rejected material physically segregated (dedicated area or locked storage)?
  • Is the status block in the ERP/warehouse system effective (to prevent inadvertent use)?
  • Is the subsequent disposition (return to supplier, destruction) supported by written records?

— 21 CFR §211.94(a), the foundational container compatibility requirement

Tutorial Commentary

The Three Core Concepts of §211.94

§211.94 looks short, but it is in fact the CGMP anchor for the entire container closure system (CCS) compliance framework, and it covers three aspects:

  1. Compatibility — §211.94(a): container materials must not be reactive with the drug product, additive to it (adding impurities), or absorptive (adsorbing components of the drug product)
  2. Protection — §211.94(b): the container must provide an adequate barrier to prevent degradation or contamination of the drug product from external factors such as light, moisture and oxygen
  3. Cleanliness & Depyrogenation — §211.94(c): containers for sterile products must be depyrogenated, not merely sterile

Container Closure Integrity

The "adequate protection" requirement of §211.94(b) links directly to the Container Closure Integrity Testing (CCIT) framework.

USP <1207> series (the compendial reference standards for CCIT):

  • USP <1207>: Package Integrity Evaluation — general chapter; principles for selecting a CCIT method
  • USP <1207.1>: High-voltage leak detection (HVLD) — applicable to liquid sterile products
  • USP <1207.2>: Comparison of deterministic and probabilistic methods, such as microbial ingress, dye ingress and helium leak
  • USP <1207.3>: Seal quality testing, including cap torque, headspace analysis and others

In its 2016 guidance Container Closure Integrity Testing in Lieu of Sterility Testing as a Component of the Stability Protocol for Sterile Products, FDA states clearly that CCIT may replace sterility testing in the stability protocol for sterile products, provided that adequate scientific justification and method validation are available.

Extractables and Leachables (E&L) Framework

The "additive" requirement of §211.94(a) is the regulatory basis for E&L studies:

  • Extractables: chemical substances that can be extracted from the container material under extreme conditions (solvent, temperature, pH); they represent the worst-case potential for chemical migration
  • Leachables: chemical substances that migrate from the container material into the drug product under actual use/storage conditions; they are a direct indicator of patient safety impact

The E&L study pathway: extractables study → toxicological assessment (TTC threshold) → leachables monitoring design → specification setting → regulatory submission (CTD Module 3.2.P.2).

Notes on glass vials: Glass containers for injectables must comply with USP <660> (glass types, Type I/II/III) and EP 3.2.1. Sterile injectable solutions must use Type I borosilicate glass to prevent the release of alkaline ions (the glass delamination problem; see the FDA Warning Letters of 2011).

Special Considerations for Prefilled Syringes (PFS)

Prefilled syringes (PFS) are a combined system of drug container and primary packaging, so §211.94 should be considered together with the following standards:

  • ISO 11040 series: PFS design, materials and component testing (in particular ISO 11040-4 on glass syringe specifications)
  • USP <1> Injections general chapter: particulate matter and volume accuracy
  • Silicone oil issue: the silicone oil lubricant on the plunger stopper can become a leachable, so a silicone oil compatibility study is needed
  • Tungsten pin issue: tungsten pins are used in forming glass syringes, and trace tungsten can trigger protein aggregation (a particular risk for protein drugs)

483 Observation Patterns Related to §211.94

  • No written specifications and test methods established for containers/closures (§211.94(d))
  • No CCIT validation for injectable containers; reliance on visual inspection only
  • E&L studies missing, or only an extractables study without a leachables monitoring plan
  • Incomplete documentation of glass vial depyrogenation validation (dry heat 250°C × 30 min, D-value verification)

§211.100 — Deviation Management System

The "recorded and justified" wording of §211.100(b) is the regulatory basis for the entire deviation management system. A compliant deviation management system should cover:

  1. Real-time Documentation: record the deviation at the moment it is found; no retrospective write-up
  2. Classification & Severity Grading: grading criteria and handling pathways for Critical / Major / Minor deviations

Yield reconciliation is more than arithmetic; it is an indirect verification indicator of product integrity. An abnormal yield deviation may point to:

  • Sampling volumes in the aseptic process exceeding what the SOP specifies (affecting yield)
  • Abnormal material loss on the filling line (which may indicate an equipment problem or a data integrity problem)
  • Ongoing theft or diversion of drug product (a data integrity red flag)

§211.103 requires that "one person calculates, second person independently verifies". This is the regulatory codification of the Four-Eyes Principle. For an electronic batch record (eBR), this verification step must be built into the system's electronic signature workflow.

  • OUT OF SERVICE (red): under maintenance or awaiting validation; must not be used

§211.110 — In-Process Control Strategy

The IPC (in-process controls) required by §211.110 are not simple checkpoints; they are the bridge across the three stages of process validation:

  • Stage 1 — Process Design: during development, determine which process parameters are critical quality attributes (CQAs) and critical process parameters (CPPs)

For sterile products, IPCs include fill weight checks, particle counts (during filling), environmental monitoring and operator microbiological sampling. §211.113 — The Core Regulatory Anchor for Sterile Drug Manufacturing

§211.113 is the single most important provision for sterile drug manufacturers in all of 21 CFR Part 211. It has only two sentences, but every word carries regulatory weight. Understanding this provision requires analysis along the following dimensions.

§211.113(a) vs §211.113(b) — Different Obligations for Non-Sterile and Sterile Products

§211.113 has two tiers:

§211.113(a) — Microbiological control of non-sterile drug products:

It requires written procedures to prevent "objectionable microorganisms" in drug products. Note that the wording is not "all microorganisms" but "objectionable", meaning that non-sterile oral dosage forms need not meet a sterility standard but must not contain microorganisms that pose a risk for the particular dosage form (for example, Salmonella and Staphylococcus aureus in oral liquids, or Pseudomonas aeruginosa in ophthalmic preparations).

Practical points: establish bioburden specifications and test procedures; define the "objectionable" criteria for each dosage form (see the guidance on microbiological quality attributes in USP <1111>).

§211.113(b) — Microbiological control of sterile drug products:

It requires written procedures to prevent any microbial contamination (no longer "objectionable" only, but comprehensive prevention). The most critical additional obligation: "Such procedures shall include validation of any sterilization process."

This one sentence turns "sterility assurance" from a conceptual obligation into a concrete regulatory obligation to validate, and establishes the following core requirements:

  1. Any sterilization process (steam, dry heat, radiation, gas, filtration) must be validated
  2. Aseptic processing must demonstrate, through simulation (media fill / process simulation), its ability to control microbial contamination
  3. The environmental monitoring (EM) program must be able to effectively monitor the microbiological control status of the process

Sterilization Validation Basics: D-value, Z-value and SAL of 10⁻⁶

To understand why sterilization validation is a scientific obligation, you need to grasp the following core microbial kinetics concepts:

D-value (Decimal Reduction Value):

The time or dose required, under specified conditions (specific temperature, radiation dose, etc.), to reduce a specific microbial population by 90% (one log order of magnitude). The D-value is a quantitative indicator of a microorganism's resistance to specific sterilization conditions.

Example: at 121°C, the D-value of the heat-resistant Geobacillus stearothermophilus (the biological indicator for steam sterilization) is about 1.5–2.5 minutes. With an initial bioburden of 10⁶ CFU, reaching an SAL of 10⁻⁶ requires a 12-log reduction, i.e. a treatment time of 12D.

Z-value (Temperature Coefficient):

The temperature change (in degrees Celsius) required to reduce the D-value by 90% (i.e. to change the D-value by one log). The Z-value describes the sensitivity of a microorganism to temperature change and is used to calculate sterilization equivalence at different temperatures (the F₀ value).

F₀ value (Lethality Value):

Taking 121°C and Z = 10°C as the reference, the sterilization effectiveness of different temperature-time combinations is converted to the equivalent minutes at 121°C.

Formula: F₀ = ∫ 10^((T-121)/10) dt

FDA generally expects a minimum F₀ of ≥ 8 minutes for terminal sterilization (equivalent to an 8-log reduction of spores with D₁₂₁ = 1 min), but the specific requirement should be based on the product's bioburden data.

SAL（Sterility Assurance Level）：

Sterility Assurance Level (SAL) is the maximum probability of a viable microorganism remaining in a container after sterilization. FDA and the major pharmacopoeias require sterile injectable products to achieve an SAL ≤ 10⁻⁶, i.e. no more than one container in a million may contain a viable microorganism.

Media Fill / Process Simulation in Depth

The Media Fill Test is the most important validation tool for assessing aseptic processing capability, and it is the concrete expression of the §211.113(b) obligation to "validate any sterilization process" in the aseptic processing context.

Media selection:

  • Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB): the most commonly used; supports broad-spectrum microbial growth and is suitable for assessing control of microbial contamination during operations
  • The medium must pass a growth promotion test (GPT) to verify its ability to support microbial growth

Simulation design principle (Worst-Case Simulation):

The media fill must simulate the "worst case" of commercial production, including:

  • Maximum Interventions: simulate all the operational interventions that may occur in production (changing needles, clearing jammed vials, personnel entering and leaving the isolator)
  • Maximum Exposure Duration: use the longest permitted production time (linked to the time limits of §211.111)
  • Maximum Batch Size: cover the commercial batch size
  • All Shifts: verify consistency across operators on different shifts
  • All Fill Lines: each production line must be validated independently

Acceptance Criteria:

The explicit expectations of the FDA 2004 aseptic processing guidance:

  • Containers filled <5,000: zero contamination (0 positive containers)
  • Containers filled 5,000–10,000:
    • 1 positive container → investigation + consider revalidation
    • 2 or more positive containers → investigation + revalidation (failure)
  • Containers filled >10,000: contamination rate <0.1% (i.e. no more than 10 positives per 10,000 containers)

Note: any media fill failure requires a comprehensive root cause investigation, including review of batch records, EM trends, operator behavior records, equipment maintenance records, etc.

Validation frequency:

  • Initial validation: three consecutive successful media fill runs (three-batch confirmation)
  • Periodic requalification: every 6 months (each line, each shift), an explicit expectation of the FDA 2004 guidance
  • Event-triggered revalidation: after major equipment modifications, facility renovation, changes to aseptic operation SOPs, or deteriorating EM trends

§211.113 and the FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance (2004)

The Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing — Current Good Manufacturing Practice issued by FDA in 2004 (commonly called the "FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance") is the detailed interpretive document for §211.113(b). Although the guidance itself is not legally binding, FDA in practice evaluates compliance against its standards during inspections.

The main areas covered by the guidance:

  • Facility design (construction materials, airflow design, pressure differentials, HVAC)
  • Equipment (barrier technologies such as RABS/isolator, aseptic sealing of lyophilizers, filling equipment design)
  • Personnel (gowning procedures, personnel monitoring, management of personnel interventions)
  • Production (component preparation, sterilizing filtration, filling and closing)
  • Aseptic process simulation (media fill design, acceptance criteria)
  • Microbiological monitoring (EM program design)
  • Laboratory controls (sterility testing, endotoxin testing)

For sterile manufacturing practitioners, the FDA Aseptic Processing Guidance (2004) must be read together with §211.113(b) to fully understand the whole picture of CGMP obligations.

Reading §211.113 Together with PIC/S Annex 1 (2022)

PIC/S Annex 1 (the 2022 revision, which is also EU Annex 1) is the most detailed guidance on sterile pharmaceutical manufacturing to date, and builds from the overall perspective of the contamination control strategy (CCS) a framework of requirements more detailed than §211.113:

  • Contamination Control Strategy (CCS): a new concept requiring a written site-wide contamination control strategy, covering an overall risk assessment of the building environment, personnel, materials, equipment and process
  • Barrier Technologies: detailed requirements for RABS (restricted access barrier systems) and isolators; Annex 1 encourages the use of isolators to improve sterility assurance
  • Real-time EM: emphasizes continuous particle monitoring during filling
  • Parametric Release: the conditions and requirements for parametric release of terminally sterilized products (release on physical/chemical data in place of sterility testing)

§211.113 provides the "floor" of regulatory obligation, while PIC/S Annex 1 and the FDA 2004 guidance provide the "scientific roadmap" for reaching that floor. Manufacturers seeking access to global markets must comply with both frameworks.

FDA 483 and Warning Letter Patterns Related to §211.113

The following are the most common §211.113 citation patterns in publicly available FDA 483 observations and Warning Letters (in order of frequency):

  1. "Failure to validate aseptic processing operations" (most common)
    Typical findings: no comprehensive root cause investigation after a media fill failure; or inadequate initial validation (not covering all shifts and all intervention types)
  2. "Inadequate environmental monitoring program"
    Typical findings: sampling locations do not cover critical operating areas; alert/action limits lack scientific justification; inadequate investigation after EM excursions
  3. "Failure to validate sterilization process"
    Typical findings: errors in F₀ calculation; improper use of biological indicators; steam sterilization validation that does not cover the most difficult-to-sterilize location (cold spot)
  4. "Inadequate written procedures for preventing microbiological contamination"
    Typical findings: no EM program SOP; aseptic technique SOP that does not address handling of abnormal situations; gowning procedures not documented
  5. "Failure to conduct adequate investigation of sterility test failures"
    Typical findings: inadequate root cause investigation after a positive sterility test (TST failure), with insufficient assessment of batches produced during the same period

§211.115 Reprocessing of Sterile Products

§211.115 requires written reprocessing procedures, but for sterile products the scope for reprocessing is extremely limited:

  • Permitted reprocessing: certain intermediates (such as unfilled bulk solution) may be reprocessed under specific conditions (for example, refiltration after a filtration failure, provided that the bioburden remains within control limits and time limits have not been exceeded)
  • Generally not permitted: batches with filled containers that failed sterility testing, or batches with EM excursions, normally cannot be reprocessed (they are rejected instead)
  • Reprocessing validation: if a company plans to allow reprocessing, it must have validation data demonstrating that reprocessing has no adverse effect on product quality (especially microbiological safety)

QU approval of reprocessing (§211.115(b)) must be handled with particular care for sterile products, because a reprocessing failure with a sterile product can directly threaten patient safety (with intravenous administration, the body's natural defenses cannot filter out contamination).

Sterilization Method Hierarchy

FDA's position (stated explicitly in Appendix 1 of the 2004 FDA aseptic processing guidance and in the FDA PQLI report): terminal sterilization is preferred over aseptic processing.

FDA prefers terminal sterilization because aseptic processing is by nature a "probabilistic" rather than "absolute" assurance of sterility; the target failure rate for a media fill is usually <0.1% (no more than 1 contaminated unit per 10,000 simulated containers).

Environmental Monitoring (EM) Program and §211.113

The obligation in §211.113(b) for "procedures designed to prevent microbiological contamination" implies a necessary precondition: there must be a monitoring system to confirm the effectiveness of those procedures. This is the regulatory basis for the EM program.

The four components of an EM program:

  1. Sampling Locations: graded by risk (ISO 5/Grade A critical zone → ISO 7/Grade B background → ISO 8/Grade C/D support areas), with sampling points and their scientific justification defined
  2. Sampling Methods: settle plates (passive sampling), contact/RODAC plates (surface sampling), active air sampling (instruments such as RCS and SAS); personnel monitoring (glove fingerprints, gown sampling)
  3. Alert Levels & Action Levels: an Alert Level excursion triggers a trend investigation; an Action Level excursion triggers a CAPA and an assessment of batch impact
  4. Trend Analysis: periodic analysis of EM data trends to identify early signs of deterioration (excursion trending)

An EM action limit excursion does not automatically lead to batch rejection, but the QU must investigate and assess:

  • Did the excursion occur during critical filling operations (vs. during cleaning/maintenance)?
  • What type of microorganism was isolated (opportunistic pathogen vs. common environmental flora)?
  • Is the location in a critical area close to exposed product or containers?
  • What are the sterility test results of the same batch or related batches?

4.1 §211.122 — Labeling Materials: Examination and Usage Criteria

Regulatory Text

§ 211.122(a) There shall be written procedures describing in sufficient detail the receipt, identification, storage, handling, sampling, examination, and/or testing of labeling and packaging materials; such written procedures shall be followed. Labeling and packaging materials shall be representatively sampled, and examined or tested upon receipt and before use in packaging or labeling of a drug product.

§ 211.122(b) Any labeling or packaging materials meeting appropriate written specifications may be approved and released for use. Any labeling or packaging materials that do not meet such specifications shall be rejected to prevent their use in operations for which they are unsuitable.

§ 211.122(c) Records shall be maintained for each shipment received of each different labeling and packaging material indicating receipt, examination or testing, and whether accepted or rejected.

§ 211.122(d) Labels and other labeling materials for each different drug product, strength, dosage form, or quantity of contents shall be stored separately with suitable identification. Access to the storage area shall be limited to authorized personnel.

§ 211.122(e) Obsolete and outdated labels, labeling, and other packaging materials shall be destroyed.

§ 211.122(f) Use of gang-printed labeling for different drug products, or different strengths or net contents of the same drug product, is prohibited unless the labeling from gang-printed sheets is adequately differentiated by size, shape, or color.

§ 211.122(g) If cut labeling is used, it is subject to the following labeling identity requirements:

  1. Separate storage areas or physical segregation of labels from labeling for different drug products, or for different strengths or net contents of the same drug product, to prevent mix-ups.
  2. Identification of the drug product, strength, quantity of contents, and lot or control number of the drug product for which the labeling is intended on the containers of cut labeling.

Labeling defined under 21 CFR 201.1 (for context):

  • Container labels (primary and secondary)
  • Package inserts / prescribing information
  • Carton labeling
  • Patient medication guides
  • Any written, printed, or graphic matter accompanying a drug product
"Labeling and packaging materials shall be representatively sampled, and examined or tested upon receipt and before use in packaging or labeling of a drug product." — 21 CFR § 211.122(a)

Tutorial Commentary

Why are label mix-ups the most common cause of FDA recalls?

According to FDA's annual recall data, labeling mix-ups have long ranked among the top three causes of recalls. Almost every year, some manufacturer issues a Class I recall (the highest-risk recall class) because the wrong label was affixed to a product. A drug with the wrong label can lead a patient to take the wrong drug or the wrong dose, which at best means no therapeutic effect and at worst endangers life. For this reason, Subpart G builds multiple layers of prevention, controlling labeling from receipt of materials through release of the finished product.

§211.122 is built on the logic of "control from the moment of receipt": from the moment labeling materials are received from the supplier, there must be written procedures governing receipt, identification, storage, handling, sampling, examination, and approval or rejection. A failure at any single step can let the wrong label reach the production line.

Risks and Controls for Gang-Printed Labeling

Gang-printed labeling means labeling printed on a single sheet that carries labels for different products, different strengths, or different dosage levels. It saves printing cost but carries a very high mix-up risk: if the cutting step goes wrong, an operator may pick up a label of the wrong strength.

§211.122(f) prohibits gang-printed labeling unless the labels are adequately differentiated by size, shape, or color. In practice, most compliant manufacturers avoid gang-printed labeling entirely and use dedicated label rolls for a single product/strength, which eliminates the risk at its source. Automated labeling systems combined with bar code verification add a further layer of protection.

Contents of the Label Specification

§211.122(b) requires "appropriate written specifications". A complete label specification should cover:

  • Visual/text requirements: font, font size, color codes (Pantone or CMYK values), layout, and required legal statements
  • Physical specifications: dimensions (length × width ± tolerance), material (paper stock, type of film), and adhesive specification
  • Print quality: bar code readability (Grade B or better), clarity standards, no ink bleeding, no color deviation
  • Compliance statements: conformity with the labeling requirements of 21 CFR Part 201 and consistency with the approved NDA/ANDA/BLA labeling version

Common 483 Observations

★ Unrepresentative sampling: Only one outer carton was sampled at receipt, with no representative sampling of the whole label lot under an AQL sampling plan; or the sampling location and quantity were not recorded.

★ Obsolete labels not destroyed: §211.122(e) requires that obsolete or outdated labels be destroyed, but the inspection found old-version labels for a discontinued strength still held in the warehouse, or old-version labels that were not destroyed per procedure and without records.

★ Cut labels not individually identified: §211.122(g)(2) requires that containers of cut labels be identified with the drug product name, strength, dosage form and lot number, but the inspection found cut labels loose in an unidentified box, so the lot they belonged to could not be confirmed.

★ Inadequate access control to the storage area: The label storage area was not effectively restricted to authorized personnel: the door had no lock or the lock was not used, and no access log was kept.

Label Control Design for CDMOs Serving Multiple Clients

A CDMO takes on filling and packaging for many clients at the same time, so putting §211.122 into practice is especially critical:

  • Segregated storage: Each client's labels are stored in a separate locked compartment (cage or dedicated cabinet), with the keys held by the quality unit so that unauthorized personnel cannot enter. Labels of different clients must not be stored together in the same compartment.
  • Version management: Whenever a client updates its label design, all old-version labels must be destroyed and the quantity destroyed recorded. After the new version is received into stock, the full receipt examination procedure is repeated (including visual confirmation against the approved version).
  • Automated verification systems: Advanced CDMOs use camera vision systems to verify label content 100% in-line, detecting SKU codes, lot number format, bar codes, and expiration date format. This greatly reduces the error rate of manual checking and also satisfies the §211.125(f) provision that 100% examination exempts the line from reconciliation.
  • Mix-up risk assessment: For client labels that look alike (similar fonts, similar sizes, the same color scheme), perform a Label Mix-up Risk Assessment and design additional safeguards such as warning markings or color identification codes.

How Label Material Specification Review Works in Practice

Take a CDMO that accepts a new client as an example. A practical §211.122-compliant workflow is as follows:

  1. Specification set-up: The client supplies the approved label artwork (PDF, AI, or EPS format), and the CDMO's QA and packaging engineers jointly prepare the Label Material Specification, covering visual requirements, dimensional tolerances, bar code specifications, and print quality standards. The specification takes effect only after written approval by the client's QA.
  2. Supplier qualification: The label printer must pass the CDMO's supplier qualification assessment, confirming that its print quality control system meets requirements.
  3. First-lot receipt confirmation: When new labels are first received, QA performs an Enhanced Incoming Inspection: 100% of the first lot is visually checked against the approved artwork, and once confirmed, a sample is retained as the Reference Standard Sample for subsequent receipts.
  4. Routine receipt: Later lots are received according to the sampling plan, using the retained reference standard sample as the comparison basis. Any deviation leads to immediate rejection and notification of the supplier.
4.2 §211.125 — Labeling Issuance

Regulatory Text

§ 211.125(a) Procedures shall be utilized to control label issuance to prevent errors and provide for reconciliation.

§ 211.125(b) Labels for a drug product shall be issued to manufacturing personnel only by authorized personnel.

§ 211.125(c) Written and approved specifications shall exist for each label.

§ 211.125(d) Procedures shall specify the use of a lot or control number that can be used to determine the complete manufacturing history of the drug product.

§ 211.125(e) All cut or gang-printed labels or labeling, bearing lot or control numbers, shall be carefully examined to ensure that the labeling used in the packaging operation is the correct labeling for the drug product being processed.

§ 211.125(f) — Key reconciliation requirement:

  • Reconciliation is required for all labels issued, used, and returned.
  • Reconciliation shall be between the quantity of labeling issued, used, and returned, and the number of units processed.
  • Discrepancies found in the reconciliation shall be investigated.
  • Labeling reconciliation is waived for cut or gang-printed labeling if a 100 percent examination for correct labeling is performed in conjunction with the labeling operation.

§ 211.125(g) All excess labeling bearing lot or control numbers shall be destroyed.

Typical reconciliation calculation:

  • Labels Issued (A) = Labels Used (B) + Labels Returned (C) + Labels Destroyed — damaged (D)
  • Yield % = B ÷ Units Produced × 100
  • Variance = A − (B + C + D) — must equal zero or be within acceptable limit; otherwise, deviation investigation is required
"Reconciliation shall be between the quantity of labeling issued, used, and returned, and the number of units processed. Discrepancies found in the reconciliation shall be investigated." — 21 CFR § 211.125(f)

Tutorial Commentary

The Core Purpose of Label Reconciliation

Label reconciliation is the mechanism that checks that the number of labels issued equals the number used plus the number returned plus the number destroyed. Its purpose is not to save labels but to ensure that no label disappears unaccounted for, because any unexplained missing label may have been affixed to the wrong product or lost somewhere in the facility, becoming a potential mix-up risk.

The exemption in §211.125(f): reconciliation may be waived only where a 100 percent examination is performed. This means that without a 100% automated or visual verification system, full label reconciliation must be performed, and any discrepancy must trigger a deviation investigation.

Integration with Electronic Batch Records (eBR) and 21 CFR Part 11

In traditional paper batch records, label reconciliation is calculated by hand: operators count, record, and calculate the variance. Modern manufacturers increasingly adopt Electronic Batch Records (eBR), combined with a warehouse management system (WMS) and labeling machine counters, to perform label reconciliation automatically.

When an eBR system performs this function, the whole system must comply with 21 CFR Part 11 (electronic records and electronic signatures):

  • Audit Trail: The time stamps and user records of all label issuance and reconciliation actions cannot be deleted or modified
  • Access Control: The functions for issuing labels and confirming reconciliation are granted only to specific roles
  • Electronic Signature: The issuer, the operator, and the QA reviewer each confirm with their own electronic signature, which has the same legal effect as a handwritten signature
  • Computer System Validation (CSV): The eBR system itself must be fully validated (IQ/OQ/PQ) to ensure the calculation logic is correct

The Two-Gate Principle for Label Issuance

★ §211.125(b) provides that labeling may be issued to production personnel only by authorized personnel. In practice, many manufacturers require that label issuance be signed off by QA or by warehouse staff with written authorization, and production personnel may not go to the label warehouse and take labels themselves.

★ §211.125(g) states explicitly that excess labeling bearing lot or control numbers must be destroyed, not returned to stock for later use. This is the key control that prevents labels of lot A from ending up on lot B: excess labels bearing an old lot number must never re-enter production. The method of destruction (tearing, shredding, or incineration) and the destruction records must both be defined in written procedures.

★ §211.125(c) requires an approved written specification for each label. This echoes §211.122(b): receipt examination is performed against the specification, and issuance is confirmed against the specification too. The specification must be the latest approved version.

Design Points for the Label Issuance Log

A compliant label issuance log should include the following fields:

  • Label name, material number, and version number (to confirm the correct version is used)
  • Corresponding batch/lot number, drug product name, and strength
  • Issued quantity, including the per-roll or per-carton quantity calculation for roll labels
  • Used quantity, taken from the labeling machine counter or a manual count
  • Returned quantity: complete unused labels (only labels not bearing a lot number may be returned)
  • Destroyed quantity: excess labels bearing a lot number, with the signature of the person witnessing destruction
  • Variance and explanation (where the variance exceeds the acceptable limit, the deviation report number must be attached)
  • Issuer's signature/electronic signature, receiver (production operator) confirmation, and QA review signature

The Logic for Setting an Acceptable Label Variance Limit

A reconciliation variance does not necessarily mean labels were used incorrectly. A small number of labels are genuinely lost during labeling to machine jams, test runs, and set-up checks. Manufacturers therefore usually set an Acceptable Variance Limit, and a formal deviation investigation is triggered only when it is exceeded.

Ways to set the acceptable variance limit:

  • Based on historical data: Analyze label reconciliation data from the past 12–24 months, calculate the mean and standard deviation of normal losses, and use the mean ± 3σ as the limit range
  • Based on equipment characteristics: Automated high-speed labeling machines usually have lower loss rates than semi-automatic equipment, so limits may differ between lines and must be established separately
  • Do not set limits too wide: FDA will review whether the acceptable limit is reasonable; an overly wide limit (such as ±10%) may be judged unable to detect label mix-up events effectively
  • Any negative variance (quantity used > quantity issued) has zero tolerance: Usage exceeding issuance should be impossible in theory; if the records show it, there must be a recording error or a more serious problem, and it must be investigated immediately

Regulatory Text

§ 211.130(a) There shall be written procedures designed to assure that correct labels, labeling, and packaging materials are used for drug products; such written procedures shall be followed.

§ 211.130(b) Prevention of mix-ups and cross-contamination by physical or spatial separation from operations on other drug products.

§ 211.130(c) Identification of the drug product with a lot or control number that permits determination of the history of the manufacture and control of the batch.

§ 211.130(d) Examination of packaging and labeling materials for suitability and correctness before packaging operations, and documentation of such examination in the batch production record.

Key control elements required:

  • Line clearance (§211.130(e)) — documented inspection before each batch run; all residuals from prior operations removed
  • Physical/spatial separation (§211.130(b)) — no concurrent packaging of different products unless adequately segregated
  • Pre-operational material check (§211.130(d)) — correctness of all labeling/packaging materials confirmed and documented before start
  • Lot/batch number assignment (§211.130(c)) — unambiguous lot identification maintained throughout packaging operations
"Inspection of the packaging and labeling facilities immediately before use to assure that all drug products have been removed from previous operations." — 21 CFR § 211.130(e)

Tutorial Commentary

Line Clearance: The Most Important Anti-Mix-Up Gate

Line clearance is a mandatory compliance gate before the start of every batch. The operator and QA must jointly confirm that all product, labels, packaging materials, and documents from the previous batch have been removed or properly disposed of, and cannot remain on the line and cause a mix-up.

Line clearance failures are frequent flyers in FDA 483 observations. Typical deficiencies include: product from the previous batch left on the conveyor, labels from the previous batch stuck in corners of the equipment or in the label reel clamps, previous-batch record documents mixed into the new batch folder, and empty cartons or waste containers not emptied. Any one of these can trigger a production stop and a deviation investigation, and in serious cases the entire batch may be scrapped.

Four Design Principles for a Line Clearance SOP

  • Line Clearance Checklist: Itemize the things to be cleared or confirmed (all sides of the equipment, conveyors, label reel spindles, overhead racks, waste containers, the floor near the equipment, and so on). It must cover every hidden corner where items may be left behind, with nothing omitted.
  • Two-Person Verification: After the operator performs the clearance, QA independently rechecks (QA must not sign merely on the basis of the operator's record). Both signatures are required, and each should carry a time stamp.
  • BPR Integration: The line clearance record is an integral part of the batch production record (BPR), and its retention period follows §211.180 (at least one year after the product's expiration date, or at least three years after batch release, whichever is longer).
  • Failure handling procedure: If line clearance finds residue from the previous batch, work on the new batch must stop and the deviation procedure must be initiated. The impact of the residue on both the previous and the new batch is assessed, and QA makes the final decision on the disposition of both batches.

Special Considerations for Sterile Injectables: Labeling Operations and CCI

For sterile injectables, the labeling operation requirements of §211.130 are closely tied to Container Closure Integrity (CCI) checks:

  • Before labeling, confirm that containers have passed CCI testing (such as visual inspection, pressure testing, or dye ingress testing); nonconforming containers must be completely removed before labels are applied.
  • The mechanical pressure of automatic labeling machines can stress fragile glass vials, so equipment validation must evaluate the potential effect of the labeling machine on container integrity, usually during the PQ (Performance Qualification) stage.
  • After labeling, the inspection window must not be covered, so that the end user can still visually check the contents of the container before use.

Common FDA 483: "Failure to perform adequate line clearance"

Typical 483 wording in an FDA inspection report:

"Failure to adequately perform and document line clearance between batches of different drug products in the packaging area. Specifically, labels from [Product A] were found in the packaging area during the set-up for [Product B]."

CAPA design direction:

  • Immediate Action: stop the affected batches, assess whether there is a risk of product mix-up, and assess the safety of batches already distributed
  • Short-term CAPA: revise the line clearance SOP and add a photo documentation requirement as objective evidence
  • Long-term CAPA: introduce a bar code scanning verification system (confirming that all materials on the line belong to the current batch); increase the frequency of QA on-the-floor supervision; perform a quarterly process walk-through audit of the line clearance procedure
  • Training: retrain all packaging operators and QA on the line clearance procedure, and evaluate training effectiveness (pre/post assessment)
4.4 §211.132 — Tamper-Evident Packaging for OTC Human Drug Products

Regulatory Text

§ 211.132(a) General requirement: Because most OTC drug products are not dispensed pursuant to a prescription, it is the responsibility of manufacturers and packagers to protect the public from products that have been deliberately tampered with after they leave the place of manufacture. OTC human drug products subject to this section shall be packaged in tamper-evident packaging.

§ 211.132(b) Requirement for tamper-evident package features: Each manufacturer and packer who packages an OTC human drug product (except a dermatological, dentifrice, insulin, or lozenge product) for retail sale shall package the product in a tamper-evident package, if this product is accessible to the public while held for sale. A tamper-evident package is one having one or more indicators or barriers to entry which, if breached or missing, can reasonably be expected to provide visible evidence to consumers that tampering has occurred.

§ 211.132(b)(1) — Labeling requirement: Each retail package of an OTC human drug product covered by this section shall bear a statement that is prominently placed on the label of the product that alerts consumers to the specific tamper-evident feature of the package. The tamper-evident feature and any required label statements are an integral part of the labeling.

§ 211.132(b)(2) — Two-piece hard gelatin capsules: Products packaged in two-piece hard gelatin capsules shall employ at least two tamper-evident packaging features unless the two-piece hard gelatin capsule is sealed using an acceptable tamper-evident technology.

§ 211.132(c) Exemptions:

  • Dermatological drug products
  • Dentifrice drug products
  • Insulin drug products
  • Lozenge drug products
  • Products that are not packaged for retail sale
"A tamper-evident package is one having one or more indicators or barriers to entry which, if breached or missing, can reasonably be expected to provide visible evidence to consumers that tampering has occurred." — 21 CFR § 211.132(b)

Tutorial Commentary

Historical Background of Tamper-Evident Packaging: the 1982 Chicago Tylenol Murders

§211.132 was born directly out of the 1982 Chicago Tylenol Murders, in which someone injected cyanide into Tylenol capsules on retail shelves, causing 7 deaths. The incident shook the entire pharmaceutical industry and led FDA to issue emergency tamper-resistant packaging regulations in 1982, a milestone in US drug packaging regulation.

After the incident, pharmaceutical packaging design shifted from "consumer convenience" to "safety first": seal strips, shrink bands, and induction sealing became standard for OTC drugs, and consumers learned to "check the package is intact before opening".

Labeling Statement Requirements (§211.132(b)(1))

★ The tamper-evident feature must be accompanied by a prominent statement on the label that tells the consumer the specific tamper-evident mechanism. For example: "Do not use if printed seal under cap is broken or missing" or "Sealed for your protection. Do not use if inner seal is broken."

★ This statement is itself part of the labeling, so it is subject to the full set of labeling control procedures in §211.122–211.130. The version of the tamper-evident statement must match the approved NDA/ANDA labeling, and any wording change must go through the labeling change procedure.

Coordination of §211.134 Inspection with Tamper-Evident Features

§211.134 requires finished product inspection on completion of packaging. For OTC drugs with tamper-evident requirements, the inspection checklist must expressly include items confirming the integrity of the tamper-evident features:

  • Is the induction-seal foil liner tightly bonded, with no air bubbles and no lifted edges?
  • Is the shrink band evenly shrunk, with no cracks and no openings?
  • Does the capsule sealing band completely cover the seam between the two halves?
  • Is the tamper-evident statement clearly legible on the label?

"A representative sample of units shall be collected at the completion of finishing operations and shall be visually examined for correct labeling." — 21 CFR § 211.134(b)

Tutorial Commentary

The Role of §211.134: the "Final Quality Gate" of the Packaging Line

§211.134 is the final confirmation point in the entire Subpart G labeling control chain. After all labeling control procedures have been carried out, finished product inspection is the last line of defense for confirming that "every product leaving the facility carries the correct label". Its design logic is "begin with the end in mind": even if all the earlier controls are executed perfectly, finished product inspection still needs to exist independently.

During actual inspections, FDA investigators often pick up finished product samples in the packaging area and examine them personally. If an investigator finds a labeling problem (such as an unclear expiration date, or a skewed label covering key information) while the finished product inspection record says "Pass", the manufacturer's finished product inspection procedure itself becomes the target of a 483.

Designing the Sampling Timing for "Representative Samples"

§211.134(b) requires that representative samples be collected "at the completion of finishing operations". Best practices for designing the sampling plan:

  • Spread across time points: Samples should be collected from each of the start, middle, and end of the batch, not just the last few finished units, to confirm consistency across the whole batch.
  • Sampling at roll changes: For automatic labeling machines using label rolls, sample before and after each label roll change to confirm that the change does not affect label correctness, alignment, and print quality.
  • Sample quantity per written specification: The sampling quantity should be specified in writing (for example AQL Level II normal sampling, or a fixed count such as "at least 10 units per batch, plus 1 more for every 500 units"); operators must not decide the sample quantity subjectively.
  • Distinguishing retention samples from finished product samples: The samples used for finished product label inspection under §211.134 are different from the retention samples under §211.170: retention samples are kept for future stability testing or investigations, whereas finished product label inspection samples may be disposed of per procedure after use.

Compliance Positioning of Automated Vision Inspection

More and more high-speed packaging lines use camera vision inspection systems to perform 100% in-line label verification, rather than relying only on manual sampling inspection. Compliance considerations for such systems:

  • The system must be fully validated (IQ/OQ/PQ) to confirm that, at production speed, it reliably detects the various label defects (such as blurred text, bar code errors, and missing or inverted labels)
  • Nonconforming items rejected by the system (rejects) must be recorded and handled under a written procedure (usually going straight into a waste container and never allowed back onto the line)
  • Records of system validation and periodic requalification are a focus of FDA inspections
  • Even with an automated vision system, it is advisable to keep the manual check of representative samples required by §211.134(b), as an independent confirmation of system reliability

"To assure that a drug product meets applicable standards of identity, strength, quality, and purity at the time of use, it shall bear an expiration date determined by appropriate stability testing." — 21 CFR § 211.137(a)

Tutorial Commentary

Regulatory Basis for Expiration Dating: Stability Data Determine the Date

The core logic of §211.137: the expiration date must be determined by stability testing, not estimated by the manufacturer. The regulatory basis for stability testing is §211.166, and it is closely aligned with ICH standards:

  • ICH Q1A(R2): guideline on stability testing of new drug substances and products. It specifies the test conditions (temperature/humidity), time points, and the minimum number of batches (usually three or more)
  • ICH Q1E: evaluation of stability data (extrapolation). It allows the shelf life to be extrapolated by statistical methods (linear regression) when real-time data are incomplete, subject to conditions
  • ICH Q1B: photostability testing. The shelf life of light-sensitive products must take photodegradation testing into account

Two stability pathways:

  • Real-time stability: Long-term monitoring under the intended storage conditions (such as 25°C/60% RH, or 5°C refrigerated). This is the only definitive basis for the expiration date, and the final shelf life must be supported by real-time data.
  • Accelerated stability: Short-term testing under accelerated conditions (such as 40°C/75% RH). It can support setting an interim shelf life, but must be followed up and confirmed by ongoing real-time stability data.

The Relationship Between Overage and Expiration Dating

Overage means adding active ingredient in excess of the labeled amount to the formulation, to compensate for degradation losses during manufacturing or storage so that the product still meets the labeled content specification at End of Shelf Life (EoSL). For example: for a label claim of 100 mg/tablet, the formulation is charged at 105 mg to ensure the content is still ≥95% after 24 months.

FDA's position on overage:

  • Overage must be supported by stability data showing that the degradation rate is reasonable and that the overage amount is sufficient to compensate for it
  • Increasing overage cannot substitute for adequate stability studies as a way to extend the shelf life
  • An overage that is too large (usually >10%) needs adequate scientific justification, to avoid patients receiving an excess of active ingredient early in the shelf life
  • The use of overage must be declared and approved in the NDA/ANDA and cannot be adjusted on the manufacturer's own initiative without a filing

The Most Frequently Cited 483 Observation: Expiration Dating Not Supported by Stability Data

Typical 483 wording:

"The expiration dating of your drug product [Product X, Lot Y] is not supported by stability data. Specifically, stability studies on file cover only 18 months of storage data, yet the product label bears a 24-month expiration date."

Common root causes:

  • The stability program is incompletely designed and does not cover all strengths, packaging materials, or storage conditions
  • A stability sample gave an OOS result at some time point, but it was not investigated under §211.192 and the expiration date was not shortened
  • No stability study was repeated after a change in formulation or packaging material (post-change stability study)
  • The starting point for calculating the expiration date (manufacturing date vs. filling date vs. release date) is not defined in writing, so different batches use different starting points

CDMO Responsibility for Expiration Dating: Key Quality Agreement Clauses

★ Even when the stability data are supplied by the client (the NDA/ANDA holder), the CDMO that manufactures and fills the product still bears CGMP responsibility under 21 CFR 211 for the correctness of the expiration date printed on the label.

★ The Quality Agreement should clearly define the following division of responsibilities:

  • The client provides the stability data and the approved expiration date calculation rule (such as "24 months from the filling date")
  • The CDMO calculates the expiration date of each batch according to the rule provided by the client, and the result is recorded in the batch record and reviewed by QA
  • The written rule for calculating the expiration date (including the definition of the starting date and format requirements) must be approved in writing by the client's QA before it is implemented
  • If the client's stability data change (for example, the shelf life is shortened), the client must notify the CDMO immediately and update the Quality Agreement appendix

Design Points for a Complete Stability Program

A stability program that meets the requirements of §211.137 + §211.166 + ICH Q1A should include:

  • Storage conditions: long-term (25°C/60% RH or 5°C) + accelerated (40°C/75% RH) ± intermediate (30°C/65% RH)
  • Batch representativeness: at least three batches of pilot scale or larger, covering representative production and packaging material lots
  • Test time points: 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36 months (depending on the target shelf life); the minimum time points specified in ICH Q1A must not be omitted
  • Stability-Indicating Assay: assay + degradation products (HPLC) + appearance + physical properties (pH, dissolution, subvisible particulates, and so on)
  • Packaging consistency: stability batches must use exactly the same container-closure system as commercial production
  • Annual Stability: at least one commercial batch per year is placed on the ongoing stability program, so that the shelf life remains supported throughout the product lifecycle

Regulatory Text

§ 211.142(a) Quarantined drug products shall be stored in a manner to prevent unauthorized use before release by the quality control unit.

  • Controlled temperature storage — temperature monitoring with continuous recorders or calibrated data loggers
  • Humidity and light control — especially for hygroscopic or photosensitive drug products
  • Temperature excursion management — written SOP for investigation and product disposition decisions
  • Pest control — documented pest control program, no evidence of infestation
  • Access control — restricted entry to quarantine and controlled substance storage areas

"Drug products shall be stored under appropriate conditions of temperature, humidity, and light so that the identity, strength, quality, and purity of the drug products are not adversely affected." — 21 CFR § 211.142(b)

Tutorial Commentary

Four Core Dimensions of Warehouse Control

The text of §211.142 is brief, but the practical requirements are quite broad:

  • Temperature: Different storage conditions such as refrigerated (2–8°C), frozen (-20°C or -80°C), and controlled room temperature (CRT, 15–30°C) need separate storage areas and continuous temperature monitoring records (continuous recorders or data loggers with audit trails).
  • Quarantine segregation: Unreleased (quarantine) product and released product must be physically separated, or electronically segregated through a warehouse management system (WMS). The system must have access controls that prevent operators from changing the quarantine status without QA authorization.
  • Light: Light-sensitive drugs (some antibiotics, some biologics) must be stored protected from light, and the warehouse lighting design or the packaging design must meet this requirement.
  • Humidity: For hygroscopic products (such as some lyophilized products and effervescent products) or packaging systems with desiccant, warehouse relative humidity control is critical, with written limits and monitoring records.

Temperature Excursion Management Procedure

Temperature excursions are the most common GMP challenge in warehousing. A complete temperature excursion management procedure should include:

  • Alert vs. Action Limits: The alert limit is tighter than the specification limit so that investigation is triggered early. For example, with a 2–8°C storage specification, set the alert limit at 3–7°C and the action limit at 2–8°C.
  • Real-time alarm system: When the temperature goes outside the alert limit, on-duty staff are notified automatically (by phone, text message, or a SCADA alarm), without relying on periodic rounds by personnel.
  • Impact Assessment by deviation category: Classify by magnitude and duration. A minor excursion (such as +0.5°C for 15 minutes) may only need to be recorded; a major excursion (such as 12°C for 4 hours) requires a formal deviation investigation to assess the effect on product stability.
  • Mean Kinetic Temperature (MKT): Calculate the MKT from the temperature-time data during the excursion and compare it with the stability study conditions to assess whether the product is still within an acceptable range of temperature exposure. USP <1079> provides the MKT calculation formula.
  • Product disposition decision: Based on the assessment, QA decides whether the batch can be released, needs retesting, can be used with downgraded status, or must be destroyed, with written sign-off by the QA head.

Temperature Mapping of Temperature-Controlled Chambers

According to USP <1079> (Good Storage and Distribution Practices for Drug Products) and WHO guidance, warehouse refrigeration equipment must undergo temperature mapping to confirm that every point inside the equipment meets the storage specification. Key elements of the evaluation:

  • Hot/Cold Spot identification: Distribute sensors evenly (at least 9 points recommended) to find the highest and lowest temperature locations, which then become the installation positions for routine monitoring sensors
  • Door Open Recovery Time: Simulate door opening during normal use to confirm that the equipment returns to within the specification range in an acceptable time
  • Power Failure Hold Time: Evaluate how long the equipment can hold the specified temperature after a power failure (essential for defining the time window for emergency response)
  • Seasonal requalification: Perform mapping once in summer (highest ambient temperature) and once in winter (lowest ambient temperature), because ambient temperature significantly affects equipment performance

Common 483 Warehousing Deficiencies

★ Interruption of temperature records not investigated: The temperature recording system shows a gap of several hours (logger power loss or sensor failure), but no deviation report documents the temperature conditions or the impact assessment for that interruption.

★ Unclear quarantine status: Unreleased and released product in the warehouse are distinguished only by paper labels, with no physical barrier or WMS electronic control, so there is a risk of mix-up during handling.

★ Monitoring equipment out of calibration: The calibration due date of the warehouse temperature sensor or data logger has passed, yet it is still in use and its calibration status is not indicated.

4.7 §211.150 — Distribution Procedures

Regulatory Text

§ 211.150(a) Written procedures shall be established, and followed, describing the distribution of drug products. They shall include:

§ 211.150(a)(1) A procedure whereby the oldest approved stock of a drug product is distributed first. Deviation from this requirement is permitted if such deviation is temporary and appropriate.

§ 211.150(a)(2) A system by which the distribution of each lot of drug product can be readily determined to facilitate its recall if necessary.

§ 211.150(b) Records shall be maintained for each drug product. Such records shall include the name and label potency of the drug product, the strength, the name of the final dosage form, the size and type of container, the number of containers, and the date of distribution. Each shipment shall be accompanied by records indicating the name and address of the consignee.

Two interconnected purposes of §211.150:

  • FIFO/FEFO compliance — systematic inventory rotation to minimize patient risk from expired or degraded product reaching consumers
  • Recall traceability — distribution records must enable rapid identification of all recipients of any lot within 72 hours (FDA recall response expectation)

Required content of distribution records (§ 211.150(b)):

  • Drug product name and label potency
  • Strength and dosage form
  • Container size and type
  • Number of containers shipped
  • Date of distribution
  • Name and address of consignee (recipient)
  • Lot/control number
"A system by which the distribution of each lot of drug product can be readily determined to facilitate its recall if necessary." — 21 CFR § 211.150(a)(2)

Tutorial Commentary

FIFO vs. FEFO: First-In, First-Out vs. First-Expired, First-Out

FIFO (First-In, First-Out) is the stock rotation principle expressly required by §211.150(a)(1). In practice, a more advanced approach is FEFO (First-Expired, First-Out), which ships the batch closest to expiry first rather than ordering purely by manufacturing or receipt date. FEFO is more effective at preventing product from remaining in the warehouse at the end of a batch's shelf life.

Implementing this in a WMS (Warehouse Management System):

  • The system automatically designates FIFO or FEFO batches first in the pick order, preventing operators from manually selecting batches that do not follow the rotation principle
  • Any shipment that deviates from FIFO/FEFO must have a written justification (such as a client's urgent request for a specific batch), be approved by QA, and be recorded in a distribution deviation record
  • §211.150(a)(1) allows a "temporary and appropriate deviation", but "temporary" must have a defined time limit and must not become the norm

Distribution Records and the 72-Hour Recall Response

The core requirement of §211.150(a)(2) is that the complete distribution of any batch of drug product must be readily determinable, to support recall operations.

Under the recall regulations (21 CFR Part 7) and the Recall Policy Manual (RPM), FDA expects manufacturers to provide a complete distribution list within 72 hours, listing the name, address, and quantity shipped for every consignee that received the recalled batch. This is critical to recall effectiveness: after a recall ends, FDA performs a recall audit to confirm that all consignees of distributed product have been contacted.

Modern best practice: in the ERP/WMS system, entering a lot number immediately exports the complete distribution list, with no need to look up records one by one.

GDP (Good Distribution Practice) and the International Dimension of §211.150

Good Distribution Practice (GDP) is an extension of the concept in §211.150 and is systematically set out in several international regulatory frameworks:

  • EU GDP Guidelines (2013/C 343/01): The most comprehensive GDP guidance in the EU, applicable to wholesale distributors and logistics providers, covering qualification, temperature-controlled logistics, deviation management, and complaint handling
  • WHO TRS No. 957, Annex 5 (2010): WHO good storage and distribution practices, widely adopted by regulators in developing countries
  • USP <1079> (Good Storage and Distribution Practices): Technical guidance giving specific technical requirements for temperature-controlled drugs, including MKT calculation and temperature mapping
  • IATA DGR (Dangerous Goods Regulations): Applies to air transport of biological samples or drugs classed as dangerous goods

Quality Agreement Design for CDMO Cold Chain Logistics

For a CDMO handling refrigerated/frozen drug products, putting §211.150 into the Quality Agreement is critical:

  • Handover of warehouse temperature records: When handing product over to the client or a third-party logistics provider, the CDMO must provide complete warehouse temperature records, confirming that no temperature excursion occurred during CDMO custody or that any excursion was assessed and closed.
  • Responsibility for retaining distribution records: The Quality Agreement should specify which party keeps the distribution records (CDMO or client), the retention period (usually at least one year after the product's expiration date), and the SLA for retrieving records in a recall (such as within 24 hours).
  • Carrier qualification (GDP qualification): If the CDMO arranges the carrier, the carrier must undergo a documented GDP qualification assessment; if the client arranges logistics itself, the CDMO's responsibility ends at the handover point at the facility, and the Quality Agreement must define the handover point clearly (for example, "when the goods are loaded onto the client's logistics provider").
  • Multi-tier supply chain traceability: In a chain of client → CDMO filling → third-party warehouse → third-party logistics → distributor → hospital/pharmacy, the distribution records at each node must be linkable, so that in a recall it is possible to trace back from the market lot number to every intermediate node.

Distribution Record Design and Recall Drills

Design points for a compliant distribution record system:

  • Real-time updates: Records are updated within 24 hours of each shipment; retrospective entries are not allowed
  • Searchability: With the lot number as the key, a complete distribution list can be exported within 10 minutes (to support a recall)
  • Record completeness: Includes all fields required by §211.150(b), and cannot be deleted (compliant with 21 CFR Part 11 where applicable)
  • Annual Recall Drill: Perform a simulated recall (mock recall) at least once a year, simulating distribution tracing for a given lot, to evaluate whether 100% traceability can be achieved within 72 hours; the drill results are documented and used for continual improvement

§211.150 and the Companion Obligations of FDA 21 CFR Part 7

★ 21 CFR Part 7 (Enforcement Policy) sets out the manufacturer's obligations when FDA requests a recall, including: (1) submitting the distribution list within the time FDA specifies; (2) contacting all direct consignees; (3) submitting periodic recall status reports to FDA; and (4) the final recall effectiveness assessment.

★ The distribution record system established under §211.150 is the technical foundation for meeting the Part 7 recall obligations. If distribution records are incomplete or hard to query, the manufacturer cannot provide the distribution list within the required time and may be viewed by FDA as obstructing the execution of the recall, inviting additional regulatory attention.

★ Special note: §211.150(b) requires each batch's distribution record to include the "name and address of the consignee", which means that records of shipments to end institutions such as hospitals, clinics, and pharmacies must be precise to the institution's address, not just recorded down to the wholesaler. For products with multi-tier distribution, the manufacturer's direct record-keeping responsibility extends to the first direct consignee (that is, the direct customer), and tracing at later tiers falls to the record systems of the wholesalers/distributors themselves.

Systemic Compliance Improvement

From "Clause-by-Clause Compliance" to "Systematic Label Control"

Many manufacturers take a "box-ticking" approach to §211.122–211.137: every clause has a corresponding written SOP, but the procedures are not integrated with one another, so the overall label control system still has gaps. True compliance excellence has to start from system design:

  • Label Lifecycle Management: Build a control chain with no breaks: label artwork design and approval → printer supplier qualification → receipt examination → storage → issuance → use → reconciliation → destruction.
  • Risk-tiered control: Design controls of different intensity based on product risk level (for example, high-hazard HPAPI products vs. general OTC). High-risk products need extra layers of control (such as two-person verification or a camera vision system), while low-risk products can use leaner procedures.
  • KPI-driven continual improvement: Track and regularly review label-control KPIs such as label reconciliation variance rate, line clearance failure rate, and label receipt rejection rate. When a KPI trend worsens, proactively initiate a CAPA rather than waiting for an FDA inspection to respond.

The Impact of Digital Transformation on Subpart G–H Compliance

Digital transformation in the pharmaceutical industry is reshaping compliance practice under Subpart G–H:

  • Serialization and DSCSA: The labeling requirements of 21 CFR Part 211 mesh closely with the serialization requirements of the Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA), which call for a unique product identifier on each saleable unit. Each product serial number is both a labeling element (§211.130) and the basic unit of distribution traceability (§211.150). Since 2023, the DSCSA serialization and traceability requirements have been fully in effect.
  • Warehouse management system (WMS) integration: A modern WMS integrates quarantine control, FIFO/FEFO rotation, and distribution record generation on one platform, greatly reducing manual errors, but system validation (CSV) becomes a new compliance focus.
  • Internet of Things (IoT) temperature monitoring: Wireless temperature sensors plus a cloud monitoring platform (such as Dickson or AKCP) enable real-time temperature tracking in the warehouse and in transit and generate compliant records automatically, greatly improving the efficiency of §211.142 compliance. However, equipment validation and data integrity (21 CFR Part 11) must be addressed at the same time.

Pre-Inspection Self-Assessment Points for Subpart G–H

Pre-Inspection Self-Assessment checklist for Subpart G–H before an FDA inspection:

  • Spot-check label reconciliation records for the last 12 months: are there variances? Does every variance have a deviation report and root cause investigation?
  • Check the label warehouse: have all obsolete labels been destroyed? Is the storage segregation clear? Are the access records complete?
  • Review line clearance records: are there two signatures? Is the time stamp before the start of the batch?
  • Check the stability program: does every marketed strength have ongoing stability batches under test? Is there any OOS result at any time point that has not been investigated and closed?
  • Review warehouse temperature records: have there been temperature excursions in the past 12 months? Does every excursion have an impact assessment and a documented disposition decision?
  • Run a quick mock recall: pick any recent lot number and measure how long it takes from issuing the instruction to producing a complete distribution list. If it takes more than 4 hours, the searchability of the record system needs improvement.

Section Summary

  • §211.122: Labeling materials are under written procedural control from the moment of receipt, including sampling, examination, and records of approval and rejection. Tutorial Commentary

The Deming Principle: "Testing Cannot Assure Quality"

W. Edwards Deming's third principle: "Cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality." Testing is a lagging indicator: it can detect defects that have already occurred, but it cannot prevent them.

This is the philosophical basis for CGMP's emphasis on process validation (§211.100 + FDA 2011 Guidance): quality must be designed into the product, not "screened out" by testing.

Practical meaning: a batch that passes every test does not mean the process is in control. If the process drifts, the next batch may fail, and testing cannot warn you in advance. Continued Process Verification (CPV) is the answer.

Method Validation Alignment: ICH Q2(R1) + USP <1225>/<1226>

§211.160(b)(3) and §211.165(e) both require that the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility of test methods be established and documented.

FDA accepts the following frameworks as the basis for validation:

  • ICH Q2(R1): Validation of analytical procedures: characteristics, criteria, and test procedures (applicable to all method types)
  • USP <1225>: Validation of compendial procedures
  • USP <1226>: Verification of compendial procedures, the approach for procedures that appear in the USP

CDMO focus: if a CDMO uses a method supplied by the client, the CDMO must still perform method transfer verification to show that its laboratory can reproduce the client's validation results. Failed method transfers are a common 483 observation.

CDMO Pre-Release Checklist for a Batch

  • All finished product tests (identity, strength, purity, sterility, etc.) are completed and compliant
  • All OOS/OOT results have been investigated, with a justified and acceptable conclusion
  • The batch production record (§211.188) is complete with no open deviations
  • Release testing of raw materials and containers/closures meets specifications (§211.184)
  • The production records have undergone thorough review by the QCU (§211.192)
  • Any additional release conditions in the quality agreement with the client/brand owner have been met

Core Concept: CGMP Laboratory vs. GLP Laboratory: Key Differences

CGMP (21 CFR 211): Release testing of commercial-scale batches, intended to confirm that every batch meets pre-established specifications. It regulates finished dosage form (FDF) manufacturers.

GLP (21 CFR 58): A framework for the reliability of data from safety studies (toxicology, animal studies), ensuring the integrity of data in IND/NDA submissions. It regulates nonclinical research facilities.

The two may exist in the same building (for example, a company that does both R&D and commercial manufacturing), but the requirements are independent and must not be mixed.

Tutorial Commentary

ICH Q1A(R2) Stability Study Protocol Design

§211.166 provides the regulatory framework, and ICH Q1A(R2) (Stability Testing of New Drug Substances and Products) provides the specific scientific methodology. FDA recognizes this guideline as a way of meeting §211.166.

Long-term (real-time) studies:

  • Temperate climatic zones (Zone I/II): 25°C ± 2°C / 60% RH ± 5% RH, minimum 12 months
  • Hot climatic zones (Zone III/IV): 30°C ± 2°C / 65% RH or 75% RH
  • Testing frequency: 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 months, then annually

Accelerated studies:

  • 40°C ± 2°C / 75% RH ± 5% RH, minimum 6 months
  • If a significant change occurs within the 6-month accelerated data, an intermediate condition (30°C/65% RH) must be added

Bracketing & Matrixing（ICH Q1D）

When a drug product has multiple strengths or package sizes, running a full stability study on every combination is both costly and unnecessary. ICH Q1D permits two reduced designs:

Bracketing: Only the extremes of strength and/or fill size are tested, and the stability of the intermediate presentations is inferred from them. Prerequisite: the same dosage form, the same process, and the same container closure system.

Matrixing: At each time point only a subset of the samples is tested, but in the end every batch/strength/package has data at every time point. It suits products whose influencing factors are known and quantifiable.

Risk: if an unexpected failure occurs in one cell of the matrix, the whole matrix may need to be supplemented with additional testing, which can end up costing more.

CDMO Scenario: Ownership of the Stability Program

This is one of the GMP topics that most often causes disputes in CDMO business. The regulatory position is clear:

§211.166 requires the establishment that holds the drug product to set up the stability program. In a CDMO arrangement:

  • If the CDMO is the NDA/ANDA holder (505(b)(2)): the CDMO is fully responsible for the stability program
  • If the brand owner is the NDA holder and the CDMO is the manufacturer: the brand owner is responsible for stability program design and the expiry dating decision; the CDMO is responsible for supplying representative batches for stability and supporting execution.
  • The quality agreement must specify: who is responsible for the annual review, who analyzes trend data, and who decides on shortening the expiry period.

Shelf Life Calculation Logic

Shelf life is not a "guess" but the result of a statistical calculation. The standard approach (ICH Q1E):

  1. Select an appropriate degradation model (linear, Arrhenius kinetics, etc.)
  2. Perform linear regression on the long-term real-time data
  3. Calculate the prediction interval
  4. Find the time at which the 95% one-sided lower confidence limit crosses the acceptance specification
  5. That time point is the maximum permissible shelf life

A business decision (the proposed shelf life) may be shorter than the statistically calculated value, but it may not exceed it.

Tutorial Commentary

What the "Thorough Review" Standard Means in Practice

§211.192 requires the QCU to perform a "thorough review", but the regulation does not define "thorough". FDA inspections and warning letters reveal the following expectations:

Thorough review means:

  • More than "signing to confirm": the QCU must actually read and understand every record
  • Proactively identifying results that are "within specification but close to the limit" (OOT, Out of Trend)
  • Confirming that every unplanned event has a deviation report
  • Confirming that every deviation has a completed CAPA and that the effectiveness of the CAPA has been verified
  • Performing quantity reconciliation for any yield discrepancy
  • Confirming that label reconciliation has been completed

The Special Case of "Problems Found After Release"

§211.192 contains a key phrase: "whether or not the batch has already been distributed" (regardless of whether the batch has already been released).

This means:

  • Problems found after batch release must still be investigated
  • The investigation conclusion may lead to a market recall, and FDA should be notified
  • You cannot skip the investigation or reduce its depth because "the batch has already been sold"
  • Even under pressure from the client to close quickly, the CDMO must insist on a complete investigation; this is the QCU's legal responsibility

CDMO Batch Release Authority: A Non-Transferable QCU Responsibility

§211.192 provides that batches are reviewed and approved by the quality control unit (QCU): not the client (brand owner), not the commercial unit, not the production department.

In CDMO practice, this has important legal implications:

  • The brand owner may require participation in batch review in the quality agreement, but the final release decision must be made by the CDMO's QCU
  • If the client says "this batch can be released" but the CDMO's QCU has open deviations, the CDMO may not release it
  • During an FDA inspection, the CDMO is fully responsible for every batch it released, even if the client applied pressure
  • The quality agreement should specify that the client has the right to give "review comments", but no right to "override the QCU decision"
The most common §211.192 violation patterns (based on analysis of the FDA warning letter database):
  1. Incomplete investigation: The root cause was not identified, and the investigation was closed as "cause unknown"
  2. Inadequate extension of the investigation: An OOS was investigated only for that batch and not extended to other batches from the same production period
  3. CAPA ineffectiveness: The same type of deviation recurs repeatedly, and the CAPA never resolved the root problem
  4. Yield deviations not investigated: A yield deviation beyond the range set in the MPR did not trigger a deviation report
  5. Investigation not closed before batch release: The batch was released on a "preliminary investigation conclusion" without waiting for the full investigation to finish

Tutorial Commentary

OOS Investigation Procedure: The FDA 2006 OOS Guidance Framework

In 2006 FDA issued the guidance "Investigating Out-of-Specification (OOS) Test Results for Pharmaceutical Production", giving industry a clear investigation framework. The guidance is widely regarded as the implementation detail for §211.192 + §211.194.

Phase I: Laboratory Investigation

Conducted by the Laboratory Supervisor, usually within a time limit of 5–10 working days:

  • Review of calculation errors (confirm that the numerical calculations are correct)
  • Review of sample preparation (dilution steps, concentration conversions)
  • Confirmation of instrument calibration status
  • Confirmation of reagent/reference standard expiry
  • Confirmation of analyst qualification
  • Whether the method was followed correctly

There are only two possible Phase I conclusions:

  1. Assignable cause confirmed: A clear, confirmed laboratory error is found (such as instrument malfunction or an incorrect dilution) → the original result may be declared invalid (invalid assay) and a retest performed
  2. No assignable cause found: The result is a genuine OOS → proceed to Phase II

Phase II: Full-Scale Investigation

Led by the QCU; any OOS that Phase I cannot explain must proceed to Phase II:

  • Process investigation: Review the batch production record for equipment malfunctions, process deviations, and operator errors
  • Materials investigation: Review the raw material lots used, including whether the same raw material lot was used in other batches
  • Plant investigation: Extend to other batches where a systemic problem is indicated
  • Additional testing: Additional testing may be performed in Phase II, but only under a pre-approved plan; the additional results may not be used selectively

Key principle: "All Results Must Be Reported"
All results of all tests in Phase II, whether passing or failing, must be recorded. Earlier failing results may not be deleted because later results pass.

When Can an Invalid Assay Be Declared?

This is the topic in OOS management most prone to abuse, and the one FDA inspectors examine most closely.

A legitimate invalid assay declaration requires a confirmed assignable laboratory cause

  • HPLC instrument shutdown with the system problem documented
  • A quantitative transfer error by the analyst during the operation is confirmed
  • Reference standard expired or stored under non-conforming conditions
  • System suitability test failure

Illegitimate invalid assay declarations (frequently found by FDA):

  • "Because the result seems unlikely"
  • "The retest passed, so the original result may be wrong"
  • "The analyst said he felt he had performed the work correctly that day, but was not certain"
  • "Removing outliers under SOX statistical principles" (with no outlier removal rule specified in advance in an SOP)

The Historical Impact of the Barr Laboratories Case (1993)

In 1993, the US Federal District Court (Judge Alfred Wolin) issued its ruling in United States v. Barr Laboratories, which became the legal cornerstone of OOS investigations.

Core rulings (summary):

  • An OOS result may not be retested before an assignable laboratory cause has been found
  • Statistical averaging may not be used to "dilute" a failing result; every individual OOS result must be investigated
  • The number of retest samples and the conditions must be specified in advance and cannot be adjusted after the fact
  • The assumption that "a failing result is just an isolated event" is not acceptable without scientific justification

After this ruling, FDA took 13 years to formally write these principles into a guidance document in 2006. Before that, however, FDA inspectors were already issuing 483 observations based on this decision.

The "All Original Data" Principle: The Core of Data Integrity

§211.194(a)(6) requires retention of "all original data", including charts and spectra. In the digital age, this has specific meaning:

  • HPLC/UPLC data files must be retained in the original .raw or .cdf format, not just as PDF reports
  • The system must not allow users to delete or overwrite sequences
  • The audit trail must record all changes, including who changed what and when
  • A "test, fail, delete, retest" pattern is the most serious data integrity violation, and can lead to a consent decree or criminal prosecution

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Management of §211.170 Reserve Samples

The reserve sample system is designed so that when a problem arises in the market (complaint, recall), the original batch can be traced and tested.

The meaning of "twice the quantity": Enough to perform all required regulatory tests (excluding sterility and pyrogen tests), but still at least two portions: one for the routine annual visual inspection, and one retained as potential litigation/investigation evidence.

Storage conditions: Must be consistent with the product labeling (not merely room temperature). If the product requires refrigeration (2–8°C), the reserve samples must also be refrigerated; this is a common 483 item, especially when equipment has a temperature excursion.

Annual visual inspection: Must be documented, usually performed by warehouse personnel and reviewed by QA. If the reserve sample shows any change in appearance (color, precipitate, crystals), the QCU must be notified and a decision made on whether to trigger a stability investigation.

§211.176 Penicillin Contamination: Zero-Tolerance Policy

Trace contamination with penicillins/cephalosporins can be fatal to allergic patients. FDA's position is: detectable penicillin contamination = the product may not be marketed, however low the level of contamination.

This is the regulatory basis for FDA's requirement of a dedicated facility for penicillin-class products in GMP plants. Most international regulations (EU GMP, PIC/S) have similar requirements, but the wording of 21 CFR is the strictest: there is no "acceptable minimum contamination level" standard.

  • The name and strength of the drug product
  • Lot number
  • Name of complainant
  • Nature of complaint
  • Reply to complainant

Where an investigation is conducted under §211.192, a written record shall be made of the findings of the investigation and follow-up. The record or copy of the record of the investigation shall be maintained at the establishment where the investigation occurred in accordance with §211.180(c).

"Such procedures shall include provisions for review to determine whether the complaint represents a serious and unexpected adverse drug experience which is required to be reported to the Food and Drug Administration…"
— 21 CFR §211.198(a), the mandatory reporting trigger

Tutorial Commentary

§211.196 Distribution Records: The Foundation of Recall Operations

Distribution records may seem unremarkable in day-to-day operations, but once a recall is needed, their importance becomes immediately clear:

  • FDA expects a recalling firm to identify within 24–72 hours which batches went to which channels
  • The information required by §211.196 (name, strength, lot number, consignee name and address, date, quantity) is the minimum for precise tracing
  • If distribution records are incomplete, FDA may require a "meaningful" recall to be expanded to a Class I recall, the highest level

CDMO note: after the CDMO ships to the brand owner, the brand owner is responsible for distribution to the final channels. But the CDMO must keep its own distribution records (down to the brand owner level). The quality agreement should specify data-sharing obligations when a recall occurs.

Signal Detection from Complaint Trending

The text of §211.198 focuses on the handling of individual complaints, but FDA's expectations have evolved:

In the Annual Product Review (APR, §211.180(e)), complaint data should be trended:

  • Is the complaint rate (complaints per million units distributed) rising?
  • Are specific types of complaints (foreign matter, color change, ineffectiveness) clustered in a particular period?
  • Does geographic clustering suggest a temperature control problem in a particular distribution channel?

The ICH Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System makes complaint trend analysis a required input to Continual Improvement.

CDMO Scenario: Division of Responsibility for Complaint Handling

Where the CDMO manufactures and the brand owner sells, the division of complaint handling responsibilities must be clearly set out in the Quality Agreement:

  • Who receives consumer complaints? Usually the brand owner, because consumers do not know who manufactured the product
  • Who conducts the quality investigation? If the complaint involves a manufacturing batch problem, the CDMO's QCU leads the investigation
  • Who notifies FDA? The NDA holder (usually the brand owner) is responsible for IND/NDA adverse event reporting
  • Recall decision: The NDA holder decides, and the CDMO provides support (batch records, investigation reports)
  • Record retention: Each party keeps the relevant records and provides copies to the other

Complaint vs. Adverse Event: When Must It Be Reported to FDA?

§211.198(a) requires the firm to evaluate whether a complaint constitutes a reportable "serious and unexpected adverse drug experience".

Definition of "Serious" (21 CFR 312.32): an event that results in, or may result in, death, a life-threatening condition, hospitalization, disability, a congenital anomaly, or that requires medical intervention to prevent permanent harm.

5.7 §§211.204–211.208 — Subpart K: Returned and Salvaged Drug Products

Original Text

§211.204 — Returned drug products: Returned drug products shall be identified as such and held. If the conditions under which a returned drug product has been held, stored, or shipped before or during its return, or if the condition of the drug product, its container, carton, or labeling, as a result of storage or shipping, casts doubt on the safety, identity, strength, quality, or purity of the drug product, the returned drug product shall be destroyed unless examination, testing, or both indicate that the drug product meets its established standards and specifications and the conditions under which the drug product was held, stored, or shipped do not cast doubt on its safety, identity, strength, quality, or purity. The examination and testing of returned drug products shall be adequately documented in the batch records.

§211.208 — Drug product salvaging: Drug products that have been subjected to improper storage conditions including extremes in temperature, humidity, smoke, fumes, pressure, age, or radiation due to natural disasters, fires, accidents, or equipment failures shall not be salvaged and returned to the marketplace. Whenever there is a question whether drug products have been subjected to such conditions, salvaging operations may be conducted only if there is (a) evidence from laboratory tests and assays (including animal feed-out studies where applicable) that the drug products meet all applicable standards of identity, strength, quality, and purity and (b) evidence from inspection of the premises, equipment, and the area of the disaster that the salvaging was proper. Organoleptic examinations shall be acceptable only as supplemental evidence that the drug products meet appropriate standards of identity, strength, quality, and purity. Where laboratory tests are not feasible, the drug products shall be destroyed.

"Drug products that have been subjected to improper storage conditions… shall not be salvaged and returned to the marketplace."
— 21 CFR §211.208, opening clause — establishing the default prohibition on salvaging

Tutorial Commentary

§211.204 The "When in Doubt, Destroy" Principle for Returned Drug Products

§211.204 establishes an important burden-of-proof principle: the default disposition of a returned drug product is destruction, unless adequate evidence (examination and testing) shows that it meets standards.

Situations that raise doubt: any one of the following can trigger mandatory destruction:

  • Storage or shipping temperature records are missing or abnormal
  • The packaging is visibly damaged (container, carton, label)
  • The reason for return involves a complaint about product efficacy or safety
  • The environmental conditions of the storage location are unknown (for example, returns after a natural disaster)

Many companies' SOPs simply state "all returns are destroyed and not reprocessed or resold". This is the simplest compliance strategy: although it is commercially costly, it avoids all regulatory risk.

§211.208 Disposition of Drug Products After Natural Disasters

§211.208 mainly addresses extreme circumstances: hurricanes, floods, fires, warehouse power outages, and the like. Key points:

  • Organoleptic examination is only supplementary evidence: "it looks fine" is not enough to permit re-marketing
  • There must be positive results from laboratory assays
  • There must be documented assessment of the environmental conditions at the affected site
  • "Product that cannot be laboratory tested must be destroyed": no exceptions

After major natural disasters (such as Hurricane Katrina and Hurricane Maria in Puerto Rico), FDA issued guidance to affected firms, asking them to proactively identify potentially affected batches.

Key Design Points for a Returns Handling SOP

  • Quarantine returns immediately on receipt (label "QUARANTINE"; never mix with the released product area)
  • Record the reason for return, who returned it, the return date, and the lot number
  • Evaluate storage conditions: did the customer provide temperature records?
  • QCU decision matrix: which cases are destroyed outright, and which may be tested and then evaluated for reuse?
  • All return records must be linked to the batch record of the original batch (required by the last sentence of §211.204)
  • Trend the return rate and include it in the Annual Product Review (APR)
Chapter Summary

The Overall Regulatory Logic of Subparts I–K

Subparts I–K of 21 CFR 211 form the "closed loop of quality assurance" in drug manufacturing:

  1. The laboratory builds the scientific foundation (Subpart I): Method validation ensures the tests are valid; stability testing establishes the expiry period; reserve samples ensure traceability
  2. Records capture every action (Subpart J): Equipment logs, component records, and batch records together make up the complete manufacturing history
  3. The QCU makes the final judgment (§211.192): Thorough review ensures that nonconforming batches do not reach the market
  4. Deviations and anomalies are investigated (§211.192 + §211.194): The OOS procedure ensures problems are not covered up
  5. Problems feed back from the market to the plant (§211.198): Complaint handling closes the loop, and signal detection triggers improvement
  6. Abnormal product must not circulate (Subpart K): Strict control of returns and salvage protects consumers

CDMO Special Note: Five Responsibility Boundaries

  1. Batch release: The CDMO's QCU has the final decision and cannot be overridden by the client
  2. OOS investigation: The CDMO leads the laboratory investigation (Phase I), and the brand owner may take part in the Phase II process investigation
  3. Stability: The NDA holder designs the program, and the CDMO supplies batches and executes it
  4. Complaints: The brand owner receives complaints, and manufacturing issues are investigated by the CDMO
  5. Record retention: Each party is responsible for its own retention, and the quality agreement specifies access rights
