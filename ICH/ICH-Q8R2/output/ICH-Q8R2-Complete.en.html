1.1 Objective of the Guideline

Original Text

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Objective of the Guideline

This guideline describes the suggested contents for the 3.2.P.2 (Pharmaceutical Development) section of a regulatory submission in the ICH M4 Common Technical Document (CTD) format.

The Pharmaceutical Development section provides an opportunity to present the knowledge gained through the application of scientific approaches and quality risk management (for definition, see ICH Q9) to the development of a product and its manufacturing process. It is first produced for the original marketing application and can be updated to support new knowledge gained over the lifecycle of a product.

The Pharmaceutical Development section is intended to provide a comprehensive understanding of the product and manufacturing process for reviewers and inspectors. The guideline also indicates areas where the demonstration of greater understanding of pharmaceutical and manufacturing sciences can create a basis for flexible regulatory approaches. The degree of regulatory flexibility is predicated on the level of relevant scientific knowledge provided.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • CTD 3.2.P.2 (Pharmaceutical Development): One of the most important CMC sections of an NDA/MAA, it sets out the scientific rationale for "why you designed this product and process the way you did."
  • ICH M4 (Common Technical Document): The drug application format recognized jointly by the three major regulatory regions (US/EU/JP), organized into five modules (M1-M5). 3.2.P.2 sits under Module 3 (Quality).
  • Flexible Regulatory Approaches: If you demonstrate sufficient scientific understanding in 3.2.P.2, regulators can give you more latitude, for example, changes within the Design Space need no submission.

Analogy

Think of 3.2.P.2 as the architect's design statement for a building. Government reviewers do not just want to see what you built; they want to see "why you designed it this way": why this material, why this structure, why this earthquake protection. The more completely you explain it and the stronger the scientific basis, the more comfortable the reviewers are, and the later you can make minor layout changes without reapplying for a building permit.

Conversely, if you hand over only a drawing with no explanation, moving even a single column means a new application. That is the price of not submitting Q8-style development studies.

Key Notes

  • Regulatory flexibility is predicated on scientific explanation: "The degree of regulatory flexibility is predicated on the level of relevant scientific knowledge provided." This sentence is the core spirit of Q8. The more scientific knowledge you provide, the more flexibility you gain.
  • A living document: 3.2.P.2 is not a one-time document; new knowledge gained throughout the product lifecycle can be used to update it. This is the embodiment of "knowledge management" in Q10 (quality system).
  • The Q8 + Q9 + Q10 triangle: Q8 provides the framework for development knowledge, Q9 provides the risk assessment tools, and Q10 provides the mechanism for continual improvement. The three are inseparable.

Practical Application for CDMOs

For a CDMO, helping a client prepare a high-quality 3.2.P.2 is a high-value service. Many innovator companies (especially biotechs) still have a very weak 3.2.P.2 when they transfer technology to a CDMO. If the CDMO can help build a complete development package, it not only speeds up review but also enjoys more process optimization flexibility at the later commercialization stage.

1.2 Scope

Original Text

1.2 Scope

This guideline is intended to provide guidance on the contents of Section 3.2.P.2 (Pharmaceutical Development) for drug products as defined in the scope of Module 3 of the Common Technical Document (ICH guideline M4). The guideline does not apply to contents of submissions for drug products during the clinical research stages of drug development.

However, the principles in this guideline are important to consider during those stages as well. This guideline might also be appropriate for other types of products. To determine the applicability of this guideline to a particular type of product, applicants can consult with the appropriate regulatory authorities.

2. PHARMACEUTICAL DEVELOPMENT

The aim of pharmaceutical development is to design a quality product and its manufacturing process to consistently deliver the intended performance of the product. The information and knowledge gained from pharmaceutical development studies and manufacturing experience provide scientific understanding to support the establishment of the design space, specifications, and manufacturing controls.

Information from pharmaceutical development studies can be a basis for quality risk management. It is important to recognize that quality cannot be tested into products; i.e., quality should be built in by design.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • Scope: Q8(R2) formally applies to commercial NDA/MAA submissions (it does not apply to submissions during the clinical research stage), but its principles should be considered at every stage of clinical development. This is very important for CDMOs during early-stage technology transfer.
  • Quality Built In by Design: This is the soul of QbD. The traditional approach is "make it first, then test, and if it passes, that is enough." The QbD approach is "understand the relationship between each critical attribute (CQA) and process parameter (CPP), so that the process itself assures quality."
  • Design Space: The multidimensional combination range of material attributes and process parameters within which operating assures product quality. Adjusting within the Design Space is not a "major change" and does not require a post-approval supplement.

Analogy

"Quality cannot be tested into products": Imagine you are cooking the signature steak at a restaurant. The traditional method is to taste every steak after cooking and throw away any that is not good enough. The QbD method is to work out the precise combination of heat (what temperature, how many minutes), beef thickness (how many centimeters) and resting time (how many minutes); as long as you operate within this "design space," every steak will be perfect, and you do not need to taste each one.

This operating range of "what temperature and how many minutes, how many centimeters thick, how many minutes of resting" is the Design Space. Adjusting the heat by ±5°C within the range does not require reapplying to the health department for a menu change.

Key Notes

  • Applicability at early stages: Although the Q8 regulatory requirement concerns commercial applications, the Annex (Part II) specifically addresses the "phase-appropriate approach," meaning how much development work is appropriate at each clinical phase. CMC staff should start planning the Q8 framework as early as Phase 1.
  • "Quality cannot be tested into products" is the most important sentence: This sentence often appears in FDA 483s and Warning Letters. If you rely only on QC release without process understanding, regulators will consider your quality control inadequate.
  • Design Space vs. Proven Acceptable Ranges (PAR): A Design Space is a multivariate range that accounts for interactions; a PAR is a single range for each individual parameter (univariate). A Design Space requires more DOE data but gives greater operating flexibility in return.

Conceptual Framework

Traditional approach vs. QbD approach

Section 2 Overview: Introduction to Pharmaceutical Development (QbD Framework Tour)

Original Text

Changes in formulation and manufacturing processes during development and lifecycle management should be looked upon as opportunities to gain additional knowledge and further support establishment of the design space. Similarly, inclusion of relevant knowledge gained from experiments giving unexpected results can also be useful.

"Design space is proposed by the applicant and is subject to regulatory assessment and approval. Working within the design space is not considered as a change. Movement out of the design space is considered to be a change and would normally initiate a regulatory post approval change process."

In addition, the applicant can choose to conduct pharmaceutical development studies that can lead to an enhanced knowledge of product performance over a wider range of material attributes, processing options and process parameters. This scientific understanding facilitates establishment of an expanded design space. In these situations, opportunities exist to develop more flexible regulatory approaches, for example, to facilitate:

  • risk-based regulatory decisions (reviews and inspections);
  • manufacturing process improvements, within the approved design space described in the dossier, without further regulatory review;
  • reduction of post-approval submissions;
  • real-time quality control, leading to a reduction of end-product release testing.

To realise this flexibility, the applicant should demonstrate an enhanced knowledge of product performance over a range of material attributes, manufacturing process options and process parameters. This understanding can be gained by application of, for example, formal experimental designs, process analytical technology (PAT), and/or prior knowledge.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • Regulatory status of the Design Space: It is proposed by the applicant and approved by the regulatory authority. Once approved, adjusting operating conditions within the Design Space is "not considered a change." This is the core commercial value QbD delivers.
  • Post-Approval Change: In a traditional process, modifying any condition (such as mixing time or temperature range) usually requires a CBE-30 or Prior Approval Supplement, which is time-consuming and laborious. Companies with a Design Space can avoid a large number of later filings.
  • PAT (Process Analytical Technology): Using real-time analytical tools (such as NIR or Raman spectroscopy) to monitor CQAs directly during the process, without waiting for QC testing of the finished product. PAT is the technical foundation of Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT).
  • Experimental Designs: DOE (Design of Experiments) systematically changes multiple factors at the same time to build multivariate understanding, rather than the traditional one-factor-at-a-time experiment where the others are held fixed.

Analogy

"Failed experiments are also knowledge." This is often overlooked in industry. Q8 states explicitly: "inclusion of relevant knowledge gained from experiments giving unexpected results can also be useful."

Imagine you are testing a new tablet manufacturing process, and one batch was over-mixed because the mixing time was too long, producing granules that were too fine and tablets with insufficient friability strength. This "failed" experiment tells you where the boundary of the Design Space lies: the upper limit of mixing time. Writing this data into 3.2.P.2 provides the scientific basis for that Design Space boundary.

Key Notes

  • The four regulatory flexibilities: Q8 explicitly lists four benefits of the Enhanced Approach: (1) risk-based regulatory review, (2) changes within the Design Space need no further review, (3) fewer post-approval submissions, and (4) reduced end-product testing through RTRT. These four points are the best arguments for persuading a client to invest in QbD studies.
  • Prior Knowledge counts too: Literature, platform technology and historical batch data can all serve as the basis for establishing a Design Space; you do not have to run every experiment from scratch. For a CDMO, accumulated multi-product platform experience is itself a competitive advantage.
  • Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT): Traditional release requires waiting for 28-day stability and various QC results; RTRT uses PAT data to judge quality in real time, which can greatly shorten cycle time. It is especially valuable for products such as injectables that need to reach the market quickly.

Practical Application for CDMOs

The Design Space as commercial negotiating leverage: When a CDMO helps a client establish a complete Design Space, that Design Space becomes the "legitimate boundary" for flexible operation by both parties in the future. If the client later wants to change API supplier (which may affect CMAs) or scale up the batch size, as long as the change stays within the Design Space, no resubmission is needed, and both sides save a great deal of time and cost. This is an important differentiating selling point in contract negotiations.

  • Initial investment: Establishing a Design Space requires more DOE experiments (development cost +20-40%)
  • Long-term return: Fewer post-approval supplements over the lifecycle, saving roughly $50K-$200K and 6-18 months each time

Practice Questions

  1. After commercialization, a pharmaceutical company finds that the particle size distribution of a batch of API is wider than usual, and this change could affect the dissolution rate. How would the company handle it under the traditional approach versus under QbD (with a Design Space)? Which is more efficient?
  2. What prerequisites are needed to introduce PAT? When implementing an NIR content uniformity test, what data must the company submit to the regulatory authority?
  3. Why is DOE (Design of Experiments) better than OFAT (One Factor At a Time)? Explain with a specific process development scenario.

Section Summary

  • Position of Q8(R2): It governs the content of 3.2.P.2 (Pharmaceutical Development) in CTD Module 3, the formal place to present development scientific knowledge
  • Core spirit of QbD: Quality built in by design. Quality is designed in, not tested in; the amount of regulatory flexibility depends on the depth of scientific knowledge you provide
  • Design Space: A multidimensional operating range proposed by the applicant and approved by the regulatory authority; adjusting within it is not a "change," and it is the most direct commercial return of QbD
  • Toolbox: DOE (building multivariate understanding) + PAT (real-time monitoring) + Risk Assessment (identifying CQAs/CPPs) together support the QbD framework
  • Q8 + Q9 + Q10: The three guidelines support one another; Q8 is the systematic presentation of knowledge, Q9 provides risk assessment methods, and Q10 ensures knowledge management and continual improvement

Learning Objectives

2.1 Components of the Drug Product - 2.1.1 Drug Substance

2.1.1 Drug Substance

The physicochemical and biological properties of the drug substance that can influence the performance of the drug product and its manufacturability, or were specifically designed into the drug substance (e.g., solid state properties), should be identified and discussed.

Examples of physicochemical and biological properties that might need to be examined include solubility, water content, particle size, crystal properties, biological activity, and permeability. These properties could be inter-related and might need to be considered in combination.

To evaluate the potential effect of drug substance physicochemical properties on the performance of the drug product, studies on drug product might be warranted. For example, the ICH Q6A Specifications describes some of the circumstances in which drug product studies are recommended (e.g., Decision Tree #3 and #4 (Part 2)). This approach applies equally for ICH Q6B for Biotechnology/Biological Products.

The knowledge gained from the studies investigating the potential effect of drug substance properties on drug product performance can be used, as appropriate, to justify elements of the drug substance specification (3.2.S.4.5).

The compatibility of the drug substance with excipients listed in 3.2.P.1 should be evaluated. For products that contain more than one drug substance, the compatibility of the drug substances with each other should also be evaluated.

Commentary

Core Concept: Linking Drug Substance Properties to Drug Product Quality

Q8(R2) requires section 3.2.P.2 to discuss the physicochemical and biological properties of the drug substance systematically. The core logic is:

  • Solubility: directly affects bioavailability and the setting of dissolution specifications
  • Particle size: affects dissolution rate and manufacturing uniformity (especially for low-dose products)
  • Crystal properties: polymorphism affects solubility and stability
  • Water content: affects the stability and flowability of solid dosage forms
  • Permeability: a key parameter for BCS classification

These properties are interrelated and must be evaluated together, not in isolation.

Analogy: Drug Substance Properties Are Like Ingredient Quality

Think of formulation development as cooking: the physicochemical properties of the drug substance are the nature of the ingredients (freshness, texture, moisture content). However skilled the chef (the process), if the ingredients are inherently unstable (for example, an uncertain polymorph), the final dish (drug product quality) cannot be guaranteed to be consistent.

This is why Q8 requires you to understand the ingredients first, then design the cooking method.

Regulatory Focus: Links to CTD Documents

The results of drug substance characterization studies should be linked to:

  • 3.2.S.4.5: Drug Substance Specification Justification
  • 3.2.P.1: list of drug product components (compatibility must be assessed)
  • ICH Q6A Decision Trees #3, #4: determine whether drug product-level testing is needed

Compatibility testing (DS-excipient compatibility) is an essential step in formulation development, and the results should be documented here.

CDMO Practical Application

When a CDMO accepts a new client API for formulation development, it must first carry out API characterization, including BCS classification, solid-state analysis (XRPD, DSC) and the pH-solubility profile. If the API is BCS Class II (low solubility/high permeability), the formulation strategy may need particle size control or an amorphous solid dispersion (ASD).

2.1.2 Excipients

2.1.2 Excipients

The excipients chosen, their concentration, and the characteristics that can influence the drug product performance (e.g., stability, bioavailability) or manufacturability should be discussed relative to the respective function of each excipient. This should include all substances used in the manufacture of the drug product, whether they appear in the finished product or not (e.g., processing aids).

Compatibility of excipients with other excipients, where relevant (for example, combination of preservatives in a dual preservative system), should be established. The ability of excipients (e.g., antioxidants, penetration enhancers, disintegrants, release controlling agents) to provide their intended functionality, and to perform throughout the intended drug product shelf life, should also be demonstrated.

The information on excipient performance can be used, as appropriate, to justify the choice and quality attributes of the excipient, and to support the justification of the drug product specification (3.2.P.5.6).

Information to support the safety of excipients, when appropriate, should be cross-referenced (3.2.P.4.6).

Commentary

Core Requirement: Three Levels of Excipient Evaluation

Q8 sets requirements for excipients at three levels:

  • Functionality: the rationale for selecting each excipient, the basis for its amount, and verification of its function (throughout the shelf life)
  • Compatibility: compatibility between excipients, and between excipients and the drug substance (solid-state compatibility studies)
  • Safety: for novel or high-level excipients, cross-reference the safety data in 3.2.P.4.6

Common Pitfall: Processing Aids Are Easily Overlooked

The guideline is explicit: all substances used in manufacturing must be discussed, whether or not they appear in the final product. Commonly overlooked items include:

  • Granulation solvents (such as ethanol): residual limits must be discussed
  • Plasticizers used in coating: their effect on release behavior must be discussed
  • Filter membrane material: sorption must be considered

  1. If a drug product uses a combination of two preservatives (dual preservative system), how should 3.2.P.2.1.2 document their compatibility?
  2. How does excipient information support 3.2.P.5.6 (Justification of Specification(s))? Give an example.
  3. To which CTD section should the safety data for a novel excipient be cross-referenced?

2.2.1 Formulation Development

2.2.1 Formulation Development

A summary should be provided describing the development of the formulation, including identification of those attributes that are critical to the quality of the drug product, taking into consideration intended usage and route of administration. Information from formal experimental designs can be useful in identifying critical or interacting variables that might be important to ensure the quality of the drug product.

The summary should highlight the evolution of the formulation design from initial concept up to the final design. This summary should also take into consideration the choice of drug product components (e.g., the properties of the drug substance, excipients, container closure system, any relevant dosing device), the manufacturing process, and, if appropriate, knowledge gained from the development of similar drug product(s).

Any excipient ranges included in the batch formula (3.2.P.3.2) should be justified in this section of the application; this justification can often be based on the experience gained during development or manufacture.

A summary of formulations used in clinical safety and efficacy and in any relevant bioavailability or bioequivalence studies should be provided. Any changes between the proposed commercial formulation and those formulations used in pivotal clinical batches and primary stability batches should be clearly described and the rationale for the changes provided.

Information from comparative in vitro studies (e.g., dissolution) or comparative in vivo studies (e.g., bioequivalence) that links clinical formulations to the proposed commercial formulation described in 3.2.P.1 should be summarized. A successful in vitro/in vivo correlation (IVIVC) can assist in the selection of appropriate dissolution acceptance criteria, and can potentially reduce the need for further bioequivalence studies following changes to the product or its manufacturing process.

Any special design features of the drug product (e.g., tablet score line, overfill, anti-counterfeiting measure as it affects the drug product) should be identified and a rationale provided for their use.

Commentary

Core Concept: The Story of How the Formulation Evolved

Section 2.2.1 calls for a complete "formulation development history": regulatory reviewers need to see the full scientific narrative of where the formulation started, why it changed, and how it reached the commercial version. It must cover:

  • Initial concept design (driven by the QTPP)
  • Formulation changes at each development stage and their rationale
  • An explanation of the differences between the clinical batch formulation and the commercial formulation
  • The process of identifying critical formulation attributes (CQAs)

Analogy: Formulation Evolution Is Like Product Iteration

Just as the iPhone changed in design from the first model to the latest, Apple has to explain the reason for each change. Formulation development is the same: reviewers need to see the scientific reason behind every change from the first-in-human (FIH) formulation to the commercial formulation, rather than a final formulation that appears suddenly with no context.

Regulatory Focus: Clinical Bridging

If the commercial formulation differs from the formulation used in the pivotal study, a "bridge" must be established through the following:

  • In vitro dissolution comparison: dissolution profile comparison (f2 similarity factor)
  • In vivo bioequivalence (BE): if the in vitro bridge is insufficient
  • IVIVC: if established, it can support waiving BE after future formulation changes

These data must be summarized in 2.2.1 and cross-referenced to the study reports in Module 5.

Practical Challenge: Using DoE in Formulation Development

Q8 specifically mentions the role of formal experimental designs (DoE) in identifying critical interacting variables. Common applications:

  • Full factorial design: screening the effects of excipients
  • Response Surface Methodology (RSM): optimizing the formulation composition and its ranges
  • Mixture design: optimizing the proportions of multiple excipients

DoE results can be used to support the establishment of a Design Space (see Part II Annex 2.4).

2.2.2 Overages & 2.2.3 Physicochemical and Biological Properties

2.2.2 Overages

In general, use of an overage of a drug substance to compensate for degradation during manufacture or a product's shelf life, or to extend shelf life, is discouraged. Any overages in the manufacture of the drug product, whether they appear in the final formulated product or not, should be justified considering the safety and efficacy of the product.

Information should be provided on the:

  1. Amount of overage
  2. Reason for the overage (e.g., to compensate for expected and documented manufacturing losses)
  3. Justification for the amount of overage

The overage should be included in the amount of drug substance listed in the batch formula (3.2.P.3.2).

2.2.3 Physicochemical and Biological Properties

The physicochemical and biological properties relevant to the safety, performance or manufacturability of the drug product should be identified and discussed. This includes the physiological implications of drug substance and formulation attributes.

Studies could include, for example, the development of a test for respirable fraction of an inhaled product. Similarly, information supporting the selection of dissolution vs. disintegration testing, or other means to assure drug release, and the development and suitability of the chosen test, could be provided in this section.

See also ICH Q6A Decision Tree #4 (Part 3) and Decision Tree #7 (Part 1) or ICH Q6B for Biotechnology/Biological Products. The discussion should cross-reference any relevant stability data in 3.2.P.8.3.

Commentary

Regulatory Focus: Overages

The regulators' basic position on overages is that they are discouraged, especially when used to compensate for degradation or to extend shelf life. If an overage is genuinely needed, three pieces of information must be provided:

  • Amount: the specific amount of the overage (%)
  • Reason: for example, expected losses during manufacturing (supported by documentation)
  • Justification of the amount: the size of the overage itself needs a scientific basis and must not be excessive

Note: the overage of API must be included in the API quantity calculation in the batch formula (3.2.P.3.2).

Analogy: An Overage Is Like an Engineering Safety Factor

An overage is like the safety factor in bridge engineering: a little is added on top of the theoretical requirement. But the engineer must explain how much, why that amount, and whether the safety margin is exaggerated. An excessive overage can cause an overdose early in the life of the product, raising safety concerns.

Core Concept: Decision Framework for Drug Product Physicochemical Properties (2.2.3)

Section 2.2.3 is the key bridge linking drug product properties to the choice of test methods:

  • Solid oral dosage forms: choosing between a dissolution test and a disintegration test (per ICH Q6A Decision Tree #4 Part 3)
  • Inhaled products: respirable fraction (particle size distribution); an NGI or ACI test needs to be developed
  • Biologics: characterization according to ICH Q6B
  • Link to stability: the discussion of physicochemical properties must cross-reference the stability data in 3.2.P.8.3

Self-Test

  1. Why do regulators discourage overages? Which kind of overage is relatively acceptable?
  2. If the commercial formulation has a 5% API overage to compensate for manufacturing losses, which CTD section must include this amount?
  3. For a BCS Class II oral solid dosage form, how should the in vitro release test method be selected and justified in 2.2.3?

Key Takeaways

  • 2.1.1 Drug Substance: physicochemical and biological properties (solubility, particle size, crystal form, etc.) must be evaluated systematically and linked to the 3.2.S.4.5 specification justification; DS-excipient compatibility is an essential step
  • 2.1.2 Excipients: all excipients (including processing aids) must be discussed for functionality, compatibility and safety; novel excipients must be cross-referenced to 3.2.P.4.6
  • 2.2.1 Formulation Development: the evolution from initial concept to commercial formulation must be presented in full; differences between the clinical batch and the commercial formulation must be clearly explained and supported with bridging data (dissolution comparison or BE)
  • 2.2.2 Overages: discouraged in principle; if used, the amount, reason and justification must be stated, and the overage must be included in the batch formula calculation
  • 2.2.3 Physicochemical Properties: supports the choice of test method (dissolution vs disintegration); decisions should follow the ICH Q6A Decision Trees, with cross-reference to stability data (3.2.P.8.3)

2.3 Manufacturing Process Development

2.3 Manufacturing Process Development

The selection, the control, and any improvement of the manufacturing process described in 3.2.P.3.3 (i.e., intended for commercial production batches) should be explained. It is important to consider the critical formulation attributes, together with the available manufacturing process options, in order to address the selection of the manufacturing process and confirm the appropriateness of the components. Appropriateness of the equipment used for the intended products should be discussed.

Process development studies should provide the basis for process improvement, process validation, continuous process verification (where applicable), and any process control requirements. Where appropriate, such studies should address microbiological as well as physical and chemical attributes.

The manufacturing process development programme or process improvement programme should identify any critical process parameters (CPP) that should be monitored or controlled (e.g., granulation end point) to ensure that the product is of the desired quality.

For those products intended to be sterile an appropriate method of sterilization for the drug product and primary packaging material should be chosen and the choice justified.

Significant differences between the manufacturing processes used to produce batches for pivotal clinical trials or primary stability studies and the commercial process described in 3.2.P.3.3 should be discussed. The discussion should summarise the influence of the differences on the performance, manufacturability and quality of the product.

The information should include, for example: (1) the identity and use of the batches produced, (2) the manufacturing site, (3) the batch size, and (4) any significant equipment differences (e.g., different design, operating principle, size).

In order to provide flexibility for future process improvement, when describing the development of the manufacturing process, it is useful to describe measurement systems that allow monitoring of critical attributes or process end-points. Collection of process monitoring data during the development of the manufacturing process can provide useful information to enhance process understanding.

An assessment of the ability of the process to reliably produce a product of the intended quality (e.g., the performance of the manufacturing process under different operating conditions, at different scales, or with different equipment) can be provided. An understanding of process robustness can be useful in risk assessment and risk reduction (see ICH Q9) and to support future manufacturing and process improvement.

Commentary

Core concept: process development, CPP identification, and process validation as a logical chain

The core mission of 2.3 is to build "process understanding", the scientific foundation that underpins every downstream manufacturing control strategy:

  • Step 1: Select the process (why was this manufacturing method chosen?)
  • Step 2: Identify CPPs (which process parameters affect CQAs?)
  • Step 3: Build process understanding (CPP ranges and interactions)
  • Step 4: Support PV/CPV (provide the scientific rationale for the validation protocol)

This logical chain links directly 3.2.P.2.3 (process development) to 3.2.P.3.3 (commercial process) to 3.2.P.3.5 (process validation).

Special requirements for sterile products

Q8 explicitly states that sterile products require a specific explanation of the selection and justification of the sterilization method. The decision basis typically includes:

  • Terminal sterilization is the first consideration, and is preferred by both FDA and EMA
  • If terminal sterilization is not possible, the need for aseptic processing must be justified
  • The sterilization method for the primary packaging materials must also be discussed

This is fully consistent with the requirements of PIC/S Annex 1 and EU GMP Annex 1.

Analogy: process robustness is like seismic design for a bridge

Process robustness is the ability of a process to keep producing product that meets quality requirements despite variability in materials, fluctuations in operating conditions, and differences between equipment. A bridge must withstand not only normal loads but also remain structurally safe in a typhoon or an earthquake.

Q8 recommends evaluating robustness during process development. This gives a scientific basis for "flexibility margins" in future process improvements (scale-up, equipment changes).

Bridging documentation between clinical and commercial batches

Regulatory reviewers need to confirm that the batches used in clinical trials and the commercial production batches are the "same product". The explanation of differences should cover four items:

  • Batch number and purpose (e.g., BE study batch XY-001)
  • Manufacturing site (if changed)
  • Batch size (justification for the scale-up)
  • Equipment differences (e.g., from a laboratory-scale granulator to a production-scale one)

The impact of the differences on product quality must be supported by data (e.g., comparative batch analysis).

2.4 Container Closure System

2.4 Container Closure System

The choice and rationale for selection of the container closure system for the commercial product (described in 3.2.P.7) should be discussed. Consideration should be given to the intended use of the drug product and the suitability of the container closure system for storage and transportation (shipping), including the storage and shipping container for bulk drug product, where appropriate.

The choice of materials for primary packaging should be justified. The discussion should describe studies performed to demonstrate the integrity of the container and closure. A possible interaction between product and container or label should be considered.

The choice of primary packaging materials should consider, e.g.:

  • Choice of materials
  • Protection from moisture and light
  • Compatibility of the materials of construction with the dosage form (including sorption to container and leaching)
  • Safety of materials of construction

Justification for secondary packaging materials should be included, when relevant.

If a dosing device is used (e.g., dropper pipette, pen injection device, dry powder inhaler), it is important to demonstrate that a reproducible and accurate dose of the product is delivered under testing conditions which, as far as possible, simulate the use of the product.

Commentary

Core concept: multi-layered considerations for the container closure system

2.4 requires a systematic evaluation of the container closure system (CCS). The core question is: can this packaging protect the product and maintain its quality throughout the shelf life?

  • Material selection rationale: the choice of primary packaging must be scientifically justified
  • Seal integrity: Container Closure Integrity Testing (CCIT)
  • Interactions: sorption, leachables, and extractables
  • Suitability for storage and transport: protection against temperature, humidity, light, and vibration

Regulatory focus: Extractables & Leachables (E&L)

Q8 cites leaching as a necessary consideration in evaluating the container closure system, which links directly to the industry-standard E&L assessment:

  • Extractables: chemical substances extracted from the packaging material under aggressive solvent conditions
  • Leachables: substances that migrate into the drug product under actual conditions of use
  • Safety concerns must be addressed through toxicological assessment (analytical evaluation threshold, AET)
  • Results should be discussed in 3.2.P.2.4 and may be cross-referenced to 3.2.P.7

E&L assessment is especially critical for injectables and inhalation products.

Analogy: the packaging system is like a spacesuit

Drug packaging is like a spacesuit: it must protect the contents (the drug) from a hostile external environment, ensure complete isolation between inside and outside (seal integrity), and must not itself "release" harmful substances into the contents (leachables). If a spacesuit seal fails or its material ages, the consequences are severe; the same is true of drug packaging.

Special requirements for dosing devices

Q8 specifically notes that products with a dosing device (e.g., pen injectors, dry powder inhalers) need to demonstrate dose reproducibility and accuracy under simulated use conditions. This is especially important for the following products:

  • Insulin pens (pen injectors): dose accuracy directly affects safety
  • Dry powder inhalers (DPI): the amount of particles delivered to the lungs must be verified
  • Metered dose inhalers (MDI): dose consistency per actuation

Device performance test data are usually documented in 3.2.P.2.4 and 3.2.P.3.4 (container closure description).

2.5 Microbiological Attributes & 2.6 Compatibility

2.5 Microbiological Attributes

Where appropriate, the microbiological attributes of the drug product should be discussed in this section (3.2.P.2.5). The discussion should include, for example:

  • The rationale for performing or not performing microbial limits testing for non-sterile drug products (e.g., Decision Tree #8 in ICH Q6A and ICH Q6B)
  • The selection and effectiveness of preservative systems in products containing antimicrobial preservative or the antimicrobial effectiveness of products that are inherently antimicrobial
  • For sterile products, the integrity of the container closure system as it relates to preventing microbial contamination

Although chemical testing for preservative content is the attribute normally included in the drug product specification, antimicrobial preservative effectiveness should be demonstrated during development. The lowest specified concentration of antimicrobial preservative should be demonstrated to be effective in controlling micro-organisms by using an antimicrobial preservative effectiveness test.

The concentration used should be justified in terms of efficacy and safety, such that the minimum concentration of preservative that gives the required level of efficacy throughout the intended shelf life of the product is used. Where relevant, microbial challenge testing under testing conditions that, as far as possible, simulate patient use should be performed during development.

2.6 Compatibility

The compatibility of the drug product with reconstitution diluents (e.g., precipitation, stability) should be addressed to provide appropriate and supportive information for the labelling. This information should cover the recommended in-use shelf life, at the recommended storage temperature and at the likely extremes of concentration.

Similarly, admixture or dilution of products prior to administration (e.g., product added to large volume infusion containers) might need to be addressed.

Commentary

Regulatory focus: preservative effectiveness testing

Q8 emphasizes two key principles:

  • Preservative content in the specification (a chemical test) is not the same as the preservative effectiveness that must be demonstrated during development
  • Effectiveness must be demonstrated at the lowest specified concentration, not at the average or highest concentration

Test standards: the antimicrobial effectiveness test (AET / Preservative Efficacy Test) in the relevant pharmacopoeias: USP <51>, Ph. Eur. 5.1.3, BP Appendix XVI C.

If the product is for multi-dose use, challenge testing must be performed under conditions simulating patient use.

Analogy: the lowest effective preservative concentration is like the lowest effective antibiotic dose

Clinically, antibiotic dosing is based on the MIC (minimum inhibitory concentration): higher is not better; the aim is to find the lowest amount that is effective enough, balancing efficacy and safety. The logic of verifying preservative effectiveness is the same: demonstrate efficacy at the lowest specified concentration, so that the product remains effective throughout its shelf life.

2.6 Compatibility: dilution/reconstitution in the use environment

For products that must be reconstituted or diluted before use, 2.6 requires the following:

  • Diluent compatibility: which diluents can be added while the product remains stable? Is there any precipitation?
  • In-use shelf life: how long can the product be stored after dilution/reconstitution, and at what temperature?
  • Concentration range: stability at the lowest and highest dilution concentrations
  • Large volume infusion bags: if the product is to be added to 500 mL/1000 mL bags, compatibility must be evaluated

Compatibility data directly support the preparation instructions in the product labeling (PI/PIL).

Key compatibility considerations for injectables

Injectables (e.g., intravenous infusion products) usually need the most complete compatibility data, including:

  • Compatibility with 0.9% NaCl, 5% dextrose, and lactated Ringer's solution
  • Compatibility with common infusion containers (PVC, non-PVC) (E&L considerations)
  • Photodegradation stability after dilution (if protection from light is required during storage)
  • Y-site compatibility (if multiple drugs are infused simultaneously)

These data are usually tied directly to clinical medication guidelines.

Core Terms (1): Continuous Process Verification & Design Space

3. Glossary — Part I

Continuous Process Verification

An alternative approach to process validation in which manufacturing process performance is continuously monitored and evaluated.

Design Space

The multidimensional combination and interaction of input variables (e.g., material attributes) and process parameters that have been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality.

Working within the design space is not considered as a change. Movement out of the design space is considered to be a change and would normally initiate a regulatory post approval change process. Design space is proposed by the applicant and is subject to regulatory assessment and approval.

Commentary

Continuous Process Verification (CPV)

Official definition: An alternative approach to process validation in which manufacturing process performance is continuously monitored and evaluated.

CPV reflects the "Stage 3" concept promoted by the FDA 2011 Process Validation Guidance. Compared with the traditional one-time "three-batch validation" approach, CPV represents a paradigm shift in process validation:

  • Traditional PV: three batches pass validation → process declared validated → process fixed
  • CPV: continuous data collection → statistical process control (SPC) → ongoing confirmation that the process remains in a state of control

CPV requires a sound process monitoring system, including inline/atline/online measurement techniques, and is usually used together with PAT tools.

Design Space — the most important Q8 term

Breaking down the official definition:

  • Multidimensional combination: not the range of a single variable, but the space of interactions among multiple input variables
  • Input variables: include both material attributes and process parameters
  • Demonstrated to provide assurance of quality: must be supported by scientific data

The most important regulatory implication: operating within the Design Space is not considered a "change" and does not require a post-approval change submission (supplement). This is the greatest regulatory flexibility that QbD offers.

Design Space vs Normal Operating Range (NOR) vs PAR

For the detailed relationship, see Part II Annex 2.4.5.

Core Terms (2): Formal Experimental Design & Lifecycle

Formal Experimental Design

A structured, organized method for determining the relationship between factors affecting a process and the output of that process. Also known as "Design of Experiments".

Lifecycle

All phases in the life of a product from the initial development through marketing until the product's discontinuation.

Commentary

Formal Experimental Design (DoE)

Official definition: A structured, organized method for determining the relationship between factors affecting a process and the output of that process, also known as "Design of Experiments".

Q8 refers to the use of DoE in the following situations:

  • Identifying critical formulation attributes (2.2.1)
  • Identifying the relationship between CPPs and CQAs (2.3)
  • Establishing the Design Space (Part II Annex 2.4)

Common DoE designs: Plackett-Burman (screening) → Central Composite Design (optimization) → Mixture Design (formulation).

Definition of Lifecycle and the ICH Q trilogy

Official definition: All phases in the life of a product from the initial development through marketing until the product's discontinuation.

The Q8 "lifecycle" concept is the foundational philosophy of the whole ICH Q trilogy:

"Lifecycle management" means that Section 3.2.P.2 (Pharmaceutical Development) can be updated throughout the product lifecycle to incorporate new knowledge.

Core Terms (3): PAT, Process Robustness & Quality

Process Analytical Technology (PAT)

A system for designing, analyzing, and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements (i.e., during processing) of critical quality and performance attributes of raw and in-process materials and processes with the goal of ensuring final product quality.

Process Robustness

Ability of a process to tolerate variability of materials and changes of the process and equipment without negative impact on quality.

Quality

The suitability of either a drug substance or drug product for its intended use. This term includes such attributes as the identity, strength, and purity (from ICH Q6A Specifications).

Commentary

Process Analytical Technology (PAT)

Core of the official definition: A system for designing, analyzing, and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements (i.e., during processing) of critical quality and performance attributes of raw and in-process materials.

The three dimensions of PAT:

  • Designing: establish measurement methods during development to understand the process
  • Analyzing: real-time data analysis to understand the state of the process
  • Controlling: make real-time process adjustments based on measurement results

Common PAT tools: NIR (near-infrared spectroscopy) for moisture/content monitoring, Raman spectroscopy, online particle size analysis, pH/temperature sensors. PAT is the technical foundation of CPV.

Analogy: PAT is like an aircraft autopilot

Traditional manufacturing = a pilot navigating by sight and checking the instruments now and then (few in-process measurements, relying only on end-point testing). PAT = a fully automatic autopilot: continuous sensor data → real-time adjustment → an accurate flight path. PAT shifts process control from "inspection after manufacture" to "assurance during manufacture", which is the technical prerequisite for Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT).

Process Robustness

Official definition: The ability of a process to tolerate variability of materials and changes of the process and equipment without negative impact on quality.

Robustness is the practical expression of the Design Space concept:

  • Design Space defines "the range within which operation is permitted"
  • Process Robustness describes "how well the process resists fluctuation within that range"
  • High robustness → routine operation stays far from the edge of failure → low manufacturing risk

Robustness is usually evaluated with DoE, and the results support setting the CPP operating ranges.

The official definition of Quality — note its limited scope

ICH Q8 takes its definition of "quality" directly from ICH Q6A: the suitability of either a drug substance or drug product for its intended use, including such attributes as identity, strength, and purity.

This definition is relatively "narrow", focusing mainly on the physicochemical and microbiological aspects. Note how it differs from everyday use of the word "quality":

  • It does not directly include "efficacy" or "safety" (these are linked indirectly through CQAs)
  • "Identity, strength, and purity" are the three core dimensions of specification testing
  • Q8 later introduces the QTPP and CQAs to broaden the meaning of quality

Annex 1: Introduction & Approaches to Pharmaceutical Development

Part II Annex — Section 1: Introduction

This guideline is an annex to ICH Q8 Pharmaceutical Development and provides further clarification of key concepts outlined in the core guideline. In addition, this annex describes the principles of quality by design (QbD). The annex is not intended to establish new standards or to introduce new regulatory requirements; however, it shows how concepts and tools (e.g., design space) outlined in the parent Q8 document could be put into practice by the applicant for all dosage forms.

Pharmaceutical development should include, at a minimum, the following elements:

  • Defining the quality target product profile (QTPP) as it relates to quality, safety and efficacy, considering e.g., the route of administration, dosage form, bioavailability, strength, and stability
  • Identifying potential critical quality attributes (CQAs) of the drug product, so that those product characteristics having an impact on product quality can be studied and controlled
  • Determining the critical quality attributes of the drug substance, excipients etc., and selecting the type and amount of excipients to deliver drug product of the desired quality
  • Selecting an appropriate manufacturing process
  • Defining a control strategy

An enhanced, QbD approach to product development would additionally include:

  • A systematic evaluation, understanding and refining of the formulation and manufacturing process, including identifying material attributes and process parameters that can have an effect on product CQAs, and determining the functional relationships that link them
  • Using the enhanced product and process understanding in combination with quality risk management to establish an appropriate control strategy which can include a proposal for a design space and/or real-time release testing
It is the knowledge gained and submitted to the authorities, and not the volume of data collected, that forms the basis for science- and risk-based submissions and regulatory evaluations.

Commentary

Key clarification: the Annex adds no new requirements

The Q8 Annex explicitly states that it does not establish new standards or introduce new regulatory requirements. This means:

  • A submission can be approved without a QbD approach, as long as the minimal elements are met
  • QbD is an "opportunity", not an "obligation", but applicants who choose the QbD path enjoy greater flexibility
  • The "quality of knowledge" matters more than the "quantity of data"

Core concept: minimal elements of QbD vs enhanced approach

The Annex clearly distinguishes two levels, which is key to understanding regulatory expectations under Q8:

Rewards of the enhanced approach: greater operational flexibility (no supplement needed within the Design Space), support for RTRT (replacing some end-product testing), and potentially fewer post-approval changes.

Annex 2.1 & 2.2: QTPP & CQA

2.1 Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP)

The quality target product profile forms the basis of design for the development of the product. Considerations for the quality target product profile could include:

  • Intended use in clinical setting, route of administration, dosage form, delivery systems
  • Dosage strength(s)
  • Container closure system
  • Therapeutic moiety release or delivery and attributes affecting pharmacokinetic characteristics (e.g., dissolution, aerodynamic performance)
  • Drug product quality criteria (e.g., sterility, purity, stability and drug release) appropriate for the intended marketed product

2.2 Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs)

A CQA is a physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality. CQAs are generally associated with the drug substance, excipients, intermediates (in-process materials) and drug product.

CQAs of solid oral dosage forms are typically those aspects affecting product purity, strength, drug release and stability. CQAs for other delivery systems can additionally include more product specific aspects, such as aerodynamic properties for inhaled products, sterility for parenterals, and adhesion properties for transdermal patches.

For drug substances, raw materials and intermediates, the CQAs can additionally include those properties (e.g., particle size distribution, bulk density) that affect drug product CQAs.

Potential drug product CQAs derived from the QTPP and/or prior knowledge are used to guide the product and process development. Relevant CQAs can be identified by an iterative process of quality risk management and experimentation that assesses the extent to which their variation can have an impact on the quality of the drug product.

Commentary

QTPP: the "target-setting brief" for drug product design

The QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile) is the starting point of drug product development: it defines what the ideal product should look like. It is not a specification sheet but a vision document that drives all subsequent development decisions.

Five dimensions considered in the QTPP:

  • Clinical use: indication, route of administration, dosage form (e.g., oral, injectable, inhalation)
  • Dosage strength: therapeutic dose range
  • Container closure: intended packaging format
  • Drug release: dissolution characteristics, aerodynamic performance (corresponding to PK targets)
  • Quality criteria: sterility, purity, stability

Analogy: the QTPP is like the client brief for a building design

Before a building is constructed, the owner states the requirements (number of floors, use, budget, seismic rating). This brief is the QTPP: it drives every design decision made by the architect (the formulation scientist). Without a clear QTPP, development may drift away from clinical needs.

CQA: the list of quality attributes derived from the QTPP

Official definition: a physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality.

Difference between a CQA and a specification:

  • A CQA is a development tool: it identifies which attributes need to be studied and controlled
  • A specification is a regulatory requirement: the acceptance criteria for the final commercial product
  • The CQA list is dynamic during development and is revised as knowledge increases

Characteristic CQAs by dosage form:

  • Oral solid: purity, potency, dissolution, stability
  • Inhalation products: aerodynamic particle size distribution (APSD), MMAD
  • Injectables: sterility, particulates, visible/subvisible particles
  • Patches: adhesion, skin permeation

Drug substance CQAs also affect drug product CQAs

The CQA concept extends to drug substances and intermediates: the particle size distribution (PSD) and bulk density of the API, although attributes of the API, directly affect the content uniformity (a CQA) of the drug product.

This "upstream CQA to downstream CQA" linkage is the core subject of risk assessment analysis (see Section 2.3).

Annex 2.3 & 2.4: Risk Assessment & Design Space in Detail

2.3 Risk Assessment: Linking Material Attributes and Process Parameters to CQAs

Risk assessment is a valuable science-based process used in quality risk management (see ICH Q9) that can aid in identifying which material attributes and process parameters potentially have an effect on product CQAs. Risk assessment is typically performed early in the pharmaceutical development process and is repeated as more information becomes available and greater knowledge is obtained.

Risk assessment tools can be used to identify and rank parameters (e.g., process, equipment, input materials) with potential to have an impact on product quality, based on prior knowledge and initial experimental data. The initial list of potential parameters can be quite extensive, but can be modified and prioritized by further studies (e.g., through a combination of design of experiments, mechanistic models).

Once the significant parameters are identified, they can be further studied (e.g., through a combination of design of experiments, mathematical models, or studies that lead to mechanistic understanding) to achieve a higher level of process understanding.

2.4 Design Space — Six Sub-Topics

2.4.1 Selection of Variables

A description should be provided in the application of the process parameters and material attributes considered for the design space, those that were included, and their effect on product quality. The rationale for inclusion in the design space should be presented. In some cases it is helpful to provide also the rationale as to why some parameters were excluded.

2.4.2 Describing a Design Space in a Submission

A design space can be described in terms of ranges of material attributes and process parameters, or through more complex mathematical relationships. It is possible to describe a design space as a time dependent function (e.g., temperature and pressure cycle of a lyophilisation cycle), or as a combination of variables such as components of a multivariate model. Scaling factors can also be included if the design space is intended to span multiple operational scales.

2.4.3 Unit Operation Design Space(s)

The applicant can choose to establish independent design spaces for one or more unit operations, or to establish a single design space that spans multiple operations. While a separate design space for each unit operation is often simpler to develop, a design space that spans the entire process can provide more operational flexibility.

2.4.4 Relationship of Design Space to Scale and Equipment

A design space can be developed at any scale. The applicant should justify the relevance of a design space developed at small or pilot scale to the proposed production scale manufacturing process. If the design space is proposed to be applicable to multiple scales, it should be described in terms of scale-independent parameters (e.g., shear rate rather than agitation rate).

2.4.5 Design Space Versus Proven Acceptable Ranges

A combination of proven acceptable ranges does not constitute a design space. However, proven acceptable ranges based on univariate experimentation can provide useful knowledge about the process.

2.4.6 Design Space and Edge of Failure

It can be helpful to determine the edge of failure for process parameters or material attributes, beyond which the relevant quality attributes cannot be met. However, determining the edge of failure or demonstrating failure modes are not essential parts of establishing a design space.

Commentary

2.3 Risk assessment: from a "list of possible impacts" to "confirmation of critical parameters"

Risk assessment in drug product development is an iterative process:

  1. Initial risk assessment (early development): based on prior knowledge, list all material attributes and process parameters that may affect CQAs; this list is usually long
  2. Risk ranking: use FMEA, fishbone diagrams, and risk matrices to screen for high-priority parameters
  3. Experimental verification (DoE): confirm which parameters have a statistically significant effect on CQAs
  4. Continuous updating: repeat the assessment as development progresses, so the list becomes progressively more focused

The "significant parameters" finally confirmed become CPPs and enter the Design Space design.

2.4.5 The most common misunderstanding: a combination of PARs is not a Design Space

This is one of the most important clarifications in Q8:

  • PAR (Proven Acceptable Range): the acceptable range of a single parameter, established by univariate experiments without considering the effect of other parameters
  • Design Space: a multidimensional, interactive space of multiple CPPs and material attributes, which accounts for interactions among parameters and is established by multivariate experiments such as DoE

For example: the PAR for granulation water is 15-25%, and the PAR for spray rate is 5-10 g/min. But the combination of these two PARs (a rectangular region) is not equal to the Design Space, because quality may be unacceptable under certain combinations (high water + high spray rate).

Analogy: DS vs PAR

Imagine the safe load-bearing range of a house:
PAR approach = testing only the maximum load of each column (individually)
DS approach = testing the overall structural load under all column combinations (considering the supporting interactions among columns)

Clearly, the DS gives a more accurate understanding of the "safe operating space".

2.4.4 Scale-up and scale-independent parameters

When a DS must span laboratory, pilot and commercial manufacturing scales, it must be described using scale-independent parameters:

  • Agitation-sensitive products: use shear rate rather than rotational speed (RPM)
  • Drying processes: use dimensionless numbers such as the Froude number
  • Lyophilized products: use the functional form of the time-temperature-pressure profile

This allows the same DS to be valid on equipment of different scales, supporting manufacturing flexibility after commercialization.

Annex 2.5, 2.6 & Section 3: Control Strategy, Lifecycle & CTD Submission

2.5 Control Strategy

A control strategy is designed to ensure that a product of required quality will be produced consistently. A control strategy can include, but is not limited to, the following:

  • Control of input material attributes (e.g., drug substance, excipients, primary packaging materials) based on an understanding of their impact on processability or product quality
  • Product specification(s)
  • Controls for unit operations that have an impact on downstream processing or product quality
  • In-process or real-time release testing in lieu of end-product testing (e.g., measurement and control of CQAs during processing)
  • A monitoring program (e.g., full product testing at regular intervals) for verifying multivariate prediction models
Sources of variability that can impact product quality should be identified, appropriately understood, and subsequently controlled. This process understanding can enable an alternative manufacturing paradigm where the variability of input materials could be less tightly constrained — instead it can be possible to design an adaptive process step.

2.6 Product Lifecycle Management and Continual Improvement

Throughout the product lifecycle, companies have opportunities to evaluate innovative approaches to improve product quality (see ICH Q10). Process performance can be monitored to ensure that it is working as anticipated to deliver product quality attributes as predicted by the design space.

Expansion, reduction or redefinition of the design space could be desired upon gaining additional process knowledge. Change of design space is subject to regional requirements.

Commentary

RTRT (Real-Time Release Testing)

RTRT is one of the greatest manufacturing benefits of QbD: real-time in-process measurements replace some end-product testing:

  • Condition: a reliable mathematical model has been established between the PAT measurement and the CQA
  • Example: real-time NIR determination of content uniformity replaces the traditional content uniformity test
  • Limitation: RTRT can replace end-product testing but cannot replace the batch review and QA release steps required by GMP
  • Benefits: faster release and higher quality assurance (continuous data vs sampled testing)

Analogy: the control strategy as multilayer defense

The control strategy is like airport security: it does not rely on a single checkpoint but on multiple layers of defense: border control (incoming materials), baggage X-ray (in-process IPC), personal screening (in-process testing), boarding gate verification (end-product testing). Each layer addresses a different risk, and together they ensure that "non-conforming product never reaches the passenger (the patient)".

Regulatory requirements for Design Space changes

Q8 Annex 2.6 states clearly that changes to a Design Space (expansion, reduction, or redefinition) are subject to regional regulatory requirements. This means:

  • Once approved, a Design Space cannot be changed arbitrarily
  • Post-approval knowledge updates (such as model maintenance) that do not change the DS itself can be managed within the company quality system (no regulatory filing required)
  • To expand the DS range, a filing under the post-approval change process is required

This reflects the DS characteristic of being "flexible but with boundaries".

Part II Annex Glossary (Section 4: Glossary) — Complete Set of Core Terms

4. Glossary — Part II (Complete)

Control Strategy

A planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding that ensures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control. (ICH Q10)

Critical Process Parameter (CPP)

A process parameter whose variability has an impact on a critical quality attribute and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces the desired quality.

Critical Quality Attribute (CQA)

A physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality.

Design Space

The multidimensional combination and interaction of input variables (e.g., material attributes) and process parameters that have been demonstrated to provide assurance of quality. Working within the design space is not considered as a change. Movement out of the design space is considered to be a change and would normally initiate a regulatory post approval change process. Design space is proposed by the applicant and is subject to regulatory assessment and approval. (ICH Q8)

Lifecycle

All phases in the life of a product from the initial development through marketing until the product's discontinuation. (ICH Q8)

Proven Acceptable Range (PAR)

A characterised range of a process parameter for which operation within this range, while keeping other parameters constant, will result in producing a material meeting relevant quality criteria.

Quality

The suitability of either a drug substance or a drug product for its intended use. This term includes such attributes as the identity, strength, and purity. (ICH Q6A)

Quality by Design (QbD)

A systematic approach to development that begins with predefined objectives and emphasizes product and process understanding and process control, based on sound science and quality risk management.

Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP)

A prospective summary of the quality characteristics of a drug product that ideally will be achieved to ensure the desired quality, taking into account safety and efficacy of the drug product.

Real Time Release Testing (RTRT)

The ability to evaluate and ensure the quality of in-process and/or final product based on process data, which typically include a valid combination of measured material attributes and process controls.

Commentary: In-Depth Review of Core Terms

Three Key Elements of the Official QbD Definition

The Part II Glossary gives the official definition of QbD, which has three key elements:

  • "Begins with predefined objectives": start from the QTPP, not from the drug substance properties or the process
  • "Emphasizes product and process understanding": scientific understanding is the core, not simply the accumulation of data
  • "Based on sound science and quality risk management": QbD is not a stand-alone method; it is used together with Q9 (QRM) and Q10 (PQS)

Precise Definition of a CPP: Only Parameters That Affect a CQA Qualify

The definition of a CPP is very precise: a process parameter whose variability has an impact on a CQA. Note:

  • If variation of a process parameter within its normal operating range does not affect any CQA, it is not a CPP
  • Identifying CPPs relies on risk assessment (2.3) and confirmation by DoE experiments
  • The CPP list must be submitted to the regulators and documented in 3.2.P.3.3 and 3.2.P.3.4

Common mistake: calling every process parameter that needs to be controlled a CPP. Only those confirmed to affect a CQA are CPPs.

Relationship Between PAR and CPP

PAR (Proven Acceptable Range) and CPP are concepts at different levels:

  • CPP = "this parameter has a critical impact on quality" (a classification judgement)
  • PAR = "what is the acceptable operating range of this CPP" (range determination)

Logical chain: identify the CPP → determine the PAR through experiments → optionally integrate into the Design Space (if multivariate analysis is performed).

Analysis of the Core Differences Across the Six Aspects

Appendix 1 is the most intuitive explanatory material on QbD in Q8. Its six comparison dimensions reveal the fundamental philosophical difference between the "traditional approach" and the "QbD approach":

  • Pharmaceutical development: single-variable OVAT (One Variable At a Time) → multivariate DoE (accounting for interactions)
  • Manufacturing process: fixed process → adjustable within the design space
  • Process control: offline end-point determination → inline real-time monitoring with PAT
  • Product specification: the primary means of control → part of the overall control strategy
  • Control strategy: after-the-fact testing → risk-based upstream control
  • Lifecycle management: reactive (act only when problems occur) → proactive prevention + continual improvement

Practical Consideration: Industry Practice Usually Falls Between the Two

Q8 states explicitly that actual industry practice usually falls between the minimal approach and the full QbD approach. This is important: you do not need to implement QbD in full to pass regulatory review. Many companies adopt a "hybrid approach":

  • Some CQAs have a full Design Space (attributes with high risk or high uncertainty)
  • Other CQAs are controlled with a traditional PAR
  • Some unit operations use PAT, while others retain traditional IPC

The applicant should clearly explain the development strategy and the extent of development chosen in the submission.

Appendix 2: Illustrative Examples — Risk Assessment & Design Space Presentations

Appendix 2: Illustrative Examples

A. Use of a Risk Assessment Tool (Ishikawa + FMEA)

A cross-functional team of experts could work together to develop an Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram that identifies potential variables which can have an impact on the desired quality attribute. The team could then rank the variables based on probability, severity, and detectability using failure mode effects analysis (FMEA) or similar tools based on prior knowledge and initial experimental data. Design of experiments or other experimental approaches could then be used to evaluate the impact of the higher ranked variables.

Example Ishikawa diagram for a solid oral dosage form (tablet content uniformity) identifies variables from six branches: Drying, Granulation, Raw Materials, Compressing, Plant Factors, and process conditions such as temperature, moisture content (LOD), particle size (P.S.), spray rate, mixer speed, feeder speed, press speed.

B. Depiction of Interactions

A figure depicts the presence or absence of interactions among three process parameters on the level of degradation product Y. Three parameters: initial moisture content (IMC), temperature, and mean particle size (d50) of the drying operation of a granulate.

Key finding from the interaction plots: Initial moisture content and temperature are interacting; but IMC and mean particle size are not, nor are temperature and mean particle size. The relative slopes of the lines within a plot indicate if interaction is present (non-parallel = interaction).

C. Presentations of Design Space

Example 1: Response graphs for dissolution depicted as surface plot (Figure 1a) and contour plot (Figure 1b). Parameters 1 and 2 are factors of a granulation operation affecting dissolution rate (e.g., excipient attribute, water amount, granule size).

  • Non-linear DS (Figure 1c): DS defined by non-linear combination of parameter ranges — the acceptable range of one parameter is dependent on the value of the other. Maximises the operating range.
  • Linear DS (Figure 1d): DS defined as smaller rectangular region based on linear combination. More limiting but operationally simpler.

Example 2: Design space determined from the common region of successful operating ranges for multiple CQAs (tablet friability AND dissolution). The proposed design space is the overlap region of the acceptable ranges for both CQAs simultaneously.

Example 3: Design space for a drying operation dependent upon the path of temperature and/or pressure over time. Operating above upper limit → excessive impurity formation; below lower limit → excessive particle attrition. DS is expressed as a time-path corridor.

Commentary

A. Ishikawa + FMEA: A Two-Step Risk Assessment

The risk assessment flow described in Q8 Appendix 2A is an industry-standard method:

  1. Step 1: Ishikawa (fishbone diagram)— brainstorm to identify all factors that could affect the CQA (cast a wide net)
    • Six main bones: raw materials, granulation, drying, compression, facility factors, process conditions
    • Each main bone is then broken down into specific parameters (e.g., granulation → water amount, spray rate, mixing speed...)
  2. Step 2: FMEA ranking— score each factor (narrow the focus)
    • Severity: if a failure occurs, how serious is the impact on the patient/quality?
    • Probability: how likely is the failure to occur?
    • Detectability: how quickly can the failure be detected?
    • RPN (Risk Priority Number) = S × P × D → rank and then select the high-risk parameters
  3. Step 3: DoE confirmation — run experiments on the high-risk parameters to confirm their true impact

B. Identifying Interactions: Parallel vs Non-Parallel Lines

The core teaching point of Appendix 2B: how to tell from a two-dimensional interaction plot whether an interaction exists:

  • Parallel lines = no interaction → the two parameters can be considered independently
  • Non-parallel lines = interaction present → they must be considered together in multiple dimensions

Conclusion of the Q8 example: there is an interaction between IMC (initial moisture content) and temperature. At high temperature, the effect of moisture content on degradation product Y is much greater than at low temperature. This is exactly why "a combination of PARs does not equal a Design Space": using only single-variable PARs would overlook this dangerous interaction.

Analogy: Overlapping DS for Multiple CQAs Is Like Overlapping Projections

Imagine three projectors of different colors casting three circles on a wall (each circle represents the acceptable operating region for one CQA). The area where all three circles overlap (the intersection) is the Design Space: only within this area do all three CQAs meet their quality requirements at the same time.

This is the core of Example 2 in the Q8 appendix: the DS is the operating space that satisfies all CQAs at once, not the space that satisfies any single one.

Practical Significance of the Example 3 Lyophilization Design Space

The DS of a lyophilization process is a typical time-dependent design space:

  • It is not a static "temperature between X and Y °C", but rather "the temperature-time path over the whole drying cycle must stay within a corridor"
  • Above the upper limit (temperature too high, time too short) → excess degradation products
  • Below the lower limit (temperature too low, time too short) → excess particle attrition
  • This kind of DS needs to be presented as an equation or software model and is hard to express with simple numerical values

This dynamic DS is especially important for lyophilization of biologics: any batch-to-batch deviation in the temperature profile must be assessed against the DS to decide whether it constitutes a "change".
