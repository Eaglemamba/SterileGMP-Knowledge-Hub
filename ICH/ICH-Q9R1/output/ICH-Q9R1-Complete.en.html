Section 1 — Introduction (first three paragraphs): the nature of risk, subjectivity and patient protection

Original Text

1. INTRODUCTION

Risk management principles are effectively utilized in many areas of business and government including finance, insurance, occupational safety, public health, pharmacovigilance, and by agencies regulating these industries. In the pharmaceutical sector, the principles and framework of ICH Q9, coupled with the official ICH training material that supports this guideline, are instrumental in enhancing the application of effective quality risk management by industry and regulators. The importance of quality systems has been recognized in the pharmaceutical industry and it is evident that quality risk management is a valuable component of an effective quality system.

It is commonly understood that risk is defined as the combination of the probability of occurrence of harm and the severity of that harm. However, achieving a shared understanding of the application of risk management among diverse stakeholders is difficult because each stakeholder might perceive different potential harms, place a different probability on each harm occurring and attribute different severities to each harm. In addition, subjectivity can directly impact the effectiveness of risk management activities and the decisions made. Therefore, it is important that subjectivity is managed and minimized. In relation to pharmaceuticals, although there are a variety of stakeholders, including patients and medical practitioners as well as government and industry, the protection of the patient is of prime importance when managing the risk to product quality and availability, when availability risks arise from quality/manufacturing issues.

The manufacturing and use of a drug (medicinal) product, including its components, necessarily entail some degree of risk. The risk to its quality is just one component of the overall risk. It is important to understand that product quality is assured based on appropriate risk-based decision-making throughout the product lifecycle, such that the attributes that are important to the quality of the drug (medicinal) product are maintained and the product remains safe and effective.

Tutorial Commentary

Core concept: the definition of risk

Risk = Probability of harm × Severity of harm

This is the official ICH Q9 definition of risk and the basis for the calculations in all QRM tools (FMEA, HAZOP, FTA, etc.). Note that many tools add a third dimension, detectability; for example, the FMEA RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection.

Q9 itself defines only two dimensions. This is a deliberately simple framework that avoids mandating any particular method.

Analogy: the insurance actuary's view

Risk = probability × severity follows exactly the same logic insurers use to calculate premiums. A chemical plant located in an earthquake zone (high probability + high severity) pays far higher premiums than a warehouse on flat ground.

The difficulty in QRM is that different stakeholders often estimate the probability and judge the severity of the same event differently. A process engineer may consider a mixing step low risk (because they run it every day), while QA or regulatory affairs may see higher risk (because they have seen more deviation cases). This is the "subjectivity problem", and R1 specifically stresses the need to "manage and minimize subjectivity".

New emphasis in R1: product availability = a patient protection issue

Q9(R1) plants the seed of an important revision as early as the first section:

"the protection of the patient is of prime importance when managing the risk to product quality and availability, when availability risks arise from quality/manufacturing issues."

This was not in the original 2005 Q9. R1 states explicitly that if a manufacturing or quality problem causes a drug shortage so that patients cannot obtain their medicine, this is also a patient protection issue. The global drug supply chain crisis during COVID-19 was one of the drivers of this revision.

Practical implications for pharmaceutical manufacturers and CDMOs: supply chain risk management, stock-holding strategies and sole-source issues for critical materials should all come within the scope of QRM.

CDMO practice: QRM as a tool for multi-party consensus

A CDMO serves multiple clients at once, and different clients often accept different levels of risk for the same process. For example, for a given mixing-time deviation, Client A (an oncology drug with no alternative) may demand an immediate line stop and investigation, while Client B (an OTC health product) may accept continued production with additional testing.

Establishing a formal QRM framework (risk policy + risk criteria) makes decisions traceable, keeps them consistent in a multi-client environment, and lets you explain clearly during client audits why a given decision was made.

Section 1 — Introduction (last four paragraphs): proactive QRM, new risks from digitalization, the concept of formality

Original Text

An effective quality risk management approach can further ensure the quality of the drug (medicinal) product to the patient by providing a proactive means to identify and control potential quality issues during development, manufacturing, and distribution. This includes an appropriate application of root cause analysis that can identify and address the root cause(s) and other causal factors (e.g., human-related) of such issues. A proactive approach to quality risk management is beneficial, as it facilitates robust product design and continual improvement, and it is of strategic importance in achieving an effective pharmaceutical quality system. (See ICH Q10 for guidance in relation to an effective pharmaceutical quality system.) Additionally, use of quality risk management can improve the decision-making if a quality problem arises.

In the development phase and as part of validation, quality risk management is part of building knowledge and understanding risk scenarios, so that appropriate risk control can be decided upon for use during the commercial manufacturing phase. In this context, knowledge is used to make informed risk-based decisions, trigger re-evaluations and stimulate continual improvements. Effective and proactive quality risk management can enable better, more informed and timely decisions throughout the lifecycle. This can provide regulators with greater assurance of a company's ability to deal with potential risks and avert problems, and can beneficially affect the extent and level of direct regulatory oversight.

The application of digitalization and emerging technologies in the manufacture and control of drug (medicinal) products can lead to risk reduction, when such technologies are fit for their intended use. However, they can also introduce other risks that may need to be controlled. The application of quality risk management to the design, validation and technology transfer of advanced production processes and analytical methods, advanced data analysis methods and computerized systems is important.

The purpose of this document is to offer a systematic approach to quality risk management that leads to better, more informed, and timely decisions. It serves as a foundation or resource document that is independent of, yet supports, other ICH Quality documents and complements existing quality practices, requirements, standards, and guidelines within the pharmaceutical industry and regulatory environment. It specifically provides guidance on the principles and some of the tools of quality risk management that can enable more effective and consistent risk-based decisions, both by regulators and industry, regarding the quality of drug substances and drug (medicinal) products across the product lifecycle. It is not intended to create any new expectations beyond the current regulatory requirements.

"An understanding of formality in quality risk management may lead to resources being used more efficiently, where lower risk issues are dealt with via less formal means, freeing up resources for managing higher risk issues and more complex problems that may require increased levels of rigor and effort."

Appropriate use of quality risk management can facilitate but does not obviate industry's obligation to comply with regulatory requirements and does not replace appropriate communications between industry and regulators. Quality risk management should not be used in a manner where decisions are made that justify a practice that would otherwise, in accordance with regulations and/or guidance, be deemed unacceptable.

Tutorial Commentary

Proactive vs. reactive QRM

Q9(R1) stresses the importance of proactive QRM. The contrast is as follows:

  • Reactive: a deviation occurs → CAPA → a risk assessment to decide whether to release. Risk assessment is "firefighting".
  • Proactive: during development, use FMEA/HAZOP to identify potential failure modes → build controls for these risks into the process design and control strategy → the probability of problems in commercial production drops substantially.

Proactive QRM also includes root cause analysis (RCA): the aim is not only to find the direct cause but also the systemic root cause (including human factors), so that recurrence can truly be prevented.

New emphasis in R1: digitalization and emerging technologies as a double-edged sword

This is another important addition in Q9(R1) compared with the 2005 version. At that time there was no large-scale use of AI/ML, no Industry 4.0 and no advanced continuous manufacturing.

R1 acknowledges that new technologies can "reduce risk" (for example, real-time PAT monitoring replacing blind release), but also that they "introduce new risks", for example:

  • Training-data bias in AI algorithms
  • Data integrity risks in computerized systems
  • Process-monitoring failure modes in advanced continuous manufacturing
  • Cybersecurity risks in cloud data management

Therefore, QRM should be applied with particular care to the validation and technology transfer of new technologies.

Analogy: the concept of formality

Imagine you are a surgeon. Replacing a light bulb in the operating room (low risk) → you do not need to convene a multidisciplinary meeting or fill in a 10-page risk assessment form; a phone call to the facilities team is enough. But introducing a new robot-assisted surgery system (high risk) → you need a formal validation plan, training records, clinical trials, equipment qualification documents, and so on.

This is the essence of "formality commensurate with the level of risk": concentrate limited resources on the high-risk issues that really need rigorous management, rather than applying the most elaborate procedure to everything. R1 introduces this new concept right at the start of the Introduction and develops it in detail in Section 5.

Important warning: QRM is not a tool for rationalizing non-compliance

The last paragraph is a serious warning:

"QRM should not be used in a manner where decisions are made that justify a practice that would otherwise… be deemed unacceptable."

In reality, some companies do defend non-compliant practices with "we did a risk assessment and concluded it is acceptable", for example against certain GDP requirements, data integrity rules or sterile manufacturing control requirements. ICH does not endorse this practice. QRM is a tool to help you make better decisions, not an excuse to bypass regulations.

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO process engineer says: "We have run this step for hundreds of batches; it is mature and does not need a risk assessment." What is wrong with this statement? How would you respond from the perspective of Q9(R1)?
  2. Q9(R1) states that QRM can "beneficially affect the extent and level of direct regulatory oversight". Explain what this means for a manufacturer that wants to pass an FDA PAI (Pre-Approval Inspection) smoothly.
  3. If your company is introducing an AI process control system, at which points should you apply QRM according to R1? List at least three key points.
Section 2 — Scope

Original Text

2. SCOPE

This guideline provides principles and examples of tools for quality risk management that can be applied to different aspects of pharmaceutical quality. These aspects include development, manufacturing, distribution, and the inspection and submission/review processes throughout the lifecycle of drug substances, drug (medicinal) products, biological and biotechnological products (including the use of raw materials, solvents, excipients, packaging and labeling materials in drug (medicinal) products, biological and biotechnological products).

Tutorial Commentary

Scope analysis: the four areas covered by Q9

Although the Scope is only one paragraph, the coverage is very broad:

  • Development: formulation development, process development, analytical method development and stability studies. QRM should be involved from the earliest stage
  • Manufacturing: commercial batch production, process validation, change control and deviation handling. QRM is at the core of day-to-day quality decisions
  • Distribution: cold chain management, GDP compliance, and monitoring of storage and transport conditions. These are also within the scope of Q9, a point many people overlook
  • Inspection and Submission/Review: risk assessment by regulatory authorities before inspections, and risk descriptions in CTD dossiers. QRM works both ways; regulators use it too

CDMO practice: QRM applies across the whole outsourcing chain

A CDMO does more than manufacture; it also deals with raw material procurement, support for finished product distribution and so on, and the entire service chain falls within the scope of Q9. A common blind spot: a CDMO performs full QRM on its client's release testing (QC) but gives too little risk assessment to cold chain transport (for example, biologics requiring 2-8°C), leading to quality events in the distribution step. The Scope of Q9(R1) tells us clearly that every link from raw material to patient needs QRM coverage.

Section 3 — Principles of Quality Risk Management: the two principles

Original Text

3. PRINCIPLES OF QUALITY RISK MANAGEMENT

Two primary principles of quality risk management are:

  • The evaluation of the risk to quality should be based on scientific knowledge and ultimately link to the protection of the patient. (Note: Risk to quality includes situations where product availability may be impacted, leading to potential patient harm.)
  • The level of effort, formality and documentation of the quality risk management process should be commensurate with the level of risk.

Tutorial Commentary

First principle in depth: scientific knowledge + patient protection

"Based on scientific knowledge" means QRM relies not on intuition or seniority, but on:

  • Toxicological data (for example, the PDE limit of an impurity)
  • Process understanding (the relationship between CPPs and CQAs)
  • Literature data and historical batch data
  • Regulatory guidance (ICH M7, Q3C, etc.)

"Ultimately linked to the protection of the patient" is the North Star of every QRM decision. When deciding on risk acceptance, ask yourself: "If the patient and their doctor saw this decision, would they agree with it?"

Note in parentheses (new in R1): it states explicitly that "Risk to quality includes product availability impacts". A shortage is itself a harm to patients, especially for drugs that are the only available therapy (such as certain anticancer drugs).

Second principle in depth: the commensurate principle

This is one of the most important new contributions of R1. The word "commensurate" means:

  • High risk → high effort: media fill for a sterile injectable requires a comprehensive FMEA, complete documentation and a multidisciplinary team
  • Low risk → low effort: changing a non-critical office supplies vendor may need only a brief risk assessment record, not a formal QRM procedure
  • Medium risk → medium effort: adjusting the operating range of a non-critical process parameter needs a written risk assessment, but not a full multidisciplinary review committee

Being "commensurate" applies in three dimensions: Effort + Formality + Documentation. All three should be tied to the risk level.

Analogy: the commensurate principle is like "choosing tools from a toolbox"

Imagine you are a plumber or electrician. You use a screwdriver to drive screws, not a power drill; but to drill through a concrete wall, a screwdriver will not do. Choosing the wrong tool wastes time and may also damage things.

The same goes for QRM tools:

  • Minor deviation (a missed time entry) → a direct written record + QA manager sign-off, no FMEA needed
  • Batch failure (failed sterility test) → needs full RCA + cross-functional risk review + CAPA + regulatory reporting
  • New facility design → needs full HAZOP + FTA + multiple rounds of risk assessment, taking months

"Commensurate" lets you put the right effort in the right place, instead of using the heaviest procedure for everything, which exhausts resources and leads to important risks being underestimated.

CDMO practice: establishing a risk tiering system

Based on the commensurate principle, a CDMO should establish a formal risk tiering system that divides all situations needing QRM into three levels:

  • Tier 1 (high risk): sterile batch failure, patient harm reports, OOS investigations, first commercial batch of a new product → full QRM procedure, multidisciplinary team, formal documentation, possible notification to the client or regulator
  • Tier 2 (medium risk): adjustment of non-critical process parameters, supplier changes, minor equipment modifications → written risk assessment, QA review, update of related SOPs
  • Tier 3 (low risk): routine equipment maintenance, purchase of non-process office supplies → brief record, operator confirmation is enough

This tiering system is itself a best practice for the second principle of Q9(R1) (commensurate), and demonstrates a systematic QRM culture during client audits and regulatory inspections.

Practice Questions

  1. A manufacturer's only antihypertensive drug (no alternative on the market) faces production stoppage because of an API supplier quality problem. Based on the first principle of Q9(R1), how would you argue that this supply chain risk is a "patient protection" issue? What is the position of regulators (such as FDA) in this scenario?
  2. Your QA manager requires a full FMEA for every change (including changing the supplier of office printer paper). From the perspective of the second principle (commensurate), how would you persuade the manager to revise this requirement? What alternative would you propose?
  3. If a QRM risk assessment concludes "the risk is acceptable and no control measures are needed", but this practice is not permitted under current GMP regulations, is it right to do so? Cite the Q9(R1) text to support your answer.

Section Summary

  • Definitional basis of QRM: risk = probability × severity; but subjectivity makes consensus hard to reach, so Q9(R1) places particular emphasis on managing subjectivity (see Section 5.3)
  • One of the biggest highlights of R1: the scope of "patient protection" now extends to product availability risk. Supply chain shortage = patient harm, which raises the supply chain risk management responsibilities of manufacturers and CDMOs
  • Proactive QRM: identify and control potential risks from the development stage; do not wait for a problem before doing a risk assessment, but let good design reduce problems at the source
  • Digitalization as a double-edged sword: new technologies reduce some risks but introduce new ones; the design, validation and transfer of AI, continuous manufacturing and computerized systems should be subject to QRM
  • First principle: risk evaluation is based on scientific knowledge and ultimately linked to patient protection (including availability)
  • Second principle (commensurate): the level of effort, formality and documentation should be commensurate with the level of risk. This is the core new contribution of R1 and the key to using limited QA resources efficiently
  • The boundary of QRM: QRM supports compliance but cannot be used to rationalize non-compliant practices; "I did a risk assessment" is not a shield for violating regulations

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

  • "Unacceptable" is not only about regulatory non-compliance: even if a risk breaches no regulation, if it exceeds the organization's own internal acceptance criteria, you must return to the risk assessment step. This is a self-imposed requirement based on science and business standards, not merely a regulatory floor.
  • A non-linear process: a decision to "go back a step" can be made at any node. QRM is an iterative cycle, not a one-way straight line.
  • "Other models could be used": Q9(R1) does not mandate a specific tool; organizations may choose FMEA, FTA, HACCP and others according to the nature of the risk (see Section 5).
4.1 Responsibilities

Original Text

4.1. Responsibilities

Quality risk management activities are usually, but not always, undertaken by interdisciplinary teams. When teams are formed, they should include experts from the appropriate areas (e.g., quality unit, product development, business development, engineering, regulatory affairs, production operations, sales and marketing, supply chain, legal, statistics and clinical) in addition to individuals who are knowledgeable about the quality risk management process.

Decision makers should

  • take responsibility for coordinating quality risk management across various functions and departments of their organization;
  • assure that a quality risk management process is defined, deployed and reviewed and that adequate resources and knowledge are available; and
  • assure that subjectivity in quality risk management activities is managed and minimized, to facilitate scientifically robust risk-based decision-making.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • Interdisciplinary teams: QRM is not a one-man show for the QA department. The functions listed in the original text include Quality, R&D, Business Development, Engineering, Regulatory, Production, Sales, Supply Chain, Legal, Statistics and Clinical, which is almost the whole company. People with different perspectives will read the same risk very differently, and that is exactly why a cross-functional team is needed.
  • The three responsibilities of decision makers: (1) coordinate QRM across functions, (2) ensure that the process is defined, deployed and reviewed, and (3) manage and minimize subjectivity (new in Q9(R1)). The third point is an important addition made by the R1 revision.

New in Q9(R1): Managing Subjectivity

The original Q9 did not explicitly discuss subjectivity. The R1 revision adds a requirement in 4.1 that decision makers must "assure that subjectivity... is managed and minimized".

Why does it matter? Risk scores (such as RPN) depend heavily on the scorer's subjective judgment. Different people may apply very different standards when deciding whether severity is a 7 or an 8. R1 expects organizations to establish mechanisms to manage this subjectivity, for example:

  • Establish uniform scoring criteria and definitions (specific criteria that define "high / medium / low")
  • Use multiple scorers and take the average or a consensus
  • Support scores with objective evidence such as historical data and literature
  • Subject the scoring results to peer review

Practical Application in a CDMO

In a CDMO environment, assembling a cross-functional QRM team is especially complex, because it must bring together client representatives (Client QA / Regulatory) and CDMO experts (process engineers, analytical chemists, regulatory affairs specialists).

  • Technology transfer QRM: when starting a new client project, set up a cross-functional QRM team immediately to identify technology transfer risks (process parameter differences, equipment fit, analytical method validation).
  • Example of managing subjectivity: a CDMO can set up a "Risk Scoring Matrix" worksheet that requires every scorer to score each risk item independently first, and then hold a discussion meeting to resolve differences. This meets the Q9(R1) requirement to "manage subjectivity".
4.2 Initiating a QRM Process

Original Text

4.2. Initiating a Quality Risk Management Process

Quality risk management should include systematic processes designed to coordinate, facilitate and improve science-based decision-making with respect to risk. Possible steps used to initiate and plan a quality risk management process might include the following:

  • Define the problem and/or risk question, including pertinent assumptions identifying the potential for risk;
  • Assemble background information and/or data on the potential hazard, harm or human health impact relevant to the risk assessment;
  • Identify a leader and necessary resources;
  • Specify a timeline, deliverables and appropriate level of decision-making for the risk management process.

Tutorial Commentary

CDMO Case: Client Changes API Supplier

When a client tells the CDMO "we are changing our API supplier", the CDMO should immediately initiate a QRM process:

  • Risk Question: "Can the new supplier's API (batches ABC-001 to ABC-005) be used under the current process conditions to produce a finished product that meets all CQA specifications?"
  • Background information: the new API's CoA (impurity profile, particle size distribution, polymorphic form), comparative data against the old API, and the design space parameters of the current process
  • Responsible persons: process engineer (lead) + analytical chemist + QA + client RA representative
  • Timeline: initial Risk Assessment completed within 4 weeks; decision-making level: CDMO Site Head + client VP of Quality
4.3 Risk Assessment (the Most Important Section)

Original Text

4.3. Risk Assessment

Risk assessment consists of the identification of hazards and the analysis and evaluation of risks associated with exposure to those hazards (as defined below). Quality risk assessments begin with a well-defined problem description or risk question. When the risk in question is well defined, an appropriate risk management tool (see examples in section 5) and the types of information needed to address the risk question will be more readily identifiable. As an aid to clearly defining the risk(s) for risk assessment purposes, three fundamental questions are often helpful:

  1. What might go wrong?
  2. What is the likelihood (probability) it will go wrong?
  3. What are the consequences (severity)?

Hazard identification is a systematic use of information to identify hazards referring to the risk question or problem description. Information can include historical data, theoretical analysis, informed opinions, and the concerns of stakeholders. Hazard identification addresses the "What might go wrong?" question, including identifying the possible consequences. This provides the basis for further steps in the quality risk management process.

Risk analysis is the estimation of the risk associated with the identified hazards. It is the qualitative or quantitative process of linking the likelihood of occurrence and severity of harms. In some risk management tools, the ability to detect the harm (detectability) also factors in the estimation of risk.

Risk evaluation compares the identified and analyzed risk against given risk criteria. Risk evaluations consider the strength of evidence for all three of the fundamental questions.

"In doing an effective risk assessment, the robustness of the data set is important because it determines the quality of the output. Revealing assumptions and reasonable sources of uncertainty will enhance confidence in this output and/or help identify its limitations."

Uncertainty is due to a combination of incomplete knowledge about a process and its expected or unexpected variability. Typical sources of uncertainty include gaps in knowledge, gaps in pharmaceutical science and process understanding, sources of harm (e.g., failure modes of a process, sources of variability), and probability of detection of problems.

The output of a risk assessment is either a quantitative estimate of risk or a qualitative description of a range of risk. When risk is expressed quantitatively, a numerical probability is used. Alternatively, risk can be expressed using qualitative descriptors, such as "high", "medium", or "low", which should be defined in as much detail as possible. Sometimes a "risk score" is used to further define descriptors in risk ranking. In quantitative risk assessments, a risk estimate provides the likelihood of a specific consequence, given a set of risk-generating circumstances. Thus, quantitative risk estimation is useful for one particular consequence at a time. Alternatively, some risk management tools use a relative risk measure to combine multiple levels of severity and probability into an overall estimate of relative risk.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: The Doctor's Three-Step Diagnosis

Risk assessment is like a doctor diagnosing a disease, in three steps:

  • Hazard Identification = taking the history / identifying symptoms: Where does it hurt? What might the problem be? (What might go wrong?) When the doctor asks "How long have you had the headache? Do you have a fever?", that is hazard identification.
  • Risk Analysis = assessing how serious it is: How severe is the symptom? How often does it occur? (Probability × Severity) "Is the headache occasional or constant? Mild or severe?" is risk analysis.
  • Risk Evaluation = deciding whether treatment is needed: compare against health standards and decide whether intervention is needed. (Is treatment needed?) When the doctor decides "this needs immediate hospitalization vs. observe for a few days first", that is Risk Evaluation.

Without correct "symptom identification" (hazard identification), the later diagnosis (analysis) and treatment decision (evaluation) will go astray.

Managing Uncertainty

Q9(R1) treats "uncertainty" as one of the core themes of the R1 revision. Its sources include:

  • Knowledge gaps: insufficient understanding of process mechanisms (for example, a new process with no historical data)
  • Pharmaceutical science gaps: for example, the compatibility of a new excipient has not been fully studied
  • Failure modes: failure modes that may occur in the process but have not been identified
  • Probability of detection: some hazards are difficult to detect with existing analytical methods

Practical advice: in an FMEA worksheet, clearly state the "confidence level" or "data source" for each score. If a score relies mainly on "informed opinion" rather than measured data, flag it as "high uncertainty" in the Risk Assessment Report and recommend verification experiments.

Risk Quantification Formula and Output Types

Quantitative method (typical FMEA formula):

RPN = Severity (S) × Probability (P) × Detectability (D)

S: 1-10 (1 = no impact, 10 = most severe)
P: 1-10 (1 = almost impossible, 10 = almost certain)
D: 1-10 (1 = almost certain to be detected, 10 = almost impossible to detect)
RPN range: 1 to 1000

Note: a higher D value means harder to detect (the opposite of S/P)
Common threshold: RPN ≥ 100 → action required

4.4 Risk Control

Original Text

4.4. Risk Control

Risk control includes decision-making to reduce and/or accept risks. The purpose of risk control is to reduce the risk to an acceptable level. The amount of effort used for risk control should be proportional to the significance of the risk. Decision makers might use different processes, including benefit-cost analysis, for understanding the optimal level of risk control.

Risk control might focus on the following questions:

  1. Is the risk above an acceptable level?
  2. What can be done to reduce or eliminate risks?
  3. What is the appropriate balance among benefits, risks and resources?
  4. Are new risks introduced as a result of the identified risks being controlled?

Risk reduction focuses on processes for mitigation or avoidance of quality risk when it exceeds a specified (acceptable) level (see Fig. 1). Risk reduction might include actions taken to mitigate the severity and probability of harm. Processes that improve the detectability of hazards and quality risks might also be used as part of a risk control strategy. The implementation of risk reduction measures can introduce new risks into the system or increase the significance of other existing risks. Hence, it might be appropriate to revisit the risk assessment to identify and evaluate any possible change in risk after implementing a risk reduction process.

Risk acceptance is a decision to accept risk. For some types of harms, even the best quality risk management practices might not entirely eliminate risk. In these circumstances, it might be agreed that an appropriate quality risk management strategy has been applied and that quality risk is reduced to a specified (acceptable) level. This (specified) acceptable level will depend on many parameters and should be decided on a case-by-case basis.

Tutorial Commentary

The Risk Compensation Paradox

"Reducing a risk may introduce a new risk" is the most important and most easily overlooked concept in 4.4. A few real-life analogies:

  • Aseptic process example: to reduce the risk of microbial contamination, a UV irradiation step is introduced → new risk: UV degrades the API and affects potency (increased risk of chemical degradation)
  • Filtration example: an additional sterilizing filtration step is added to reduce bioburden → new risk: filter compatibility problems, and extractables contaminating the drug solution
  • Automation example: an automated filling system is introduced to reduce the risk of human intervention → new risk: more equipment failure modes and greater computerized system validation (CSV) needs

Conclusion: whenever a risk reduction measure is implemented, the risk assessment must be revisited to evaluate whether a new risk has been introduced or other existing risks have been amplified. This forms the "inner loop" of QRM.

Choosing Between Risk Reduction and Risk Acceptance

Risk control has two exits: Risk Reduction or Risk Acceptance.

Accepting a risk does not mean "giving up on managing it". It is a conscious, documented decision: "We have fully assessed this risk and consider it acceptable at present; the following is our rationale..."

By contrast, an undocumented "acceptance" (that is, doing nothing) is a GMP non-conformance, whereas a well-documented Risk Acceptance is a fully compliant QRM output.

4.5 Risk Communication

4.5. Risk Communication

Risk communication is the sharing of information about risk and risk management between the decision makers and others. Parties can communicate at any stage of the risk management process (see Fig. 1: dashed arrows). The output/result of the quality risk management process should be appropriately communicated and documented (see Fig. 1: solid arrows).

Key Concepts

  • The meaning of the dashed arrows: in the Figure 1 flowchart, risk communication is shown with "dashed lines" rather than solid lines, meaning it can occur at any stage and not only at specific nodes. This differs from the "solid lines" of a one-way flow.

"Communicated and documented": Q9(R1) emphasizes that QRM outputs must be recorded.

4.6 Risk Review

Original Text

4.6. Risk Review

Risk management should be an ongoing part of the quality management process. A mechanism to review or monitor events should be implemented.

The output/results of the risk management process should be reviewed to take into account new knowledge and experience. Once a quality risk management process has been initiated, that process should continue to be utilized for events that might impact the original quality risk management decision, whether these events are planned (e.g., results of product review, inspections, audits, change control) or unplanned (e.g., root cause from failure investigations, recall). Tutorial Commentary

Review Frequency Is Tied to Risk Level

"The frequency of any review should be based upon the level of risk": this is a proportionality principle:

  • High risk: for example, critical control points in aseptic processing and sterilization parameters → review every quarter or every batch
  • Medium risk: for example, excipient supplier qualification → review every six months or annually
  • Low risk: for example, outer carton packaging specifications → review every 3 years or upon change

This principle lets an organization focus resources on what really matters, rather than applying the same review frequency to all QRM activities.

CDMO Practice: Embedding Risk Review in the APR Process

A CDMO should explicitly require, in its annual product review (APR/PQR) SOP, a review of "all QRM documents related to the product":

  • Confirm whether any new deviations, OOS results or complaints in the past year triggered a need to update the QRM
  • Review existing Risk Acceptance decisions to see whether they remain valid (what batch data has been added? Are there any new regulatory developments?)
  • If a client's product undergoes a Change Control for process or specification, it should automatically trigger a re-evaluation of the related QRM documents
  • The annual QRM review results should be a separate section of the APR and be submitted to senior management (Management Review)

5. RISK MANAGEMENT METHODOLOGY

Quality risk management supports a scientific and practical approach to decision-making. It provides documented, transparent and reproducible methods to accomplish steps of the quality risk management process based on current knowledge about assessing the probability, severity and sometimes detectability of the hazards, and their associated risks. While detectability may not be a discrete factor in some quality risk management methods, detection controls are important as they can reduce the probability of occurrence of harm.

Traditionally, risks to quality have been assessed and managed in a variety of ways (empirical and/or internal procedures) based on, for example, compilation of observations, trends and other information. Such approaches continue to provide useful information that might support topics such as handling of complaints, quality defects, deviations and allocation of resources.

Additionally, the pharmaceutical industry and regulators can assess and manage risk using recognized risk management tools and/or internal procedures (e.g., standard operating procedures). Below is a non-exhaustive list of some of these tools (further details in Annex 1 and section 8):

  • Basic risk management facilitation methods (flowcharts, check sheets etc.);
  • Failure Mode Effects Analysis (FMEA);
  • Failure Mode, Effects and Criticality Analysis (FMECA);
  • Fault Tree Analysis (FTA);
  • Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP);
  • Hazard Operability Analysis (HAZOP);
  • Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PHA);
  • Risk ranking and filtering;
  • Supporting statistical tools.

It might be appropriate to adapt these tools for use in specific areas pertaining to drug substance and drug (medicinal) product quality. Quality risk management methods and the supporting statistical tools can be used in combination (e.g., Probabilistic Risk Assessment). Combined use provides flexibility that can facilitate the application of quality risk management principles.

"The degree of rigor and formality of quality risk management should reflect available knowledge and be commensurate with the level of uncertainty, importance and complexity of the issue to be addressed."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • The three core attributes of QRM: (1) Documented, (2) Transparent, (3) Reproducible. These three attributes are what fundamentally separate QRM from decisions made purely on intuition.
  • The standing of traditional approaches: Q9(R1) explicitly affirms that traditional empirical approaches (observation, trend analysis) still have value, especially in routine work such as complaint handling, quality defects, and deviation management. Traditional approaches are not wrong; in high-risk situations they simply need to be supplemented with something more systematic.
  • Flexibility in tool selection: No single tool is universal. The guideline specifically notes that several tools can be used in combination, for example screening with PHA first and then going deeper with FMEA. This tiered strategy is very practical in complex manufacturing sites.

Practical Application in a CDMO

5.1 Formality in Quality Risk Management NEW in R1

Original Text

5.1 Formality in Quality Risk Management

Formality in quality risk management is not a binary concept (i.e. formal/informal); varying degrees of formality may be applied during quality risk management activities, including when making risk-based decisions. In this way, formality can be considered a continuum (or spectrum), ranging from low to high.

When determining how much formality to apply to a given quality risk management activity, certain factors may be considered. These may include, for example, the following:

  • Uncertainty: The term "uncertainty" in quality risk management means lack of knowledge about hazards, harms and, consequently, their associated risks. The level of uncertainty that is associated with the area being risk assessed informs how much formality may be required to manage potential risks. Systematic approaches for acquiring, analysing, storing and disseminating scientific information are essential for generating knowledge, which in turn informs all quality risk management activities. Uncertainty may be reduced via effective knowledge management, which enables accumulated and new information (both internal and external) to be used to support risk-based decisions throughout the product lifecycle.
  • Importance: The more important a risk-based decision may be in relation to product quality, the higher the level of formality that should be applied, and the greater the need to reduce the level of uncertainty associated with it.
  • Complexity: The more complex a process or subject area is to a quality risk management activity, the higher the level of formality that should be applied to assure product quality.

Higher levels of uncertainty, importance or complexity may require more formal quality risk management approaches to manage potential risks and to support effective risk-based decision-making.

The overall approach for determining how much formality to apply during quality risk management activities should be described within the quality system. Resource constraints should not be used to justify the use of lower levels of formality in the quality risk management process. Risk scores, ratings and assessments should be based on an appropriate use of evidence, science and knowledge. Regardless of how much formality is applied, the robust management of risk is the goal of the process.

The following may be characteristics of higher levels of formality:

  • All parts of the quality risk management process (risk assessment, risk control, risk review and risk communication) are explicitly performed, and stand-alone quality risk management reports or related documents which address all aspects of the process may be generated and are documented (e.g., within the quality system).
  • Quality risk management tools, including those shown in Annex 1, are used in some or all parts of the process.
  • A cross-functional team is assembled for the quality risk management activity.
  • Use of a facilitator, with experience and knowledge of the quality risk management process, may be integral to a higher formality process.

The following may be characteristics of lower levels of formality:

  • One or more parts of the quality risk management process are not performed as stand-alone activities but are addressed within other elements of the quality system which may have risk assessment and risk control activities embedded within them.
  • Quality risk management tools might not be used in some or all parts of the process.
  • A cross-functional team might not be necessary.
  • Stand-alone quality risk management reports might not be generated. The outcome of the quality risk management process is usually documented in the relevant parts of the quality system.
"Note: As indicated above, degrees of formality between the above higher and lower levels also exist and may be used."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Formality Spectrum

This is the most important breakthrough concept in Q9(R1). The 2005 edition of Q9 left industry in a dilemma: "Do we need to write a formal risk assessment report this time?" The answer from Q9(R1) is: it is not either/or; the task is to find the appropriate level.

The three factors that determine the level of formality can be remembered as U-I-C:

  • U = Uncertainty: the less is known about the hazard, the higher the formality
  • I = Importance: the greater the impact of the decision, the higher the formality
  • C = Complexity: the more complex the subject, the higher the formality

Analogy: Hospital Emergency Triage

Choosing the level of formality is like triage in an emergency room:

  • A small cut (low formality): the nurse makes a quick assessment, disinfects and bandages the wound, and notes it in the nursing record. There is no need to call in a surgeon or write a five-page assessment report.
  • A suspected fracture (medium formality): an X-ray is needed to confirm, a physician evaluates, and the rationale for the treatment is written in the medical record.
  • A potential outbreak of infectious disease (high formality): infectious disease specialists, epidemiology and the hospital infection control committee are convened, formal isolation procedures are started, everything is documented throughout, and the authorities are notified.

No one would handle an infectious disease outbreak like a minor cut just because "we are short-staffed". This is the spirit of the statement that "resource constraints must not be a reason to reduce formality".

Key Note: The Most Important Sentence

"Resource constraints should not be used to justify the use of lower levels of formality."

This is one of the most forcefully worded provisions in Q9(R1). Its regulatory implication is very clear: if an inspection finds that a company skipped formal QRM steps because of "insufficient staff" or "time pressure", this would constitute a direct finding.

Practical recommendation: the organization should describe in its quality system (SOPs) exactly how the level of formality of each QRM activity is decided, and require that this decision and its rationale be recorded at the start of the QRM document.

Practical Application in a CDMO

Implementing the formality decision in an SOP: we recommend creating a "QRM formality assessment matrix". Before any QRM activity starts, the responsible person scores the three U-I-C dimensions (for example 1-3 points each) and uses the total score to decide which level of formality applies. Filing this matrix, together with the rationale for the decision, in the QRM document package lets you answer auditor questions.

  • Total U + I + C of 3-4: low formality; embedding the assessment in existing system documents is sufficient
  • Total U + I + C of 5-7: medium formality; partly standalone documents, with cross-department involvement as needed
  • Total U + I + C of 8-9: high formality; a full QRM report, a cross-functional team and a facilitator

Formality Spectrum

Note: the grey zone in the middle is the norm, and cases cannot always be classified cleanly. The key is to record the rationale for why this level of formality was chosen.

5.2 Risk-Based Decision-Making NEW in R1

Original Text

5.2 Risk-Based Decision-Making

Risk-based decision-making is inherent in all quality risk management activities; it provides an essential foundation for decision makers in an organization. Effective risk-based decision-making begins with determining the level of effort, formality and documentation that should be applied during the quality risk management process. The decisions made from quality risk management activities include those in relation to what hazards exist, the risks associated with those hazards, the risk controls required, the acceptability of the residual risk after risk controls, and also the communication and review of quality risk management activities and outputs.

As all decision-making relies on the use of knowledge, see ICH Q10 for guidance in relation to knowledge management. It is important also to ensure the integrity of the data that are used for risk-based decision-making.

Approaches to risk-based decision-making:

There are different processes that may be used to make risk-based decisions; these are directly related to the level of formality that is applied during the quality risk management process. (See Section 5.1 above for guidance on what constitutes formality in quality risk management.)

Higher levels of formality in quality risk management may require higher levels of structure in relation to risk-based decision-making. There can be varying degrees of structure with regard to approaches for risk-based decision-making. These degrees of structure can be considered to be on a continuum (or spectrum). Below are descriptions of highly structured vs. less structured processes, and for rule-based processes when making risk-based decisions:

  • Highly structured: Some risk-based decision-making processes are highly structured and can involve a formal analysis of the available options that exist before making a decision. They involve an in-depth consideration of relevant factors associated with the available options. Such processes might be used when there is a high degree of importance associated with the decision, and when the level of uncertainty and/or complexity is high.
  • Less structured: Other risk-based decision-making processes are less structured; here, simpler approaches are used to arrive at decisions, and they primarily make use of existing knowledge to support an assessment of hazards, risks and any required risk controls. Such processes might still be used when there is a high degree of importance associated with the decision, but the degree of uncertainty and/or complexity is lower.
  • Rule-based: Decisions might also be made using rule-based (or standardized) approaches, which do not require a new risk assessment to make such decisions. This is where there are SOPs, policies or well understood requirements in place which determine what decisions must be made. Here, rules (or limits) may be in place which govern such decisions; these may be based on a previously obtained understanding of the relevant risks and they usually lead to predetermined actions and/or expected outcomes.

The above approaches to risk-based decision-making are beneficial because they address uncertainty through the use of knowledge, facilitating informed decisions by regulators and the pharmaceutical industry in a multitude of areas. They also help recognize where uncertainty remains, so that appropriate risk controls (including improved detection) may be identified to enhance understanding of those variables and further reduce the level of uncertainty.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Essence of RBDM

Q9(R1) states clearly: RBDM (Risk-Based Decision-Making) is not a special activity; it is a fundamental way of thinking embedded in all QRM activities. Every time you ask "What level of assessment do we need?", "Is this risk acceptable?" or "What controls are needed?", you are doing RBDM.

Effective RBDM begins with deciding:

  • How much effort to invest
  • Which level of formality to apply
  • What depth of documentation is needed

Analogy: Institutional Memory

The importance of documenting RBDM lies in preserving institutional memory. Imagine this scenario:

Five years ago, the QA team evaluated an abnormal particle size distribution and decided to accept the batch of raw material because they had sound scientific justification. Five years later, the original team has left, and new QA staff facing a similar situation have no idea why that decision was made. They can only repeat the assessment from scratch, wasting a great deal of time, and may even reach a different conclusion, creating inconsistency.

If the original decision had a complete RBDM record (rationale, data, assumptions), this problem would not occur. This is the core value of the decision documentation that Q9(R1) emphasizes: it lets future reviewers (whether internal auditors, regulatory inspectors, or colleagues five years from now) understand the context of the original decision.

Key Note: Link to ICH Q10

Here Q9(R1) directly refers to the Knowledge Management section of ICH Q10 (Pharmaceutical Quality System), stressing that all decisions depend on knowledge and that knowledge needs to be managed systematically.

This cross-reference is very important: when building an RBDM framework, an organization needs to build sound knowledge management mechanisms at the same time, including:

  • Systematic storage and searchability of historical batch data
  • Archiving and indexing of past QRM decisions
  • Tracking of scientific literature and regulatory updates
  • Knowledge transfer procedures when employees leave

In addition, data integrity is the foundation of RBDM. If the input data cannot be trusted, any decision loses its support.

Practical Application in a CDMO

Building an RBDM decision record template: to preserve institutional memory, we recommend that a CDMO include the following standard fields in every important QRM document:

  • Decision Question: state specifically what question this QRM exercise is trying to answer
  • Rationale: which data, knowledge and assumptions were used, and what conclusion was reached
  • Outcome: what action was decided and what residual risk was accepted
  • Review Trigger: which events require this decision to be revisited
  • Decision Makers: the people who took part in the decision and their roles, so that they can be consulted in the future

This standardized format ensures that RBDM documents can be "understood by your future self and by others": transparent, supported by rationale, traceable, and revisable.

5.3 Managing and Minimizing Subjectivity NEW in R1

Original Text

5.3 Managing and Minimizing Subjectivity

Subjectivity can impact every stage of a quality risk management process, especially the identification of hazards and the estimation of probability of occurrence and severity of harm. It can also impact the estimation of risk reduction and the effectiveness of decisions made from quality risk management activities.

Subjectivity can be introduced in quality risk management through differences in how risks are assessed and in how hazards, harms and risks are perceived by different stakeholders, (e.g., bias). Subjectivity can also be introduced when risk questions are inadequately defined, and when tools have poorly designed risk scoring scales.

While subjectivity cannot be completely eliminated from quality risk management activities, it may be controlled by addressing bias and assumptions, the proper use of quality risk management tools and maximizing the use of relevant data and sources of knowledge (see ICH Q10, Section 1.6.1).

All participants involved with quality risk management activities should acknowledge, anticipate, and address the potential for subjectivity.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Nature of Subjectivity

For the first time, Q9(R1) addresses in its main text an issue the industry has long avoided: subjectivity in risk assessment cannot be eliminated, but it can be managed.

Sources of subjectivity include:

  • Differences between people: assessors with different backgrounds perceive the severity of the same hazard differently (the perspectives of a process engineer vs. QA vs. a clinician)
  • Framing bias: how a risk question is asked affects the direction of the answer, for example "Will this step cause contamination?" vs. "How do we make sure this step does not cause contamination?"
  • Poorly designed scoring scales: if the severity/occurrence/detectability scales in an FMEA are not clearly defined, different assessors may give widely different scores
  • Confirmation bias: the tendency to look for evidence that supports an existing view
  • Groupthink: group pressure suppresses minority opinions and diverse viewpoints are lost
  • Anchoring bias: over-reliance on the first number or opinion encountered

Analogy: Blind Tasting

Imagine a professional wine tasting. If the judges know beforehand which bottle comes from a famous estate and which is a cheap wine, their scores will inevitably be influenced. This is subjectivity (label bias).

The solution is blind tasting: hide the labels so judges score only on the qualities of the wine itself. Many famous tastings (such as the 1976 Judgment of Paris) used exactly this method to break through prejudice.

In QRM, similar strategies include:

  • Not disclosing other members' scores before scoring (to avoid the anchoring effect)
  • Having members from different departments score independently first, then consolidating and discussing (Delphi method)
  • Guiding scoring with pre-designed scales rather than free-form assessment
  • Using a facilitator to prevent senior staff from "setting the direction"

Key Note: Three Main Control Approaches

The strategies Q9(R1) proposes for controlling subjectivity can be organized at three levels:

  • 1. Address Bias and Assumptions: before QRM begins, have all participants state their assumptions and possible sources of bias explicitly, for example "I assume the OOS rate for this step is the same as last year", so that implicit assumptions become explicit statements that can be challenged.
  • 2. Proper Use of QRM Tools: tools must be used as designed, and scores must not be "back-filled" to obtain a desired result. Scale definitions must be clear and scoring criteria consistent.
  • 3. Maximizing Use of Relevant Data: replace subjective impressions with objective data. "I feel this equipment is very stable" is weaker than "This equipment's OEE over the past three years was 98.5%, with no unplanned downtime".

Practical Application in a CDMO: Designing a Structured QRM Meeting

To minimize subjectivity in QRM meetings, we recommend that a CDMO adopt the following structured procedure:

  • Step 1: Assemble a diverse team: ensure representation across departments (process, QA, engineering, regulatory, client side) to avoid the blind spots of a homogeneous team. Include members of different seniority so that newcomers' perspectives are heard.
  • Step 2: Appoint a facilitator: the facilitator does not score and is responsible only for controlling the process. The facilitator should stop senior managers from stating their positions too early (so as not to influence other members).
  • Step 3: Independent scoring first: before the discussion, each member fills in scores independently on an individual score sheet; the scores are then consolidated and compared for differences. Items with large differences get focused discussion (these are often the most valuable findings).
  • Step 4: Clear scale definitions: before FMEA or any other tool begins, ensure that all assessors share a common understanding of what each score means (for example, what specific situation "severity = 5" represents).
  • Step 5: Record dissenting opinions: even if the final consensus is to accept a risk, the minority opinion and its rationale should be recorded. This protects the organization's critical thinking and leaves a trail for future review.

  1. In an FMEA meeting, a company's QA head volunteers: "I have done this step for 15 years; the severity is definitely 1", and the other members immediately agree. Which subjectivity problems described in Section 5.3 appear in this scenario? If you were the facilitator, how would you handle it?
  2. Under Section 5.1, a CDMO is carrying out a risk assessment for a client on "changing the antifoam supplier". How should the Uncertainty, Importance and Complexity of this activity be assessed, and how should the appropriate level of formality be decided on that basis?

  • The standing of Section 5: the most important revisions in Q9(R1) are concentrated in this section. The three new subsections 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3 represent a major upgrade in ICH's guidance on QRM practice, evolving from "providing a list of tools" to "guiding how to think about the depth and breadth of QRM".

  • Packaging and labeling;
  • Supply chain control.

Examples for regulatory operations (see Annex II):

  • Inspection and assessment activities.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • QRM does not replace regulatory compliance obligations: the original text states explicitly that "appropriate use of quality risk management does not obviate industry's obligation to comply with regulatory requirements". QRM is a supporting tool, not an exemption tool. You cannot perform a risk assessment and then say, "I know about this deviation, but I accept it, so no investigation is needed."
  • The impact of QRM on regulatory oversight: effective QRM can influence "the extent and level of direct regulatory oversight". In other words, if your QRM system gives inspectors confidence in your ability to manage yourself, they may reduce the frequency or depth of inspections. This is an important institutional benefit of QRM.
  • A common language creates mutual trust: when industry and regulators communicate in the same QRM language (such as FMEA and risk matrices), they can build mutual confidence, which supports globally consistent regulatory decisions.

Analogy

Think of QRM integration as a fire protection system. A building has sprinklers, smoke detectors and evacuation drills (the QRM tools). These give the fire department (the regulator) confidence in the building. This does not mean the building may ignore building codes (GMP obligations), but during routine inspections the fire department may allocate more resources to buildings without a sound system.

Moreover, if every building in the city uses the same standard fire protection system (a common QRM language), the fire department can also coordinate across districts more efficiently. This is what "mutual confidence among regulators" means.

Key Notes

  • Two-way training matters: the original text stresses that both industry AND regulatory personnel need QRM training. This means FDA/EMA inspectors themselves are expected to apply risk-based thinking in inspection decisions, rather than checking purely clause by clause.
  • QRM has a very broad scope: from development to supply chain control, it covers almost all pharmaceutical operations. Inspectors can also use QRM to decide the depth and scope of an inspection (risk-based inspection).
  • Regulation remains a regional decision: even with a common QRM language, regulators in each region retain their own decision-making authority. This is a political reality, but QRM promotes consistency at the technical level.
Section 6.1: Product Availability Risks (NEW in R1)

Original Text

6.1 The role of Quality Risk Management in Addressing Product Availability Risks Arising from Quality/Manufacturing Issues

Quality/manufacturing issues, including non-compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP), are a significant cause of product availability issues (e.g., product shortages). The interests of patients are served by risk-based drug shortage prevention and mitigation activities that help to proactively manage supply chain complexities and ensure availability of needed drug (medicinal) products.

While manufacturing and supply chain diversity can be enablers of product availability, increasingly complex supply chains lead to interdependencies that can introduce systemic quality/manufacturing risks impacting supply chain robustness. The application of quality risk management enables the proactive identification and implementation of preventive measures that support product availability.

An effective pharmaceutical quality system drives both supply chain robustness and sustainable GMP compliance. The pharmaceutical quality system, including management responsibilities, also uses quality risk management and knowledge management to provide an early warning system that supports effective oversight and response to evolving quality/manufacturing risks from the pharmaceutical company or its external partners. When risk-based drug shortage prevention and mitigation activities are performed, the level of formality that is applied to those activities may vary (see Section 5.1) and should be commensurate with the level of risk associated with a loss of availability of the product(s).

Quality/manufacturing factors that can affect supply reliability, and hence product availability, include, but are not limited to, the following:

a) Manufacturing Process Variability and State of Control:
Processes that exhibit excessive variability (e.g., process drift, non-uniformity) have capability gaps that can result in unpredictable outputs (e.g., quality, timeliness and yield) and consequently can adversely impact product availability. Quality risk management can help design monitoring systems that are capable of detecting departures from a state of control and deficiencies in manufacturing processes, so they can be investigated to address root causes.

b) Manufacturing Facilities and Equipment:
A robust facility infrastructure can facilitate reliable supply; it includes suitable equipment and well-designed facilities for manufacturing (including packaging and testing). Robustness can be affected by multiple factors, such as an aging facility, insufficient maintenance or an operational design that is vulnerable to human error. Risks to supply can be reduced by addressing these factors, as well as through the use of modern technology, such as digitalization, automation, isolation technology, amongst others.

c) Oversight of Outsourced Activities and Suppliers:
Quality system governance includes assuring the acceptability of supply chain partners over the product lifecycle. Approval and oversight of outsourced activities and material suppliers is informed by risk assessments, effective knowledge management, and an effective monitoring strategy for supply chain partner performance. A successful manufacturing partnership is strengthened by appropriate communication and collaboration mechanisms (See Section 2.7 of ICH Q10). When substantial variability is identified in the quality and safety of supplied materials or in the services provided, enhanced review and monitoring activities are justified. In some cases, it may be necessary to identify a new supply chain entity (e.g., a pre-qualified alternative option) to perform a function.

Note that the guidance in Annex II.2, in relation to the application of quality risk management as part of Regulatory Operations, can be useful to consider in the context of product availability risks.

Tutorial Commentary

A major R1 addition: why is this section so important?

Section 6.1 is one of the most important additions in ICH Q9(R1) compared with the original Q9. It changes the thinking framework of QRM:

  • Thinking in the original Q9: QRM is about "quality risk", the risk that a defect harms the patient
  • New thinking in R1: "availability risk" is also a form of patient harm. If the medicine cannot be obtained, patients are harmed just the same
  • Therefore: QRM must consider both kinds of risk and strike a balance between them

This concept came directly from the lessons of drug shortages during the COVID-19 pandemic, and from many years of critical drug supply interruptions caused by GMP problems (for example antibiotics, steroids and chemotherapy drugs).

Core analogy: The Blood Bank Analogy

Imagine a hospital blood bank finds that a batch of type O blood bags had a slight temperature deviation during storage (+1°C above the upper specification limit for 2 hours). At the same time, 3 seriously injured car accident patients in the emergency room need large transfusions, and this is the hospital's only type O blood in stock.

Pure quality-risk thinking (the traditional approach): temperature deviation → quarantine and destroy the blood bags per SOP → quality is assured

Balanced QRM thinking (the spirit of Q9 R1):

  • Quality risk: how much actual harm does +1°C for 2 hours do to blood quality? Does it really exceed the safety threshold?
  • Availability risk: if the blood is destroyed, the 3 patients have no blood available and the risk of death is very high
  • Decision: use the QRM framework to evaluate the relative severity of the two risks, rather than destroying the blood automatically

This analogy maps directly onto the pharmaceutical setting: a critical anticancer drug is found to have a minor labeling deviation, and the drug is currently in global shortage. Should it be recalled immediately?

Analysis of the three factors affecting supply reliability

  • a) Process variability and state of control: an unstable process (such as process drift) affects not only quality but also capacity and delivery time, which is the root of supply risk. QRM should be used to design monitoring systems that detect drift early.
  • b) Facility and equipment: aging equipment, inadequate maintenance and designs with human-factor risks are all precursors of supply interruption. Digitalization, automation and isolator technology are modern means of reducing such risks.
  • c) Outsourced activities and supplier oversight: supply chain complexity brings "systemic risk", where a problem at one supplier can trigger a chain reaction. Knowledge management, an effective monitoring strategy and alternative suppliers are the three elements of supply chain resilience.

Practical Application for CDMOs

The dual role of a CDMO in availability risk management

A CDMO is both the manufacturing partner of its client and a critical node in the supply chain. Section 6.1 has the following direct implications for a CDMO:

  • Contract negotiation: the contract should include a mechanism for assessing supply interruption risk and an agreement on emergency production priority. When a client's product is a "critical / sole-source" drug, the CDMO needs to flag it specifically in the Quality Agreement (QAA) and include it in QRM considerations.
  • Batch defect decisions: if a minor defect is found in a batch, the client must decide whether to release it. The CDMO should help the client carry out a complete QRM exercise: assessing not only the risk of the defect itself but also the risk to patient availability if the batch is withheld. This decision needs to be documented (a documented QRM decision withstands inspection better than a subjective judgment).
  • Supplier qualification management: if a raw material or excipient supplier used by the CDMO is a single source, a supply interruption risk assessment should be performed in the spirit of this section, and a list of backup suppliers should be established.
  • Supply risk from aging equipment: point b) is a direct reminder to CDMOs that aging equipment is not only a GMP issue but also a supply security issue for the client. Equipment replacement plans should be included in QRM and disclosed transparently to the client.

Dual-Risk Decision Framework

When facing the dilemma of "quality defect vs. supply interruption", the recommended QRM framework is as follows:

Quality risk assessment:
  Severity    × Probability = Quality risk score
  → Likelihood that the defect causes patient harm

Availability risk assessment:
  Shortage severity   × Alternative availability = Availability risk score
  → Is the product the sole supply? Do patients have alternative therapies?

Decision matrix:
  High quality risk + low availability risk  → Withhold/destroy (traditional judgment)
  Low quality risk + high availability risk  → Conditional release (requires communication with the regulator)
  High quality risk + high availability risk → Most difficult scenario, regulator involvement in the decision is needed
  Low quality risk + low availability risk   → Handle routinely

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO is manufacturing an immunosuppressant for post-organ-transplant use. Only 2 manufacturers of this drug exist worldwide (one has already suspended production because of GMP problems). In final release testing, an excipient content in this batch is found slightly below the lower specification limit (the measured value is 99.2% of the lower limit). As the QP (Qualified Person), how would you apply the spirit of Section 6.1 to make a decision? What additional assessments are needed?
  2. A CDMO's critical tablet press has been in use for 18 years, and maintenance records show 3 unplanned stoppages in the past 6 months. The client's product is a first-line drug for a common chronic disease with stable market demand. In the spirit of 6.1(b), how should the CDMO present this supply risk to the client, and what actions should it recommend?
  3. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many countries experienced severe shortages of ventilator-related drugs (such as propofol and midazolam). Looking back at these shortages, which fit the risk factors described in 6.1(a), (b) and (c)? If these manufacturers had applied the QRM framework of Section 6.1 early, which shortages might have been preventable?

Tutorial Commentary

Overview of the Definition System

The definitions in Q9(R1) form the linguistic foundation of QRM. Understanding the hierarchy among these definitions is very important for carrying out QRM correctly:

  • Hazard chain: Hazard (the source of harm) → results in Harm when it occurs → the Severity of the harm is the core dimension of assessment
  • Risk formula: Risk = Probability × Severity (sourced from ISO/IEC Guide 51:2014)
  • Assessment levels: Hazard Identification → Risk Analysis → Risk Evaluation → together called Risk Assessment
  • Control actions: Risk Reduction (reducing probability or severity) → Risk Control (implementing management decisions) → Risk Acceptance (an informed decision to accept the residual risk)
  • Oversight cycle: Risk Review (monitoring and updating, taking new knowledge into account)

Important definitions new or revised in R1

  • Detectability: new in R1. In the original Q9, the three FMEA dimensions (severity, occurrence, detectability) were mentioned in the methods and tools but not formally defined in the definitions section. R1 includes it, reflecting the importance of the dimension "can the hazard be found?".
  • Harm: revised in R1 to add "loss of product quality or availability". This is a companion revision to the spirit of Section 6.1. The original definition referred only to damage to health; now "the medicine cannot be obtained" is explicitly included in the scope of harm. This is the most important definition revision in R1.
  • Risk-Based Decision-Making: new in R1, emphasizing that the decision process needs to consider "whether the risk has reached an acceptable level". This echoes the discussion of formality and subjectivity in Section 5.
  • Stakeholder: new in R1, explicitly listing four main groups of stakeholders: patients, healthcare professionals, regulators and industry. This reinforces the patient-centered spirit of QRM.

The far-reaching significance of the revised Harm definition

Harm definition in the original Q9: "Damage to health."

R1 revised version: "Damage to health, including the damage that can occur from loss of product quality or availability."

This revision adds only a few words, but it has profound practical implications:

  • "Loss of product quality" harm: a quality defect leads to insufficient efficacy or adverse reactions
  • "Loss of availability" harm: the drug cannot be obtained, and patients cannot receive treatment

Both kinds of harm now fall within the scope of QRM. This means that when you perform a Risk Assessment, the "severity of loss of availability" must be quantified and compared side by side with the "severity of a quality defect".

In a regulatory inspection, if the inspector asks, "Why did you decide to release this batch with a minor defect?", a documented QRM decision (including an availability assessment) is far stronger than one without documentation.

  1. According to the definitions, what three sub-steps does Risk Assessment include? Give one practical example from sterile injectable manufacturing for each, showing how the three steps are carried out.
  2. "Risk Acceptance" requires an "informed decision". What documents and evidence can demonstrate that a risk acceptance decision is "informed"? During an inspection, which records do inspectors usually look at?
  3. R1 adds a definition of "Detectability". In FMEA, if a failure mode has very low detectability (for example, microbial contamination is difficult to detect in batch release testing), how should it be prioritized? How does this connect to the idea that "when severity cannot be reduced, you can only work on probability and detectability"?

ANNEX I: QUALITY RISK MANAGEMENT METHODS AND TOOLS

The purpose of this annex is to provide a general overview of and references for some of the primary tools that might be used in quality risk management by industry and regulators. The references are included as an aid to gain more knowledge and detail about the particular tool. This is not an exhaustive list. Key Notes

  • There is no universal tool: "no one tool or set of tools is applicable to every situation". This is a reminder that tool selection must be based on judgment of the situation, not on applying a formula.
  • Formality is not mandatory: Q9(R1) states clearly that informal tools (such as flowcharts and check sheets) are also acceptable. For a small CDMO or an early development stage, a full FMEA is not always necessary.
  • Three dimensions of tool selection: (1) timing (early development vs. post-commercialization); (2) completeness of information (little information: use PHA; more information: use FMEA/HACCP); (3) direction of the question (finding causes: use FTA; finding preventive measures: use FMEA/HACCP).
I.1 Basic Risk Management Facilitation Methods

Original Text

I.1 Basic Risk Management Facilitation Methods

Some of the simple techniques that are commonly used to structure risk management by organizing data and facilitating decision-making are:

  • Flowcharts;
  • Check Sheets;
  • Process Mapping;
  • Cause and Effect Diagrams (also called an Ishikawa diagram or fish bone diagram).

Tutorial Commentary

The Four Basic Tools Explained

  • Flowchart: visualizes process steps and decision points. A sterile filling flowchart covers "raw material receipt -> compounding -> filtration -> filling -> lyophilization -> sealing -> visual inspection -> release", and each step becomes a unit of analysis in the subsequent FMEA.
  • Check Sheet: a structured data collection form. Examples are an environmental monitoring check sheet (recording EM sampling results) or an autoclave sterilization record check sheet.
  • Process Mapping: more detailed than a flowchart, showing material flow, information flow and personnel flow; suited to building process understanding before a HACCP analysis.
  • Ishikawa (Cause-and-Effect) Diagram: the problem (effect) is the "fish head", and six categories of causes extend to the left: Man, Machine, Material, Method, Environment and Measurement. It is commonly used for the initial classification of causes in a deviation investigation.

Practical Application in a CDMO

At the start of a technology transfer, the CDMO first builds a complete Process Map together with the client. This is the basis for all later QRM analyses; without a complete process map, an FMEA or HACCP analysis will be fragmentary and unreliable. Fishbone diagrams often appear in CAPA documents as the first step in classifying deviation root causes, helping the investigation team avoid missing any category of cause.

I.2 Failure Mode Effects Analysis (FMEA)

Original Text

I.2 Failure Mode Effects Analysis (FMEA)

FMEA (see IEC 60812) provides for an evaluation of potential failure modes for processes and their likely effect on outcomes and/or product performance. Once failure modes are established, risk reduction can be used to eliminate, contain, reduce or control the potential failures. FMEA relies on product and process understanding. FMEA methodically breaks down the analysis of complex processes into manageable steps. It is a powerful tool for summarizing the important modes of failure, factors causing these failures and the likely effects of these failures.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

FMEA can be used to prioritize risks and monitor the effectiveness of risk control activities. FMEA can be applied to equipment and facilities and might be used to analyze a manufacturing operation and its effect on product or process. It identifies elements/operations within the system that render it vulnerable. The output/results of FMEA can be used as a basis for design or further analysis or to guide resource deployment.

Tutorial Commentary

FMEA vs. FMECA

  • FMEA: evaluates failure modes and their effects, and outputs an RPN.
  • FMECA (I.3): adds a "Criticality Analysis" on top of FMEA. The P × S combination is plotted on a "Criticality Matrix" to clearly distinguish which failures are "critical risks". FMECA is better suited to safety-critical systems (such as injectable drugs).

Practical Application in a CDMO

When a CDMO introduces a new filling line for a client, an FMEA must be completed before equipment qualification. A common approach is to complete the first version of the FMEA at the DQ (design qualification) stage, update it after IQ/OQ, and file the final version after PQ. The FMEA is also a key document reviewed in an FDA PAI (Pre-Approval Inspection): the inspector will ask, "How did you identify the critical risks of the filling process?" An answer backed by an FMEA document is far stronger than a verbal explanation.

FMEA Core Formula: RPN (Risk Priority Number)

RPN = P × S × D

The higher the RPN, the higher the priority. A common practice is to use RPN ≥ 100 or S ≥ 8 as the threshold that requires CAPA (company standards differ).

Note: the D score is counterintuitive. The easier a failure is to detect, the lower (better) the D score.

FMEA Example for Sterile Filling

The "operator interferes with the filling path" failure mode, with RPN = 200, needs CAPA first: it can be addressed through automation, stronger gowning training, or redesign of the equipment layout.

I.3 Failure Mode, Effects and Criticality Analysis (FMECA)

Original Text

I.3 Failure Mode, Effects and Criticality Analysis (FMECA)

FMEA might be extended to incorporate an investigation of the degree of severity of the consequences, their respective probabilities of occurrence, and their detectability, thereby becoming a Failure Mode Effect and Criticality Analysis (FMECA; see IEC 60812). In order for such an analysis to be performed, the product or process specifications should be established. FMECA can identify places where additional preventive actions might be appropriate to minimize risks.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

FMECA application in the pharmaceutical industry should mostly be utilized for failures and risks associated with manufacturing processes; however, it is not limited to this application. The output of an FMECA is a relative risk "score" for each failure mode, which is used to rank the modes on a relative risk basis.

Tutorial Commentary

When to Choose FMEA and When to Choose FMECA

  • Use FMEA: for general process risk assessment, equipment qualification and change control, when a ranked RPN list is needed quickly.
  • Use FMECA: for critical processes that affect patient safety (such as sterile filling of injectables or highly potent APIs), or when a regulator requires more rigorous risk documentation (for example, a response to an FDA CGMP Form 483). FMECA provides a fuller justification of "criticality".

I.4 Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)

Original Text

I.4 Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)

The FTA tool (see IEC 61025) is an approach that assumes failure of the functionality of a product or process. This tool evaluates system (or sub-system) failures one at a time but can combine multiple causes of failure by identifying causal chains. The results are represented pictorially in the form of a tree of fault modes. At each level in the tree, combinations of fault modes are described with logical operators (AND, OR, etc.). FTA relies on the experts' process understanding to identify causal factors.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

FTA can be used to establish the pathway to the root cause of the failure. FTA can be used to investigate complaints or deviations in order to fully understand their root cause and to ensure that intended improvements will fully resolve the issue and not lead to other issues (i.e. solve one problem yet cause a different problem). Fault Tree Analysis is an effective tool for evaluating how multiple factors affect a given issue. The output of an FTA includes a visual representation of failure modes. It is useful both for risk assessment and in developing monitoring programs.

FTA vs. FMEA: Complementary Tools Working in Opposite Directions

  • FMEA (bottom-up, inductive): starts from known failure modes and asks "What effect will this failure cause?" It is prevention-oriented and used at the design stage.
  • FTA (top-down, deductive): starts from an adverse event that has occurred and asks "What caused this result?" It is investigation-oriented and used for deviation/OOS analysis.
  • Best practice: use FMEA first for prevention, and use FTA to investigate after an event occurs. The two complement each other rather than replace each other.

Practical Application in a CDMO

After receiving an OOS notification from a client, a CDMO often presents the root cause analysis path as an FTA in the deviation investigation report. FDA inspectors readily accept FTA because it clearly shows the logic and completeness of the investigation. A complete FTA should contain: (1) a definition of the top event, (2) a systematic expansion of all possible causes, (3) investigation evidence for each branch, and (4) the finally confirmed root cause (marked "Confirmed Root Cause").

I.5 Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP)

Original Text

I.5 Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP)

HACCP is a systematic, proactive, and preventive tool for assuring product quality, reliability, and safety (see WHO Technical Report Series No 908, 2003 Annex 7). It is a structured approach that applies technical and scientific principles to analyze, evaluate, prevent, and control the risk or adverse consequence(s) of hazard(s) due to the design, development, production, and use of products.

HACCP consists of the following seven steps:

  1. conduct a hazard analysis and identify preventive measures for each step of the process;
  2. determine the critical control points;
  3. establish critical limits;
  4. establish a system to monitor the critical control points;
  5. establish the corrective action to be taken when monitoring indicates that the critical control points are not in a state of control;
  6. establish system to verify that the HACCP system is working effectively;
  7. establish a record-keeping system.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

HACCP might be used to identify and manage risks associated with physical, chemical and biological hazards (including microbiological contamination). HACCP is most useful when product and process understanding is sufficiently comprehensive to support identification of critical control points. The output of a HACCP analysis is risk management information that facilitates monitoring of critical points not only in the manufacturing process but also in other life cycle phases.

Tutorial Commentary

The Food Industry Origins of HACCP

HACCP was first developed by NASA in the 1960s for astronaut food safety and later became the mandatory standard of the global food industry (Codex Alimentarius). The pharmaceutical industry borrowed the concept, and it is particularly suited to sterile manufacturing. One key difference: in the food industry, CCPs are mostly physical/chemical hazards (such as heat sterilization temperature); in pharmaceuticals, besides microbial control, cross-contamination and mix-up hazards must also be considered. These are safety risks unique to medicinal products.

Practical Application in a CDMO

For the sterile filling department of a CDMO, the HACCP plan should be part of the Site Master File. When the CDMO undergoes an EU QP audit or an FDA PAI, the HACCP document is the strongest evidence of "systematic hazard control". It is recommended to re-evaluate the HACCP plan every 1-2 years or after a major deviation, to ensure the CCP list reflects the latest state of the process.

CCP vs. CP vs. OP: The Differences Between the Three Types of Control Points

Identifying CCPs is the most critical step in HACCP. The criteria are: (1) Can this step eliminate the hazard or reduce it to an acceptable level? (2) Is there a later step that can remedy it? If there is no remedy, it is a CCP.

I.6 Hazard Operability Analysis (HAZOP)

Original Text

I.6 Hazard Operability Analysis (HAZOP)

HAZOP (see IEC 61882) is based on a theory that assumes that risk events are caused by deviations from the design or operating intentions. It is a systematic brainstorming technique for identifying hazards using so-called "guide-words". "Guide-words" (e.g., No, More, Other Than, Part of, etc.) are applied to relevant parameters (e.g., contamination, temperature) to help identify potential deviations from normal use or design intentions. It often uses a team of people with expertise covering the design of the process or product and its application.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

HAZOP can be applied to manufacturing processes, including outsourced production and formulation as well as the upstream suppliers, equipment and facilities for drug substances and drug (medicinal) products. It has also been used primarily in the pharmaceutical industry for evaluating process safety hazards. As is the case with HACCP, the output of a HAZOP analysis is a list of critical operations for risk management. This facilitates regular monitoring of critical points in the manufacturing process.

Tutorial Commentary

Strengths and Weaknesses of HAZOP

  • Strengths: highly systematic and unlikely to leave blind spots; performed by a cross-functional team that brings together knowledge from several fields; particularly effective for equipment/facility design review.
  • Weaknesses: very time-consuming (a HAZOP of a complex process may need 2-5 days of expert meetings); requires design drawings (P&ID) and detailed process documents; not suitable for early development stages where little information is available (PHA is more appropriate then).
  • When to use: design review (Design Review) of new facility design, introduction of new equipment, and major process changes.

Practical Application in a CDMO

When building a new facility or renovating an old one, a CDMO should run the HAZOP after Detail Design is complete and before construction starts. Participants should include process engineers, equipment engineers, QA, safety staff and a production operator representative. HAZOP action items should be tracked to closure and used as an input document to DQ (design qualification). A common HAZOP finding: "More pressure on filtration manifold" may cause the filter to rupture. The solution is to install a pressure relief valve and set an upper-limit alarm.

I.7 Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PHA)

Original Text

I.7 Preliminary Hazard Analysis (PHA)

PHA is a tool of analysis based on applying prior experience or knowledge of a hazard or failure to identify future hazards, hazardous situations and events that might cause harm, as well as to estimate their probability of occurrence for a given activity, facility, product or system. The tool consists of: 1) the identification of the possibilities that the risk event happens, 2) the qualitative evaluation of the extent of possible injury or damage to health that could result, 3) a relative ranking of the hazard using a combination of severity and likelihood of occurrence, and 4) the identification of possible remedial measures.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

PHA might be useful when analyzing existing systems or prioritizing hazards where circumstances prevent a more extensive technique from being used. It can be used for product, process and facility design as well as to evaluate the types of hazards for the general product type, then the product class, and finally the specific product. PHA is most commonly used early in the development of a project when there is little information on design details or operating procedures; thus, it will often be a precursor to further studies. Typically, hazards identified in the PHA are further assessed with other risk management tools such as those in this section.

Tutorial Commentary

PHA: Early Screening That Paves the Way for Deeper Analysis

PHA is the "lightest" formal QRM tool and is particularly suitable in the following situations:

  • Early in new drug development (before Phase 1), when only the mechanism of action and a preliminary formulation concept exist
  • Site selection assessment, when only the location and a preliminary design concept exist
  • Assessing the potential hazards introduced by a completely new process technology (such as continuous manufacturing)
  • Rapid screening: initially classify a large number of potential hazards by severity × probability, and decide which deserve deeper analysis with FMEA/HACCP

Practical Application in a CDMO

When a CDMO evaluates whether to accept a new product (such as a highly potent oncology drug or an mRNA vaccine), PHA is an important tool for the business decision: it assesses whether the existing facility and equipment can safely handle the hazard level of the new product, and how much must be invested in modifications. The result of this early PHA directly affects the CDMO's quotation and delivery timeline.

PHA Output Format Example

The output of a PHA is the "roadmap" for later tools: high-level hazards -> in-depth FMEA/HACCP; medium hazards -> standard control procedures; low hazards -> routine monitoring is enough.

I.8 Risk Ranking and Filtering

Original Text

I.8 Risk Ranking and Filtering

Risk ranking and filtering is a tool for comparing and ranking risks. Risk ranking of complex systems typically requires evaluation of multiple diverse quantitative and qualitative factors for each risk. The tool involves breaking down a basic risk question into as many components as needed to capture factors involved in the risk. These factors are combined into a single relative risk score that can then be used for ranking risks. "Filters," in the form of weighting factors or cut-offs for risk scores, can be used to scale or fit the risk ranking to management or policy objectives.

Potential Areas of Use(s)

Risk ranking and filtering can be used to prioritize manufacturing sites for inspection/audit by regulators or industry. Risk ranking methods are particularly helpful in situations in which the portfolio of risks and the underlying consequences to be managed are diverse and difficult to compare using a single tool. Risk ranking is useful when management needs to evaluate both quantitatively-assessed and qualitatively-assessed risks within the same organizational framework.

Tutorial Commentary

Risk Ranking and Filtering: A Tool for Comparing Across Systems

FMEA suits in-depth analysis of the risks of a single process; but when management needs to compare the relative risk between different product lines, different sites, or different systems, Risk Ranking and Filtering is needed.

How it works:

  1. Identify all the risk dimensions to be compared (for example: potential for patient harm, regulatory compliance risk, supply chain stability, financial impact)
  2. Set a weighting factor for each dimension to reflect its relative importance
  3. Score each system/product/site
  4. Calculate the weighted risk score and rank
  5. Set a "filter/cut-off threshold": above the threshold, prioritize resources

Practical Application in a CDMO

A multi-product CDMO faces a resource allocation challenge every year: on which product lines should limited QA staff focus their monitoring and review? A Risk Ranking model helps management make objective, documented resource allocation decisions, and also shows clients that the CDMO has a systematic risk management culture, which is a source of competitiveness for high-value services.

I.9 Supporting Statistical Tools

Statistical tools can support and facilitate quality risk management. They can enable effective data assessment, aid in determining the significance of the data set(s), and facilitate more reliable decision-making. Tutorial Commentary

Statistical Tools Are Not Primary QRM Tools

Q9(R1) lists statistical tools as "Supporting" tools. They cannot carry out a complete risk assessment on their own, but provide quantitative data to support primary tools such as FMEA and HACCP. For example, when scoring "probability (P)" in an FMEA, historical batch control chart data should be consulted to quantify the failure frequency, rather than relying on subjective judgment alone.

Practical Application in a CDMO

In a Continued Process Verification (CPV) report, statistical tools are the core content: every CQA and CPP should be tracked with a control chart, Cpk calculations show process capability, and Pareto analysis reveals the main sources of deviation. This CPV report is both a GMP requirement (FDA 21 CFR 211.180(e); EU GMP Annex 15) and the statistical basis for the Annual Product Review (APR), and it is also an input to updated versions of the FMEA.

  1. Scenario 1: Your CDMO is building a new lyophilizer (freeze dryer) filling line for a client. Before the equipment arrives and IQ qualification starts, you want to systematically identify possible operating hazards of this filling line (such as temperature excursions, door seal failure and condensate contamination). Which QRM tool should you use? Why is FTA not suitable?

  2. Scenario 2: Before batch release, you receive an environmental monitoring (EM) report in which the grade A viable air monitoring result exceeds the Alert Level. You need to run a deviation investigation to find the source of contamination. Which tool should you choose? How should your "Top Event" be defined?
    Reference answer: FTA. Define the top event as "grade A viable air count exceeds the alert level", then expand downward through an OR gate: operator contamination | environmental system abnormality | equipment surface contamination | culture media issue.
  3. Scenario 3: Your company plans to add a sterile lyophilized injectable product line. It is a new dosage form, and your QA head requires a preliminary risk assessment before Phase 2 clinical trial manufacturing, but the process details are not yet fixed. Which tool should you choose? What should the output contain?
    Reference answer: PHA. The output should include a list of hazard types (microbial, chemical, physical, cross-contamination), a preliminary severity and probability level for each type of hazard, a ranking of hazard levels (high/medium/low), and recommendations for further analysis (which hazards need FMEA/HACCP).
  4. Scenario 4: You manage a CDMO site with 5 filling lines and must decide where to focus this year's QA resources (enough to deeply review only 2 filling lines). Each filling line differs in product type, historical deviation frequency and client importance. Which QRM tool should you use?
    Reference answer: Risk Ranking and Filtering. Build a weighted scoring matrix with dimensions such as product type (sterile injectable vs. oral), OOS frequency over the past 12 months, client contract value, and time since the last in-depth QA review.
  5. Scenario 5: The operating range in the lyophilizer Shelf Temperature control SOP is -40°C to +30°C. You suspect that in some batches the lyophilizer may have caused a failure of sterility assurance. When using FMEA to assess the Shelf Temperature process step, how do you rate the RPN? Which dimension (P/S/D) most affects the priority of this failure mode?
    Reference answer: S (severity) is highest (= 10, patient safety). For D, assess whether complete temperature records and trend monitoring exist; if a PI recorder (programmer/controller) raises automatic alarms, the D score is low (good). P depends on historical failure records. If S=10, D=2 and P=2, the RPN is 40, but because S=10 it should still be handled first (the decision cannot rest on RPN alone).

Section Summary - Annex I: QRM Methods and Tools

  • Each of the nine tools has its own role: there is no universal tool; choose the most suitable one on three dimensions: "direction of the question (prevention vs. investigation)", "timing (before design vs. after commercialization)" and "completeness of information".
  • FMEA / FMECA: inductive; starts from process steps to assess failure modes; RPN = P × S × D; FMECA adds a Criticality Matrix and is better suited to patient-safety-critical processes.
  • FTA: deductive; starts from the top event (a failure that has occurred) and traces root causes using AND/OR logic gates; the best tool for OOS and deviation investigation.
  • HACCP: a systematic seven-step framework that identifies CCPs and sets up monitoring; a CCP is a point in the process where, once control is lost, there is no remedy, and it needs 100% monitoring. Suited to control of the entire sterile manufacturing process.
  • HAZOP: applies guide words (No/More/Less/Reverse, etc.) to process parameters to systematically identify design deviation risks; best for new facility/new equipment design review, but time-consuming.
  • PHA: an early screening tool when information is limited; uses prior knowledge to identify hazard levels and produces a roadmap for deeper analysis; most often used in early development and new product evaluation.
  • Risk Ranking and Filtering: a tool for comparing and ranking across systems/sites/products; uses weighted scoring and a filter threshold to give management an objective basis for resource allocation.
  • Statistical tools (Control Charts, DOE, Pareto, Cpk): supporting tools that give the primary QRM tools quantitative data; control charts and process capability analysis are the core of CPV and an input for continually updating the FMEA.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: II.1 Integrated Quality Management

II.1 covers the day-to-day operation of the whole quality system. Think of it this way: every decision point in the quality system should be backed by QRM.

  • Documentation: use QRM to decide which processes need an SOP and how detailed the SOP should be. High-risk operations need tighter document control.
  • Training: not everyone needs the same training. QRM helps identify which operations have the greatest impact on quality, so training resources can be focused.
  • Quality Defects: deviations, OOS results and complaints. QRM provides a systematic way to assess how serious these events are (for example, the Severity score in FMEA).
  • Auditing: audit frequency and scope should be set according to risk. ICH Q9 lists 11 factors that influence audit decisions (see the original text).
  • Change Control: QRM assesses the impact of a change and determines what re-qualification or re-validation is needed. This directly affects how many resources are committed.

Core Concepts: II.2 Regulatory Operations

II.2 is the application of QRM on the regulator's side, that is, using QRM from the perspective of the regulators (FDA, EMA, PMDA and others).

  • Risk-Based Inspections: regulators cannot inspect every site, so they use the factors in the II.1 auditing section to plan which sites to inspect and how deeply. High-risk sites (for example, those with a recall history or complex sterile manufacturing processes) are inspected more often and more broadly.
  • Dossier Evaluation: assessors use scientific risk assessment methods to judge the soundness of an application. A submission that combines Q8 and Q9 (with a Design Space and CQA analysis) can reach a faster and more confident review conclusion.
  • Communication between inspectors and assessors: inspectors and dossier assessors should share risk information so that their understanding of new technologies such as PAT and parametric release is consistent.

Analogy: How Regulators Use QRM

Think of a regulator's inspection plan as a police patrol schedule: with limited officers, patrol frequency depends on the assessed crime risk of each area (crime history, population density, time of day). Pharmaceutical sites work the same way. A site with a good track record, a sound QRM system and a relatively simple process may be inspected only once every five years. A site with recalls or high-risk products may be inspected every year.

This also means that building a robust QRM system is not only the right thing to do, it can also reduce how often you are inspected, which is a concrete, measurable business benefit.

Key Note: QRM Classification in Change Control

In practice, QRM is most often used in the impact assessment of change control to decide the change category:

  • Major Change: high-risk impact, requires a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS)
  • Moderate Change: medium risk, CBE-30 (Changes Being Effected in 30 days)
  • Minor Change: low risk, an Annual Report is sufficient

QRM provides the scientific basis for the classification, so that both the company and the regulator accept that the category is justified rather than a subjective judgment.

CDMO in Practice

For a CDMO, the Auditing part of II.1 is especially important: the frequency with which clients (brand-owner companies) audit a CDMO is determined by exactly these 11 risk factors. If a CDMO can demonstrate:

  • A robust QRM system (Robustness of QRM activities)
  • A good compliance history (Compliance history)
  • A low number and significance of quality defects (Low number/significance of quality defects)

then clients may reduce audit frequency, for example from once a year to once every 18 months, saving resources on both sides. This is also an important competitive indicator in CDMO business development.

When designing a sterile manufacturing facility, a classic question arises: use a conventional RABS (Restricted Access Barrier System) or an isolator?

The QRM way of thinking:

  • Risk identification: under normal and abnormal operation, which technology has the lower probability of microbial contamination?
  • Risk assessment: an isolator offers a higher Sterility Assurance Level, but the consequence of a failed decontamination (VHP) cycle is also greater
  • Risk control: what monitoring and validation does each option require?
  • Decision: choose the most suitable option based on product characteristics (lyophilized vs liquid injectable), production scale and the skill level of personnel

This decision-making process is itself QRM in action, and it must be documented to demonstrate that the choice is justified.

Key Note: Risk-Based Decisions on Qualification Scope (Risk-Based Qualification)

The most important use of QRM in qualification is this: not all equipment needs the same level of IQ/OQ/PQ.

  • High-risk equipment (direct product contact, critical processes): full IQ/OQ/PQ plus periodic requalification
  • Medium-risk equipment (indirect contact, support systems): IQ/OQ, with PQ simplified
  • Low-risk equipment (office equipment, general warehouse): IQ alone or an equipment logbook may be enough

This tiered qualification approach (Risk-Based Qualification Strategy) is recommended by both ISPE GAMP 5 and ICH Q9, and can save a great deal of qualification effort.

Key Note: QRM Basis for Cleaning Validation Limits

For cleaning validation in a multi-product facility, the central question is: what is the acceptable limit for residues?

The QRM approach (based on the EMA guidance on cleaning validation):

  • Assess the toxicity of the previous batch's API (LD50, PDE value) to determine the health-based exposure threshold
  • Assess the degree of shared equipment surfaces to determine the probability of contamination transfer pathways
  • Set scientific limits based on Health-Based Exposure Limits (HBEL) rather than the traditional "0.1% of batch / 10 ppm" criteria alone

CDMO in Practice

What II.3 means for a CDMO: when a client transfers technology, if the client already has QRM-driven CQA/CPP documentation (that is, a Q8-style development package), the CDMO's technology transfer risk assessment can be built directly on it, greatly shortening the transfer time. If the client does not, the CDMO has to prepare it itself, which is an important chargeable service.

What II.4 means for a CDMO: CDMO facilities usually serve several clients and many products, so cleaning validation (preventing cross-contamination) and shared-equipment risk are core issues. A complete risk-based cleaning validation program is a must-have for passing client audits.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: II.5 Materials Management

QRM in materials management covers the full chain from supplier selection through receipt of raw materials to storage and distribution:

  • Supplier evaluation: not every supplier needs an on-site audit. QRM helps decide which suppliers need an On-site Audit and which can be handled with a Questionnaire or Document Review alone.
  • Variability of starting materials: different batches of the same API may differ in route of synthesis and impurity profile. QRM assesses how much this variability affects the finished product.
  • Use of quarantined materials: can material not yet released by QC go on to the next processing step? QRM weighs the risk of doing so against the time saved.
  • Cold chain management: for temperature-sensitive products (such as biologics and vaccines), a temperature excursion in storage or transport needs a QRM assessment of its actual effect on product quality, rather than automatic rejection.

Core Concepts: QRM in II.6 Production

  • Validation scope: a process has dozens of steps, and it is not possible to validate every step fully. QRM identifies the critical steps so that validation resources can be concentrated.
  • In-process control frequency: decisions on sampling frequency (for example, hourly vs every 30 minutes) should be based on risk. Too little sampling means high risk, while too much wastes resources and may even affect the process.
  • PAT implementation: introducing PAT tools such as near-infrared (NIR) for real-time content monitoring requires QRM to assess the accuracy and failure risk of the method, so that its reliability can be explained to regulators.
  • Production scheduling: in a multi-product facility, the production sequence (whether to run campaigns) needs a QRM assessment of cross-contamination risk to determine cleaning cycles and intervals between productions.

Analogy: Tiered Supplier Management

Imagine you run a company with a complex supply chain (such as a car maker). You cannot manage every parts supplier with the same intensity. Your strategy is:

  • Tier 1 (critical suppliers): supply core parts, single source, no alternative. Annual on-site audit plus real-time data sharing
  • Tier 2 (important suppliers): have alternative sources and a good quality record. Audit every 2-3 years plus a periodic questionnaire
  • Tier 3 (general suppliers): standard materials with many substitutes on the market. Document review is enough

Supplier management in a pharmaceutical company should work the same way. QRM provides the scientific basis for tiering, avoiding both "over-management" and "under-management" caused by treating all suppliers alike.

Key Note: Risk-Based Design of Process Validation

The traditional validation mindset: "Run 3 batches, and if every batch passes, validation is complete."

The risk-based validation mindset (ICH Q9 + Q10 + FDA Process Validation Guidance 2011):

  • Stage 1 (Process Design): use QRM to identify which parameters are CPPs and need focused study
  • Stage 2 (Process Qualification): the number of validation batches is decided by risk. High-risk processes need more batches and a broader sampling plan
  • Stage 3 (Continued Process Verification): the monitoring frequency and indicators are decided by QRM, to ensure the process remains in a state of control

CDMO in Practice

A CDMO carries a dual role in II.5 and II.6:

  • As a contract manufacturer: the CDMO is itself a link in the client's supply chain and falls under II.5 (the client audits the CDMO)
  • As a purchaser: the CDMO must also manage its own raw material suppliers (API suppliers and excipient suppliers), using the II.5 approach for tiered evaluation

In II.6 (production), a CDMO's validation strategy needs to be platform-based: build risk assessment templates that apply across products, so that validation decisions for each new product are faster and more consistent. This is one of the core elements of a CDMO's technical competitiveness.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: II.7 Laboratory Controls and Stability

  • QRM in OOS investigations: when an analytical result is out of specification, QRM (in particular an Ishikawa diagram plus FMEA) is used to systematically identify whether the root cause is a laboratory error (Phase I) or a process problem (Phase II). This avoids an FDA 483 observation for "data invalidated but problem unresolved" caused by weak investigation logic.
  • Scientific basis for retest period/shelf life: the stability study design required by ICH Q1A (storage conditions, sampling time points) is itself an application of QRM. Based on the product's expected degradation risk, it decides how much data is needed to support the claimed shelf life.

Core Concepts: II.8 Packaging and Labeling

  • Container closure system (CCS) selection: QRM assesses how different CCS options (glass vs plastic vials, stopper materials) affect drug compatibility, adsorption and oxygen transmission, as well as the risk to patient safety.
  • Preventing labeling mix-ups: labeling errors are among the most common causes of recalls. QRM analyzes failure points in the labeling system (such as manual label changeover and look-alike labels for different strengths) and designs mistake-proofing (poka-yoke) controls such as visual verification systems, barcode confirmation and physical segregation procedures.

Core Concepts: II.9 Supply Chain Control (New Section in Q9(R1))

II.9 is the most important new content in Q9(R1) compared with the original Q9, a direct response to the fragility of the global pharmaceutical supply chain exposed by COVID-19. Its core logic is:

"Understanding hazards to quality/manufacturing is critical to maintaining supply predictability. When risks are well understood and controlled, a higher confidence in product availability can be attained."

Three application themes:

  • Process variability and state of control: process drift and non-uniformity are internal root causes of unstable supply. Monitoring systems are needed to give early warning before they develop into major defects or recalls
  • Reliability of facilities and equipment: equipment failure is one of the most common causes of supply interruption. Maintenance programs, spare-parts inventory and equipment design that guards against human error (mistake-proofing) are all ways to reduce this risk
  • Supplier oversight: when the quality of materials from an external supplier shows substantive fluctuation, review should be strengthened. This corresponds directly to the supplier management requirements in ICH Q10 Section 2.7

Analogy: The Post-Pandemic Significance of II.9 Supply Chain QRM

The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020-2022 exposed three critical vulnerabilities in the pharmaceutical supply chain:

  • Single-source API risk: many key APIs (such as ibuprofen and amoxicillin) are concentrated in a handful of manufacturers in India and China. If those sites stop production, drug shortages follow worldwide
  • Geopolitical risk: export bans, customs delays and transport bottlenecks
  • CDMO concentration risk: many pharmaceutical companies outsource commercial production to a small number of CDMOs, so a CDMO's quality or supply problem directly affects product availability

II.9 expects pharmaceutical companies to use QRM to quantify and rank these risks and to establish mitigation plans (such as a Dual-Source Strategy, safety stock levels and pre-qualified alternative manufacturing sites). This is not just GMP compliance, but a requirement for Business Resilience.

Key Note: Single-Source Supplier Risk

This is one of the most important practical applications in II.9:

  • Identify: build a supplier list and flag which materials have only one approved source (Sole-Qualified Supplier)
  • Assess: combine the supplier's share of the global market supply, compliance history, geographic concentration risk and financial stability
  • Control options：
    • Dual Sourcing: pre-approve a second supplier, even if it is not purchased from in normal times
    • Strategic Stock: maintain a safety stock of 6-12 months
    • Technology Transfer Contingency: prepare in advance a technology transfer document package for a backup site
  • Communicate: ICH Q9(R1) encourages industry to communicate with regulators early about high-risk supply situations, and regulators may offer expedited review pathways

CDMO in Practice: II.9 Supply Chain Risk

The CDMO's special position in the supply chain (see the figure below):

  • The CDMO is a "critical supplier" to the client: from the client's perspective, the CDMO is itself a high-risk node in the supply chain (especially under a single-CDMO strategy). Clients should perform a supply chain risk assessment of the CDMO, covering facility redundancy, critical equipment spares and technology transfer capability.
  • The CDMO's management of its own raw material suppliers: a CDMO must manage suppliers of API, excipients and packaging materials. If these risks are not controlled, they directly affect the CDMO's delivery commitments to clients.
  • The business opportunity of II.9 for CDMOs: a CDMO that can offer supply chain risk assessment services (such as dual-source plans and backup-site technology transfer) has an added competitive advantage in the post-pandemic era. For clients that depend on a single production site in particular, a CDMO network (multi-site manufacturing) is itself a supply chain resilience solution.
