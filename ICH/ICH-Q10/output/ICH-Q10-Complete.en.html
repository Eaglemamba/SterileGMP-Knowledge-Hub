1.1 Introduction

Original Text

1. PHARMACEUTICAL QUALITY SYSTEM

1.1 Introduction

This document establishes a new ICH tripartite guideline describing a model for an effective quality management system for the pharmaceutical industry, referred to as the Pharmaceutical Quality System. Throughout this guideline, the term "pharmaceutical quality system" refers to the ICH Q10 model.

ICH Q10 describes one comprehensive model for an effective pharmaceutical quality system that is based on International Standards Organisation (ISO) quality concepts, includes applicable Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) regulations and complements ICH Q8 "Pharmaceutical Development" and ICH Q9 "Quality Risk Management". ICH Q10 is a model for a pharmaceutical quality system that can be implemented throughout the different stages of a product lifecycle. Much of the content of ICH Q10 applicable to manufacturing sites is currently specified by regional GMP requirements. ICH Q10 is not intended to create any new expectations beyond current regulatory requirements. Consequently, the content of ICH Q10 that is additional to current regional GMP requirements is optional.

ICH Q10 demonstrates industry and regulatory authorities' support of an effective pharmaceutical quality system to enhance the quality and availability of medicines around the world in the interest of public health. Implementation of ICH Q10 throughout the product lifecycle should facilitate innovation and continual improvement and strengthen the link between pharmaceutical development and manufacturing activities.

Tutorial Commentary

Key point: Q10 does not create new regulatory requirements

"ICH Q10 is not intended to create any new expectations beyond current regulatory requirements."

This is a common misunderstanding at many companies (especially small and mid-sized manufacturers and CDMOs). Q10 is a model, not a new set of mandatory regulations. If you already comply with FDA 21 CFR Part 211 or EU GMP Part I, anything in Q10 that goes beyond GMP requirements is optional.

That does not make Q10 unimportant. It provides a systematic framework that lets a company proactively go beyond the minimum requirements and build a genuinely effective quality culture.

Analogy: what is a PQS?

Think of a pharmaceutical plant as a high-end restaurant. GMP regulations are the minimum hygiene standards set by the health authority (handwashing, ingredient temperatures, and so on). ISO standards are international best practice for restaurant management systems (such as ISO 22000 for food safety management). Q8 is the chef's philosophy of recipe design (designing every dish scientifically).

ICH Q10 (PQS) is the "operating management system" of the whole restaurant: the complete framework running from purchasing, prep, kitchen management, tasting and quality checks, and complaint handling, through to staff training and continual menu improvement. It integrates hygiene standards (GMP) with recipe philosophy (Q8/Q9), so that high quality is delivered every day and in every dish.

The ICH Q Trilogy (Q8, Q9, Q10)

ICH Q8, Q9 and Q10 form a mutually supporting trilogy that together provides the scientific basis for modern pharmaceutical quality management:

How the three relate: Q8 tells you "what quality to build into the product", Q9 tells you "how to assess and manage risk", and Q10 tells you "what system architecture ensures all of this keeps being carried out effectively".

1.2 Scope

Original Text

1.2 Scope

This guideline applies to the systems supporting the development and manufacture of pharmaceutical drug substances (i.e., API) and drug products, including biotechnology and biological products, throughout the product lifecycle.

The elements of ICH Q10 should be applied in a manner that is appropriate and proportionate to each of the product lifecycle stages, recognising the differences among, and the different goals of each stage (see Section 3).

For the purposes of this guideline, the product lifecycle includes the following technical activities for new and existing products:

Pharmaceutical Development:

  • Drug substance development;
  • Formulation development (including container/closure system);
  • Manufacture of investigational products;
  • Delivery system development (where relevant);
  • Manufacturing process development and scale-up;
  • Analytical method development.

Technology Transfer:

  • New product transfers during Development through Manufacturing;
  • Transfers within or between manufacturing and testing sites for marketed products.

Commercial Manufacturing:

  • Acquisition and control of materials;
  • Provision of facilities, utilities, and equipment;
  • Production (including packaging and labelling);
  • Quality control and assurance;
  • Release;
  • Storage;
  • Distribution (excluding wholesaler activities).

Product Discontinuation:

  • Retention of documentation;
  • Sample retention;
  • Continued product assessment and reporting.

Tutorial Commentary

Two details that are often overlooked

  • Biotechnology products are also in scope: Q10 explicitly includes biotechnology and biological products (such as monoclonal antibodies and vaccines). The PQS for these products is usually more complex than for small-molecule drugs, and the Q10 framework applies equally.
  • Product discontinuation does not mean "nothing left to do": three obligations remain after discontinuation: document retention (typically at least one year, or a set number of years beyond the last expiry date), retention of samples (according to regional regulations), and continued evaluation and reporting (such as post-marketing safety reports, e.g. PSUR/PBRER).

Technology transfer is a high-risk node

Technology transfer is where process knowledge is most easily lost. Imagine handing the recipe for a delicate dessert from the head chef (the development site) to another kitchen (the manufacturing site). If you hand over only a paper recipe (the NDA/CTD filing) without fully conveying "why this temperature" or "what the Design Space is for this mixing speed", the receiving kitchen can only follow the recipe literally, and if any small condition differs, the batch may fail.

Q10 requires that technology transfer systematically transfers knowledge, not just procedures. This is exactly where knowledge management (1.6.1) applies most directly at the technology transfer stage.

Practical application for CDMOs

For a CDMO, the four lifecycle stages in the Q10 scope map directly onto its business model:

  • Development services (CRO / early-stage CDMO): the pharmaceutical development stage. Build the development package and meet the Q10 knowledge management expectations
  • Technology transfer services: this is the core competence of a CDMO, namely whether it can systematically receive the client's knowledge and successfully reproduce the process
  • Commercial manufacturing (CMO): Q10 elements such as CAPA, CPV and change management are the day-to-day backbone of GMP compliance
  • Client product discontinuation: a CDMO must still retain process documentation and retained samples as required by law, even after the client has stopped paying
1.3–1.4 Relationships to GMP/ISO/ICH Q7 and Regulatory Approaches

Original Text

1.3 Relationship of ICH Q10 to Regional GMP Requirements, ISO Standards and ICH Q7

Regional GMP requirements, the ICH Q7 Guideline, "Good Manufacturing Practice Guide for Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients", and ISO quality management system guidelines form the foundation for ICH Q10. To meet the objectives described below, ICH Q10 augments GMPs by describing specific quality system elements and management responsibilities. ICH Q10 provides a harmonised model for a pharmaceutical quality system throughout the lifecycle of a product and is intended to be used together with regional GMP requirements.

The regional GMPs do not explicitly address all stages of the product lifecycle (e.g., Development). The quality system elements and management responsibilities described in this guideline are intended to encourage the use of science and risk based approaches at each lifecycle stage, thereby promoting continual improvement across the entire product lifecycle.

1.4 Relationship of ICH Q10 to Regulatory Approaches

Regulatory approaches for a specific product or manufacturing facility should be commensurate with the level of product and process understanding, the results of quality risk management, and the effectiveness of the pharmaceutical quality system. When implemented, the effectiveness of the pharmaceutical quality system can normally be evaluated during a regulatory inspection at the manufacturing site. Potential opportunities to enhance science and risk based regulatory approaches are identified in Annex 1. Regulatory processes will be determined by region.

"The regional GMPs do not explicitly address all stages of the product lifecycle (e.g., Development). The quality system elements and management responsibilities described in this guideline are intended to encourage the use of science and risk based approaches at each lifecycle stage."

Tutorial Commentary

"Commensurate": the principle of proportionality in regulation

The key word in Section 1.4 is commensurate: the intensity of regulatory oversight of a manufacturer should be commensurate with the manufacturer's level of product and process understanding, the results of QRM, and PQS performance.

This means:

  • The deeper the product/process understanding and the more robust the PQS, the more flexibility regulators can give (for example, lower inspection frequency or faster review of changes)
  • Poor PQS performance, many deviations and ineffective CAPA give regulators grounds for stricter action (Warning Letter, import alert)
  • PQS performance is usually assessed during a regulatory inspection at the site

GMP is the floor; Q10 builds the whole building

Regional GMP covers only the commercial manufacturing stage (and in varying levels of detail). FDA 21 CFR Part 211 says almost nothing about development, and only EU GMP Part II (the Q7 equivalent) covers API development. But no GMP system covers the entire product lifecycle from development to discontinuation.

Q10 fills this gap, especially the development stage (Pharmaceutical Development) and the discontinuation stage (Discontinuation). Traditional GMP barely touches these two stages, yet they are hot spots for knowledge loss and compliance risk.

Where Q10 sits in the regulatory hierarchy

It is important to understand where Q10 sits in the regulatory pyramid:

ICH Q7 is the GMP guideline specifically for APIs (active pharmaceutical ingredients). Its relationship to Q10: Q7 is the mandatory GMP requirement for APIs, and Q10 builds on Q7 with a more complete systematic framework (such as formal knowledge management and management review requirements).

1.5 ICH Q10 Objectives

Original Text

1.5 ICH Q10 Objectives

Implementation of the Q10 model should result in achievement of three main objectives which complement or enhance regional GMP requirements.

1.5.1 Achieve Product Realisation

To establish, implement and maintain a system that allows the delivery of products with the quality attributes appropriate to meet the needs of patients, health care professionals, regulatory authorities (including compliance with approved regulatory filings) and other internal and external customers.

1.5.2 Establish and Maintain a State of Control

To develop and use effective monitoring and control systems for process performance and product quality, thereby providing assurance of continued suitability and capability of processes. Quality risk management can be useful in identifying the monitoring and control systems.

1.5.3 Facilitate Continual Improvement

To identify and implement appropriate product quality improvements, process improvements, variability reduction, innovations and pharmaceutical quality system enhancements, thereby increasing the ability to fulfil quality needs consistently. Quality risk management can be useful for identifying and prioritising areas for continual improvement.

Tutorial Commentary

The logic linking the three objectives

The three objectives are not parallel and unrelated; they follow an inherent logical order:

  • First achieve (Product Realisation): a system designed to make good product is the most basic starting point
  • Then maintain (State of Control): once good product can be made, you need a monitoring system to ensure this condition persists and does not drift over time
  • Finally improve (Continual Improvement): on a stable, controlled foundation, actively look for room to improve. This is what marks a truly "mature" quality system

Many companies' PQS reaches only the first level (able to release batches), lacking a monitoring system (level two) and a culture of improvement (level three). The essence of Q10 is to push manufacturers through all three.

"State of Control" does not mean "zero deviations"

The meaning of state of control is often misunderstood as "no deviations or out-of-specification results at all". The correct understanding is: process performance is within the expected range, and sources of variability are understood and controlled.

Even if deviations occasionally occur, the PQS can still be considered in a "state of control" as long as (1) they are detected promptly, (2) the root cause is investigated, and (3) effective CAPA is taken. Conversely, if the data show process capability (Cpk) steadily declining and trend alerts going unfollowed, that is a warning sign of loss of control even if there has been no OOS in the short term.

1.6 Enablers: Knowledge Management and Quality Risk Management

Original Text

1.6 Enablers: Knowledge Management and Quality Risk Management

Use of knowledge management and quality risk management will enable a company to implement ICH Q10 effectively and successfully. These enablers will facilitate achievement of the objectives described in Section 1.5 above by providing the means for science and risk based decisions related to product quality.

1.6.1 Knowledge Management

Product and process knowledge should be managed from development through the commercial life of the product up to and including product discontinuation. For example, development activities using scientific approaches provide knowledge for product and process understanding. Knowledge management is a systematic approach to acquiring, analysing, storing and disseminating information related to products, manufacturing processes and components. Sources of knowledge include, but are not limited to prior knowledge (public domain or internally documented); pharmaceutical development studies; technology transfer activities; process validation studies over the product lifecycle; manufacturing experience; innovation; continual improvement; and change management activities.

1.6.2 Quality Risk Management

Quality risk management is integral to an effective pharmaceutical quality system. It can provide a proactive approach to identifying, scientifically evaluating and controlling potential risks to quality. It facilitates continual improvement of process performance and product quality throughout the product lifecycle. ICH Q9 provides principles and examples of tools for quality risk management that can be applied to different aspects of pharmaceutical quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Knowledge management analogy: the electronic medical record (EMR) system

Knowledge management for medicines is like a hospital's Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system.

Before EMRs, doctors in each department could see only their own records. Internal medicine did not know the patient's orthopedic surgery history, and the emergency room did not know the patient's allergies, leading to duplicate tests and medication risks.

With an EMR, all records are integrated in one system, and any attending physician at any location can look up the patient's complete treatment history, allergy information and genetic reports.

The ideal for pharmaceutical knowledge management is the same: from DoE data in early development, to batch records from technology transfer, to CAPA from commercial production, to the Annual Product Quality Review (APQR), all knowledge should be stored in a structured way, searchable across departments, and not lost when people move on. The root cause of many GMP findings (483 observations) is a failure of knowledge management: the root cause of a process anomaly was investigated ten years ago, but nobody can find that record.

The nine sources of knowledge

Q10 explicitly lists the sources of information for knowledge management. It is worth understanding what each looks like in practice:

  • Prior knowledge: literature, public databases, historical data from company platform technologies
  • Pharmaceutical development studies: DoE, formulation development reports, stability studies
  • Technology transfer activities: technology transfer reports (TTR), comparability studies
  • Process validation studies: PPQ batch reports, validation summary reports
  • Manufacturing experience: batch records, deviation reports, APQR
  • Innovation: new process technologies, new analytical methods, introduction of PAT
  • Continual improvement: improvement proposals, results of Six Sigma projects
  • Change management activities: change control requests (CCR) and their assessment results

QRM is "proactive", not "reactive"

The key word in quality risk management (QRM) is proactive: identify and control risk before problems occur, rather than firefighting afterwards.

Within a PQS, QRM should run through the entire lifecycle:

  • Development stage: FMEA identifies critical process steps and guides DoE prioritisation
  • Technology transfer: risk assessment determines which parameters need to be revalidated
  • Commercial manufacturing: periodic risk review identifies early warning signs of process drift
  • Change management: pre-change risk assessment determines the extent of the change and the level of regulatory filing required

ICH Q9 provides the toolbox (FMEA, HACCP, fishbone diagrams, fault trees, etc.); Q10 tells you when and within which system to use these tools.

1.7 Design and Content Considerations

Original Text

1.7 Design and Content Considerations

(a) The design, organisation and documentation of the pharmaceutical quality system should be well structured and clear to facilitate common understanding and consistent application.

(b) The elements of ICH Q10 should be applied in a manner that is appropriate and proportionate to each of the product lifecycle stages, recognising the different goals and knowledge available for each stage.

(c) The size and complexity of the company's activities should be taken into consideration when developing a new pharmaceutical quality system or modifying an existing one. The design of the pharmaceutical quality system should incorporate appropriate risk management principles. While some aspects of the pharmaceutical quality system can be company-wide and others site-specific, the effectiveness of the pharmaceutical quality system is normally demonstrated at the site level.

(d) The pharmaceutical quality system should include appropriate processes, resources and responsibilities to provide assurance of the quality of outsourced activities and purchased materials as described in Section 2.7.

(e) Management responsibilities, as described in Section 2, should be identified within the pharmaceutical quality system.

(f) The pharmaceutical quality system should include the following elements, as described in Section 3: process performance and product quality monitoring, corrective and preventive action, change management and management review.

(g) Performance indicators, as described in Section 4, should be identified and used to monitor the effectiveness of processes within the pharmaceutical quality system.

Tutorial Commentary

"Site Level": PQS performance is demonstrated at the site

1.7(c) specifically points out that PQS performance is demonstrated at the site level. This has several important implications:

  • A company may have a corporate-level quality policy, but each site has its own manufacturing authorisation, inspection history and GMP compliance responsibilities
  • When FDA/EMA inspect, they examine how the PQS operates at the specific site, not the policy documents at corporate headquarters
  • For a CDMO, the PQS at each manufacturing site must be independent and complete, even if the sites belong to the same group

Quality control of outsourced activities (1.7(d)): the CDMO's dual responsibility

Outsourced activities have a double meaning in the CDMO context:

  • The CDMO as the contract acceptor: the client's (pharmaceutical company's) PQS must ensure that activities outsourced to the CDMO meet quality requirements, including periodic supplier audits, a signed Quality Agreement, and access to process data
  • The CDMO also outsources: a CDMO may outsource some testing to third-party laboratories, or outsource packaging and sterilisation services. The CDMO's PQS must also control these downstream outsourced activities

The Quality Agreement is the core document for implementing 1.7(d). It should clearly define the boundaries of each party's responsibilities, information-sharing requirements and the deviation notification process.

1.8 Quality Manual

Original Text

1.8 Quality Manual

A Quality Manual or equivalent documentation approach should be established and should contain the description of the pharmaceutical quality system. The description should include:

(a) The quality policy (see Section 2);

(b) The scope of the pharmaceutical quality system;

(c) Identification of the pharmaceutical quality system processes, as well as their sequences, linkages and interdependencies. Process maps and flow charts can be useful tools to facilitate depicting pharmaceutical quality system processes in a visual manner;

(d) Management responsibilities within the pharmaceutical quality system (see Section 2).

Tutorial Commentary

The Quality Manual in CDMO practice

For a CDMO, the Quality Manual usually adopts a two-tier structure:

  • Corporate level (Corporate Quality Manual): defines the quality policy, core values and overall PQS architecture shared across the group, applicable to all sites
  • Site level (Site Quality Manual / Site Quality System): building on corporate policy, describes the PQS scope of a specific site (for example, sterile dosage forms only), site-specific process maps and the local management responsibility structure

During FDA and EMA inspections, inspectors usually ask first to see the Site Quality Manual or an equivalent document, so they can quickly understand the site's PQS architecture in a short time. A clearly structured Quality Manual greatly improves inspection efficiency and also demonstrates the maturity of the company's PQS.

Note that Q10 allows "or equivalent documentation approach": the Quality Manual can be a single consolidated document, or a documentation system made up of several SOPs/policy documents, as long as it clearly presents the four elements above.

Process Maps: a powerful way to visualise the PQS

Q10 specifically mentions process maps / flow charts as a way to present the PQS processes. In practice:

  • Turtle Diagram: a process analysis tool commonly used in ISO 9001, showing a process's inputs/outputs, required resources, methods and monitoring indicators
  • SIPOC diagram: shows the high-level process relationships Supplier, Input, Process, Output, Customer
  • PQS interaction matrix: a one-page diagram showing the triggers and interactions among PQS subsystems (CAPA, change management, deviations, document control, etc.)

During an inspection, these visual tools let inspectors quickly understand the site's PQS architecture and are important showcase material for demonstrating "PQS maturity".

  2. A CDMO takes on both early clinical (Phase I) and commercial manufacturing business. How does the Q10 principle of "appropriate and proportionate" require them to differentiate their PQS design? What are the key differences in PQS requirements between the two stages?
  3. Of the three Q10 objectives, what is the essential difference between "establish and maintain a state of control" and "continual improvement"? Using CPV (Continued Process Verification) as an example, explain how these two objectives coexist in the same system.
  4. A company's Quality Manual contains only the quality policy and the PQS scope, with no process maps and no management responsibility section. In an FDA inspection, what kind of finding (483 observation or Warning Letter citation) might the inspector raise?

The four required elements of a Quality Manual

Q10 requires that the Quality Manual (or equivalent documentation) cover four aspects:

Section Summary

  • The essence of Q10: a PQS model based on ISO quality concepts, incorporating GMP requirements and complementing Q8/Q9; it does not create new regulatory requirements, and content beyond GMP is optional
  • Scope: covers the entire lifecycle of both drug substances and drug products (including biotechnology/biological products) across four stages: pharmaceutical development, technology transfer, commercial manufacturing and product discontinuation
  • Regulatory positioning: regional GMP + ICH Q7 + ISO form the base layer; Q10 provides a unified lifecycle-wide PQS model on top of it; the intensity of regulatory oversight should be commensurate with the manufacturer's PQS performance
  • Three objectives: (1) Product Realisation, then (2) State of Control, then (3) Continual Improvement. The three follow a logical progression
  • Two enablers: knowledge management (systematically managing product/process knowledge so it is not lost across the lifecycle) + quality risk management (proactively identifying and controlling quality risk, linked to ICH Q9)
  • PQS design points: clear structure, proportionate to lifecycle stage and company size, includes control of outsourced activities, clear management responsibility, covers the four PQS elements, and establishes performance indicators
  • Quality Manual: must contain four core elements: quality policy, PQS scope, process maps and linkages, and management responsibility; a CDMO usually adopts a two-tier corporate + site structure

Section 2 Overview

Original Text

2. MANAGEMENT RESPONSIBILITY

Leadership is essential to establish and maintain a company-wide commitment to quality and for the performance of the pharmaceutical quality system.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The opening line of Q10, "Leadership is essential", is not a courtesy. Management responsibility is the foundation of the entire PQS architecture.

Think of a hospital building: the quality system is the structure, the departments (Production, QC, QA) are the floors, and the foundation is management's commitment. If the foundation is not stable, the building will collapse no matter how well it is finished.

Core Concept: Overview of Management Responsibilities 2.1-2.8

Section 2 of Q10 is the "backbone" of the whole guideline: without genuine management commitment, any quality system is just paperwork. Below is a quick index of the eight management responsibilities:

2.1 Management Commitment

Original Text

2.1 Management Commitment

(a) Senior management has the ultimate responsibility to ensure an effective pharmaceutical quality system is in place to achieve the quality objectives, and that roles, responsibilities, and authorities are defined, communicated and implemented throughout the company.

(b) Management should:

  1. Participate in the design, implementation, monitoring and maintenance of an effective pharmaceutical quality system;
  2. Demonstrate strong and visible support for the pharmaceutical quality system and ensure its implementation throughout their organisation;
  3. Ensure a timely and effective communication and escalation process exists to raise quality issues to the appropriate levels of management;
  4. Define individual and collective roles, responsibilities, authorities and inter-relationships of all organisational units related to the pharmaceutical quality system. Ensure these interactions are communicated and understood at all levels of the organisation. An independent quality unit/structure with authority to fulfil certain pharmaceutical quality system responsibilities is required by regional regulations;
  5. Conduct management reviews of process performance and product quality and of the pharmaceutical quality system;
  6. Advocate continual improvement;
  7. Commit appropriate resources.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: The Hospital CEO and Patient Safety

Imagine a hospital CEO. Surgery is performed by surgeons, but if something goes wrong, who bears ultimate responsibility for patient safety? The CEO.

The CEO is ultimately responsible for all of the hospital's clinical quality systems (staff training, equipment maintenance, infection control, medication safety processes), even though he or she does not perform the surgery personally. The CEO must ensure that the right systems are in place, that sufficient resources are provided, and that mechanisms exist to detect problems promptly and escalate them.

The same applies to senior management in a pharmaceutical company: they are ultimately responsible for the effectiveness of the PQS, even though day-to-day GMP execution is carried out by Production and QA.

"Ultimate responsibility cannot be delegated": this is the most important spirit of Q10 2.1(a).

Regulatory Focus: Independent Quality Unit

2.1(b)(4) states clearly: "An independent quality unit/structure with authority to fulfil certain pharmaceutical quality system responsibilities is required by regional regulations."

Why is an independent quality unit so important?

  • Commercial pressure vs. quality decisions: If QA reports to the VP of Production, then when a batch has a minor deviation and the delivery deadline is tight, the QA head may face a dilemma between releasing and rejecting, because the boss's KPI is shipment volume, not quality. An independent QA structure removes this conflict of interest.
  • Regulatory requirement: FDA 21 CFR 211.22 explicitly requires the quality control unit to have independent authority, including the authority to approve or reject the release of any batch. EU GMP Annex 16 has similar requirements.
  • The CDMO challenge: In a CDMO setting, clients may pressure the CDMO's quality unit to release problematic batches. An independent QA structure gives the CDMO's QA head the organizational authority to say "no".

In short, independent QA is a firewall that stops commercial pressure from eroding quality judgment.

Key Concepts

  • "Visible support": It is not enough to sign documents. Senior managers need to be actually present in quality activities: attending management review, responding on CAPA progress, and talking about quality at all-hands meetings. "Visible" is the keyword; staff must be able to see that leadership cares about quality.
  • "Escalation process": Quality issues need an open channel for escalation. For example, when a critical deviation occurs, is there a clear SOP stating who must be informed and within how many hours? Many FDA Warning Letters involve quality problems that were not escalated to senior management in a timely manner.
  • The seven management responsibilities: Note that these seven items are phrased as "Management should", which makes them expectations, not options. Q10 treats them as the basic elements of an effective PQS.
2.2 Quality Policy

Original Text

2.2 Quality Policy

(a) Senior management should establish a quality policy that describes the overall intentions and direction of the company related to quality.

(b) The quality policy should include an expectation to comply with applicable regulatory requirements and should facilitate continual improvement of the pharmaceutical quality system.

(c) The quality policy should be communicated to and understood by personnel at all levels in the company.

(d) The quality policy should be reviewed periodically for continuing effectiveness.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • The essence of a quality policy: The quality policy is senior management's declaration to the whole company of "our position and direction on quality". It is usually a short statement (one page or less), posted on the plant wall and printed on the first page of the Quality Manual. It should be specific enough to guide decisions, yet not so detailed that it needs frequent revision.
  • Two Required Elements (2.2b)：
    • Comply with regulatory requirements: this is the baseline
    • Facilitate continual improvement: this is the direction
  • "Understood by" vs. "Communicated to": Note that Q10 does not only say "communicated"; it also requires "understood". If an inspector asks a plant operator "What is your company's quality policy?", the operator should be able to explain it in his or her own words rather than recite a passage.
  • Periodic review (2.2d): The quality policy is not a one-and-done document. When company strategy changes (for example, entering a new market or adding a new product line) or the external environment changes (for example, new regulatory requirements), the quality policy should be updated accordingly.

Inspector Hot Spots

  • The quality policy is posted on the wall but employees cannot explain its meaning: "communicated but not understood"
  • The quality policy has not been reviewed for years and is out of step with the current business scope
  • The quality policy does not mention continual improvement and only stresses regulatory compliance, lacking the forward-looking direction that Q10 requires
2.3 Quality Planning

Original Text

2.3 Quality Planning

(a) Senior management should ensure the quality objectives needed to implement the quality policy are defined and communicated.

(b) Quality objectives should be supported by all relevant levels of the company.

(c) Quality objectives should align with the company's strategies and be consistent with the quality policy.

(d) Management should provide the appropriate resources and training to achieve the quality objectives.

(e) Performance indicators that measure progress against quality objectives should be established, monitored, communicated regularly and acted upon as appropriate as described in Section 4.1 of this document.

Tutorial Commentary

Quality Hierarchy: Deployment of the Quality Policy

The Q10 quality planning framework can be understood through the following three-tier structure:

Quality Policy (senior management statement, directional)
    ↓  "We are committed to providing patients with safe and effective medicines"
    ↓
Quality Objectives (department/annual, measurable)
    ↓  "2024: OOS rate < 0.5%; on-time CAPA closure rate > 95%"
    ↓
KPIs / Performance Indicators (operational level, trackable)
       "Monthly OOS count, average CAPA closure days, deviation trend analysis"

This structure ensures that senior management's quality intent can be turned into numbers that are tracked daily and weekly, rather than empty slogans.

Management Parallel: Hoshin Kanri (Analogy)

The Q10 quality planning framework closely matches Hoshin Kanri (policy deployment / strategy deployment) from Japanese management practice. The core ideas of Hoshin Kanri are:

  • Catchball: Top management proposes a policy, and each level below "catches, adjusts and passes it back", ensuring that every level understands and agrees with the objectives. This corresponds to Q10 2.3(b), "supported by all relevant levels".
  • X-Matrix: A single matrix links strategic objectives, annual objectives, improvement projects, metrics (KPIs) and owners. This corresponds to Q10 2.3(c), "align with company's strategies".
  • PDCA cycle: KPI progress is tracked regularly, and if targets are missed the root cause is analyzed and adjustments are made. This corresponds to Q10 2.3(e), "monitored, communicated regularly and acted upon".

For a CDMO, integrating the Q10 quality plan into the company's annual Hoshin plan is an effective way to bring "quality" into the core of company strategy.

Key Concepts

  • SMART objectives: Quality objectives should be Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. "Improve quality" is not an objective; "reduce the Q3 OOS rate from 1.2% to 0.8%" is.
  • Training is also a resource (2.3d): Many companies invest heavily in equipment and facilities but neglect personnel training. Q10 explicitly lists training as one of the resources needed to achieve quality objectives.
  • The role of performance indicators: Section 4.1 explains further how KPIs drive management review. Note that KPIs here are not just for "looking at the numbers"; they must be "acted upon", meaning that action is required when the numbers look bad.
2.4 Resource Management

Original Text

2.4 Resource Management

(a) Management should determine and provide adequate and appropriate resources (human, financial, materials, facilities and equipment) to implement and maintain the pharmaceutical quality system and continually improve its effectiveness.

(b) Management should ensure that resources are appropriately applied to a specific product, process or site.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • Five categories of resources: Q10 explicitly lists five categories of resources: human, financial, materials, facilities and equipment. Failures of the quality system can often be traced to a shortage or inappropriate allocation of one of these categories.
  • "Adequate and appropriate": Simply "having resources" is not enough. Resources must be suitable for the task. For example, if an analytical method requires a PhD-level scientist but is assigned to an untrained technician, the resource is not appropriate even if headcount is sufficient.
  • The importance of 2.4(b): resource allocation: In large pharmaceutical companies or CDMOs with many products and sites, resources (especially QA staff and equipment) must be allocated across products and sites. Q10 requires that this allocation be "appropriately applied": higher-risk products and sites should receive a proportionally larger share of resources, not an equal split.

Inspector's Perspective (FDA)

  • Inspectors often ask: "How many people in your QA department oversee how many batches?" Clearly insufficient staffing is a common Form 483 observation.
  • Insufficient budget for calibration and maintenance, leading to critical instruments being used past their due dates, is another common problem.
  • "We are short-staffed" is not a legitimate excuse for a GMP violation; management is responsible for ensuring that resources are adequate (2.4a).
2.5 Internal Communication

Original Text

2.5 Internal Communication

(a) Management should ensure appropriate communication processes are established and implemented within the organisation.

(b) Communications processes should ensure the flow of appropriate information between all levels of the company.

(c) Communication processes should ensure the appropriate and timely escalation of certain product quality and pharmaceutical quality system issues.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • Two-way communication: 2.5(b) emphasizes "all levels". Quality information flows not only top-down (policies, objectives) but also bottom-up (deviation reports, operational problems) and laterally (cross-department quality issues).
  • "Timely escalation": This is the most critical element of quality communication. Recalls, batch failures and major deviations must reach senior management within hours, not days, so that an effective crisis response is possible.
  • Communication processes need to be documented: Q10 requires communication processes to be "established" and "implemented", which means an SOP or procedure must define who reports what, to whom, and within what timeframe.

Real-World Example

In the 2022 infant formula contamination incidents, communication failure was one of the core problems: plant operators found positive environmental monitoring (EM) results, but this information was not escalated to management and the FDA in time.

An effective internal communication process should work like a "fire alarm system": triggered automatically, notifying immediately and reaching the right people. It should not depend on someone "remembering to speak up".

2.6 Management Review

Original Text

2.6 Management Review

(a) Senior management should be responsible for pharmaceutical quality system governance through management review to ensure its continuing suitability and effectiveness.

(b) Management should assess the conclusions of periodic reviews of process performance and product quality and of the pharmaceutical quality system, as described in Sections 3 and 4.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

  • "Governance": This word is chosen very precisely. Management review is not just listening to reports; it is governance of the overall direction of the PQS, confirming that the system remains suitable and effective. Senior managers should make decisions in the review, not merely "take note".
  • Management Review vs. Product Quality Review (PQR)：
    • The PQR (Product Quality Review) is an annual product-by-product review (Section 3)
    • Management review (Section 4) is a senior-level review of the entire PQS, with inputs that include the conclusions of PQRs across multiple products
    • The two feed each other as inputs and outputs, forming a closed loop of quality management
  • "Periodic" does not mean "annual": Q10 says "periodic" and does not mandate a frequency. High-risk products or systems undergoing improvement may need more frequent management review (for example, quarterly).

Common Pitfall

The most common failure mode of management review: the meeting is held, signed, and the records are filed, but there are no action items, or the action items are never tracked.

Inspectors will check whether the outputs (action items) of management review are followed up. Are owners and due dates assigned? If action items from the previous review are still open, is there a reasonable explanation?

2.7 Management of Outsourced Activities and Purchased Materials

Original Text

2.7 Management of Outsourced Activities and Purchased Materials

The pharmaceutical quality system, including the management responsibilities described in this section, extends to the control and review of any outsourced activities and quality of purchased materials. The pharmaceutical company is ultimately responsible to ensure processes are in place to assure the control of outsourced activities and quality of purchased materials. These processes should incorporate quality risk management and include:

(a) Assessing prior to outsourcing operations or selecting material suppliers, the suitability and competence of the other party to carry out the activity or provide the material using a defined supply chain (e.g., audits, material evaluations, qualification);

(b) Defining the responsibilities and communication processes for quality-related activities of the involved parties. For outsourced activities, this should be included in a written agreement between the contract giver and contract acceptor;

(c) Monitoring and review of the performance of the contract acceptor or the quality of the material from the provider, and the identification and implementation of any needed improvements;

(d) Monitoring incoming ingredients and materials to ensure they are from approved sources using the agreed supply chain.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Principle: Outsourcing Does Not Transfer Accountability

The core principle of Q10 2.7: "The pharmaceutical company is ultimately responsible."

No matter who you outsource manufacturing, testing or packaging to, you remain the responsible party before patients and regulators. If the outsourced party fails (batch contamination, data falsification, unauthorized changes), it is you, not the contractor, who has your license revoked or receives a Warning Letter.

This principle was learned the hard way in the 2008 heparin contamination (Baxter/SPL): Baxter's API supplier (SPL) in China adulterated the heparin with oversulfated chondroitin sulfate (OSCS), causing 81 deaths. Baxter, as the company holding the NDA, bore the main legal liability and public blame, even though it had not adulterated the product itself.

CDMO Application

For a CDMO, the 2.7 perspective involves a switch worth noting:

The CDMO is the Contract Acceptor: Q10 is written from the perspective of the pharmaceutical company (Contract Giver) and sets out what the Contract Giver must do. But as a Contract Acceptor, the CDMO needs to understand the obligations its clients (Contract Givers) have under Q10, so it can prepare compliant conditions of acceptance in advance:

  • What 2.7(a) means for a CDMO: The client is obliged to assess the CDMO's "suitability and competence" before formally outsourcing. This means the CDMO must be ready to accept client audits and provide documentation on facilities, equipment and personnel competence. A CDMO that cannot pass the client's qualified supplier assessment cannot become a qualified Contract Acceptor.
  • 2.7(b) Written agreement: Q10 requires that quality responsibilities for outsourced activities be clearly defined in a written contract. This is a key document in negotiations between a CDMO and its client: the QAA (Quality Agreement) must cover all the quality-related division of responsibilities that Q10 requires.
  • 2.7(c) Ongoing oversight: The client (Contract Giver) has an obligation to monitor the CDMO's performance continuously through periodic audits, KPI tracking and deviation sharing. The CDMO needs to establish mechanisms that support this transparency.
  • 2.7(d) Supply chain control: If the CDMO uses raw materials that it purchases itself, the client must confirm that these materials come from suppliers on the client-approved Approved Vendor List. The CDMO may not change raw material suppliers on its own unless it obtains the client's written approval.

CDMO business development advice: Proactively showing clients that your PQS fully covers the requirements of Q10 2.7 is an effective way to win their trust. Preparing a "Contract Acceptor Compliance Package" containing a QAA template, audit history and a KPI dashboard can significantly shorten the client's due diligence time.

Key Concepts

  • Ownership Change ScenariosQ10 gives "through acquisitions" as an example, but the actual scenarios are broader:

Practical Example: A Pharmaceutical Company Acquires a CDMO

Suppose ABC Pharma acquires XYZ CDMO. After the acquisition is completed:

  • Definition of responsibilities (2.8a): ABC must decide how responsibility for QA release of XYZ's existing client products will transition. Does the existing Qualified Person (QP) designation at XYZ remain valid? Which batches will continue to be released in XYZ's name, and which will be released in ABC's name?
  • Information transfer (2.8b): All of XYZ's batch records, equipment validation reports, personnel training records and client QAA contracts must be properly transferred under ABC's PQS management framework. If XYZ uses paper records, ABC needs to assess the resources required for digitization and integration.
  • Complete PQS transfer: Q10 requires that the entire PQS be transferred, not just the documents. ABC needs to assess whether XYZ's PQS meets Q10 standards and draw up a plan to close the gaps; otherwise ABC, as the new owner, will inherit these compliance risks.

This scenario is very common in the frequent pharmaceutical M&A of recent years: pharmaceutical companies buy CDMOs to gain manufacturing capacity, but often underestimate the complexity and cost of PQS integration.

Practice Questions

  1. The CEO of a small pharmaceutical company says: "Quality is the QA department's job; I only handle business objectives." From the perspective of Q10 2.1, explain the regulatory risks of this attitude and the systemic problems it may cause.
  2. At a CDMO, the VP of QA is in conflict with the VP of Sales: the VP of Sales demands release of a batch of injectables with a minor appearance deviation (the client is pressing for delivery), while the VP of QA believes a full investigation is needed before a decision can be made. Given the "independent quality unit" requirement of Q10 2.1(b)(4), how should the company's organizational structure be designed to protect QA's independent decision-making authority?
  3. A pharmaceutical company plans to outsource the sterile filling of one of its antibiotic products to an overseas CDMO. Under Q10 2.7, list the specific work the company must do before outsourcing, at contract signing, during execution and when materials arrive on site.
  4. A large group company acquires a mid-sized API manufacturer. During the 90-day integration period after closing, what must management ensure has been addressed under Q10 2.8? If a critical batch record is lost during the transition, what are the regulatory consequences?

Section Summary

  • Management responsibility is the foundation of the PQS: The eight clauses of Q10 Section 2 form the management framework of the PQS. Without genuine commitment from senior management, no quality system can operate effectively over the long term.
  • Ultimate responsibility cannot be delegated (2.1a): Senior Management is ultimately responsible for the effectiveness of the PQS, including operations in which they are not directly involved, just as a hospital CEO is responsible for patient safety.
  • An independent quality unit is a hard regulatory requirement (2.1b4): Preventing commercial pressure from eroding quality decisions is an explicit FDA/EMA GMP requirement, not an option.
  • The three-tier structure of Quality Policy → Quality Objectives → KPIs (2.2-2.3): Quality intent must be translated into measurable objectives and performance indicators to take effect in practice, which corresponds to the Hoshin Kanri strategy deployment framework.
  • Outsourcing does not transfer accountability (2.7): The company remains ultimately responsible for outsourced activities, and a written agreement (QAA) is a regulatory requirement. From the CDMO perspective, you need to understand the client's (Contract Giver's) obligations in order to fulfil the Contract Acceptor role well.
  • A change in product ownership requires a complete PQS transfer (2.8): Defining responsibilities and transferring information are the core quality tasks in an acquisition or divestiture, and underestimating them will expose major gaps in regulatory inspections.

Section 3 Overview: Continual Improvement of Process Performance and Product Quality

Original Text

3. CONTINUAL IMPROVEMENT OF PROCESS PERFORMANCE AND PRODUCT QUALITY

This section describes the lifecycle stage goals and the four specific pharmaceutical quality system elements that augment regional requirements to achieve the ICH Q10 objectives, as defined in Section 1.5. It does not restate all regional GMP requirements.

The four PQS elements are:

  • Process performance and product quality monitoring system;
  • Corrective action and preventive action (CAPA) system;
  • Change management system;
  • Management review of process performance and product quality.

These elements should be applied in a manner that is appropriate and proportionate to each of the product lifecycle stages, recognising the differences among, and the different goals of, each stage. Throughout the product lifecycle, companies are encouraged to evaluate opportunities for innovative approaches to improve product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Is Q10's "Additional Contribution"?

Section 3 opens by saying that it describes content that "augments regional requirements" (supplements regional GMP requirements); it does not repeat GMP. In other words, if you only comply with GMP, you already have the beginnings of some of these elements. Q10 requires you to make them systematic and structured, and to apply them throughout the product lifecycle.

The relationship among the four elements is: PPQMS (monitor) → CAPA (respond) → Change Management (change) → Management Review (overview), forming a closed-loop mechanism for continual improvement.

3.1 Lifecycle Stage Goals

Original Text

3.1 Lifecycle Stage Goals

The goals of each product lifecycle stage are described below.

3.1.1 Pharmaceutical Development

The goal of pharmaceutical development activities is to design a product and its manufacturing process to consistently deliver the intended performance and meet the needs of patients and healthcare professionals, and regulatory authorities and internal customers' requirements. Approaches to pharmaceutical development are described in ICH Q8. The results of exploratory and clinical development studies, while outside the scope of this guidance, are inputs to pharmaceutical development.

3.1.2 Technology Transfer

The goal of technology transfer activities is to transfer product and process knowledge between development and manufacturing, and within or between manufacturing sites to achieve product realisation. This knowledge forms the basis for the manufacturing process, control strategy, process validation approach and ongoing continual improvement.

3.1.3 Commercial Manufacturing

The goals of manufacturing activities include achieving product realisation, establishing and maintaining a state of control and facilitating continual improvement. The pharmaceutical quality system should assure that the desired product quality is routinely met, suitable process performance is achieved, the set of controls are appropriate, improvement opportunities are identified and evaluated, and the body of knowledge is continually expanded.

3.1.4 Product Discontinuation

The goal of product discontinuation activities is to manage the terminal stage of the product lifecycle effectively. For product discontinuation, a pre-defined approach should be used to manage activities such as retention of documentation and samples and continued product assessment (e.g., complaint handling and stability) and reporting in accordance with regulatory requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Four Lifecycle Stages

  • Pharmaceutical Development: the design stage of the product and process. Q10 links with Q8: Q8 provides the specific development methodology (QbD, Design Space), and Q10 brings this stage into the overall PQS framework.
  • Technology Transfer: knowledge is transferred from development to manufacturing, or between manufacturing sites. Note that the success of technology transfer directly affects subsequent process validation and commercial quality.
  • Commercial Manufacturing: five key phrases: product realisation, state of control, continual improvement, improvement opportunities identified, and body of knowledge expanded. These five points are the core tasks of the PQS at the commercial manufacturing stage.
  • Product Discontinuation: an often-neglected stage. Q10 requires a pre-defined approach to discontinuation, covering retention of documentation and samples, continued complaint handling, and stability evaluation.

Analogy: The Lifecycle as Four Stages of a Building

Development = the architect draws the plans and runs structural tests (Q8 ensures the design is robust)

Technology transfer = the construction crew receives the plans and builds a trial section (ensuring the design intent can be realised)

Commercial manufacturing = the building is in use and needs ongoing maintenance and monitoring of power, plumbing and elevators (state of control), while continually looking for ways to optimize

Discontinuation = before demolition, archive the records, relocate the residents and follow up afterwards (ensuring no leftover problems affect residents' safety)

Discontinuation is not "the end"; it is an "orderly wind-down". Stock is still on the market, patients are still using the product, and the quality responsibility has not gone away.

Key Point: PQS Implications of Technology Transfer

Q10 emphasizes that technology transfer conveys "knowledge", not just SOPs. A traditional transfer hands over only a table of process parameters; a transfer in the spirit of Q10 also conveys:

  • Why this parameter range was chosen (the scientific understanding behind it)
  • The relationship between critical quality attributes (CQAs) and process parameters (CPPs)
  • Known process risks and the strategies to address them
  • Previous failures and lessons learned

If this knowledge is not transferred systematically, the receiving site can only follow the instructions by rote without real understanding, and will struggle to find the root cause when the process deviates.

Original Text

3.2.1 Process Performance and Product Quality Monitoring System

Pharmaceutical companies should plan and execute a system for the monitoring of process performance and product quality to ensure a state of control is maintained. An effective monitoring system provides assurance of the continued capability of processes and controls to produce a product of desired quality and to identify areas for continual improvement.

The process performance and product quality monitoring system should:

  6. Provide knowledge to enhance process understanding, enrich the design space (where established), and enable innovative approaches to process validation.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: The PPQMS Is the Process's "Health Monitor"

Imagine a patient wearing a continuous blood pressure monitor and a glucose monitor. Values are measured every few minutes. While they stay within the normal range (state of control), things carry on; if they start to drift high (trend alert), the doctor steps in to adjust (feedforward), acting before illness sets in; if a value suddenly goes abnormal (out of control), an alarm sounds immediately and CAPA is triggered.

The PPQMS plays exactly this role for the process:

  • Blood pressure/glucose = CQAs and CPPs (critical quality attributes and process parameters)
  • Normal range = state of control
  • Trend alert = feedforward
  • Sudden abnormality = trigger for CAPA (corrective and preventive action)
  • Long-term health report = Management Review

Core Concept: Control Strategy

Q10 Glossary definition: "A planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality."

Components of a control strategy (item a):

  • Parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components
  • Facility and equipment operating conditions
  • In-process controls (IPC)
  • Finished product specifications
  • The associated methods and frequency of monitoring

Key point: the control strategy must "facilitate timely feedback/feedforward". This means real-time feedback and feedforward, not after-the-fact remediation, but proactive prediction and management.

Key Point: The Regulatory Strategic Significance of Item (f)

Item (f) of the PPQMS says that the knowledge produced by the monitoring system can "enrich the design space" and support innovative approaches to process validation.

This means the real batch data accumulated during commercial manufacturing can be used to:

  • Expand or adjust the original Design Space (a regulatory filing is required)
  • Support the data package for Continued Process Verification
  • Provide historical support for Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT)

This is the direct link between the Q10 PPQMS and Stage 3 (Continued Process Verification) of the FDA process validation guidance.

CDMO Practical Application

A CDMO typically manufactures products for dozens of clients at the same time. The challenge for the PPQMS is how to design monitoring systems proportionate to the complexity of different products.

  • High-risk products (e.g., lyophilized injectables): detailed CPP trend analysis for every batch, SPC control charts, periodic Cpk calculation
  • Medium-risk products (e.g., oral solid dosage forms): IPC trending at critical steps + quarterly batch review
  • Low-risk products: annual statistical analysis is sufficient

The proportionate approach is the spirit of Q10: not every product needs the same depth of monitoring, and QRM is used to decide the monitoring intensity.

Key Point: The Most Common CAPA Deficiencies (Audit Findings)

  • Root cause analysis stops at the surface: "operator error" is not a root cause; the real question is why the system allowed the error to happen.

The three systems are tightly interconnected and cannot operate in isolation:

  • The results of all three systems → are consolidated into Management Review
  • Management Review decisions → feed back to guide the prioritization of the three systems

This cycle is the concrete embodiment of Q10's PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act) at the level of the quality management system.

In-Depth Analysis: Correction vs. Corrective Action vs. Preventive Action

These are the three concepts most often confused in the pharmaceutical industry, and distinguishing them clearly is the foundation of a CAPA system:

ISO 9000:2005 definition note: "Corrective action is taken to prevent recurrence whereas preventive action is taken to prevent occurrence." This sentence is the distinction most often cited in exams and audits.

Key Point: The Importance of "Prospective Evaluation Criteria"

A special challenge for CDMOs: the same change (e.g., an equipment replacement) may affect the products of several clients at once, each subject to different regional regulatory requirements.

  • Site change: involves a site transfer, usually an FDA Major Supplement + EMA Type II variation, with significant time and cost
  • Efficiency from a Design Space: for client products with a Design Space, equipment parameter adjustments within the Design Space do not require regulatory notification, saving considerable time and cost. This is the long-term payoff for a CDMO that helps clients establish a Design Space.

Structurally, Management Review is the point where information from the first three elements (PPQMS, CAPA, Change Management) comes together:

  • PPQMS monitoring conclusions → feed into Management Review item (a)(2)(ii)
  • CAPA effectiveness evaluation → feeds into Management Review item (a)(2)(iii)
  • Change Management outcomes → feed into Management Review item (a)(2)(iii)
  • Regulatory inspection findings + audit results → feed into Management Review item (a)(1)

Q10 emphasizes a "timely and effective communication and escalation process". Management review is not a one-off annual formality but a continuously operating escalation mechanism.

CDMO Practical Application: The APR/PQR Is Where Management Review Lands in Practice

The Annual Product Review (APR) / Product Quality Review (PQR) is the annual product quality report required by GMP worldwide, and it is the most direct practical expression of Q10 Management Review (3.2.4) + PQS Management Review (4.1).

A complete APR/PQR should cover the following (mapped to the Q10 review content):

  • Batch conformance statistics (← PPQMS conclusions)
  • OOS/deviation trend analysis (← PPQMS + CAPA)
  • CAPA completion status and effectiveness (← CAPA system)
  • List of changes for the year and impact assessments (← Change Management)
  • Stability data trends (← PPQMS)
  • Complaint and recall data (← external feedback)
  • Regulatory inspection findings and status of commitments (← 3.2.4(a)(1))
  • Supplier quality performance (← PPQMS extension)
  • Improvement action plan for the next year (← 3.2.4(b))

For a CDMO, a high-quality APR/PQR is an important interface for demonstrating quality management capability to clients, and also the basis on which clients show regulators that manufacturing is under control.

Key Point: Designing "Management Review at Various Levels"

Q10 allows Management Review to be "a series of reviews at various levels". Large companies usually design a multi-level review:

  • Operational level (per batch/weekly): shift supervisors review batch records and IPC data
  • Department level (monthly): QA monthly report, deviation/CAPA status
  • Site level (quarterly): product quality trends, review of major events
  • Company level (annually): APR/PQR consolidation, senior management decisions (resource allocation, strategic adjustments)

Key: the results of each level of review must have an "escalation pathway" to ensure that important quality issues reach decision-makers in time. This is precisely the core spirit of Q10 Section 4.

4. CONTINUAL IMPROVEMENT OF THE PHARMACEUTICAL QUALITY SYSTEM

This section describes activities that should be conducted to manage and continually improve the pharmaceutical quality system.

4.1 Management Review of the Pharmaceutical Quality System

Management should have a formal process for reviewing the pharmaceutical quality system on a periodic basis. The review should include:

  1. Measurement of achievement of pharmaceutical quality system objectives;
  2. Assessment of performance indicators that can be used to monitor the effectiveness of processes within the pharmaceutical quality system, such as:
    1. Complaint, deviation, CAPA and change management processes;
    2. Feedback on outsourced activities;
    3. Self-assessment processes including risk assessments, trending, and audits;
    4. External assessments such as regulatory inspections and findings and customer audits.

4.2 Monitoring of Internal and External Factors Impacting the Pharmaceutical Quality System

Factors monitored by management can include:

  1. Emerging regulations, guidance and quality issues that can impact the Pharmaceutical Quality System;
  2. Innovations that might enhance the pharmaceutical quality system;
  3. Changes in business environment and objectives;
  4. Changes in product ownership.

4.3 Outcomes of Management Review and Monitoring

The outcome of management review of the pharmaceutical quality system and monitoring of internal and external factors can include:

  1. Improvements to the pharmaceutical quality system and related processes;
  2. Allocation or reallocation of resources and/or personnel training;
  3. Revisions to quality policy and quality objectives;
  4. Documentation and timely and effective communication of the results of the management review and actions, including escalation of appropriate issues to senior management.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Section 3 vs. Section 4

Both Sections involve "Management Review", but at different levels:

  • Section 3.2.4: reviews the process performance and quality status of individual "products" (product-level review): how are the PPQMS results for this product, is CAPA effective, did the changes achieve what was expected?
  • Section 4.1: reviews the soundness of the whole "PQS" itself (system-level review): is the CAPA system operating efficiently? Can the Change Management process handle changes of varying complexity? Is our overall quality policy still appropriate?

This is the difference between "improving product quality" and "improving the quality system itself": Section 4 is the meta-layer of the PQS.

Analogy: The PQS "Annual Check-up"

Section 3.2.4 = monitoring each patient's blood pressure/glucose (the health status at product level)

Section 4.1 = assessing whether the whole hospital's medical system is working well (system level):

  • Are there enough doctors (quality staff)?
  • Is the diagnostic process (investigations and CAPA) efficient?
  • Is the hospital equipment (monitoring tools) keeping up with the latest technology?
  • Are there new regulations (new drug laws/new GMP guidelines) that require the hospital to change its processes?

Replace the hospital with a pharmaceutical plant and the questions map exactly. Section 4 is the annual check-up of the "hospital" that is the quality management system.

Key Point: The Practical Meaning of 4.2 Monitoring of External Factors

Q10 requires proactive monitoring of "external factors". For pharmaceutical companies this means specifically:

  • (a) Emerging regulations: FDA issues a new Process Validation Guidance (such as the 2011 version), or the EMA revises a GMP Annex; the quality system must respond to the update
  • (b) Innovations: once ICH Q12 (Life Cycle Management) is adopted, evaluate whether the Change Management system can use the new flexibility offered by the PACMP (Post-Approval Change Management Protocol)
  • (c) Business environment changes: the company is acquired, enters new markets, or expands its product portfolio; the PQS resource allocation and process design must be adjusted accordingly
  • (d) Product ownership changes: product licences are transferred out or assigned to a CDMO; special PQS interface management is needed

CDMO Practical Application: The APR as a Systematic Improvement Tool

In a CDMO setting, the Section 4 PQS management review is usually carried out through two mechanisms:

  • Site Quality Management Review: held every six months or annually, chaired by the site head/head of quality, reviewing the site-wide CAPA completion rate, deviation trends, audit findings, status of regulatory commitments, and adequacy of resources
  • Quality System Metrics Dashboard: visualizes the performance indicators required by 4.1(b), so management can see the overall health of the PQS every month

Clients have the right to ask the CDMO for a summary of the results of the site-level Quality System Review as part of their oversight. Q10 Section 2.7 (management of outsourced activities) is directly relevant here.

Section Summary

  • Four lifecycle stages: development → technology transfer → commercial manufacturing → discontinuation; Q10 requires the four PQS elements to operate at every stage in an appropriate and proportionate manner
  • PPQMS: establish the control strategy using QRM, continually monitor process performance and product quality, identify sources of variation, maintain the state of control, and provide the knowledge base for continual improvement
  • CAPA: three distinct concepts: Correction (fix this event), Corrective Action (eliminate the cause of recurrence), Preventive Action (eliminate the cause of potential occurrence); depth of investigation commensurate with risk level; effectiveness must be verified
  • Change Management: dual regulatory track: changes within the Design Space need no regulatory filing but still need internal Change Management; changes outside the Design Space or affecting approved documents must be filed per regional requirements; achievement of objectives must be verified after implementation
  • Management Review (3.2.4): a product-level review integrating the results of the PPQMS, CAPA and Change Management; the APR/PQR is its most direct practical implementation
  • Section 4: Continual Improvement of the PQS: the PQS itself must also be reviewed and improved (PDCA at the system level); monitoring external factors (new regulations, technological advances, business changes) ensures the PQS remains suitable and effective
  • Interconnection of the four elements: PPQMS → CAPA → Change Management → Management Review form a complete closed loop, and this loop is Q10's core added value over basic GMP

Tutorial Commentary

How the Glossary Is Built

The ICH Q10 glossary has one important feature: it does not define everything itself, but borrows existing ICH and ISO definitions first, and develops its own Q10 definition only when no suitable one exists. This reflects the systematic and consistent approach of ICH guidelines.

  • Source: ISO 9000:2005: general quality management terms (e.g. Capability, Continual Improvement, Corrective/Preventive Action, Quality Manual, Quality Policy, Quality Planning)
  • Source: ICH Q8: Design Space (a pharmaceutical development concept)
  • Source: ICH Q9: Quality, Quality Risk Management
  • Defined in ICH Q10: core concepts such as Change Management, Control Strategy, Enabler, Knowledge Management, PQS, Product Realisation, State of Control

Notes on the First Few Terms

  • Capability of a Process: in statistical terms, the Cp/Cpk indices. ICH Q10 deliberately includes this term to stress the quantitative side of State of Control: your process must not only be "compliant", it must also have the statistical capability to keep delivering acceptable product.
  • Change Management: note that this is a "systematic approach", comprising five steps: propose, evaluate, approve, implement, review. It differs from CAPA: CAPA is a response to a problem, whereas Change Management controls any change (including planned improvements).
  • Continual Improvement: emphasizes "recurring" activity. Continual improvement is not a one-off event but a mechanism built into the system. ISO uses "continual" (intermittent, deliberate) rather than "continuous" (uninterrupted).
  • Control StrategyQ8Q10: this is one of the outputs of the Q8 QbD framework and also a core tool of the Q10 PQS. It covers the whole control network from CQAs to CPPs/CMAs, and is more than a specification: it also includes monitoring frequency and methods.
  • Design SpaceQ8: the definition comes from Q8 but appears in the Q10 glossary, a direct reflection of how Q8, Q9 and Q10 are integrated.
  • Feedback / Feedforward: Q10 specifically explains that this pair of concepts can be applied at the technical level (process control systems) as well as the management level (information flow in the quality system).

Knowledge Management

Q10 defines knowledge management through four actions: acquiring, analysing, storing and disseminating.

For a CDMO, this means that the client process data received at technology transfer, experimental results from process development, and production data from commercial batches should all be managed systematically rather than locked away on individual engineers' computers. Knowledge management is the key mechanism that gives the PQS a "memory".

Product Realisation

This is one of the three main objectives of ICH Q10. Note that its definition covers three kinds of customer:

  • Patients: the end users; safety and efficacy
  • Healthcare professionals + Regulatory authorities: external regulatory needs
  • Internal customers: the needs of downstream processes, packaging, warehousing and so on

Product Realisation is not just "making the product", but "making a product that meets the needs of all stakeholders".

State of Control vs. State of Compliance: An Important Distinction

State of Compliance: your product/process meets regulatory requirements and specifications. This is the minimum threshold: you have "met the standard".

State of Control: a set of controls that consistently provides assurance of continued process performance and product quality. This is proactive management: you are "in command".

An analogy: a car whose brake pads are right at the wear limit (within specification) is in a State of Compliance. A well-run vehicle maintenance system that replaces the brake pads before they approach the wear limit, so that braking performance is always good, is a State of Control.

The FDA position: State of Control is an important dimension that FDA CDER evaluates in PQAS (Process Quality Assurance Section) inspections. Simply having "no OOS" does not equal State of Control. You also need to demonstrate trend management, preventive maintenance, periodic process capability assessment (PPQR) and similar mechanisms.

Quality Risk Management (QRM)

Q9

An Analogy for the Q8, Q9 and Q10 Trilogy: Architect + Safety Consultant + Building Management System

Q8 (Design) = the architect: uses a scientific approach to design a building of excellent quality: choice of materials, structure, Design Space (the tolerance range of the building), Control Strategy (the construction acceptance criteria).

Q9 (Risk) = the safety consultant: assesses what risks could arise at every point in the building: fire, earthquake, water leaks. Uses tools such as FMEA and FTA to identify the risks to address first, and passes these to the architect and the building manager for reference.

Q10 (System) = the building management system (BMS): once the building is complete, monitors every system (power, HVAC, fire protection) 24/7 to confirm it is working normally. Starts CAPA immediately when an abnormality is found, performs regular management reviews, and ensures that the building remains in a State of Control throughout its lifecycle.

A building without a BMS (Q10) will deteriorate over time, however perfect the design. A BMS without an architect (Q8) manages a building without knowing where the critical points are. None of the three can be left out.

Tutorial Commentary

Practical Meaning of Risk-Based Inspections

When a manufacturer demonstrates an effective PQS (Scenario 2), the regulatory authority can apply risk-based inspection, which includes:

  • Lower inspection frequency: for a manufacturer with a good history, FDA CDER may move from inspecting every 2 years to every 3-4 years
  • Narrower inspection scope: inspectors can use the manufacturer's Annual Product Review and PPQR to focus first on the highest-risk products
  • Surveillance vs. For-Cause: an effective PQS means the manufacturer mainly faces routine surveillance inspections rather than for-cause inspections triggered by recalls or OOS results

Example: under the FDA GDUFA system, a manufacturer's GDUFA fees are linked to inspection frequency. A manufacturer with a mature PQS and a good inspection history often moves into a lower-frequency inspection cycle.

CDMO Practice: PAI Preparation and PQS Maturity

Annex 1 is especially important for a CDMO:

  • Pre-Approval Inspection (PAI): an FDA inspection of the manufacturing site before an NDA/ANDA is approved. A mature PQS (Scenario 4) lets the PAI inspector build confidence quickly, shortens the inspection, and lowers the likelihood of Form 483 observations.
  • GDUFA review goal dates: FDA commits to completing review of an ANDA under GDUFA within 10 months, but if there is a problem at the PAI, the whole timeline slips. A well-prepared PQS is insurance against delaying the review timeline.
  • Leverage in client negotiations: a CDMO can show clients the maturity of its PQS (even its FDA inspection history) as a selling point: "reducing the client's regulatory risk".
  • Science-based review (Scenario 3): when a CDMO helps a client build full Process Understanding (Q8 QbD), reviewers in the FDA CMC review can understand and approve the product more quickly. A shorter review cycle is critical to the client's commercial launch timeline.

Analogy: Credit Score and Loan Interest Rate

Annex 1 works like a credit rating system in finance. A manufacturer (the borrower) that merely "complies with GMP" (Scenario 1) is like a credit score at the minimum threshold: it can borrow, but at the highest interest rate (most frequent inspections, strictest review).

As the manufacturer builds a more mature PQS and accumulates a good inspection record, its "credit score" rises and it starts to enjoy a lower "interest rate" (fewer inspections, faster reviews, greater flexibility for post-approval changes).

This analogy shows why the PQS is a long-term investment rather than a short-term cost: building a Q10-compatible PQS takes resources at first, but the regulatory trust accumulated over time brings the company lasting commercial returns.

Reading the PQS Model Diagram: Three Layers × Four Lifecycle Stages

The model diagram in Annex 2 is the "master architecture chart" of ICH Q10. Using a two-dimensional matrix, with the horizontal direction as the timeline and the vertical direction as the system level, it shows how the PQS runs through the whole product lifecycle.

  • Commercial Manufacturing: the main arena of the PQS, where PPQMS, CAPA and Change Management operate at full strength
  • Product Discontinuation: market withdrawal planning, retention sample management and archiving of technical documents, ensuring a smooth transition for continued patient supply

Vertical axis: the three system levels

  • Management Responsibilities: the top-level driving force, ensuring the PQS has resources, policy and culture
  • PQS Elements: the four elements in Section 3: PPQMS, CAPA, Change Management and Management Review
  • PQS Enablers: Knowledge Management (KM) and Quality Risk Management (QRM), the underlying infrastructure

Technology Transfer: the Core Business of a CDMO

The Technology Transfer stage in the model diagram is often the most frequent and most complex stage for a CDMO (every new client or product goes through it). The core tasks of the Q10 PQS at this stage are:

  • Knowledge Transfer: ensure that the Process Understanding from development is fully passed to the CDMO, including CPP ranges, CQA linkages and FMEA results
  • Validation Bridging: how lab-scale and pilot-scale data support the design of the PPQ at commercial scale
  • Criticality of Change Management: any change during technology transfer that departs from the development specifications requires a formal Change Management process, to ensure regulatory transparency
  • Advance CAPA preparation: during technology transfer, identify potential failure modes in advance (FMEA) and put corresponding CAPA contingency plans in place

  • EMA GMP inspection frequency: EMA applies a risk-based inspection interval to manufacturers with an effective PQS (based on EudraGMDP scoring), which may lengthen the interval between inspections
  • Client audit efficiency: RFQs from new clients come with supplier questionnaires and site audits. A manufacturer with a mature PQS can respond with systematic documentation, reducing "audit cost" (person-hours)
  • Faster ANDA/NDA CMC review: when a CDMO has built full Q8 Process Understanding for a client, FDA CMC reviewers can complete the technical review more quickly, shortening the approval timeline

    • At the Q10 level: how should the Change Management System handle this "major change"? Which PQS documents are needed (change request, risk assessment, validation report, regulatory notification)?
    • Finally, if continuous manufacturing is implemented successfully and real-time release testing (RTRT) is established, which Scenario in the Annex 1 matrix is the company in? What regulatory opportunities can it obtain?

Analytical Framework for the Answers

Question 1 Framework

This question tests Annex 1 scenario identification and the operational definition of State of Control.

  • Effective PQS (Q10) + product/process understanding (Q8) + QRM (Q9) = Scenario 4 (the highest level)
  • State of Control indicators the PAI focuses on: completeness of the APR, CAPA effectiveness, PPQR trend analysis, process capability indices (Cpk)
  • Inadequate CAPA root cause analysis points to a deficiency in the Section 3.2.2 CAPA System, specifically an insufficient "investigate the cause" step

Question 2 Framework

Tests practical understanding of Management Responsibilities (Section 2).

  • A Quality Policy should include: a quality commitment, a statement of applicability to the organization, a framework for establishing/reviewing Quality Objectives, and a requirement that it be communicated and understood (required by Section 2.1)
  • Examples of Quality Objectives: "CAPA on-time closure rate > 95% in 2026" and "80% of site processes with Cpk > 1.33"
  • Section 4 (PQS management review) = evaluates the soundness of the PQS itself; Section 3.2.4 (PP&PQ management review) = evaluates the process performance of specific products

Question 3 Framework

Tests how PPQMS, CAPA and Change Management work together.

  • PPQMS detects a trend, which triggers a formal investigation (deviation report), then CAPA (eliminate the root cause: HEPA replacement interval too long, so revise the PM plan)
  • Revising the preventive maintenance plan (PM schedule) is a planned change, so Change Management is needed (assess the impact on the process and product quality; regulatory filing may not be required)
  • Role of Knowledge Management: the investigation report, root cause analysis and CAPA effectiveness assessment for this event should become part of the site's "environmental monitoring knowledge base", for use in future APRs and training

Question 4 Framework (Integration of the Q8/Q9/Q10 Trilogy)

This is the most difficult integration question, testing how the three guidelines work together in a "technological innovation" scenario.

  • Q8 aspect: the Design Space for continuous manufacturing must cover the time dimension (the time needed to reach steady state) and real-time variability of material attributes. Real-time monitoring is usually built with PAT (NIR, Raman), which is more complex than a traditional batch process
  • Q9 aspect: new risks include quality assurance during the transition to steady state, propagation of failure modes across equipment connected in series, and the impact of PAT sensor failure. FMEA is best for identifying equipment-level failures; HACCP suits hazard analysis of the whole process flow
  • Q10 aspect: this is a "Major Change" that requires a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) filing with FDA. The Change Management document package: (1) Change Request, (2) Impact Assessment, (3) Risk Assessment (citing Q9 FMEA results), (4) Validation Protocol & Report (PPQ), (5) Regulatory Strategy Memo
  • Annex 1 positioning: successful continuous manufacturing + RTRT + a complete PQS = Scenario 4. Regulatory opportunities: science-based review of post-approval changes (future process optimization without frequent filings) + risk-based inspections + innovative process validation (new validation approaches for continuous manufacturing)

Complete Document Summary of ICH Q10

  • Position and integration: ICH Q10 is the top-level framework for pharmaceutical quality systems. Built on the ISO 9001 quality management system, it integrates ICH Q8 (pharmaceutical development) and Q9 (risk management) to form the "Q trilogy": Q8 designs quality, Q9 manages risk, Q10 maintains the system
  • Three main objectives: (1) Product Realisation (meeting the product quality needs of all stakeholders); (2) State of Control (proactively managing process variability on a continuing basis); (3) Continual Improvement (periodically strengthening PQS capability)
  • Four PQS elements (Section 3): PPQMS (process performance and product quality monitoring system) → CAPA (corrective and preventive action) → Change Management System → Management Review of PP&PQ (management review of process performance and product quality)
  • Two Enablers: Knowledge Management (systematic knowledge management) + Quality Risk Management (from Q9). These two are the underlying infrastructure of the PQS and run through all four lifecycle stages
  • Core message of Annex 1: PQS maturity (Scenario 1 to 4) determines the degree of flexibility a manufacturer can obtain from regulatory authorities; full integration of Q8+Q9+Q10 (Scenario 4) brings the greatest regulatory opportunities, including risk-based inspection, science-based review, optimization of post-approval changes, and RTRT
  • Core message of Annex 2: the PQS model is a two-dimensional matrix. The horizontal axis is the four lifecycle stages (development, technology transfer, commercial manufacturing, discontinuation) and the vertical axis is the three system levels (management responsibilities, PQS elements, PQS enablers); the three Q10 objectives run through the whole matrix
  • State of Control vs. Compliance: compliance is the minimum threshold; State of Control is the advanced state of proactively managing process variability and continuously assuring quality. This is the fundamental step up that Q10 asks of the pharmaceutical industry
  • Key takeaways for CDMOs: technology transfer is the lifecycle stage a CDMO faces most often; a mature PQS directly affects PAI pass rates, client audit efficiency and long-term regulatory relationships; the Quality Manual is the "front door" through which the PQS is presented externally
