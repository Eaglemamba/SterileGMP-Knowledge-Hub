1. Introduction

Original Text

PART I: CONTINUOUS MANUFACTURING OF DRUG SUBSTANCES AND DRUG PRODUCTS

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Objective

This guideline describes scientific and regulatory considerations for the development, implementation, operation, and lifecycle management of continuous manufacturing (CM). Building on existing ICH Quality guidelines, this guideline provides clarification on CM concepts and describes scientific approaches and regulatory considerations specific to CM of drug substances and drug products.

1.2. Scope

This guideline applies to CM of drug substances and drug products for chemical entities and therapeutic proteins. It is applicable to CM for new products (e.g., new drugs, generic drugs, biosimilars) and the conversion of batch manufacturing to CM for existing products. The principles described in this guideline may also apply to other biological/biotechnological entities.

CM involves the continuous feeding of input materials into, the transformation of in-process materials within, and the concomitant removal of output materials from a manufacturing process. While this description may apply to an individual unit operation (e.g., process chromatography, tableting, perfusion cell culture), this guideline focuses on the integrated aspects of a CM system in which two or more unit operations are directly connected. In this context, any changes made in a unit operation of a CM system may have impact on downstream and upstream unit operations (e.g., back pressure resulting in forward mixing) and output material quality.

Fundamental aspects of CM that are generally not specific to technology, dosage form, or molecule type are described within the main body of this guideline. Annexes are provided to augment the main guideline by providing illustrative examples and considerations specific to certain modalities (e.g., chemical entities, therapeutic proteins), technologies, and production methods (e.g., integration of drug substance and drug product manufacturing). The examples and approaches described in these annexes are illustrative, and alternative approaches can be used. Topics that are broadly applicable to both CM and batch manufacturing are not in the scope of this guideline, and other existing ICH guidelines should be used as appropriate.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point | Q13 focuses on the "integrated system", not on a single unit operation

The scope of Q13 is "CM systems in which two or more unit operations are directly connected" - this seemingly technical definition has deep regulatory implications:

  • A single continuous unit operation (such as perfusion cell culture or continuous chromatography) is not the primary focus of Q13
  • A true CM process is an "integrated system" - there is direct material flow and mutual influence between the upstream and downstream unit operations
  • This integration creates a distinct challenge: a disturbance in one unit operation can affect the output quality of the whole system through the material flow

The Q13 control strategy must therefore be designed from a "system integration" perspective, rather than simply adding up the control strategies of the individual unit operations.

Analogy | CM vs. batch manufacturing is like an "assembly line" vs. a "custom workshop"

Traditional batch manufacturing is like a "custom workshop": each batch is a separate product, the next batch starts only after the previous one is finished, and every time is a fresh start. Continuous manufacturing is like a "car assembly line": raw materials are fed in and product comes out without interruption, and the whole system runs as one continuous whole. On an assembly line, if one station (unit operation) has a problem, it affects more than the product at that station - it changes the state of the entire line: material piles up upstream and the supply to downstream is cut off. This "pull one hair and the whole body moves" characteristic is exactly why Q13 needs to set specific expectations.

2. CM Concepts

Original Text

2. CM CONCEPTS

2.1. Different Modes of CM

CM can be applied to some or all unit operations in a manufacturing process. Examples of CM modes include:

  • A manufacturing approach in which some unit operations operate in a batch mode while others are integrated and operate in a continuous mode
  • A manufacturing approach in which all unit operations of a drug substance or drug product manufacturing process are integrated and operate in a continuous mode
  • A manufacturing approach in which drug substance and drug product unit operations are integrated across the boundary between drug substance and drug product to form a single CM process (i.e., the drug substance is continuously formed and processed into the drug product through integrated unit operations)

A manufacturing approach may incorporate surge lines or tanks to maintain a constant flow of material inputs and outputs in any mode of CM described above.

2.2. Batch definition

The ICH Q7 definition of a batch is applicable to all modes of CM, for both drug substances and drug products. Based on this definition, the size of a batch produced by CM can be defined in terms of one of the following:

  • Quantity of output material
  • Quantity of input material
  • Run time at a defined mass flow rate

Other approaches to define batch size are possible, if scientifically justified based on the characteristics of the CM process and Good Manufacturing Practice.

A batch size can also be defined as a range. For example, a batch size range can be established by defining a minimum and maximum run time.

Tutorial Commentary

Three ways to define CM batch size and what they mean

Option 1: Output Quantity
  Example: each run produces 500 kg of drug product
  Advantage: intuitive and closest to the traditional batch concept
  Challenge: the output of a CM system may vary (run time, yield fluctuation)

Option 2: Input Quantity
  Example: each run charges 10 kg API + 90 kg excipients
  Advantage: cost control is clear
  Challenge: input quantity ≠ output quantity (losses, material diversion)

Option 3: Run Time at a constant mass flow rate
  Example: running for 8 hours at a mass flow rate of 1 kg/min = one batch
  Advantage: most directly reflects the "time-space" nature of CM
  Common: the minimum/maximum batch size is mostly defined this way (Run Time Range)

Batch size defined as a range:
  Minimum run time (min run time) → minimum batch
  Maximum run time (max run time) → maximum batch
  → Flexibly responds to fluctuations in market demand without submitting a new batch size change application each time

Key Point | The regulatory advantage of a batch size "range"

Defining the batch size as a range (for example, "minimum 4 hours, maximum 12 hours of run time") is an important regulatory advantage of CM:

  • In traditional batch manufacturing, changing the batch size (for example, scale-up) usually requires a Prior Approval or Notification submission
  • If the CM system has a batch size range defined in advance in the application, adjusting within that range does not constitute an EC change
  • This allows the manufacturer to adjust the duration of each run flexibly according to market demand, while keeping the same validated process and control strategy

This is one of the major regulatory efficiency advantages of CM over batch manufacturing.

Analogy | CM batch definition is like a "shift-based garment factory"

A garment production line running 24 hours a day can define "one production batch" in different ways:

  • By output quantity: "producing 1000 pieces" is one batch - but each batch may take a different amount of time
  • By input quantity: "feeding in 50 rolls of fabric" is one batch - but how many pieces will be cut is uncertain
  • By run time (most common): "one shift (8 hours)" is one batch - the clearest, most traceable, and with the most complete GMP records
  • Time range: "anything from 4 to 12 hours counts as one batch, depending on order volume" - the most flexible, allowing flexible production planning

Practical Application | Strategic starting point for a CDMO introducing CM

For a CDMO, the strategy for moving from batch manufacturing to CM should consider the following:

  • Choose a hybrid mode as the entry point: first introduce continuous mode in high-turnover, relatively simple unit operations (such as blending and granulation) to build CM operating experience
  • Batch size definition strategy: adopting a "run time range" definition is recommended; pre-setting the minimum/maximum run time in the MAA maximizes future production flexibility
  • Invest in PAT infrastructure: effective operation of a CM system depends on real-time monitoring, so investment in PAT tools is a prerequisite for CM transformation and an important supporting element of the application dossier

Practice Questions

  1. In a hybrid mode of "partly continuous + partly batch", how should the batch size be defined? How can material tracking across the mode boundary be achieved?
  2. Q13 says "other approaches to defining batch size are possible, as long as they are scientifically justified" - propose a reasonable definition beyond the existing three, and explain its applicable scenarios and potential challenges.

The three CM modes in detail

Surge tanks/lines: in all modes, surge tanks are used to balance differences in mass flow rate between upstream and downstream and to prevent system interruptions caused by short-term flow mismatches. This is an important element of CM system design and directly affects the stability of system integration.

Section Summary

  • Position of Q13: the application layer of ICH Q7/Q8/Q9/Q10/Q11 in integrated continuous manufacturing systems; focuses on two or more directly connected unit operations
  • Defining features of CM: continuous feeding + continuous process transformation + continuous discharge; Q13 emphasizes the perspective of the "integrated system", not individual unit operations
  • Scope: DS and DP of chemical drugs and therapeutic proteins; new drugs/generics/biosimilars; and CM conversion of batch processes
  • Three CM modes: partly continuous (hybrid mode) → fully continuous → integrated across DS/DP; complexity increases in this order, and so do the control strategy challenges
  • Batch size definition: the Q7 definition of a batch applies; three approaches (output quantity/input quantity/run time); a "time range" definition is strongly recommended for maximum production flexibility
  • Surge tank: buffers differences in mass flow rate between unit operations; a key hardware element for maintaining the stability of system integration

3.1 Control Strategy

Original Text

3. SCIENTIFIC APPROACHES

3.1. Control Strategy

The development of a control strategy for CM is enabled by a holistic approach, considering aspects specific to CM (discussed below), principles outlined in ICH Q7, Q8, Q10 and Q11, and quality risk management described in ICH Q9.

3.1.1. State of Control

A state of control (ICH Q10) is a condition that provides assurance of continued process performance and product quality. The condition may vary, depending on the mode of CM and the specific process steps. For example, a state of control can be demonstrated for CM processes when a set of parameters (e.g., process parameters, quality attributes) are changing within specified ranges, but the processes are not necessarily in a steady state. Elements of the control strategy monitor the state of control and, when necessary, take appropriate actions to maintain control of the process. It is important to have mechanisms in place to evaluate the consistency of unit operations and the system, and to identify when parameters drift or trend within the specified range.

3.1.2. Process Dynamics

Knowledge of process dynamics is important to maintain a state of control in CM. Understanding the impact of transient events helps to identify risks to material quality and to develop an appropriate control strategy. Transient events that occur during CM operation may be planned (e.g., process start-up, shutdown and pause) or unplanned (e.g., disturbances) and can be described through time, process parameter and quality attribute values.

Process dynamics should be characterised to understand how output material quality is impacted by transient events. This characterisation could be done by determining properties such as residence time distribution (RTD). RTD characterises the time available for material transport and transformation, and is specific to the process, composition/formulation, material properties, equipment design and configuration, etc.

Process dynamics should be characterised over the planned operating ranges and anticipated input material variability using scientifically justified approaches. Appropriate methodologies (e.g., RTD studies, in silico modeling with experimental confirmation) should be used. Step testing by making changes to the quantitative composition of the process stream is another useful technique to determine the RTD.

3.1.3. Material Characterisation and Control

Material attributes can impact various aspects of CM operation and performance, such as material feeding, process dynamics, and output material quality. Input materials may require evaluation and control of attributes beyond those typically considered for a material specification used in batch manufacturing. For example:

  • In a solid dosage form process, particle size, cohesiveness, adhesiveness, hygroscopicity, static charge, or specific surface area may impact powder feeding and material flow.
  • In a chemically synthesised drug substance process, viscosity, concentration, or the multiphase nature of the feed may impact flow properties or conversion.
  • In a therapeutic protein process, lot-to-lot variability of cell culture media or feed components may impact cell culture performance or process consistency.

3.1.4. Equipment Design and System Integration

The design of equipment and their integration to form a CM system affect process dynamics, material transport and transformation, output material quality, etc. When developing a CM process and its control strategy, it is important to consider the characteristics of the integrated systems. These include the system's ability to maintain an integrated flow, manage potential disruption to CM operations, and complete the intended transformation of the material stream within the respective planned operational ranges.

  • Design and configuration of equipment (e.g., compatibility and integrity for maximum run time; geometry to promote desired transformation; spatial arrangement to facilitate material flow)
  • Physical connections and digital control interfaces between equipment (e.g., use of a surge tank between two unit operations to mitigate temporary differences in mass flow rates)
  • Locations of material diversion and sampling points

3.1.5. Process Monitoring and Control

Process monitoring and control support the maintenance of a state of control during production. Process analytical technology (PAT) (ICH Q8) is well-suited for CM. Example applications include in-line UV flow cells to monitor therapeutic protein concentration, in-line near-infrared spectroscopy to assess blend uniformity or water content, and on-line HPLC to monitor conversion of a chemical reaction.

An appropriate sampling strategy is an important aspect of process monitoring and control. The variables monitored, monitoring method and frequency, amount of material sampled, sampling location, statistical method, and acceptance criteria depend on the intended use of the data and process dynamics. The intended use of data may include detection of rapid changes such as disturbances, assessment of quality of a batch when RTRT is used, or analysis of process trends or drifts.

3.1.6. Material Traceability and Diversion

CM processes may include periods when non-conforming materials are produced, for example, during system start-up and shutdown or when disturbances are not appropriately managed. The ability to detect and divert potential non-conforming material from the product stream during production is an important characteristic of CM.

Understanding the RTD and process dynamics of individual unit operations and integrated systems over planned operating conditions enables tracking of the distribution of materials over time. Material traceability, understanding how disturbances affect material quality, and the use of appropriate measurements (e.g., PAT) allow for real-time determination of when to start and stop material collection or diversion. Criteria should be established to trigger the start and end of the diversion period and restart of product collection.

3.1.7. Process Models

Process models can be used for development of a CM process or as part of a control strategy for commercial production, including the diversion strategy. Process models may also be used to predict quality attributes in real time, enabling timely process adjustments to maintain a state of control. During development, process models can support the establishment of a design space by explaining how inputs and outputs are related.

  • A process model is specific to system design and configuration and relevant material properties.
  • Model development requires understanding of underlying model assumptions (e.g., plug flow versus mixed flow systems).
  • Model performance depends on factors such as mathematical constructs and the quality of model inputs.
  • Model validation assesses the fitness of the model for its intended use based on predetermined acceptance criteria.
  • During commercial manufacture, model maintenance and monitoring of model performance should occur on a routine and ongoing basis.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: State of control in CM ≠ steady state

This is one of the most fundamental conceptual differences between CM and batch manufacturing. In batch manufacturing, "Steady State" is usually the basis of quality assurance - the process must remain stable. But the "State of Control" in CM allows parameters to vary dynamically within specified ranges, as long as the overall process and the output quality remain in control.

Example: on a continuous blending-tableting line, the feeder flow rate may fluctuate with slight batch-to-batch differences in the flow properties of the powder, but as long as the critical CQAs (such as content uniformity) continue to meet specification, the system is in a "state of control" - even if it is not in a "steady state".

This concept of "dynamic but controlled" is why PAT and real-time release testing (RTRT) are particularly important in CM: real-time measurement is needed to confirm that the system remains in control at all times.

Key Point | CM material specifications may need to go beyond pharmacopoeial standards

An important point in §3.1.3: in a CM process, input materials may need to be controlled for attributes "beyond traditional batch manufacturing specifications". For example:

  • In continuous manufacturing of solid dosage forms, attributes affecting powder flow, such as particle size distribution, hygroscopicity, and electrostatic charge, are usually not part of the specification in batch processes but may be EC in CM
  • Where a pharmacopoeial standard exists, CM-specific control attributes may need to be added on top of it

For a CDMO, this means that when converting a process to CM, all input material specifications must be re-evaluated to confirm whether CM-specific material attribute controls need to be added. If the material from the existing supplier does not meet the new CM requirements, it may be necessary to change suppliers or ask the supplier to adjust its process.

Framework for designing a material diversion strategy

Design of diversion trigger criteria:

Scenario: PAT measurement shows a quality attribute trending toward failure

Step 1: Disturbance identification
  → PAT data exceed the control limit (not the specification limit)
  → Triggers investigation + preventive diversion

Step 2: Determining the diversion range
  → Based on RTD: the time range over which the disturbance signal propagates through the system
  → Add a safety margin to account for RTD uncertainty
  → If multiple PAT measurement points all show normal results → the diversion range can be narrowed

Step 3: End of diversion and resumption of collection
  → PAT data return to normal and are stable
  → The preset "resume collection" criteria are met
  → Record the start/end time of diversion and the quantity diverted

Step 4: Disposition of diverted material
  → Fate of diverted material: quarantine → offline testing → investigation → disposal/recovery
  → The PQS maintains the disposition procedure for diverted material

Analogy | RTD is like a "pollutant dispersion model in a river"

Suppose a bucket of dye (the disturbance) is poured into a river at some point upstream. The RTD tells you: when will the dye start to appear at the downstream water quality monitoring station? When will its concentration peak? When will it be diluted to a negligible level? With the RTD, you can calculate precisely the time window between "dye added upstream" and "downstream water quality starts to be affected" - and this is the scientific basis of the diversion strategy in CM. A system without a known RTD is like an engineer who does not know the river flow velocity and dispersion pattern: unable to design effective pollution control measures.

Practice Questions

  1. If the NIR probe of a CM system suddenly fails (PAT data are interrupted), how should the affected material range be determined within the RTD framework? How should the diversion strategy respond?
  2. In a CM line for solid oral dosage forms, if the particle size distribution (D90) of one lot of API is 20% larger than that of normal lots, what information can an RTD study provide to assess the impact of this lot-to-lot difference on downstream quality?

RTD (Residence Time Distribution) - the core scientific concept of CM

RTD (Residence Time Distribution) is a function describing the distribution of the time that material spends in a CM system. It is the core tool of CM science:

RTD characterization methods: tracer studies (a tracer must be selected that has flow behavior similar to the material but is inert), step testing, and in silico simulation combined with experimental confirmation.

Three application levels of process models

The regulatory expectations for a model are proportional to its "Impact Category" - the greater the model's influence on the final release decision, the stricter the requirements.

3.2 Changes in Production Output

Original Text

3.2. Changes in Production Output

Several approaches to changing production output are discussed below. For already approved products manufactured using a CM process, it is important to justify the selected approach, assess risk and understand its impact on the overall control strategy and process performance, and as needed, update the control strategy to ensure risk control.

  • Change in run time with no change to mass flow rates and equipment: Issues not observed over shorter run times may become visible as run time increases. Additional risks and constraints should be considered, including process drift, increased equipment temperatures, material build-up, exceeding the performance limit of components (e.g., validated in vitro cell age, resin cycle number), material degradation, membrane or sensor fouling, and microbial contamination. Decreasing production output generally would not imply additional risks.
  • Increase mass flow rates with no change to overall run time and equipment: The risks associated with this approach may impact output material quality and are related to changes in process dynamics and system capability to handle increased mass flow rates. This approach may require re-evaluation and modification of the control strategy, including process parameters and controls, material traceability, RTD, sampling, and diversion strategies.
  • Increase output through duplication of equipment (scale-out):
    • Replication of production lines (like-for-like): Replicating the integrated CM production line can increase production output. The replicate production lines follow the same control strategy.
    • Parallel unit operations on the same production line: Risks include maintaining control across parallel unit operations, maintenance of uniform flow distribution, synchronisation and re-integration of parallel flow streams, and material traceability.
  • Scale-up by increasing equipment size/capacity: General principles of equipment scale-up apply. As elements such as RTD, process dynamics, and system integration may change, various aspects of the control strategy may be impacted. The risks of scale-up and applicability of the original control strategy should be assessed at the new scale.

3.3. Continuous Process Verification

In CM, frequent process monitoring and control can be achieved through use of process parameters, PAT tools, soft sensors, and process models. These tools allow real-time data collection for parameters relevant to process dynamics and material quality, and hence ensure the state of control for every batch. Additionally, since CM can facilitate changes to production output without increasing equipment size, there is an opportunity to generate development knowledge at the same scale intended for commercial manufacturing. These tools, together with the system design and the control strategy, facilitate early execution of process validation activities and the adoption of continuous process verification (CPV) (ICH Q8) as an alternative approach to process validation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point | Flexibility within the batch size range

If the company defined the batch size in the original application as "minimum 4 hours - maximum 12 hours of run time", then adjusting the run time of each batch within this range (that is, "Option 1: increase run time") is an adjustment within the batch size range, can be managed under the PQS, and requires no additional regulatory reporting. But if the company wants to exceed the maximum run time (for example, needs 16 hours), this goes beyond the batch size range and requires an application update - this is exactly the regulatory advantage and the key design point of defining batch size as a "range".

Analogy | CPV is like a "smart device for continuous health monitoring" vs. a "periodic physical exam"

Traditional process validation is like "a full physical exam once a year" - health status is assessed in a concentrated way at a specified point in time. Continuous process verification is like "wearing a smart health watch that monitors 24 hours a day" - heart rate, blood pressure, and sleep quality are recorded continuously, and any abnormality triggers an immediate alert. A CM process is naturally paired with PAT and real-time monitoring, like a "manufacturing system with built-in health monitoring", which makes CPV the most logically natural approach to validation - there is no need to arrange specific "validation batches", because every batch produced is continuously being verified.

Practical Application | Strategic considerations for a CDMO choosing CPV vs. traditional PV

For a CDMO, introducing CPV requires the following prerequisites:

  • Adequate PAT infrastructure: in-line/at-line NIR, UV, and other measurement systems installed and validated
  • Statistical process control (SPC) capability: able to analyze CM data in real time and identify process drift
  • Process model support: soft sensors or analytical models for real-time quality assessment established and validated
  • Early engagement with regulators: it is recommended to hold a Type B/C meeting with FDA/EMA before submission to confirm the acceptability of the CPV approach

If the above conditions are not yet mature, it is recommended to take the traditional PV route first while building the CPV infrastructure, and then transition gradually through post-approval lifecycle management.

Section Summary

  • State of Control: CM allows "dynamic but controlled" operation; parameters can vary within specified ranges and a steady state is not required - this is the fundamental reason why PAT and RTRT are important
  • RTD (residence time distribution): the core tool of CM science, used for material traceability, diversion strategy design, sampling frequency setting, batch boundary definition, and process model development
  • Extended CM material specifications: input materials may need to be controlled for attributes uncommon in batch processes (such as powder flowability), possibly beyond pharmacopoeial standards
  • Integrated equipment design: surge tanks balance flow differences; the design of equipment connections and the locations of sampling/diversion points are the hardware basis of the control strategy
  • Three levels of process models: development tool → control strategy element → RTRT support; the greater the impact, the stricter the regulatory expectations
  • Four ways to adjust output: increase run time (lowest risk, possibly within the batch range) → increase flow rate (control strategy needs re-evaluation) → scale-out (like-for-like, low risk) → scale-up (highest regulatory burden)
  • CPV as an alternative to traditional PV: the PAT infrastructure of CM naturally supports CPV; sufficient process understanding and justification are needed; early engagement with regulators is the key to success

Section 4 Regulatory Considerations: Overview

Core Principle

The core of the Regulatory Considerations section of ICH Q13: the dossier for continuous manufacturing must still follow ICH M4Q (the format requirements for the quality part of the CTD), but must be supplemented with CM-specific information.

§4.1 to §4.10 of this section explain, CTD section by CTD section, how the characteristics of CM should be reflected, so that reviewers can fully understand the design of the control strategy.

Study Framework Tip

Section 4 can be read as a "CM edition of the CTD completion guide": which section should contain which CM-specific information, with Table 1 in §4.10 serving as the overall summary table.

§4.1 Description of Manufacturing Process and Process Controls p17

In line with ICH M4Q, a sequential narrative description of the manufacturing process and process controls should be included in sections 3.2.S.2.2 and 3.2.P.3.3 of the CTD and supported by pharmaceutical development data in 3.2.S.2.6 or 3.2.P.2.

For CM, sections 3.2.S.2.2 and 3.2.P.3.3 should be supplemented by the following, when applicable:

  • A summary of start-up, shutdown, pause and restart procedures, to describe how the integrated CM process functions
  • The strategy for material collection and diversion including criteria for product collection during routine manufacturing
  • Process parameters related to continuous flow (e.g., mass flow rate(s) or flow rate range)
  • When appropriate (e.g., solid materials), a description of how material is transported from one piece of equipment to another
  • A flow diagram with the following aspects identified, when applicable:
    • Points of material entry and exit (including diversion and collection points)
    • Sequence of unit operations, including any surge lines or tanks
    • Direction of material movement through each process step
    • Clear indication of continuous and batch process steps
    • Critical steps and location points at which process controls, intermediate tests, or final product controls are conducted (e.g., PAT measurement, feedforward or feedback process control)
  • A suitably detailed description of aspects of equipment design, configuration and system integration shown to be critical to process control or to impact product quality

§4.1 Description of Manufacturing Process and Process Controls

CTD location: 3.2.S.2.2 / 3.2.P.3.3

Traditional batch manufacturing already has a standard format for this section; CM needs to "add" the following CM-specific information on top of it:

Essential elements of a CM process flow diagram

Compared with batch manufacturing, the CM flow diagram needs to additionally indicate:

  • Material entry and exit points: raw material input points, intermediate diversion points, product collection points, waste discharge points
  • Sequence of unit operations: including surge tanks and buffer lines
  • Material flow arrows: continuous flow vs. batch steps must be clearly distinguished
  • Process control locations: PAT sensor locations, feedforward/feedback control points
Exam Focus

The "material diversion strategy" that §4.1 requires to be described follows directly from the scientific design principles in §3.1.1: the strategy explained in the CTD must be consistent with the scientific basis of the control strategy.

Discussion Question

Why does the CM flow diagram need to show "a clear distinction between continuous and batch steps"?

Answer

Many CM systems are not continuous end to end and may contain batch-type steps (such as terminal sterilization and filling). The reviewer needs to identify clearly which steps fall within the scope of RTD influence of the continuous manufacturing, in order to correctly assess the applicability of the control strategy.

§4.2 Control Strategy p17–19

The control strategy of a CM process is designed to ensure that output materials made over time are of the desired quality. The control strategy should consider the elements discussed in Section 3. The dossier should describe the relevant controls and operational aspects used during manufacturing.

Input Material Attributes

Impact of input material attributes and their variability (e.g., inter-batch, different suppliers) on continuous processing and product quality should be assessed. Proposed material attribute acceptable ranges should be justified when establishing the material specification. For input materials for which pharmacopoeial requirements exist, characterisation and control may extend beyond those requirements.

Process Monitoring and Control

An appropriate description should show a robust approach to process monitoring and maintaining a state of control. The dossier should describe how the control system uses process parameters and in-process material attribute measurements to make process- and quality-related decisions (e.g., to pause the process or divert material).

Other important aspects should be defined: sampling strategy (location, sample size, frequency, statistical approach and criteria), summary of models if used, and use of data in making in-process control decisions.

The data analysis method used should appropriately detect disturbances or variability. When data averaging is used, averaging across appropriate time intervals should be considered rather than across the entire CM run time. The time intervals should consider the relevancy of PAT measurement frequency to the RTD and process dynamics.

System Operation

Procedures should be established and maintained on site for managing system start-up, shutdown, and pauses and for handling disturbances (see Annex V). Relevant approaches should also be described at adequate level of detail in the dossier. The disposition of material impacted by transient and pause events should be justified, considering potential risks to output material quality.

Material Diversion and Collection

The material diversion and collection strategy should be summarised and justified in the dossier. The strategy should include:

  • Criteria for triggering material diversion
  • Basis for determining the extent of diverted materials
  • Conditions for resuming material collection

Factors such as sampling frequency, RTD, and amplitude/duration/propagation of disturbances should be considered. The extent of diverted material should incorporate justified safety margins, considering uncertainty of RTD and other measurements.

Procedures for managing material collection, diversion, and disposition (quarantine, offline testing, investigations) do not need to be in the dossier but should be maintained within the PQS (ICH Q10).

RTRT

RTRT as described in ICH Q8 can be applied to output material quality attributes. RTRT is not a regulatory requirement for CM implementation. When RTRT is proposed, the associated reference test method should be described.

Development of the data collection approach for RTRT should include a risk assessment of how any lapses in data collection (e.g., recalibrating a NIR probe) may affect quality decisions. The proposed control strategy should include alternative or additional quality controls to mitigate any risks posed by data lapses.

Equipment and System Integration

Aspects of equipment design and system integration that are shown to be critical to output material quality and its control should be described and justified in the context of the overall control strategy.

A summary of the control strategy should be provided in CTD section 3.2.S.2.6 or 3.2.P.2.3 with links or references to CTD sections that contain the detailed information.

§4.2 Control Strategy

Five main components of the control strategy (to be described in the CTD)
Key warning on data averaging

Q13 states explicitly that data should not be averaged over the entire CM run time. Averaging should be done by "time interval", and the interval must take into account the PAT measurement frequency and its relationship to the RTD.

Reason: a disturbance during CM may affect only the material in a specific time period; averaging over the whole run would dilute the local abnormal signal and mask the quality problem.

Design elements of a material diversion strategy

Designing a safe diversion strategy requires consideration of:

  • RTD uncertainty: the diversion range must include the upper and lower limits of the RTD prediction plus a safety margin
  • Disturbance propagation: a disturbance signal may propagate downstream through the system, so its amplitude and duration need to be tracked
  • Sampling frequency: determines "the earliest time at which the return of quality to normal can be confirmed"
Analogy: a diversion strategy is like traffic control

Imagine an accident (disturbance) on a highway. Not only is the lane at the accident site closed, but traffic is also controlled for some distance before and after it (RTD range + safety margin). "Reopening to traffic" waits until the accident is confirmed to be completely cleared; you cannot rely on the real-time traffic situation alone.

Important clarification on RTRT

RTRT is not a regulatory requirement for CM; it is an optional tool. However, if an application adopts RTRT, it must:

  • Describe the reference test method
  • Assess the impact on quality decisions of interruptions in data collection (such as recalibration of the NIR probe)
  • Propose alternative or additional quality control measures as a backup
Location of the control strategy summary in the CTD

The complete control strategy must be summarized in the following sections, with cross-references to the detailed information:

  • 3.2.S.2.6 (drug substance manufacturing development)
  • 3.2.P.2.3 (drug product development, manufacturing development part)

The summary needs to provide "links or references" to the detailed information in other CTD sections so that the reviewer can trace and verify it.

§4.3 Batch Description and Batch Size p19–20

The approach to define batch size (see Section 2.2 examples) and the proposed commercial batch size or range should be described in the dossier.

If a range is proposed, it should be justified, and the approach for achieving the range should be described (see Section 2.2). Changes in batch size within the approved batch size range can be managed within the PQS. Any post-approval change beyond the approved range should be supported by data (Section 3.2) and appropriately managed (prior approval or notification).

A suitable quantitative metric with acceptance criteria should be defined within the PQS to establish batch-to-batch consistency and system robustness. For example, when batch size is defined by amount of collected material, the amount of diverted materials relative to collected materials for each batch should be considered.

The actual intended size of a given batch should be defined before manufacturing begins.

§4.3 Description of Batch and Batch Size

Three-tier structure of a batch size application
Quantitative metric for batch consistency

Q13 requires a quantitative metric plus acceptance criteria to be defined in the PQS to assess batch-to-batch consistency.

Example: when batch size is defined as "quantity of material collected", the ratio of "quantity of material diverted / quantity of material collected" for each batch should have an acceptable range set in the PQS, as an indicator of system robustness.

Discussion Question

Why does Q13 require that "the intended size of each batch be defined before manufacturing begins" (intended size defined before manufacturing begins)?

Answer

The batch size of a CM system is directly related to run time or mass flow rate. Defining the batch size in advance makes it possible to: (1) set the run time correctly; (2) ensure a consistent basis for calculating the safety margin of the diversion strategy; (3) meet the completeness requirements of the subsequent batch records.

§4.4 Process Models p20

The level of detail provided in the dossier regarding model development, validation, and maintenance over the lifecycle should be commensurate with the model type and impact category.

The process model should be specific for the defined system (e.g., equipment, layout, connections).

Information to support models used as part of commercial manufacturing should be available during site inspection and maintained at or be accessible to the manufacturing site.

Refer to Points to Consider: ICH-Endorsed Guide for ICH Q8/Q9/Q10 Implementation for regulatory expectations on process models.

§4.4 Process Models

Core principle of model submission: commensurate with impact category

The level of detail for a model in the submission should be commensurate with the following two aspects:

  • Model type: theoretical mechanistic model vs. data-driven model vs. hybrid model
  • Impact category: used only in the development stage vs. a component of the control strategy in commercial manufacturing vs. the basis of RTRT
System-specificity requirement for models

A process model must be built for the specific system (specific for the defined system), including the equipment, layout, and connections.

Practical implication: if the equipment configuration at the manufacturing site changes, the model needs to be re-evaluated and cannot simply be transferred. This is an important consideration in technology transfer.

Regulatory expectations (by model impact level)
High-impact model (such as the basis of RTRT):
  → Full validation data (3.2.R regional information section)
  → Detailed description + acceptance criteria
  → Must be available during on-site inspection

Low-impact (such as a development tool):
  → Description of model type + description of intended use
  → Overview of the maintenance plan
  → Full validation data not required
Reference document

On the topic of models, Q13 directly refers to the "ICH Q8/Q9/Q10 Implementation Working Group Points to Consider", which shows that the detailed regulatory expectations for process models are already set out elsewhere in the ICH system, and Q13 does not repeat them.

§4.5 Drug Substance and Drug Product Stability p20–21

Regulatory expectations for the stability data package generally do not differ between CM and batch manufacturing modes (refer to ICH Q1A, ICH Q5C). Batches used to generate primary stability data should be manufactured using a manufacturing process and equipment representative of the commercial process. Primary stability batches should incorporate the variability described in the ICH stability guidelines (e.g., different drug substance batches).

Additionally, for chemical drug substances or drug products:

  • Stability batches could be obtained with a single start-up/shutdown sequence provided the aforementioned variability is incorporated into the batches (e.g., by introducing different batches of drug substances sequentially).
  • Stability batches may be produced from shorter manufacturing runs, provided that:
    • The control strategy, mass flow rate(s), and equipment are representative of the commercial process
    • It is demonstrated that a state of control is established and maintained when the process operates over the longer commercial run times

    The concept of pilot scale batch (e.g., at minimum one-tenth of full production scale) as defined in other guidelines (e.g., ICH Q1A) may not be necessarily applicable in this scenario.

  • If modes of increasing production output (other than increasing run time) are being used (e.g., increasing equipment size), applicants should justify their approach for defining primary stability batches. Applicants are encouraged to discuss their scale-up and primary stability batches approach with regulatory authorities.

§4.5 Stability of Drug Substance and Drug Product

Basic principles of CM stability

The overall requirements are the same as for batch manufacturing: follow ICH Q1A (chemical drugs) and ICH Q5C (biological drugs). Primary stability batches must be manufactured using "a representative commercial manufacturing process and equipment".

Three special flexibilities for CM stability batches
Impact of the output-increase approach on stability batches

If an output-increase approach other than "extended run time" is adopted (such as scaling up the equipment size or scale-out with parallel equipment), the applicant must provide a separate justification of how the primary stability batches are defined, and it is recommended to discuss the strategy with the regulatory authority in advance.

Reason: once the equipment size changes, the RTD, mixing characteristics, and so on may differ, so it cannot be directly inferred that stability data from a smaller scale are representative of commercial batches.

Discussion Question

Why does Q13 allow stability studies to use "batches with a shorter manufacturing time" but require that "maintenance of the state of control over the longer commercial run time must be demonstrated separately"? Is there a contradiction between the two?

Answer

There is no contradiction. A stability study evaluates "the chemical/physical/biological stability of the product itself", which can be reflected by shorter batches; whereas "the state of control over a longer run time" evaluates "manufacturing robustness", which must be demonstrated separately through process validation or CPV data. The two have different evaluation purposes, so requiring them separately is scientifically logical.

§4.6 Conversion of a Batch Process to CM p27

Changing the manufacturing mode from batch to continuous necessitates the development of an appropriate control strategy, considering factors identified in Section 3. The output materials from the batch and continuous processes should have comparable quality.

A science and risk-based approach should be used for establishing product comparability and assessing the need for additional bioequivalence, non-clinical and/or clinical studies, and stability data. Additional details regarding how to establish product comparability for therapeutic proteins can be found in ICH Q5E.

Manufacturers should seek regulatory approval before implementing a conversion of an approved batch process to a CM process. Manufacturers can seek advice from the regulatory authority to gain clarification on the regulatory expectations and acceptability of their strategy and data package for the proposed changes (e.g., when potential changes in formulation are necessary to enable conversion to CM).

§4.6 Conversion of Batch Manufacturing to Continuous Manufacturing

Core requirements for batch → CM conversion
Important clarification: conversion requires prior approval

Q13 states explicitly that regulatory approval must be obtained before the conversion is implemented; it cannot be handled by post-approval notification. It is recommended to discuss the strategy and the acceptability of the data package with the regulatory authority in advance, especially when the conversion involves formulation changes (for example, when the CM process requires adjustment of excipients), as such a change may affect the product registration.

Discussion Question

A manufacturer has an approved batch-manufactured drug product and intends to switch to a CM process. What type of regulatory submission is required? Is an additional clinical trial necessarily required?

Answer

A supplemental change application must be submitted, with approval obtained before implementation. Whether additional clinical trials are needed is decided by scientific and risk assessment: if quality comparability can be established and the process differences do not affect the drug's activity or safety, they can generally be waived. If the conversion involves a formulation change, a BE study may be needed.

§4.7 Process Validation p27–28

The requirements for process validation as established by region are similar for CM and batch manufacturing. Approaches such as traditional process validation (fixed number of batches) or continuous process verification can be used.

When continuous process verification is used, the CM system performance and output material quality is continuously monitored, such that real-time data collected demonstrate the maintenance of a state of control and production of output material with the desired quality for the run time duration.

The use of continuous process verification should be justified based on:

  • Product and process understanding
  • System design
  • Overall control strategy

This justification should be provided in the dossier (see Table 1).

When a continuous process verification approach is used to support initial product launch, applicants should justify the appropriateness of the validation activities to provide confidence in the commercial manufacturing process.

§4.7 Process Validation

CM process validation: two valid approaches
Additional requirements for using CPV at initial marketing

Q13 allows CPV to be used to support initial commercial launch, but it must be specifically explained that the validation activities provide sufficient confidence in the commercial manufacturing process. It is recommended to discuss with the regulatory authority in advance to confirm the acceptability of the strategy.

Three elements of the CPV justification (essential in the dossier)
1. Product and process understanding → good knowledge of the CQA-CPP relationships
2. System design                      → the system can maintain a state of control and detect deviations in real time
3. Overall control strategy            → includes sufficiently dense monitoring and material diversion mechanisms
Core difference between CPV and traditional validation

Traditional validation: uses N batches to demonstrate in advance that the process "is capable of" producing conforming product (one-time demonstration).

CPV: uses real-time data from every batch to demonstrate continuously that the process "is" producing conforming product (ongoing demonstration). For CM, CPV is better aligned with its "continuous" nature.

§4.8 Pharmaceutical Quality System p28

PQS expectations are the same for batch and CM processes and should follow pertinent ICH guidelines. One important operational aspect of CM is that non-conforming materials can be diverted from the rest of the batch when material traceability, process monitoring, and material diversion strategies are well established.

Procedures for material diversion, when required, should be established under the PQS (see Section 4.2). An overarching plan or decision tree that describes how disturbances are managed for various types of material diversion should be maintained under the PQS.

Diverted materials resulting from planned events (e.g., system start-up and shutdown) generally do not require investigation when the events meet established process performance criteria.

When unexpected and/or frequent disturbances occur, appropriate investigation, root cause analysis, and CAPA should be instituted (see Annex V).

§4.8 Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS)

Decision framework for disturbance management in the CM PQS
The logic for why "planned diversion" does not require investigation

Material diversion during start-up is a "pre-designed process feature" that was assessed during development and confirmed not to affect the quality of the final batch. When the diversion result meets the preset criteria, there is no need to open an OOS/deviation investigation - this is an important mechanism by which CM reduces administrative burden.

Analogy: a smart highway management system

Batch manufacturing PQS: problem occurs → full stop → complete investigation → resume traffic.

CM PQS: detect the abnormality in real time → dynamically divert non-conforming vehicles → main traffic keeps moving → decide whether an investigation is needed depending on the situation. It is more efficient but requires more sophisticated monitoring infrastructure.

Annex IV: Integrated Drug Substance and Drug Product CM p37–42

Annex IV addresses CM processes where drug substance and drug product manufacturing are fully integrated — drug substance is not isolated between the two steps.

Drug Substance Specification (§3.1)

Even though the drug substance is not isolated, a drug substance specification should be defined and justified (per ICH Q6A). Drug substance testing may not be required on a routine basis when the integrated process is appropriately controlled. Conformance to specification should be verified on a periodic and event-driven basis:

  • Periodic: Pre-selected batches or at predetermined intervals (e.g., every 5th batch or annually)
  • Event-based: Triggered by changes in supplier, starting material, synthesis conditions, or other risk factors

Attributes typically associated with drug substance quality are generally included in the drug product specification (e.g., related substances, residual solvents, elemental impurities) unless justified per ICH Q6A.

Stability in Integrated Processes (§4)

  • Drug substance stability: Re-test period not applicable (not isolated/stored). Data may support in-house reference standards, stability profiles, and hold times during production interruptions.
  • Drug product stability: ICH stability guidelines and §4.5 of main guideline are applicable.

Annex IV: Integrated Continuous Manufacturing of Drug Substance and Drug Product

Defining features of integrated CM

Annex IV discusses "fully continuous integration from drug substance to drug product": the drug substance is not isolated, dried, or stored but flows directly into the drug product manufacturing steps in the form of an intermediate (such as a crystal slurry).

Example process: chemical reaction → continuous crystallization → cross-flow filtration → wet granulation → drying → milling → tableting → coating

Specification management of the "non-isolated drug substance"

Although the drug substance is not isolated, a drug substance specification must still be established. The frequency of routine production testing can be reduced:

  • Routine production: when the process meets the preset performance criteria, it is considered "would comply if tested" and offline testing of every batch is not required
  • Periodic verification: every N batches or every year, actual samples are taken and tested to confirm compliance with the specification
  • Event-driven: high-risk events such as a change of supplier or a change in raw materials trigger testing
Special requirements for the drug product specification

The drug product specification of an integrated process usually needs to include test items that traditionally belong to the "drug substance specification" (related substances, residual solvents, elemental impurities), because the drug substance is never separately tested and released, and its quality control can only be confirmed through testing of the final drug product.

Discussion Question

In an integrated CM process, if routine monitoring finds a rising trend in impurity concentration at sampling point T1 (still within specification), what actions should be taken?

Answer

Even though the trend is within specification, you should: (1) start a trend investigation (OOT procedure) to assess whether there is a systematic root cause; (2) assess whether the trigger conditions for "event-driven" periodic verification have been reached; (3) if the trend continues, consider tightening the internal alert limit or implementing a CAPA; (4) assess whether it affects the related substances test results in the downstream drug product specification. Because of the "chained" nature of an integrated process, drug substance trends need to be evaluated together with drug product quality indicators.

Annex V: Perspectives on Managing Disturbances p43–46

This annex describes examples of approaches for managing transient disturbances during CM. The discussion is not exhaustive; alternative approaches can be used.

Background — Funnel Plot Tool

Disturbances may result in variation in material quality. The impact is affected by the amplitude, duration, and frequency of the disturbance. Funnel plot predictions based on an RTD model help understand the qualitative and quantitative impact of a disturbance's amplitude and duration on material quality.

Example: Figure 5🔍 shows that a disturbance of ±20% lasting less than 90 seconds would not cause drug concentration in the blend to exceed 90–110% LC.

Three Disturbance Scenarios

  • Example 1 (within criteria, infrequent): +20% LIWF flow spike, 40 s — within predefined acceptance criteria. Blend concentration stays within 90–110% due to back mixing. Action: No diversion, no investigation needed.
  • Example 2 (outside criteria, unexpected): +20% spike, 300 s — exceeds predefined criteria. NIR confirms blend exceeds 110%. Action: Non-conforming material diverted per pre-established procedure; process continues; concurrent investigation initiated.
  • Example 3 (within criteria but frequent): Multiple +20% spikes, 40 s each — each individually meets criteria, but system cannot dampen cumulative effect, resulting in non-conforming material. Action: Monitor closely; divert when control criteria no longer met; pause if disturbances persist; investigation required to determine root cause.

Diverted amount depends on RTD confidence intervals (safety margin), sampling strategy, and ability to trace and remove materials.

Setting acceptance criteria for frequency of disturbances is also recommended to aid management.

Annex V: Practical Cases of Disturbance Management

Design function of the funnel plot

The funnel plot uses "disturbance amplitude" as the y-axis and "disturbance duration" as the x-axis, and draws the boundary of the "acceptable region" for the corresponding material quality (such as content uniformity of 90-110% LC).

Practical significance: it helps determine which disturbances require diversion of material and which do not, and thus set reasonable amplitude/duration acceptance criteria. It is the core tool for designing a control strategy that "triggers diversion by amplitude/duration".

Decision comparison of the three disturbance scenarios
Key lesson from Example 3

"Each disturbance that meets the criteria may, when accumulated over many occurrences, exceed the limit" - a CM control strategy cannot only set acceptance criteria for a single disturbance; it also needs to set acceptance criteria for disturbance frequency. Q13 explicitly recommends: "setting acceptance criteria for the frequency of disturbances can assist disturbance management".

Comprehensive Practice Question

Why can the "diversion" in Example 2 run in parallel with the "investigation", while Example 3 needs to consider "pausing manufacturing"?

Answer

Example 2: the disturbance has ended and the process has returned to normal. The extent of the non-conforming material is determined (using the RTD safety margin), and after diversion is carried out the process can continue running normally, so the investigation does not need to interrupt production.

Example 3: the disturbance is still continuing and occurring frequently, which shows that the root cause has not yet been resolved. If operation continues during the investigation, non-conforming material may continue to be produced, and the accumulated disturbances may exceed the absorption capacity of the system. "Pausing" ensures that no material that may have to be discarded is produced before the problem is resolved, which is consistent with the principles of risk management.

§5 Glossary — Official Definitions p37–39

Active Process Controls
A system consisting of hardware and software architecture, mechanisms, and algorithms that automatically adjust a process to maintain the process output within a desired range. Examples include feedforward and feedback process controls.
Disturbances
Unplanned changes to process inputs beyond normal operating range or conditions (e.g., process parameter, material property, equipment condition, or environment) that are introduced into a system.
Diversion
Procedure in which materials are isolated and separated from the product stream in the manufacturing process.
Material Traceability
The ability to track materials throughout the manufacturing process.
Model Maintenance
A set of planned activities over the product lifecycle to monitor and sustain the model's performance to continually ensure its suitability for the intended and approved purpose.
Multivariate Statistical Process Control
The application of multivariate statistical techniques to analyse complex process data with potentially correlated variables. (Ph. Eur.)
Process Dynamics
The response of a manufacturing process to changing inputs or conditions or transient events.
Residence Time Distribution (RTD)
A measure of the range of residence times experienced by material passing through a specific process environment/vessel/unit operation.
Run Time
The time interval used to produce a quantity of output material.
Soft Sensors
A model that is used in lieu of physical measurement to estimate a variable or attribute (e.g., a quality attribute of material) based on measured data (e.g., process data). The model development, including selection of such data variables, is driven by comprehensive product and process understanding.
Steady State
A stable condition that does not change over time.
System
A manufacturing architecture that, in the context of CM, consists of individual pieces of equipment, their connections to one another, monitoring and control systems, and spatial layout.
Transient Events
A temporary condition in which a process goes through a dynamic change. This change may be due to a disturbance or an intentional alteration in the selected operating conditions (e.g., start-up, shutdown, changes from one operating condition to another).
Unit Operation
A basic step in a process. Unit operations involve a physical, chemical or biological transformation such as: reaction, crystallisation, filtration, blending, granulation, tableting, cell culture, purification or virus inactivation.

§5 Glossary: Official Definitions and Commentary

Three Pairs of Easily Confused Terms
  1. Disturbances vs. Transient Events: A disturbance is always unplanned; a transient event covers both planned events (start-up) and unplanned events (disturbances)
  2. Steady State vs. State of Control: Steady state means "no change"; state of control means "variation within an acceptable range"; CM requires only the latter
  3. Process Dynamics vs. RTD: RTD is one quantitative tool of process dynamics; process dynamics is broader and covers the response to all input changes

Comprehensive Review Question for the Full Document

A company plans to establish a brand-new CM manufacturing system for a chemical drug product (direct compression tablets), intends to use CPV from the first commercial launch, defines batch size by a time range (180-360 minutes × fixed mass flow rate), and uses an NIR model for RTRT. Please explain: (1) where the batch size range is filed in the CTD; (2) where the CPV justification is filed in the CTD; (3) where the NIR RTRT model information is filed in the CTD.

Answer

(1) Batch size range: Mainly placed in 3.2.P.3.2 (Batch Definition), describing the batch size definition approach (run time × mass flow rate) and the justification for the proposed range; a summary is provided in 3.2.P.2.3 (including the process development background supporting the batch size range).

(2) CPV justification: The justification is provided in the control strategy summary in 3.2.P.2.3 (process development), based on the three elements of process understanding, system design, and control strategy; detailed CPV plan information is placed in 3.2.R (Regional Information).

(3) NIR RTRT model: Mainly placed in 3.2.P.5 (control of drug product), including the model summary and justification of the sampling strategy, the contingency testing plan for equipment failure or data gaps, and analytical model validation information. If it is also used for in-process testing, cross-reference it in 3.2.P.3.4; the model development background goes in 3.2.P.2.3; complete validation data for a high-impact model go in 3.2.R.
