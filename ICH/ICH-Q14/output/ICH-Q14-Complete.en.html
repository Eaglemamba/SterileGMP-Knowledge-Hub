ICH Q14 in the ICH Architecture Preamble

ICH Q14 (Analytical Procedure Development, 2023) is a companion to ICH Q2(R2) (Validation of Analytical Procedures). Q14 addresses the development phase and lifecycle management of analytical procedures; Q2 covers the validation confirmation step.

Key links in the ICH quality system:

  • ICH Q8(R2): Pharmaceutical Development — systematic QbD approach applied to analytical procedure development
  • ICH Q9(R1): Quality Risk Management — risk assessment tools for identifying and ranking analytical procedure parameters
  • ICH Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System — knowledge management, PQS framework for lifecycle oversight
  • ICH Q12: Lifecycle Management tools: Established Conditions (ECs), PACMP, PLCM Document
  • ICH M4Q (CTD): Format for submitting analytical procedure development information (3.2.S.4.2, 3.2.P.5.2, etc.)

Q14 brings the Quality by Design (QbD) philosophy — previously applied to pharmaceutical development and manufacturing — formally into the domain of analytical science.

Position of ICH Q14 in the ICH Framework

ICH Q-Series Knowledge Network: Where Q14 Fits
Historical Significance of Q14

Q14 is the first ICH Q-series guideline devoted specifically to analytical procedure development. Before it, analytical procedure development had no systematic ICH guideline comparable to those for process development (Q8/Q11). Q14 formally extends the spirit of QbD into analytical science, so that procedure development, validation and post-approval management form one complete body of knowledge.

§1.1 Objective p1

This guideline describes science and risk-based approaches for developing and maintaining analytical procedures suitable for the evaluation of the quality of drug substances and drug products.

The systematic approach suggested in ICH Q8 together with principles of ICH Q9 can also be applied to the development and lifecycle management of analytical procedures. When developing an analytical procedure, a minimal (also known as traditional) approach or elements of an enhanced approach can be applied.

Furthermore, the guideline describes additional considerations for the development of multivariate analytical procedures and for real time release testing (RTRT).

This guideline complements ICH Q2 (Validation of Analytical Procedures). Using the tools described in ICH Q12, the guideline describes principles to support change management of analytical procedures based on risk management, comprehensive understanding of the analytical procedure and adherence to predefined criteria for performance characteristics.

The guideline also describes submission of analytical procedure development and related lifecycle information in the CTD format (ICH M4Q).

Key Benefit of the Enhanced Approach

The enhanced approach provides: (1) better assurance of analytical procedure performance; (2) the basis for an analytical procedure control strategy; and (3) the opportunity for more efficient regulatory approaches to post-approval changes.

§1.1 Objective

The Five Core Purposes of Q14
  1. A science- and risk-based framework: covers both the minimal and enhanced development approaches
  2. Analytical procedure lifecycle management: a complete chain from development → validation → marketing → continued post-approval management
  3. Additional considerations for multivariate analytical procedures: NIR models, chemometrics, etc.
  4. Additional considerations for RTRT: the development and validation framework for real-time release testing
  5. CTD submission framework: how to present analytical procedure development information in the CTD (3.2.S.4.2, 3.2.P.5.2, etc.)
Three Business Benefits of the Enhanced Approach

The main motivation for choosing the enhanced approach is the long-term benefit after approval:

  1. Better performance assurance: a thorough understanding of how parameters affect performance lowers the risk of procedure failure
  2. A foundation for the analytical procedure control strategy: systematic development knowledge supports more scientific setting of ECs
  3. A more efficient regulatory pathway: changes within the PAR/MODR may not require a regulatory filing, saving time and resources

§1.2 Scope p1

This guideline applies to analytical procedures used for release and stability testing of commercial drug substances and products, hereafter referred to as 'products'.

The guideline can also be applied to other analytical procedures used as part of the control strategy (ICH Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System) following a risk-based approach.

The scientific principles described in this guideline can be applied in a phase-appropriate manner to analytical procedures used during clinical development.

§1.2 Scope

Three Levels of Scope
The Practical Meaning of "Phase-Appropriate"

The principles of Q14 can be applied flexibly during clinical development in a "phase-appropriate" manner:

  • Early clinical (Phase I/II): a simpler development may be used, with the focus on confirming that the procedure is usable
  • Late clinical (Phase III): the procedure should move toward the commercial version, with the development package progressively completed
  • Commercial marketing (NDA/BLA/MAA): full compliance with Q14 + Q2(R2), with complete development and validation data

§2 General Considerations for Analytical Procedures p1–2

The goal of development is to obtain an analytical procedure fit for the intended purpose — to measure an attribute or attributes of the material with the needed specificity/selectivity, accuracy, precision over the reportable range. Details of performance characteristics are described in ICH Q2.

Platform Analytical Procedures

In certain cases, an analytical procedure can be applied to multiple products with little or no modification of measurement conditions. For a new application of such platform analytical procedures, subsequent development can be abbreviated, and certain validation tests can be omitted based on a science- and risk-based justification.

Data Reuse Across Development and Validation

Data gained during development studies — for example, robustness data from a Design of Experiments (DoE) study — could be used as part of the validation data for the related analytical procedure performance characteristics. Studies do not necessarily need to be repeated.

§2 General Considerations for Analytical Procedures

The Ultimate Goal of Analytical Procedure Development

The goal of analytical procedure development is a procedure that is "fit for the intended purpose" and, within the reportable range, delivers:

  • Specificity/Selectivity: the ability to distinguish the target analyte from interfering substances
  • Accuracy: how close the measured result is to the true value
  • Precision: the consistency of repeated measurement results

(For detailed definitions of the performance characteristics, see ICH Q2(R2).)

Platform Analytical Procedure: Applicable Conditions and Benefits

Definition: an analytical procedure that can be used for multiple products without substantial modification (for example, a generic HPLC method or a UV method for protein content).

When a platform procedure is used, the following work can be reduced:

  • Subsequent development studies can be abbreviated rather than redone from scratch
  • Some validation tests can be omitted, provided this is supported by scientific/risk-based justification
  • Robustness DoE data from the development stage can be used directly as validation data, with no need to repeat the experiments

Important prerequisite: any omission or reduction must be supported by sound scientific and risk-based justification, and the rationale must be explained in the application.

§2.1 Minimal versus Enhanced Approaches p2

Minimum Required Elements (Minimal Approach)

Analytical procedure development should include the following elements as a minimum:

  1. Identifying the attributes of the product which need to be tested
  2. Selecting an appropriate technology and related instruments or suitable apparatus
  3. Conducting studies to evaluate analytical procedure performance characteristics such as specificity, accuracy and precision over the reportable range (including calibration model, lower and/or higher range limits) and robustness
  4. Documenting the analytical procedure including the analytical procedure control strategy

Additional Elements for the Enhanced Approach

The enhanced approach offers a systematic way of developing and refining knowledge of an analytical procedure. The anticipated performance criteria should be documented in an Analytical Target Profile (ATP). In addition to the minimum elements, an enhanced approach may include:

  • Conducting risk assessment and evaluating prior knowledge to identify analytical procedure parameters that can impact performance
  • Conducting uni- or multi-variate experiments and/or modelling to explore ranges and interactions between identified parameters
  • Defining an analytical procedure control strategy including set-points and/or ranges — these could include Proven Acceptable Ranges (PARs) and/or Method Operable Design Regions (MODRs)

Applying elements of the enhanced approach can lead to better understanding of the impact of parameters on performance, and more flexibility for lifecycle management: wider operating ranges and a more appropriate set of Established Conditions (ECs) with associated reporting categories.

§2.1 Minimal Approach vs. Enhanced Approach

A Systematic Comparison of the Two Development Approaches
Analogy: Why the Dashboard Matters When Driving

The minimal approach is like driving to the destination and confirming that the car did not break down. You only know that it "runs", but not how engine temperature, fuel level and engine speed change, so if something goes wrong you can only stop the car (procedure failure).

The enhanced approach is like designing a complete dashboard that shows all the critical parameters in real time (engine temperature, fuel level, engine speed, etc.). You know not only that the car runs, but also the range of conditions within which it can be driven safely (the MODR) and which parameters will affect performance if they go out of range.

Conclusion: with a dashboard, driving is not only safer, but it is also easier to explain to the regulatory authority "why this adjustment does not affect performance", which in turn lowers the reporting category.

Key Terms of the Enhanced Approach Explained

§2.2 The Analytical Procedure Lifecycle p2–3

Figure 1🔍 depicts elements of the analytical procedure lifecycle as related to the product lifecycle. Analytical procedure development and change management approaches are described in this guideline, whereas analytical procedure validation is described in ICH Q2.

Depending on the intended purpose of the analytical procedure and the development approach taken, the order and extent of each lifecycle element could vary, and several elements could occur simultaneously.

Key lifecycle elements (from Figure 1🔍):

  • Identify measurement needs — ATP definition, informed by QTPP and CQA identification
  • Analytical procedure development — technology selection, parameter investigation, robustness studies
  • Validation (ICH Q2) — confirmation of fitness for purpose against ATP criteria
  • Routine use and ongoing monitoring — system suitability testing (SST), trending, continual improvement
  • Change management — updates driven by continual improvement, process changes, or new knowledge

§2.2 Analytical Procedure Lifecycle

The Five Stages of the Analytical Procedure Lifecycle
Analytical Procedure Lifecycle vs. Process Lifecycle: A Parallel

The Q14 analytical procedure lifecycle framework runs fully in parallel with the process development lifecycle and uses the same ICH Q8/Q9/Q10/Q12 toolbox:

  • Process: QTPP → CQA/CPP identification → process development (Q8/Q11) → process validation (PV) → commercial manufacturing → post-approval change management (Q12)
  • Analytical procedure: QTPP + CQA → ATP definition → procedure development (Q14) → procedure validation (Q2) → routine use and monitoring (Q10) → post-approval change management (Q12 + Q14)
The Line Between Development and Validation Is Blurred: An Efficiency Opportunity

Q14 states explicitly that the "order and extent" of the lifecycle elements can vary, and that several elements can take place at the same time.

Practical implication: data from development studies (such as robustness DoE) can be used directly for validation, without repeating the same experiments. This removes the traditional hard boundary between "development" and "validation", improving efficiency and reducing cost.

Review: Key Benefits of the Enhanced Approach p2

Section 1.1 and Section 2.1 together establish the rationale for investing in the enhanced approach. The guideline explicitly states that the enhanced approach provides:

  • Better assurance of analytical procedure performance
  • The basis for an analytical procedure control strategy
  • The opportunity for more efficient regulatory approaches to post-approval changes

Applying enhanced approach elements can lead to:

  • Better understanding of the impact of analytical procedure parameters on performance
  • Wider operating ranges (PAR/MODR) that can be moved within without regulatory filing
  • A more appropriate set of ECs with associated lower reporting categories for post-approval changes

Practice Exercise: The Core Advantages of the Enhanced Approach

Question

According to ICH Q14 §1.1 and §2.1, what are the key advantages of developing an analytical procedure with the enhanced approach rather than the minimal approach? Explain the chain of logic.

Answer

The core advantages of the enhanced approach follow this chain of logic:

  1. Better procedure understanding
    — risk assessment + DoE make clear which parameters affect performance and to what degree
  2. Wider operating ranges
    — establishing a PAR and/or MODR means that operating within the known acceptable range requires no filing
  3. More efficient change management
    — adjustments within the PAR/MODR are not EC changes and can be handled within the PQS
  4. Lower reporting categories
    — well-understood ECs can qualify for a lower reporting category (from Prior Approval down to Notification or internal control)

In one sentence: invest more during development → report less, face less review and incur less cost after approval.

Further Thinking

Why can the enhanced approach use "fewer and more precise ECs", while the minimal approach usually has more ECs?

Answer

The minimal approach has only a limited understanding of how parameters affect performance, so to stay safe it must designate more parameters as ECs (fixed set points) to control the potential risk.

Through systematic studies (DoE, risk assessment), the enhanced approach establishes which parameters "truly" affect performance and which do not. It can therefore:
(1) set no ECs for parameters that do not affect performance;
(2) set a PAR/MODR rather than a fixed point for parameters that do, allowing operation over a wider range without a filing.

Section 1: Analytical Target Profile & Knowledge/Risk Management

§3 Analytical Target Profile (ATP) pp 6–8

Product and process understanding (ICH Q8 and ICH Q11) leads to the identification of critical quality attributes (CQAs) requiring analytical measurement for control, which may be included in the quality target product profile (QTPP). Measurement needs can be captured in an analytical target profile (ATP), which forms the basis for development of the analytical procedure.

An ATP consists of:

  • A description of the intended purpose of the analytical procedure
  • Appropriate details on the product attributes to be measured
  • Relevant performance characteristics with associated performance criteria
  • Measurement requirements for one or more quality attributes

The ATP drives the choice of analytical technology. Multiple available analytical techniques may meet the performance criteria. Consideration of the operating environment (e.g., at-line, in-line or off-line) should be included in the technology selection.

Once a technology has been selected, the ATP serves as a foundation to derive the analytical procedure attributes and performance criteria for analytical procedure validation (ICH Q2).

Formal documentation and submission of an ATP is optional but can facilitate regulatory communication irrespective of the chosen development approach.

The ATP is maintained over the lifecycle and is used as a basis for lifecycle management to ensure that the existing, revised or new analytical procedure remains fit for the intended purpose.

Illustrative examples of ATPs are provided in Annex A.

§3 Analytical Target Profile (ATP)

Concept Clarification: QTPP → CQA → ATP, a Three-Level Quality Planning Hierarchy

Q14 establishes a three-level hierarchy for prospective quality planning, in which each level serves the next:

Logic chain: the QTPP defines what the product needs → the CQAs identify which attributes must be measured → the ATP defines the standard that the analytical procedure measuring those CQAs must meet

Analogy: The ATP Is the "Job Description" of the Analytical Procedure

Before designing an analytical procedure ("hiring an employee"), you must first define what the procedure needs to do and the standard it must meet.

The ATP is that "job description":

  • Purpose of the position → the intended purpose of the analytical procedure (e.g., quantifying a specific impurity in the release specification)
  • Job content → the details of the product attribute to be measured (e.g., chiral impurity A, concentration range 0–0.2%)
  • Performance standards → the performance characteristics and their criteria (e.g., accuracy ±10%; precision RSD ≤ 5%)

First the ATP (job description), then the technology is selected (interviewing candidates), then the procedure is developed (training the employee), then it is validated (probation assessment).

Important Clarification: Submitting the ATP Is "Optional"

Q14 states explicitly that formal documentation and submission of the ATP is optional and is not a regulatory requirement.

Whether or not it is submitted, the ATP has value as an internal development tool, helping the team reach a shared understanding of the requirements for the analytical procedure.

§3 ATP: Technology Selection & Lifecycle Role pp 7–8

The ATP drives the choice of analytical technology. Multiple available analytical techniques may meet the performance criteria defined in the ATP. The choice of technique should consider which technology best meets the performance criteria and the operating environment (e.g., at-line, in-line or off-line).

Once a technology has been selected, the ATP serves as a foundation to:

  • Derive the analytical procedure attributes and associated performance criteria
  • Establish the basis for analytical procedure validation (ICH Q2)

The ATP is maintained over the lifecycle. It is used as a basis for lifecycle management — ensuring that any existing, revised or new analytical procedure remains fit for the intended purpose.

Illustrative examples of ATPs are provided in Annex A of ICH Q14.

§3 The ATP Drives Technology Selection and Lifecycle Management

The ATP-Driven Analytical Procedure Development Sequence

Q14 describes a linear sequence that starts with the ATP:

QTPP + CQA identification
↓
ATP established
[Defines: intended purpose / attribute to be measured / performance criteria]
↓
Technology Selection
[Question: which technology can meet the ATP performance criteria? Consider the operating environment]
↓
Analytical Procedure Development
[Risk assessment → parameter studies (DoE) → PAR/MODR established]
↓
Validation per ICH Q2
[ATP performance criteria → validation acceptance criteria; development data can be used for validation]
↓
Routine use + continued monitoring
↓
Change management (assessing fit for purpose against the ATP)

Key feature: the ATP persists throughout the lifecycle and is the benchmark each time a modified procedure is assessed for whether it is still "fit for the intended purpose".

Operating Environment Considerations in Technology Selection

The ATP defines not only the analytical performance criteria but should also consider how the operating environment affects technology selection:

Operating environment requirements (such as the need for in-line real-time measurement) directly limit which analytical technologies can be chosen, so the ATP should state the operating environment requirements explicitly.

§4.1 Knowledge Management pp 8–9

As with product and manufacturing process development, knowledge management (ICH Q10) plays a critical role in analytical procedure development and during the lifecycle of the analytical procedure.

Prior knowledge is explicitly or implicitly used for informing decisions during analytical procedure development and lifecycle management. Prior knowledge can be:

  • Internal: a company's proprietary development and analytical experience
  • External: scientific and technical publications, or established scientific principles

Prior product knowledge plays an important role in identifying suitable analytical techniques. Knowledge of best practices, state-of-the-art technologies and regulatory expectations contribute to the selection of the most suitable technology for a given purpose.

Existing platform analytical procedures (e.g., protein content measurement by UV spectroscopy) can be leveraged to evaluate the attributes of a specific product without conducting additional procedure development.

As additional information is obtained, knowledge related to analytical procedures should be actively managed throughout the product lifecycle.

§4.1 Knowledge Management

Knowledge Management in the Analytical Procedure Lifecycle: Internal vs. External Prior Knowledge

The knowledge management principles of ICH Q10 are formally brought into analytical procedure development. Prior knowledge is a core asset for speeding up development and reducing risk:

Benefits of a platform analytical procedure: for an established procedure that can be applied to many products (such as a UV method for protein content), existing prior knowledge can be used directly, repeated basic development can be omitted, and some validation tests can also be omitted on scientific/risk-based grounds.

What "Actively Managed" Means in Practice

Q14 requires that knowledge be "actively managed" throughout the product lifecycle, not merely stored passively:

  • Accumulate: development data, validation results and routine monitoring data are all added to the knowledge base
  • Analyze: analyze trends regularly to identify early signals of drift in procedure performance
  • Apply: apply new knowledge to risk assessment updates (§4.2) and change decisions
  • Transfer: use the PQS (ICH Q10) to ensure that knowledge is not lost when staff change roles or leave

Knowledge management and risk management (§4.2) support each other: prior knowledge provides input to risk assessment, and the results of risk assessment in turn become new knowledge to be managed.

§4.2 Risk Management pp 9–11

The use of quality risk management (QRM) (ICH Q9) is encouraged to aid in the development of a robust analytical procedure to reduce risk of poor performance and reporting incorrect results.

Risk assessment is typically performed early in analytical procedure development and is updated as more information becomes available. Risk assessment can be formal or informal and can be supported by prior knowledge.

Risk assessment tools as described in ICH Q9 Annex 1 can be used to:

  1. Identify analytical procedure parameters (factors and operational steps) with potential impact on its performance — e.g., Ishikawa diagram (Annex A Figure 2🔍)
  2. Assess the potential impact of analytical procedure parameters on the analytical procedure performance
  3. Identify and prioritise analytical procedure parameters to be investigated experimentally
  4. Inform the need and the extent of ongoing monitoring as part of risk review

Risk communication should be used to support continual improvement of the analytical procedure performance throughout its lifecycle.

The outcome of quality risk management should be documented in the relevant parts of the applicant's pharmaceutical quality system (PQS) (ICH Q10).

§4.2 Risk Management

Four Applications of QRM in Analytical Procedure Development

The QRM tools of ICH Q9 (Ishikawa diagrams, FMEA, etc.) are explicitly applied in four areas of analytical procedure development:

Timing of Risk Assessment: "Performed Early in Development and Updated as Information Becomes Available"

Q14 stresses that risk assessment should be performed early in development and continually updated as more information is obtained:

  • Early in development: an initial risk assessment based on prior knowledge (§4.1); data are limited at this stage, so an informal approach is acceptable
  • During development: update the risk assessment once experimental data (DoE, robustness studies) have accumulated, adjusting the high/low risk classification
  • Before validation: the updated risk assessment confirms which performance characteristics need to be validated and how rigorously
  • Post-approval: routine monitoring data (trends, OOS/OOT investigations) trigger an update of the risk assessment

Risk assessment can be formal or informal; what matters is that it is documented, logical and linked to prior knowledge. The results must be recorded in the PQS (ICH Q10).

A Typical Application of the Ishikawa Diagram in HPLC Procedure Risk Assessment

Taking an HPLC purity procedure as an example, the "head of the fish" in the Ishikawa diagram is the procedure performance goal (e.g., impurity measurement accuracy ±10%)

The six typical categories of the "fishbone" (5M1E):

  • Equipment (Machine): column type, detector wavelength, temperature control, pump flow rate accuracy
  • Materials: solvent purity, buffer pH preparation, quality and expiry of the reference standard
  • Method (Parameters): flow rate, gradient program, injection volume, injection sequence
  • Man (Analyst): sample preparation steps, dissolution procedure, dilution operations
  • Measurement: integration method, choice of detection wavelength, calibration model
  • Environment: the effect of laboratory temperature and humidity on the sample and the column

Section Summary: ATP and QRM Integration pp 6–11

The ATP and QRM tools together form the foundation for systematic analytical procedure development under ICH Q14. The ATP captures measurement needs prospectively; QRM ensures that development resources are focused on the parameters and characteristics that matter most.

Key relationships:

  • ATP is informed by QTPP and CQA identification
  • ATP drives technology selection and defines validation acceptance criteria
  • Prior knowledge (§4.1) informs both ATP definition and initial risk assessment
  • Risk assessment (§4.2) prioritises experimental investigation of procedure parameters
  • ATP serves as the benchmark for lifecycle management decisions
  • QRM outcomes are documented in PQS (ICH Q10)

The degree of formality applied to both ATP documentation and risk assessment can be scaled to the complexity of the analytical procedure and the development approach chosen (minimal vs. enhanced).

Comprehensive Exercises

Application Exercise

A biopharmaceutical manufacturer plans to develop a brand-new analytical procedure for a CQA of a biological drug product.

  1. What is an ATP (Analytical Target Profile)?
  2. What core elements should an ATP contain?
  3. When should the ATP be submitted to the regulatory authority? Is submission mandatory?
Answer

1. Definition of the ATP:

The ATP (Analytical Target Profile) is a prospective description of an analytical measurement, stating the intended purpose and the performance characteristic criteria that the analytical procedure must meet. It is a "measurement requirements specification" established before the procedure is actually developed, similar to the QTPP concept in process development.

2. Core elements of the ATP (Q14 §3):

  • Intended purpose: what is the procedure used to measure? Which decision does it support (release, stability, process monitoring)?
  • Product attributes to be measured: the specific quality attribute, the type of sample matrix, and the expected concentration or content range
  • Performance characteristics with criteria: for example selectivity, target accuracy, upper limit for precision (RSD), detection limit requirements, etc.
  • Measurement requirements: operating environment (off-line / at-line / in-line), throughput requirements, etc.

3. Timing and mandatory status of ATP submission:

Formal documentation and submission of the ATP is optional and is not a regulatory requirement.

  • Recommended times to submit: when the enhanced approach is used for development; when you want to reach early agreement with the assessors on validation acceptance criteria; as a basis document for a PACMP (Post-Approval Change Management Protocol)
  • Benefits of submission: it facilitates regulatory communication and can be submitted whichever development approach is used; it can reduce review questions; it makes clear the rationale for the validation acceptance criteria
  • Not submitting is also acceptable: but the validation report must still explain the scientific justification for the performance criteria, and the ATP can be used as an internal development tool

Section 2: Robustness & Analytical Control Strategy

Covers §5 (Robustness & Parameter Ranges), §6 (Analytical Procedure Control Strategy), §6.1 (ECs for Analytical Procedures)  ·  Pages 12–17

§5.1 Robustness pp 12–13

Robustness is a measure of the capacity of an analytical procedure to meet the expected performance criteria during normal use when subjected to deliberate variations of analytical procedure parameters.

Robustness evaluation should consider the duration of analysis, including the stability of sample preparations and reagents.

Prior knowledge and risk assessment inform the selection of parameters to investigate. Parameters likely to influence procedure performance over the intended period of use should be studied.

For most analytical procedures, robustness evaluation is conducted during development and does not need to be repeated during validation (per ICH Q2). Data from validation studies (e.g., intermediate precision) can complement robustness evaluation.

For procedures with inherent high parameter variability (e.g., those requiring biological reagents), wider parameter ranges may need to be investigated.

Robustness of multivariate procedures may require additional considerations (see Chapter 8).

The outcome of robustness evaluation should be documented and reflected in the analytical procedure control strategy.

§5.1 Robustness

Core Definition: What Is "Robustness"?

Robustness measures the ability of an analytical procedure to continue to meet the expected performance criteria when procedure parameters undergo deliberate small variations.

The key is "deliberate variations": these are purposely designed experiments, not accidental errors. The aim is to find the boundaries of the procedure and make sure that small deviations under normal conditions of use do not cause failure.

Timing of evaluation: robustness testing should be performed during the development stage and need not be repeated at validation (per ICH Q2). Intermediate precision data from the validation stage can serve as a supplement.

Basis for selecting parameters:

  • Prior knowledge
  • Risk assessment
  • The parameters most likely to affect performance during the expected period of use
Special Note: High Variability of Biological Reagents

Analytical procedures that use biological reagents (such as antibodies and enzymes) need to investigate a wider range of parameters because of the high inherent batch-to-batch variability. This is especially important in biopharmaceutical potency assays.

The results of the robustness evaluation must be documented and reflected in the analytical procedure control strategy; this is a direct input to §6.

§5.2 Analytical Procedure Parameter Ranges pp 13–14

Experiments to investigate parameter ranges generate additional knowledge about procedure performance. Analytical procedure attributes and associated criteria could be derived from the ATP.

Univariate Examination

Examining a single parameter in isolation establishes a Proven Acceptable Range (PAR) for the analytical procedure — the range within which a parameter can be varied while the procedure continues to meet its performance criteria.

Enhanced Approach (Multivariate)

Ranges and interactions are investigated in multivariate experiments (DoE). Risk assessment and prior knowledge identify parameters, attributes, and ranges for investigation. Categorical variables (e.g., different instruments, different analysts) can also be included in the experimental design.

Outcomes from development studies — understanding of relationships between AP parameters (inputs) and analytical responses (outputs). Based on results:

  • Fixed set-points defined for some parameters
  • PARs defined for individual parameters that can vary within an acceptable range
  • MODRs (Method Operable Design Regions) for combined ranges of two or more parameters within which the AP is shown fit for intended purpose

Key regulatory rule: Moving within an approved PAR or MODR does NOT require regulatory notification.

AP validation for PAR/MODR is required only for performance characteristics not already covered by development data. The part of a PAR or MODR intended for routine use (typically the intended operational conditions or set point) must be covered by validation data.

For future changes within a PAR or MODR: an assessment of the need and extent of additional validation tests is required. A validation strategy (e.g., as part of a validation protocol) can define the necessary scope.

§5.2 Analytical Procedure Parameter Ranges

Comparing Three Range Concepts: Set-Point vs PAR vs MODR
Important Rule: How Changes Within a PAR/MODR Are Managed

Operational changes made within an approved PAR or MODR do not need to be reported to the regulatory authority and can be managed within the company's pharmaceutical quality system (PQS).

An internal assessment is still needed, however: are additional validation tests required? This should be defined in advance in the validation protocol or the change control procedure.

Comparison of univariate vs. multivariate procedures:

Validation Coverage Requirements

Validation data must cover the part of the PAR or MODR intended for routine use (usually the expected operating conditions or the set point). Performance characteristics already covered by development data do not need to be repeated at validation.

§6 Analytical Procedure Control Strategy pp 14–16

The AP control strategy ensures that the analytical procedure is fit for its intended purpose during routine use throughout its lifecycle. It consists of controls derived from the current understanding of the AP, including development data, risk assessment, robustness evaluation, and prior knowledge.

The AP control strategy should be defined before validation and confirmed after validation is finalised.

AP Control Strategy Includes:

  • AP parameters that need to be controlled
  • System Suitability Test (SST) — an integral part of the analytical procedure

The AP should describe steps necessary to perform each analytical test: sample, reference materials, reagents, sample/control preparations, apparatus use, calibration curve generation, number of replicates, and formulae for calculating reportable results. The level of detail should enable a skilled analyst to perform the analysis.

System Suitability Test (SST)

The SST depends on the type and intent of the AP. It is typically conducted with one or more predefined materials (positive/negative controls). The SST verifies selected AP attributes, with acceptance criteria based on AP performance criteria. Components are selected using risk assessment, knowledge, and development data.

The SST verifies that the measurement system and analytical operations are fit for intended purpose. Validity of results depends on the outcome of the SST.

In the enhanced approach, well-designed SST parameters and criteria represent an important aspect of risk mitigation.

For multivariate APs: data quality is verified using suitable software tools.

§6 Analytical Procedure Control Strategy

The Four Components of the AP Control Strategy

Timing requirement: the control strategy should be defined before validation and confirmed after validation is complete, which ensures that the validation design is consistent with the objectives of the control strategy.

Analogy: SST Is Like the Pre-Flight Checklist Before Takeoff

Before every takeoff, the captain must complete a standard pre-flight checklist (engine status, fuel, instruments, etc.) and confirm that all systems are working properly before takeoff is permitted.

The logic of SST is exactly the same: before each analytical run, predefined materials (such as a system suitability standard) are used to verify that the instrument, reagents and operating system are working properly, so that the results for the actual samples can be considered valid.

Key point: the validity of the results depends on whether the SST passes. Just as an aircraft must not skip the pre-flight check, an analyst must not report results when the SST has failed.

Sample Suitability Assessment p 15

Often used for biologics: a sample or sample preparation is considered suitable if the measurement response satisfies predefined acceptance criteria. Along with SST, this is a prerequisite for the validity of the result.

Sample suitability assessment may include:

  • Assessment of the similarity of response between the reference material and the test sample
  • Assessment of interfering signals
  • For multivariate APs: a data quality check using software to verify the sample fits within the model space

Ongoing Monitoring

Ongoing monitoring is recommended to look for trends. It facilitates lifecycle management and enables proactive intervention before the AP performance degrades.

Sample Suitability Assessment vs. System Suitability Test (SST)

Distinction: SST vs. Sample Suitability Assessment, and What Each One Verifies
The Importance of Continued Monitoring

Q14 recommends (but does not require) continued monitoring, in order to:

  • Detect trends (such as slow degradation of instrument performance)
  • Support lifecycle management
  • Intervene proactively to make improvements before procedure performance actually declines

This is "preventive maintenance" for the analytical procedure, rather than waiting for a failure and then remediating.

§6.1 Established Conditions (ECs) for Analytical Procedures pp 16–17

In line with ICH Q12, applicants may propose Established Conditions (ECs) for an analytical procedure. ECs are identified using risk assessment, prior knowledge, and uni-/multi-variate experimentation.

Minimal vs Enhanced Approach to ECs

With a minimal approach: the number of ECs may be extensive, with fixed parameters and set points described in detail.

With an enhanced approach: increased understanding facilitates identification of an appropriate, reduced set of ECs focused on AP performance (e.g., acceptable ranges) when justified by AP understanding and risk management.

What ECs Could Consist of:

  • Performance characteristics and associated criteria (e.g., as described in the ATP)
  • Analytical procedure principle (physicochemical basis or specific technology)
  • SST and sample suitability assessment criteria
  • Set points and/or ranges for one or more AP parameters

Parameters that need control, or where the need for control cannot reasonably be excluded, should be identified as ECs. If SST or performance criteria demonstrate that a parameter is under control, that parameter may not need to be defined as an EC (or may be assigned a lower reporting category).

ECs and reporting categories are proposed by the applicant and assessed by regulatory authorities.

Important: Use of the enhanced approach should NOT lead to a less detailed description of the AP in the regulatory submission. Module 3 AP descriptions must still be suitably detailed, including ECs and supportive information.

The Established Conditions (ECs) concept introduced by ICH Q12 was originally aimed at manufacturing processes: ECs are the conditions in the regulatory approval documents that fall under regulatory oversight, and changing an EC requires a regulatory submission.

Common Misconception: The Enhanced Approach Does Not Mean Less Detail in the Submission

Many people mistakenly believe that the "enhanced approach" means less information can be submitted in the regulatory filing. This is wrong.

  • Minimal approach: a list of fixed parameters
  • Enhanced approach: performance criteria + acceptable ranges + supporting development data

Application Example pp 13–14

The following scenario illustrates the regulatory implications of a MODR in the context of routine analytical operations and change management.

Key principle: once an analytical procedure parameter range (PAR or MODR) has been approved as part of the regulatory submission, the applicant has the flexibility to operate anywhere within that space without further regulatory notification.

This flexibility is predicated on the control strategy — including SST and ongoing monitoring — remaining effective to confirm the procedure is operating as intended.

For any change within a PAR or MODR, an internal assessment of whether additional validation tests are needed remains a requirement, even if no regulatory notification is triggered.

Application Exercise

Exercise

A company uses an HPLC procedure for the assay of a drug substance and has defined a MODR in its regulatory filing covering:

  • Column temperature: 30–40°C
  • Mobile phase ratio: adjustable within the defined range

The company now wants to change the column temperature for routine operation from 35°C to 38°C (still within the approved MODR).

Question: Do they need to report this change to the regulatory authority? What actions should they take?

Answer

No regulatory filing is needed.

Reason: 38°C is within the approved MODR (30–40°C). Under ICH Q14 §5.2, operational changes made within an approved PAR or MODR can be managed within the company's pharmaceutical quality system (PQS) and do not need to be reported to the regulatory authority.

Internal actions that should still be taken:

  • Internal change assessment: assess whether additional validation tests are needed (usually not, if 38°C is already covered by the development/validation data).
  • PQS record: document the change in accordance with the company's change control procedure.
  • SST confirmation: make sure the existing system suitability test criteria remain valid under the new operating conditions.
  • Validation coverage check: confirm that 38°C is included in the range covered by the validation data (per Q14 §5.2, the part of the MODR intended for routine use must be supported by validation data).

Three main drivers:

The ATP as the Basis for a PACMP

Practical Benefits of Using a PACMP for Analytical Procedures

What is a PACMP? Post-Approval Change Management Protocol: a "roadmap for future changes" agreed in advance with the regulatory authority. When specified predefined conditions are met, the change can be made under a lower reporting category.

How does the ATP become the basis for a PACMP?

  1. The ATP defines the performance criteria the procedure must meet (e.g., accuracy 95–105%, precision RSD ≤ 5%)
  2. The PACMP agrees that if a future new procedure (such as LC-MS/MS) meets the ATP requirements + a bridging study is completed, the change can be reported as a Notification rather than Prior Approval
  3. The regulatory authority approves the PACMP → when the change is made in future, it is executed directly according to the plan, greatly improving efficiency

Example scenario:

  • The company expects that within the next 3 years it may replace the potency assay from a cell-based assay with a receptor-binding assay
  • A PACMP is established at the time of the application: it defines the acceptance criteria (the ATP), the bridging study plan and the acceptable comparability criteria
  • At the time of execution: complete the studies according to the PACMP → report as a Notification rather than waiting for Prior Approval (which can save 12–18 months)

Take "baking a cake" (= an analytical procedure) as an example:

§7 Product/Process Changes and Introducing a New Analytical Procedure

How Product or Process Changes Affect the Analytical Procedure

Any significant change to the product or process may trigger reassessment of the analytical procedure:

If an ATP has been established, first assess whether the existing ATP is still applicable; if not, the ATP must be updated, and then assess whether the existing analytical procedure can still meet the updated ATP.

Three Steps When Proposing an Entirely New Analytical Procedure
  1. Comprehensive risk assessment: assess the risks introduced by the new procedure (technical complexity, CQA criticality, degree of difference from the original procedure)
  2. Establish a new procedure control strategy: confirm the ECs, SST criteria and PAR/MODR (if applicable) for the new procedure
  3. Justify the ECs in the regulatory communication: provide sufficient scientific justification in the change application to explain the choice of ECs for the new procedure and the appropriateness of the reporting category

The PQS (pharmaceutical quality system) is the "memory" for all analytical procedure changes:

  • Changes that must be reported: recorded in both the PQS and the regulatory submission
  • Changes that need not be reported (such as adjustments within the PAR/MODR): recorded in the PQS only, but the record must be complete (including the reason for the change, the assessment results and the approval record)
  • ICH Q12 compliance: the PQS change management process must conform to ICH Q12 recommendations; it cannot be a simple documentation exercise and needs a complete change assessment and approval process
Integrated Application Question

A company plans to replace the HPLC assay procedure for its drug substance with an LC-MS/MS procedure (a change in the analytical principle). (1) What bridging studies are needed? (2) Which reporting category usually applies to this change? (3) If the company wants a lower reporting category, what tools might be available?

Answer

(1) Bridging study requirements (Table 2, Scenario 1: change of analytical procedure principle):

  1. Full validation: the new LC-MS/MS procedure needs full validation per ICH Q2, including specificity, linearity, range, accuracy, precision, detection limit/quantitation limit (if applicable) and robustness
  2. Comparative analysis of representative samples: representative batch samples (including edge-of-failure batches and forced degradation samples) must be analyzed with both the new and old procedures at the same time to confirm consistency of results
  3. Reference material comparison: the results for the reference standard under the two procedures must be comparable
  4. Confirmation of discriminating power: the new procedure's ability to distinguish acceptable (conforming) from unacceptable (non-conforming) samples must be equivalent to that of the original HPLC procedure

(2) Reporting category: usually Prior Approval, because:

  • The analytical principle changes (HPLC-UV → LC-MS/MS) = a high degree of change
  • Drug substance assay = a critical CQA, directly related to safety and efficacy
  • According to the risk assessment logic of Figure 2🔍: high CQA criticality + high degree of change → Prior Approval

(3) Tools for lowering the reporting category:

  • PACMP (preferred): if a PACMP was established at the time of the original application, with the acceptance criteria and bridging study plan for LC-MS/MS defined in advance, the change can be reported as a Notification
  • ECs + sufficient prior knowledge: if the company has extensive LC-MS/MS platform knowledge and the ATP and ECs of the existing HPLC procedure are well defined, a comprehensive risk assessment can be provided in the submission to support a lower reporting category
  • Limitation: even with the tools above, the regulatory authority has the final say, and a company proposal at moderate risk may be required to be upgraded to Prior Approval

§8 Development of Multivariate Analytical Procedures p11–14

Multivariate analytical procedures are those where a result is determined through a multivariate calibration model utilising more than one input variable. The considerations here are for models using latent variables that are mathematically related to directly measured variables.

Sample and Sample Population

Multivariate models link measured model variables with values obtained from a validated reference analytical procedure or from reference samples. Care should be taken to ensure that uncertainty in the reference analytical procedure is sufficiently low in relation to the intended performance of the multivariate analytical procedure.

The sample population should encompass the sources of variability likely to occur during manufacturing and analysis: raw material quality, manufacturing process variability, storage conditions, sample preparation and testing. Development laboratory and pilot scale samples are often utilised to provide variability. Inclusion of commercial scale samples is recommended to capture variability related to specific equipment and/or processing conditions.

Data Transformation

The selection of the data transformation method(s) can be driven by the type of data, instrument or sample, the intended purpose of the model and/or prior knowledge. Any transformation should be documented and justified.

Variable Selection

Variable selection is performed during model development (e.g., wavelength range selection in spectroscopic applications). It depends on the measurement principle, application and other factors, and should be justified.

Robustness and Latent Variables

Model robustness should be built into the model by including relevant sources of variability. A critical factor is the number of latent variables — too many can result in model overfitting, potentially resulting in decreased robustness and a need for more frequent model updates.

Recalibration and Model Maintenance

Tracking calibration model performance is an important part of ongoing monitoring. Diagnostic tools include:

  • Examination of residuals (e.g., x-residuals or F-probability)
  • Outlier diagnostics (e.g., Hotelling's T-squared or Mahalanobis distance)

Continued performance should be confirmed on a periodic and event-driven basis by comparison of model predictions with known values of reference samples. Examples of events that could trigger confirmatory testing include new known process variability, unexpected process events or scheduled instrument maintenance.

Monitoring can trigger model rebuilding (recalibration) as part of continual improvement. When updating the model, new data may need to be included and old non-relevant data may be removed.

§8 Development of Multivariate Analytical Procedures

What a Multivariate Procedure Is

Multivariate analytical procedure: an analytical procedure that determines a result by using a calibration model with more than one input variable.

Typical examples: NIR (near-infrared) spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, multi-wavelength UV analysis

Core principle: measure absorbance at multiple frequencies/wavelengths (multiple input variables) → apply a mathematical model (PLS, PCR, etc.) → predict the target quality attribute (output)

Sample Selection Strategy: Representativeness Is the Key

The performance ceiling of a multivariate model is set by the diversity of samples in the calibration set. Sample selection works at three levels:

Number of Latent Variables: Enough, but Not Too Many

The number of latent variables is one of the most critical decisions in multivariate model development:

  • Too few: the model under-fits, and prediction accuracy is insufficient
  • Too many: the model over-fits, memorizing noise rather than true signal → robustness declines and more frequent recalibration is needed
  • Optimal number: the smallest number that gives good predictive performance on both the calibration set and the validation set

Q14 expects a justification for the final number of latent variables selected, which may be supported by diagnostic plots provided by the software (such as cross-validation plots).

Triggers for Model Maintenance (Event-Driven + Periodic)

Model performance should be confirmed in the following situations:

  • Periodic: at a predefined frequency, compare model predictions with known values using reference samples
  • Event-driven：
    • A new known process variation (e.g., a change of raw material supplier)
    • An unexpected process event
    • Scheduled instrument maintenance (which may affect the light source or optical path)
Three Stages of the Model Lifecycle (Figure 3)

ICH Q14 Figure 3 describes the lifecycle of a multivariate model:

  1. Model development: sample selection → data collection → calibration → internal testing → validation (independent data) → maintenance plan
  2. Routine use: outlier diagnostics with every measurement → periodic/event-driven confirmation
  3. Model maintenance: unacceptable performance detected → model re-evaluation → possible redevelopment or modification of the maintenance plan

This is an iterative lifecycle loop that allows the model to be continually improved as more process knowledge accumulates.

§9 Development of Analytical Procedures for RTRT p14–15

Real Time Release Testing (RTRT) is the ability to evaluate and ensure the quality of in-process and/or final product based on process data, which typically include a valid combination of measured material attributes and process controls (ICH Q8). RTRT measurements work in conjunction with all elements of the product control strategy.

RTRT can be based on an appropriate combination of one or more process measurements and/or material attributes to provide a value for one or more CQAs and should be specific for those CQAs. The relationship between the RTRT approach and the CQAs, as well as acceptance criteria, should be fully justified.

An RTRT analytical procedure should be validated as recommended in ICH Q2 and it should be demonstrated that the process measurements have appropriate specificity for the targeted quality attribute.

Sampling and Sample Interface

The measurement point(s) should be chosen to be representative of the entire material being processed with the sample duration or amount appropriately chosen (e.g., relative to a unit dose). The sample interface should remain consistent over the duration of manufacturing and should be robust to expected processing and environmental variations.

Product Specification

The RTRT approach should be included in the product specification (ICH Q6A, ICH Q6B). If the dossier includes a registered alternative control strategy to RTRT (e.g., traditional end-product testing when process analytics are unavailable), the related analytical procedures and when they would be applied should also be included in the product specifications.

Quantitative RTRT results should be expressed in the same units as those for traditional testing.

§9 Development of Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT) Analytical Procedures

Core Definition of RTRT

RTRT (Real Time Release Testing) (from ICH Q8):

The ability to evaluate and ensure the quality of in-process and/or final product based on process data, which typically include a valid combination of measured material attributes and process controls.

Key characteristics:

  • RTRT is not standalone; it must work together with all elements of the overall product control strategy
  • RTRT can be applied to DS (drug substance), intermediates, and DP (drug product)
  • It must have sufficient specificity for the target CQAs
Validation Requirements for RTRT

An RTRT analytical procedure must be validated in accordance with ICH Q2 (the same requirements as for a conventional procedure), and additionally needs to demonstrate:

  • The process measurement has appropriate specificity for the target quality attribute
  • The measurement point is representative of the whole material being processed (not a localized sample)
  • The sample interface remains consistent throughout the manufacturing run and is robust to process and environmental changes
Including RTRT in the Product Specification

Per ICH Q6A/Q6B, an RTRT procedure must be included in the product specification, including:

  • A reference to the analytical procedure
  • The associated acceptance criteria
  • The units in which quantitative results are expressed (which must be the same as for the conventional test)

Requirement for an alternative control strategy: if the application includes a registered alternative control strategy for RTRT (such as a conventional test to be used when the PAT equipment is unavailable), the related analytical procedure and the circumstances in which it applies must also be described in the specification.

RTRT in CM (Continuous Manufacturing)

RTRT is especially important in CM (see ICH Q13 §4.2):

  • The continuous flow of material in CM makes conventional batch end-point testing difficult to apply
  • In-line/on-line methods such as NIR can monitor CQAs (e.g., content uniformity, moisture) continuously during manufacturing
  • Development and validation of an RTRT procedure must meet the combined requirements of Q14 §8 (multivariate) and §9 (RTRT)
Integrated Review Question

A tablet manufacturer wants to replace the conventional finished-product content uniformity (CU) test with an NIR method on the tablet press feed frame. What development and validation requirements must this method meet?

Answer

This NIR method is a "multivariate analytical procedure used for RTRT," so it must satisfy the combined requirements of §8 and §9:

Development requirements (§8 multivariate):

  • Sample selection: cover all expected content variation at commercial scale (90–110% label claim)
  • Reference method: use a validated HPLC assay method to establish the true values of the calibration samples
  • Model development: determine the optimal number of latent variables; define the wavelength range; establish a maintenance plan

Special RTRT requirements (§9):

  • Representativeness of the measurement point: the NIR probe must represent the material of the whole batch, not just a localized portion
  • Specificity verification: the method must be sufficiently selective for content (vs. interferences such as color or moisture)
  • Robustness of the sample interface: the probe must remain consistent throughout the manufacturing run (including long CM runs)
  • Inclusion in the specification: list the NIR RTRT method and acceptance criteria in the DP specification (in the same units as the conventional test)
  • Fallback strategy: the conventional end-point testing plan to be used if the PAT system fails must be described in the specification

Summary of Where Analytical Procedure Information Sits in the CTD
Obligation to Distinguish ECs from Supportive Information

If the application includes ECs, it must clearly distinguish the ECs from supportive information.

This mirrors the same requirement in ICH Q12 and ensures reviewers can clearly see which conditions are regulatory-binding (ECs) and which are merely background information.

Special Submission Requirements for Multivariate Model Validation (§10.3)

The description of the validation of a multivariate model used for a specification (including RTRT) should include five additional elements:

  1. Description of the independent validation set: explain the selection strategy and representativeness of the validation set samples
  2. Performance criteria for model validation: specific performance criteria (such as RMSEP, SEP)
  3. Evaluation of model validation results: the actual validation results compared against the performance criteria
  4. Relationship between model performance criteria and specification limits: explain how the prediction accuracy of the model supports specification decisions
  5. High-level overview of the PQS elements for model monitoring and maintenance: the outlier diagnostic method and the procedure to follow when outliers occur

§11 Key Glossary Terms p16–22

§11 Glossary Essentials: Q14 vs. Q2(R2) Terminology

Terms Newly Introduced in Q14 vs. Existing Q2(R2) Terms
Key Distinction: AP Attribute vs. Performance Characteristic

AP Attribute (analytical procedure attribute): technology-specific
Example: "resolution Rs" in HPLC is meaningful only in chromatographic analysis.

Performance Characteristic: technology-independent
Example: "accuracy" applies to all procedures, including HPLC, NIR, and GC.

→ This distinction lets the ATP define requirements in technology-independent language (performance characteristics), while the SST uses technology-specific indicators (AP attributes) to verify that they are met on the specific technology.

Q14 Comprehensive Review Question

A company developed an HPLC related-substances procedure using the enhanced approach, established a PAR and MODR, and submitted the ATP and ECs. After approval, the HPLC system must be replaced because of an instrument upgrade (same method principle, new instrument brand). Describe the complete change management pathway.

Answer

1. Change identification and risk assessment:

  • This is a "change within the same principle" (Table 2)
  • Assess the impact of the new instrument on the previously approved PAR/MODR
  • Assess whether the approved SST can still effectively detect performance problems on the new instrument

2. Confirm the reporting category (based on the reporting category of the originally submitted EC):

  • If "instrument brand" is not an EC → this change requires no regulatory communication and is managed within the PQS
  • If "instrument brand" is an EC → follow the established reporting category (possibly a notification)

3. Bridging studies (per Table 2, change within the same principle):

  • Verify SST performance on the new instrument (system precision, etc.)
  • Comparative analysis (compare results for representative samples on the old and new systems)
  • Confirm that the original PAR/MODR remains valid on the new instrument

4. Documentation and follow-up:

  • Record the change in the PQS (whether or not a submission is required)
  • Update the analytical procedure description to reflect the new instrument
