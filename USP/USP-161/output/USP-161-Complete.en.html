Introduction: Scope and Device Classification

Original Text

The methods and requirements in this chapter apply to assemblies or devices labeled sterile and nonpyrogenic that are in contact directly or indirectly with the cardiovascular system, lymphatic system, or cerebrospinal fluid. This includes, but may not be limited to, the following:

  • Fluid pathways of catheters and administration sets such as solution administration sets, extension sets, transfer sets, blood administration sets, intravenous catheters, implants, extracorporeal oxygenator tubing, dialysis tubing, intramuscular drug delivery catheters, and transfusion and infusion assemblies
  • Liquid medical devices such as dialysate
  • Implantable medical devices such as heart valves and vascular grafts, and other medical devices with a nonpyrogenic claim that may come into contact with blood or cerebrospinal fluid
  • Gels with a nonpyrogenic claim including demineralized bone matrices and drug delivery systems
"The methods and requirements in this chapter apply to assemblies or devices labeled sterile and nonpyrogenic that are in contact directly or indirectly with the cardiovascular system, lymphatic system, or cerebrospinal fluid."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: How ⟨161⟩ Relates to ⟨85⟩

USP ⟨85⟩ is the general methodology chapter for the bacterial endotoxins test (BET). It defines the procedures, standards, and controls for the gel-clot, kinetic, and endpoint methods.

USP ⟨161⟩ is the application chapter written specifically for medical devices. It tells you:

  • Which devices must undergo BET
  • What the endotoxin limit is for each device
  • How to extract endotoxin from the device surface
  • How to calculate the endotoxin limit for the extract

In short: ⟨85⟩ tells you "how to test"; ⟨161⟩ tells you "what to test and what the limit is."

Analogy

Testing swimming pool water:

Think of ⟨85⟩ as a "water quality testing methods manual" that teaches you how to use reagents to detect bacteria in water. ⟨161⟩ is more like the "pool management regulation": it sets the water quality standard for each type of pool (children's pool, deep pool, spa) and the sampling approach (where to draw water and how much).

You cannot test a pool with just the methods manual; you also need to know what the pass criterion is for that pool. In the same way, device BET cannot rely on ⟨85⟩ alone; you must also follow the limits and extraction requirements of ⟨161⟩.

Key Point: CDMO Relevance

Why does a CDMO need to understand ⟨161⟩?

When your product is a combination product (for example a prefilled syringe (PFS), an autoinjector, or a drug-coated stent), the drug part follows ⟨85⟩ and the device part follows ⟨161⟩.

This applies in particular to RTU (ready-to-use) container systems:

  • PFS glass barrel + plunger + needle shield: these are all device components
  • RTU vials and syringe nest & tub: as device packaging, they must meet ⟨161⟩
  • Your CDMO customers may ask you to provide BET data for these components

Practice Questions

  1. For a prefilled syringe (PFS), do the "drug solution" part and the "syringe device" part fall under ⟨85⟩ or ⟨161⟩ respectively? How is the limit set?
  2. If a customer sends you a batch of RTU vials and asks for endotoxin testing before filling, which USP chapter should you refer to when setting the limit?
Definitions: Endotoxin Limits and Key Definitions

Original Text

Endotoxin Limit

The endotoxin limit for the finished device is NMT 20 USP Endotoxin Units per device and NMT 2.15 USP Endotoxin Units for devices in contact with cerebrospinal fluid.

For devices that directly or indirectly contact the intraocular environment, a lower endotoxin limit may apply (1).

The endotoxin limit for the device extract in Endotoxin Units/mL is calculated as:

Endotoxin Limit Formula

Result = (K × N) / V

K = limit for each device
N = number of devices tested
V = total volume of the extract

Kit

A kit is defined as a collection of individual devices in its primary package, or a variety of related devices. Each individual type of device may have its own product endotoxin limit and should be tested and evaluated on an individual basis.

Lambda (λ)

λ is the test method sensitivity for gel-clot methods. For turbidimetric or chromogenic technique, λ is equal to the lowest endotoxin concentration on the referenced standard curve.

Product Families

Product families for the purposes of BET may be defined on the basis of common materials of construction and/or common components. The choice of family members should be justified and documented. If a product family contains devices of many sizes, the device with the largest surface area claimed to be nonpyrogenic is chosen as the representative member of the family to be used in suitability testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Endotoxin Limit System

The limit logic for devices is completely different from that for drugs:

  • Drug products (⟨85⟩): the limit is dose-based: 5 EU/kg/hr (intravenous), calculated from patient body weight and rate of administration
  • Devices (⟨161⟩): the limit is per device: a fixed 20 EU/device, independent of patient body weight
  • Devices contacting cerebrospinal fluid (CSF): stricter: 2.15 EU/device (because the central nervous system is extremely sensitive to endotoxin)

This difference reflects a practical reality: the endotoxin quantity in a drug product can be calculated precisely from concentration and dose, but endotoxin on a device comes from residue on its surface and depends on device size and contact area, so "per device" is the more intuitive unit.

Formula Breakdown: Limit Calculation

Result = (K × N) / V. Let us work through a practical example:

Suppose you are testing 3 PFS barrels (a general device, not CSF-contacting):

K = 20 EU/device
N = 3 devices
V = 120 mL (3 barrels × 40 mL of extract each)

Result = (20 × 3) / 120 = 0.5 EU/mL

In other words, the endotoxin concentration of the extract must be < 0.5 EU/mL to pass.

Note: the extraction volume V may be adjusted to the size and configuration of the device, but the choice must be documented and validated.

Analogy: The Logic of a Product Family

Clothes of the same style in different sizes:

Imagine you run a garment quality testing laboratory. A customer sends T-shirts of the same style in four sizes: S, M, L, and XL. You need to run quality tests, but you cannot realistically run a full validation on every size separately.

The approach of USP ⟨161⟩ is to choose the largest size (XL) for representative testing, because it has the greatest surface area and so, in theory, the highest risk of endotoxin residue. If XL passes, the smaller sizes should be fine too.

Likewise, for a device product family, the member with the "largest surface area" is used as the representative for suitability testing.

Key Point: Testing Challenges for Kits

Kits are the category most easily confused. A surgical kit may contain many different devices, and each may have a different endotoxin limit.

USP ⟨161⟩ gives two options:

  • Test separately each device that carries a non-pyrogenic claim, with each device subject to its own limit
  • Pool and test all items together, though the limit calculation then becomes more complex

In a CDMO's RTU container nest, the combination of nest + tub + cap can also be regarded as a kind of kit, and your customer may ask you to report data for each component separately.

Extraction Methods: Methods and Conditions

Original Text

Extracting or Rinsing Solution

Water for BET [see Bacterial Endotoxins Test ⟨85⟩, Reagents and Test Solutions, Water for Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET)] or other suitable solvent demonstrated to be noninterfering with the BET assay is used to extract or rinse the medical device.

Sampling

  1. Sample: A sample for end-product testing must be in its final configuration and packaging, including all component parts that make up the final medical device.
  2. Representative sampling: A representative sample is a sample plan that is created and justified based on the assumption that the manufacturing process is validated and in a state of control.
  3. Sample size: The number of devices chosen for routine testing is dependent on the size of the lot, level of control, statistical considerations, and historical performance. In most cases, each lot of product must be tested using an appropriate number of samples, NMT 10, taken at random to represent the quality of the lot.
  4. Pre-post sterilization samples: When qualifying a BET assay for pre-sterilization samples, equivalency in test results must be shown with post-sterilization samples to ensure that sterilization and post-sterilization handling have no adverse impact on the accuracy of the test result.

Standard Extraction Method

The standard extraction method is to soak or immerse the device or flush the fluid pathway with extracting fluid that has been heated to 37 ± 1.0°C, keeping the extracting fluid in contact with the relevant surface(s) for NLT 1 h at controlled room temperature. Alternate extraction or rinsing methods may be used, but must be demonstrated to be equivalent to or better than the standard method.

Extracts from individual medical devices are typically pooled for testing.

The standard extracting fluid for extracting, rinsing, or soaking medical devices is Water for BET. If necessary, other solvents may be used after it has been demonstrated that they do not interfere with the performance of the assay.

Either the entire device if claimed to be nonpyrogenic or all components of the device that are claimed to be nonpyrogenic, including fluid pathways, must come in contact with the extraction fluid for the entire course of the extraction.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Science Behind Extraction

Why is "extraction" needed?

Endotoxin is lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which adheres to device surfaces. You cannot simply put the device into LAL reagent; you must first use water to "wash off" the endotoxin from the surface and then test that wash solution.

USP ⟨161⟩ specifies two extraction approaches:

  • Soak / Immerse: for devices with a non-pyrogenic claim on the outer surface, such as implants and bone matrix
  • Flush: for devices with a fluid pathway, such as catheters, infusion sets, and the PFS lumen

Key conditions: BET water at 37 ± 1.0°C, with contact for NLT 1 hour. This simulates the dissolution of endotoxin from the device surface at body temperature.

Analogy

The principle of brewing tea:

Extracting endotoxin from a device is like brewing tea. You need:

  • The right water temperature (37°C, not boiling water, since excessive heat can damage some device materials)
  • Enough soaking time (at least 1 hour, so the "flavor" is fully released)
  • An appropriate amount of water (too little and nothing comes out; too much and it is diluted below detection)

You must also make sure the water contacts all surfaces that carry a non-pyrogenic claim, just as tea leaves must be fully submerged in the water, with no part left sticking out.

Key Point: Practical CDMO Considerations

Extraction challenges for RTU containers and PFS:

  • PFS barrel: the lumen is the fluid pathway, so use the flush method, but make sure the extract contacts the entire inner wall, including the Luer cone
  • RTU vials: usually extracted by soaking, or by filling with extract and shaking; extraction efficiency must be validated
  • Nest & tub: the nest and outer tub for multiple vials have a large surface area, so the choice of extraction volume is critical
  • Pooling: extracts from several devices are combined and tested together. This improves efficiency, but you must make sure N and V in the calculation formula correspond correctly

Pre-sterilization vs post-sterilization sampling: if you sample before sterilization for BET (allowing earlier release), you must demonstrate equivalence using post-sterilization samples. In addition, if the product supports microbial growth (for example a device containing a sugar solution), pre-sterilization testing must not be used.

Key Point: Sample Size Limit

USP ⟨161⟩ sets the sample size for routine testing at NMT 10 (no more than 10 units). This differs from drug products, where the sample quantity is usually fixed.

The sample size depends on:

  • Lot size
  • Level of process control
  • Statistical considerations
  • Historical performance

If your process is stable and has a good history, you can use fewer samples. However, an alternative sampling plan must be supported by a complete risk assessment.

Suitability Testing

Original Text

Suitability testing (also known as test for interfering factors): Suitability studies of device extracts are designed to ensure that the device extract under test does not adversely impact the accuracy of the test result by either a) causing the assay to underestimate endotoxin (inhibition) or b) contributing to overestimation of endotoxin (enhancement).

Suitability of the BET assay must be demonstrated for each product or product family. The number of batches of product chosen for the suitability study is reflective of the level of control of the process and must be justified.

If necessary, adjust the pH of the solution to be examined (or dilution thereof) so that the pH of the mixture of the lysate and sample solution falls within the pH range specified by the lysate manufacturer, usually pH 6.0–8.0.

If glucan interference is suspected, the use of a glucan-blocking agent or endotoxin-specific lysate is permissible.

Dilution in Water for BET or other validated solvent by a factor not to exceed the maximum valid dilution (MVD) is permissible. The MVD for medical devices is:

MVD Formula for Devices

MVD = Endotoxin limit (EU/mL) / λ

λ = sensitivity of the test method (EU/mL)

Extracting or Rinsing Solution

The standard extracting fluid is Water for BET. If necessary, other solvents may be used after it has been demonstrated that they do not interfere with the performance of the assay. Extraction is not required for liquid medical devices.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Two Sides of Interfering Factors

Device extracts may contain various leachables (plasticizers, silicone oil, metal ions, colloids) that can interfere with the LAL reaction:

  • Inhibition: extracted substances inhibit the LAL reaction, so the measured endotoxin is too low, creating a false-negative risk. This is the most dangerous situation.
  • Enhancement: extracted substances promote the LAL reaction, so the measured endotoxin is too high, creating a false-positive risk that leads to rejection of acceptable product.

Glucan (β-glucan) interference: some device materials (such as cellulose-based materials and certain gels) may release β-glucan, which triggers the Factor G pathway in the LAL reaction and causes false positives. Solutions: use a glucan-blocking agent or an endotoxin-specific LAL reagent.

Analogy: What MVD Means

Dilution is like mixing a cocktail:

If the device extract interferes with the LAL reaction, the simplest remedy is dilution, which lowers the concentration of the interfering substance until it no longer interferes. But dilution also lowers the endotoxin concentration, and if you dilute too far you can no longer detect it.

The MVD is the "upper limit" of how far you can dilute. It ensures that the endotoxin concentration after dilution is still within the sensitivity (λ) range of the test. It is like mixing a cocktail: you can add ice to dilute the alcohol, but add too much and it becomes just water.

Key Point: Ongoing Maintenance of Suitability

Suitability is not "do it once and you are done." USP ⟨161⟩ explicitly requires re-evaluation in the following situations:

  • Product design change
  • Process change
  • Change of raw material supplier
  • Change of manufacturing or testing site
  • Change of BET reagent source
  • Change of extraction technique (suitability studies must be repeated)
  • Change of BET method (for example from gel-clot to kinetic chromogenic)

For a CDMO, this means that every time a customer changes the supplier of a device component, you need to assess whether suitability testing must be repeated.

Practice Questions

  1. Calculation exercise: the endotoxin limit of a device extract is 0.5 EU/mL, and the sensitivity of your LAL reagent is λ = 0.03 EU/mL. Calculate the MVD. If the suitability test passes at a 1:4 dilution, is this dilution factor within the allowed MVD range?
  2. A silicone-oil-coated PFS barrel shows clear BET inhibition in its extract. Besides dilution, what other measures can you take to overcome the interference?

  • Position of USP ⟨161⟩: the BET application chapter specific to medical devices, forming a complete system with ⟨85⟩ (methodology) and ⟨151⟩ (pyrogen test)
  • Scope: devices labeled sterile and non-pyrogenic that contact the cardiovascular system, the lymphatic system, or cerebrospinal fluid, directly or indirectly
  • Endotoxin limits: general devices ≤ 20 EU/device; CSF-contacting ≤ 2.15 EU/device; the intraocular environment may be lower
  • Limit formula: Result = (K × N) / V, which converts the per-device limit into an extract concentration limit
  • Standard extraction conditions: BET water, 37 ± 1.0°C, contact NLT 1 hour, soak or flush

  • Key CDMO point: the device part of a combination product (PFS, autoinjector) follows ⟨161⟩, not the drug-product limit logic of ⟨85⟩

Prerequisites to Testing

Original Text

Apparatus

All equipment used in the extraction or testing process must be properly qualified and/or calibrated as appropriate. This equipment includes but is not limited to water baths, heat blocks, mechanical pipetters (fixed, adjustable, and repeating pipetters), thermometers, and plate/tube/cartridge readers.

Depyrogenate all glassware and other heat-stable materials in a hot air oven using a validated process. If employing plastic apparatus such as microplates and pipet tips for automatic pipetters, use apparatus that is shown to be free of detectable endotoxin and does not interfere with the test.

Analysts must be properly trained to perform the assay.

Assay Sensitivity Confirmation

The assay sensitivity must be confirmed as described in Bacterial Endotoxins Test ⟨85⟩, Gel-Clot Technique, Preparatory Testing, Test for Confirmation of Labeled Lysate Sensitivity for gel-clot reagents and Bacterial Endotoxins Test ⟨85⟩, Photometric Quantitative Techniques, Preparatory Testing, Assurance of Criteria for the Standard Curve for quantitative methods.

Assay sensitivity confirmation must be carried out when a new batch of reagent (lysate or endotoxin) is used or when there is any change in the test conditions that could affect the outcome of the test.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Checklist of Prerequisites

What USP ⟨161⟩ stresses here is "are you ready to test?", which rests on three pillars:

  • Equipment qualification: water baths, heating blocks, micropipettes, and plate readers all require calibration and validation
  • Depyrogenation of consumables: glassware must be depyrogenated by dry heat (typically 250°C / 30 min or 180°C / 3 hr), and plastic consumables must be shown to be free of detectable endotoxin
  • Analyst training: operators must be properly trained, in both technique and interpretation of results

These look basic, but they are often a source of observations in inspections.

Analogy

A chef preparing before service:

Just as a professional chef must, before cooking, confirm the stove temperature is right (equipment calibration), make sure the cutting board and knives are clean and sterile (depyrogenation of consumables), and have had formal training (personnel qualification). If the cutting board carries residue from the previous dish (endotoxin contamination), the final dish will have problems no matter how good the ingredients are.

BET works the same way: if any prerequisite step is wrong, the result cannot be trusted.

Key Point: Reagent Lot Changes

Every time you change to a new lot of LAL reagent or CSE/RSE standard, the test sensitivity must be reconfirmed. This is not a "recommendation"; it is a mandatory USP requirement.

In a CDMO environment, this means your QC laboratory needs to maintain a complete reagent lot traceability record, with a sensitivity confirmation record every time a new reagent is opened. Common audit findings:

  • Managing the date a reagent is opened versus its expiry
  • Bridging validation when switching between old and new lots
  • Whether the r² of the standard curve consistently meets the requirement of ≥ 0.980

  2. A new analyst is about to start performing device BET independently. From training to release for independent work, which qualification steps should your SOP include?
Demonstration of Continued Suitability

Original Text

The continued suitability of the assay should be re-assessed when significant changes are made to the product, process, raw material supply, manufacturing and/or testing site, BET reagent source, or any other change that could adversely affect the accuracy of the BET result.

The results of this re-assessment should be documented, and suitability studies re-executed as necessary.

Changes to extraction techniques require a new suitability study. Because of possible changes in interference patterns between standard BET methods, a change in BET methodology (e.g., from gel-clot limits test to kinetic chromogenic) requires a re-execution of the suitability study.

Routine Testing

Routine testing of the finished medical devices must utilize the same extraction techniques that were documented in the successful suitability study.

Routine testing is to be performed as outlined in ⟨85⟩, following instructions for incubation and controls listed under the chosen assay technique.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

A driver's license and a change of vehicle:

Once you have your license (you passed the suitability test), it stays valid as long as you drive the same class of vehicle on the same road. But if you switch to a completely different vehicle (change of extraction method) or drive in a different country (change of testing site), you may have to take the test again.

In the same way, demonstrating suitability for device BET is not a one-time job. Any change that could affect the accuracy of the test requires re-evaluation.

Key Point: A Key Restriction on Routine Testing

Routine testing must use exactly the same extraction technique as the suitability study. This is often overlooked, yet it is easily caught in inspections.

Common violations in practice:

  • The suitability study used soaking, but routine testing switched to flushing (because it is more convenient)
  • The suitability study used 40 mL of extract, but routine testing uses 20 mL (to save reagent)
  • The suitability study extracted at 37°C, but routine testing is done "roughly" at room temperature

Any deviation requires the suitability demonstration to be repeated. There are no shortcuts.

Interpretation of Test Results

Original Text

See ⟨85⟩. The preparation under test complies with the test if the mean endotoxin concentration of the replicates of Solution A, after correction for dilution and concentration, is less than 20 Endotoxin Units per medical device (2.15 Endotoxin Units per medical device for those devices that come in contact with the cerebrospinal fluid.)

For liquid medical devices, the limit meets the requirements set forth in Bacterial Endotoxins Test ⟨85⟩, Determination of Maximum Valid Dilution (MVD), Endotoxin Limit, footnote 2.

If a device fails to meet this requirement, an investigation may be initiated per documented procedures. A failed, valid BET assay may not be repeated using Pyrogen Test ⟨151⟩ in its place.

"A failed, valid BET assay may not be repeated using Pyrogen Test ⟨151⟩ in its place."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Acceptance Decision Logic

Acceptance decisions for device BET results are straightforward:

  • General devices: mean endotoxin < 20 EU/device → pass
  • CSF-contacting devices: mean endotoxin < 2.15 EU/device → pass
  • Liquid medical devices: follow the MVD and limit rules for drug products in ⟨85⟩

Key restriction: if a BET is valid but the result fails, you may not switch to the rabbit pyrogen test (RPT / ⟨151⟩) to "retest". This prevents the opportunistic behavior of "failing an exam and switching to an easier one".

Analogy: You Cannot Use the RPT to Replace a Failed BET

The rules for a retake exam:

Imagine you sit a math exam (BET) and score 55 (fail). You cannot say, "I did badly in math this time, so let me take physical education (RPT) instead, since PE is easier to pass."

USP ⟨161⟩ explicitly prohibits this practice. A BET failure is a failure: you must follow the investigation process (OOS investigation), find the root cause, and then decide whether to resample, retest, or reject the whole lot.

Key Point: OOS Investigation Process

When a device BET result exceeds the limit, the CDMO should:

  1. Phase 1, laboratory investigation: check for operator error, reagent problems, and equipment malfunction
  2. Phase 2, extended investigation: if the laboratory shows no problem, trace back through the process: cleaning procedures, depyrogenation steps, the water system, and environmental monitoring data
  3. Phase 3, impact assessment: assess whether other products in the same lot are affected and whether the scope of the investigation needs to be widened

What you must not do: you must not send a failed BET sample to the RPT to "get out of trouble".

Practice Questions

  1. Your QC report shows that a lot of PFS barrels gave a BET result of 22 EU/device (limit 20 EU/device). Describe your investigation steps and decision tree.
  2. Why does USP prohibit using the RPT to replace a failed BET? Explain from a scientific perspective (sensitivity, quantitation) and from a regulatory perspective (data integrity).
Pyrogens: Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT)

Original Text

For samples that cannot be tested by BET because of nonremovable inhibition or enhancement of the test, ⟨151⟩ is applied.

Select an appropriate number of devices, NMT 10, and obtain a pooled extract utilizing preparation methods appropriate to the device as directed for bacterial endotoxins, but with volumes of rinse or extraction fluid not to exceed 40 mL of sterile saline test solution per device.

The requirements of ⟨151⟩ must be met.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: When to Use the RPT

For device testing, the RPT (rabbit pyrogen test) is the last resort and may be used only when all of the following conditions are met:

  • The BET has interference that cannot be removed (inhibition or enhancement)
  • After trying all remedies such as dilution, pH adjustment, and glucan blocking, the interference persists
  • As a result, a valid BET cannot be performed on the product

Important distinction:

  • BET "performed but failed" → cannot switch to the RPT
  • BET "cannot be performed at all" (interference cannot be eliminated) → can use the RPT

Analogy

Allergy and alternative therapy:

Think of BET as the first-choice drug (the best efficacy) and the RPT as the alternative drug. Only when the patient is "allergic" to the first-choice drug (interference that cannot be removed) will the doctor prescribe the alternative. But if the patient takes the first-choice drug and it does not work (BET failure), you cannot switch to the alternative on that basis: the problem lies in the condition itself, not in the choice of drug.

Key Point: Extraction Restrictions for the RPT

The extraction conditions for the RPT are stricter than for BET:

  • The extraction fluid is changed to sterile saline (not BET water), because it will be injected into rabbits
  • The extract volume per device is NMT 40 mL, limited by the injection volume for rabbits
  • Pooled extracts from up to 10 devices may be used

CDMO note: the RPT is now rarely used in the pharmaceutical industry worldwide, but USP ⟨161⟩ still keeps it as an alternative when BET cannot be performed. The EU is moving toward phasing out animal testing altogether, using alternatives such as recombinant Factor C (rFC).

Practice Questions

  1. For a new bioresorbable implant, the extract causes strong enhancement interference with LAL reagent that cannot be eliminated even when diluted to the MVD. What are your next options? Is the RPT a reasonable alternative?
  2. Why does the RPT use sterile saline rather than BET water? Think about it from the standpoint of animal test safety.
⟨85⟩, ⟨151⟩, ⟨161⟩: The Complete Relationship Among the Three Chapters

Original Text

For the bacterial endotoxins test (BET) assay parameters, procedures, standards, and controls for the gel-clot and kinetic and endpoint assays, proceed as directed in Bacterial Endotoxins Test ⟨85⟩, substituting the medical device eluate for the product sample.

See ⟨85⟩ for reagents and test solutions, including Water for BET.

For samples that cannot be tested by BET because of nonremovable inhibition or enhancement, ⟨151⟩ is applied.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Relationship Diagram of the Three Chapters

⟨85⟩ General methodology for BET
↓ method reference
⟨161⟩ Device-specific application
↓ alternative when BET cannot be performed
⟨151⟩ Rabbit pyrogen test (RPT)

The logic:

  • ⟨85⟩ is the foundation method: all BET operations refer to it
  • ⟨161⟩ is the application layer: on the method foundation of ⟨85⟩, it adds device-specific limits, extraction methods, and sampling rules
  • ⟨151⟩ is the fallback option: used only when BET cannot be performed because of interference

For combination products such as a PFS: the drug solution falls directly under ⟨85⟩, and the device components fall under ⟨161⟩ (which references the ⟨85⟩ method).

Analogy: The CDMO "Dual Track"

Highways and city streets:

Think of ⟨85⟩ as the "general traffic code", which applies to all roads. ⟨161⟩ is like the "highway-specific rules": it references the basic rules of the general code (keep right, obey speed limits) but adds rules specific to highways (minimum speed, on-ramp merging rules).

And ⟨151⟩ is like the "emergency stopping lane": it may be used only when the normal lanes are impassable.

As a CDMO, your plant drives on "two roads" at once: drug products follow the ⟨85⟩ main line and devices follow the ⟨161⟩ dedicated line. Your QC team needs to be familiar with both sets of rules.

Section Summary

  • Prerequisites: equipment calibration + depyrogenation of consumables + analyst training + reagent sensitivity confirmation; none can be omitted
  • Continued suitability: any significant change in product, process, supplier, site, reagent, or method may trigger revalidation
  • A change of extraction technique = suitability must be repeated, and so is a change of BET method (such as gel-clot to kinetic)
  • Interpretation of results: general devices < 20 EU/device, CSF-contacting < 2.15 EU/device
  • A BET failure cannot be replaced by the RPT: the RPT is used only where BET cannot be performed because of interference
  • The three-layer ⟨85⟩ + ⟨151⟩ + ⟨161⟩ structure: general methodology → device application → animal test fallback
  • CDMO combination product strategy: build a Combination Product BET Matrix, with the drug solution following ⟨85⟩ and device components following ⟨161⟩
