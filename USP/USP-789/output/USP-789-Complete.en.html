Introduction & Scope

Original Text

〈789〉 PARTICULATE MATTER IN OPHTHALMIC SOLUTIONS

(Note: The current official title as of USP 1-May-2024 update reads "Subvisible Particulate Matter in Intraocular Solutions" — this chapter was updated to specifically address intraocular solutions.)

All intraocular solutions should be essentially free from particles that can be observed on visual inspection using Visible Particulates in Injections 〈790〉, as directed by Ophthalmic Products — Quality Tests 〈771〉. Additionally, certain injection products require subvisible particulate matter testing by Particulate Matter in Injections 〈788〉 or 〈771〉. The tests described in this chapter are physical tests performed for the purpose of enumerating subvisible particles within specific size ranges.

Particulate matter for intraocular solutions is defined as mobile, randomly sourced, unintended, insoluble substances. Every intraocular solution is subject to the particulate matter limits referenced in 〈771〉, unless otherwise specified in the individual monograph. When higher limits are appropriate, they will be specified in the individual monograph. Refer to 〈771〉 and the specific monograph when a question of test applicability occurs.

Light obscuration and microscopic procedures for the determination of particulate matter in intraocular solutions are identical to those for parenteral injections; therefore, where appropriate, 〈788〉 is cross-referenced. This chapter provides two test procedures. The intraocular solution may be tested by either the light obscuration method (LO) or the microscopic method, depending on which one is most appropriate for the product being tested. Many stakeholders choose LO as the first choice, especially when silicone oil may be present.

For certain products, which are limited by volume, viscosity, or color, the membrane microscopic method may be preferred. Limits are the same for either method. In some instances, the viscosity of a material to be tested may be sufficiently high so as to preclude its analysis by either test method. In this event, a quantitative dilution with an appropriate diluent may be made to decrease viscosity, as necessary, to allow the analysis to be performed, following the guidance in 〈1788〉 with appropriate documentation.

"In the tests described below, the results obtained by examining a discrete unit or group of units for particulate matter cannot be extrapolated with certainty to other units that remain untested. Thus, sampling plans based on known operational factors must be developed if valid inferences are to be drawn from observed data to characterize the level of particulate matter in a large group of units."

Sampling plans need to be based on consideration of product volume, particle numbers historically found to be present in comparison to limits, size distribution of particles present, and variability of particle counts between units.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Evolution of <789>: Ophthalmic to Intraocular

USP <789> was historically titled "Particulate Matter in Ophthalmic Solutions", but after the May 2024 update it was renamed "Subvisible Particulate Matter in Intraocular Solutions".

This renaming is significant:

  • Ophthalmic: a broad term covering eye drops, ointments, contact lens care solutions and other products used on the eye
  • Intraocular: specifically products injected directly into the eyeball, such as anti-VEGF drugs (for macular degeneration) and intraocular surgical irrigating solutions

The update to <789> reflects the rapid growth of the intraocular injectable market (anti-VEGF biologics are among the largest markets worldwide) and the stricter particulate control these products demand.

Analogy - Why Are Intraocular Products Held to a Stricter Standard Than Ordinary Injections?

Imagine injecting a drug into a sealed glass sphere (the eyeball). This sphere has no immune protection, no bloodstream to dilute the drug and no macrophages to clear foreign matter, so any particle stays inside the eye.

The special vulnerability of the eye:

  • No way to "clear" foreign matter: particles from an intravenous injection are eventually cleared by macrophages in the liver or spleen, but particles inside the eye may persist permanently
  • Optical precision requirements: the eye is a precision optical organ, and even transparent particles can affect vision
  • Sensitivity to inflammation: endophthalmitis, even if mild, can lead to permanent blindness

For this reason, <789> adds a ≥50 µm size limit that <788> does not have, imposing stricter control on large particles.

Key Point - The Hierarchy Between <789> and <771>

Particulate testing of ophthalmic products is governed by several USP chapters, in the following hierarchy:

  • <771> Ophthalmic Products - Quality Tests: the general quality-test chapter for ophthalmic products, requiring that they be "essentially free from visible particulates"
  • <790> Visible Particulates in Injections: the visual inspection method for visible particulates
  • <789>: the subvisible particle count test specifically for intraocular solutions, an ophthalmic-specific version of <788>

The requirements of an individual monograph take precedence over general chapters; in case of doubt, the monograph prevails.

Formulas and Calculations - Allowable Particle Counts for a Typical Intraocular Injection

Typical intravitreal injection volume: 0.05–0.1 mL

Calculated for 0.05 mL (the smallest common dose):
─────────────────────────────────────
≥10 µm: 0.05 × 50 = 2.5 particles/dose → average must not exceed 2.5 particles
≥25 µm: 0.05 × 5  = 0.25 particles/dose → average must not exceed 0.25 particles
≥50 µm: 0.05 × 2  = 0.1 particles/dose  → extremely strict!

Comparison with <788> SVP (based on a 5 mL vial):
  <788> ≥10 µm: 6,000 particles/container = 1,200/mL
  <789> ≥10 µm: 50/mL

Conclusion: the <789> limit is 1/24 of the <788> limit - intraocular products are held to
a standard more than 24 times stricter than ordinary injections.

Key Point - Clinical Significance of the ≥50 µm Limit

<789> has one more size category than <788>, ≥50 µm (limit 2/mL), which is unique among all the USP particulate chapters.

Clinical background:

  • Ophthalmic research shows that particles with a diameter >25 µm are harder to clear naturally from the vitreous cavity
  • Large particles ≥50 µm can directly obstruct the visual field, causing patient discomfort even if only transient
  • The presence of such large particles often points to a particle contamination problem in the process (such as unevenly siliconized syringes or aggregation during compounding) and is an indicator of process quality

Analogy - LO vs. Microscopy: When to Choose Which?

Think of the LO method as a "fully automatic traffic counter" and microscopy as a "taxi driver identifying vehicles by eye":

  • LO as the first choice: fast, automated, objective results, and particularly suitable for products containing silicone oil (silicone oil microdroplets are also counted by LO, and they are in fact something that needs to be monitored)
  • Microscopy as a complement: used when the product itself is colored (absorbs light and interferes with LO), very highly viscous (cannot flow through the LO sensor), or when morphological identification is needed (what type of particle is it?)

<789> states explicitly that the limits are the same for both methods; which method to use depends on product characteristics and must be determined during method validation.

Core Concept - Comparing the <789> and <788> Limits

The <789> limits differ fundamentally from the <788> SVP limits:

Calculated for a typical intraocular injection (0.1 mL):

  • ≥10 µm: at most 5 particles (0.1 mL × 50/mL)
  • ≥25 µm: at most 0.5 particles (an average value; in practice the batch average must not exceed this)
  • ≥50 µm: at most 0.2 particles (meaning very strict control)
Microscopic Particle Count Test

Original Text

MICROSCOPIC PARTICLE COUNT TEST

Some articles cannot be tested meaningfully by light obscuration. In such cases, individual monographs clearly specify that only a microscopic particle count is to be performed. The microscopic particle count test enumerates subvisible, essentially solid, particulate matter in intraocular solutions after collection on a microporous membrane filter.

Some intraocular solutions (e.g., high viscosity solutions that do not filter readily) may be effectively analyzed using the microscopic test, with appropriate dilution and proper selection of the filtration membrane.

When performing the microscopic test, do not attempt to size or enumerate amorphous, semiliquid, or otherwise morphologically indistinct materials that have the appearance of a stain or discoloration on the membrane surface. These materials show little or no surface relief, do not cast a shadow and present a gelatinous or film-like appearance (See 〈1788〉).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - How Membrane Microscopy Works

Microscopic Particle Count Test: a known volume of sample is passed through a membrane with a pore size of about 1 µm (usually polycarbonate), the particles are retained on the membrane surface, and they are then counted and measured for their maximum dimension under a microscope (usually at 100× magnification) using both transmitted and reflected light.

Key technical points:

  • Do not count indistinct material: gel-like or film-like material, or material that looks like a stain, is not included in the count - this is an important judgment requirement that calls for an experienced analyst
  • Membrane selection: a suitable membrane material must be chosen based on viscosity and chemical compatibility
  • Subjectivity of manual reading: compared with the LO method, microscopy relies more on operator judgment, and inter-laboratory reproducibility is relatively lower

Key Point - The Limits Are Identical for the Two Methods

An important feature of <789>: the acceptance criteria for the LO method (Table 1) and the microscopic method (Table 2) are identical (50/5/2 per mL).

This differs from <787> - in <787>, the microscopic method uses the membrane microscopic limits of <788>, and the results of the two methods are explicitly declared not equivalent.

The rationale for a single set of limits in <789>: intraocular products generally do not contain complex protein aggregates; the particles are mainly extrinsic or intrinsic solid matter, and the two methods give fairly similar counts for such particles, so a single set of limits can be set.

Practical Application - Test Method Selection Decisions at an Ophthalmic CDMO

Suppose your CDMO takes on the following three intraocular products; how would you choose the test method?

  1. Anti-VEGF mAb intraocular injection (clear, low viscosity, 0.05 mL dose): LO is preferred - the volume is small (several units must be pooled), and an LO sensor head for small volumes is used
  2. Silicone oil-containing vitreous replacement fluid (high viscosity): consider LO after dilution, or microscopy - the small droplets of the silicone oil itself interfere with LO counting, so method validation is needed to confirm
  3. Colored ophthalmic irrigating solution (slightly yellow): microscopy is preferred - the color interferes with the LO light path and causes falsely high counts

Analogy - How Hard Is the "Do Not Count Gel-Like Material" Judgment?

Imagine looking at a filter membrane under a microscope. On it you see solid grains of sand (to be counted) and some things that look like jelly or water stains (not to be counted).

<789> requires the analyst to distinguish:

  • Count: solid particles with a clear boundary, a three-dimensional appearance on the surface and the ability to cast a shadow
  • Do not count: amorphous, semi-liquid material that looks like a stain or oil film (these may be non-solid substances, such as lipids or precipitates of certain excipients)

This judgment depends heavily on operator training and on how clearly the laboratory SOP describes it, and it is the main source of subjectivity in the microscopic method.

Chapter Comparison - Overview of <787>, <788> and <789>

Original Text - Regulatory Context

The three USP particulate matter chapters serve distinct but related purposes:

Key distinctions:

  • 〈789〉 uses per-mL limits only (not per-container), reflecting the small volumes typical of intraocular products
  • 〈789〉 adds a ≥50 µm size class not present in 〈787〉 or 〈788〉
  • 〈789〉 cross-references 〈788〉 for full procedural details — there is no separate detailed procedure in 〈789〉 itself
  • 〈789〉 can be used with either LO or microscopic method, with identical limits for both

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Why Is the Intraocular Limit Expressed per Milliliter Rather Than per Container?

<789> uses limits per milliliter (/mL) rather than per container for an intuitive reason: intraocular injections have extremely small volumes (0.05–0.1 mL), so per-container limits would be tiny numbers that are hard to manage.

For example, if the limit were set at 10 particles per container, a 0.05 mL product would convert to 200/mL - a number far too large to reflect ocular safety needs.

Using /mL as a common basis makes the limits independent of volume, which suits the diverse product presentations in ophthalmology (from 0.05 mL anti-VEGF products to 10 mL ophthalmic irrigating solutions).

Key Point - The Procedural Provisions of <789> Rely on <788>

<789> is a "lightweight" chapter - it does not contain complete test procedures itself, but instead refers directly to:

  • LO method → "perform as described in <788> Method 1"
  • Microscopic method → "perform as described in <788> Method 2"

Practical implication: ophthalmic QC laboratories need to be familiar with both the complete procedures of <788> and the specific limits of <789>. When writing SOPs, the procedure section references <788> and the acceptance criteria reference <789>.

Impact on CDMOs: the same instrument and method can be used for ordinary injections (<788>) and intraocular injections (<789>), reducing equipment investment and method development costs.

Practical Application - Chapter Citation Strategy in Regulatory Filings

When filing an intraocular injection with the FDA (NDA/BLA), the particulate test specification is referenced as follows:

  • In CTD 3.2.P.5 (Control of Drug Product), the particulate test should cite USP <789> as the basis for the method
  • If the LO method is used, the procedure should comply with <788> Method 1; if the microscopic method is used, with <788> Method 2
  • The acceptance criteria must meet the three size limits in <789> Table 1 or Table 2 (50/5/2 per mL)
  • If an individual USP monograph exists, the monograph specification takes precedence; if there is no monograph, the <789> general chapter applies

Chapter Summary

  • <789> applies to intraocular (intravitreal) injections and is the ophthalmic-specific version of <788>; it was formally renamed "Intraocular Solutions" in 2024
  • Definition of particles: mobile, randomly sourced, unintended insoluble matter, with /mL as the basis for limits
  • Three size limits (the same for the LO and microscopic methods): ≥10 µm (50/mL), ≥25 µm (5/mL), ≥50 µm (2/mL) - about 24 times stricter than <788>, with an added ≥50 µm limit
  • The LO method is the first choice (especially when silicone oil is present), but for products that are highly viscous, colored or volume-limited, the microscopic method is a suitable alternative, and both methods use the same limit criteria
  • <789> refers to Method 1/Method 2 of <788> for procedures and itself provides only the scope and acceptance criteria
  • The requirements of an individual monograph take precedence over the <789> general chapter; dilution must be performed per <1788> and properly documented
