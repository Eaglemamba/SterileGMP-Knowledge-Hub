Chapter Scope and Background

Original Text

〈787〉 SUBVISIBLE PARTICULATE MATTER IN THERAPEUTIC PROTEIN INJECTIONS

This chapter can be used as an alternative to USP general chapter Particulate Matter in Injections 〈788〉. It specifically addresses therapeutic protein injections and related preparations, allowing use of smaller test product volumes and smaller test aliquots to determine particulate matter content, with sample-handling instructions that take into account the issues associated with the analysis of these materials. Although the methodology and limits presented in this chapter are preferred for therapeutic protein injections, the use of regulatory-acceptable alternative analytical methods with adequately developed subvisible particulate limits is acceptable.

Therapeutic protein injections are biotechnology-derived products of protein or peptides and other therapeutic protein injections such as naturally sourced therapeutic proteins, including their final infusion preparations. Certain preparations that are not amenable to these tests, such as prophylactic vaccines, are not included in this guidance.

Particulate matter in therapeutic protein injections consists of mobile undissolved substances that may originate from various sources. The particles may be:

  • (a) truly foreign, or "extrinsic" — e.g., unexpected foreign material, such as cellulose
  • (b) "intrinsic" — resulting from addition or by insufficient cleaning during manufacturing, such as tank metals or gaskets, lubricants, filling hardware, or resulting from instability
  • (c) "inherent" — such as particles of the protein or formulation components

All of these particle types may be detected and counted in the test method described in this chapter. In addition, for light obscuration (LO) methods, gas bubbles are commonly counted during particulate matter testing and are counting artifacts.

For the determination of subvisible particles in therapeutic protein injections, the Light Obscuration Particle Count Test is the preferred method. The Microscopic Particle Count Test similar to that described in 〈788〉 may be useful in cases where the particles being analyzed are primarily nonproteinaceous, or in cases where examination of particle characteristics may be important. The results from this test are not equivalent to those from the LO test.

Not all therapeutic protein injections can be examined directly for subvisible particles by the LO method. When the test method is not directly applicable to specific test samples, as indicated by issues with method verification (reproducibility, linearity), a quantitative dilution with an appropriate diluent can be made to allow analysis by light obscuration. Method verification may be required to ensure the appropriateness of the sample handling procedures and the performance of the method for each drug product.

"In the tests described below, the results obtained by examining a discrete unit or group of units for particulate matter cannot be extrapolated with certainty to other units that remain untested. Thus, sampling plans based on known operational factors must be developed to support valid inferences from observed data in order to characterize the level of particulate matter in a large group of units."

Sampling plans should be based on considerations of product volume, particle numbers historically found in comparison to limits, particle size distribution, and variability of particle counts between units. Products that are used with a final filter during administration (in-line) are exempt from these requirements, providing that scientific data are available to justify the exemption.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Do Proteins Need a Separate Chapter?

Subvisible Particulate Matter: particles of 1–100 µm that cannot be seen with the unaided eye but can be counted by instruments.

<787> was split off from <788> for three core reasons:

  • Volume limits: biologics are often dosed in small volumes (0.5–2 mL), and the test volumes required by <788> are simply not available for many protein products
  • Protein aggregates: proteins themselves form "inherent" particles; these are a property of the drug product and should not be treated as contaminants, but they still need to be monitored
  • Silicone oil interference: prefilled syringes commonly use silicone oil as a lubricant, which is counted as false-positive particles by LO methods

Analogy: Three Types of Particles Are Like Three Sources of Kitchen Contamination

Imagine you are cooking a pot of soup:

  • Extrinsic: dust blown in through the window. It should never be there, and its presence means environmental or container contamination is out of control
  • Intrinsic: flakes of rust from the pot or fragments of a silicone gasket. They are introduced by the process or equipment and can be eliminated through process improvement
  • Inherent: proteins in the broth that coagulate into small clumps when heated. This is a physicochemical property of the ingredient (the drug molecule); it is hard to avoid completely but can be minimized through formulation design

For protein injections, inherent particles are the hardest challenge to manage because they sit between "an acceptable property of the drug product" and "a potential safety risk".

Key Point: Immunogenicity Risk

Why is particulate control for biologics stricter than for small-molecule drugs? The answer is immunogenicity.

Protein aggregates can trigger a patient's immune system to produce antibodies. At best this reduces efficacy (anti-drug antibodies); at worst it causes severe allergic or autoimmune reactions. FDA and EMA have recorded several clinical safety events caused by protein aggregation.

This is why <787> places particularly strict requirements on sample handling (avoiding agitation, gentle mixing): every handling step can generate more aggregates.

Light Obscuration Particle Count Test

Original Text

LIGHT OBSCURATION PARTICLE COUNT TEST

Analysts should use a suitable instrument that is based on the principle of light blockage and that allows an automatic determination of particle size and number.

The sensor selected must be appropriate for the intended particle size range and anticipated particle count. Several standardization steps such as sample volume accuracy, sample flow rate, sensor resolution, calibration, and particle count accuracy are important for the proper operation of the apparatus and are further reviewed in general information chapter 〈1788〉.

For equipment calibration purposes, use of NIST-traceable particle size standards or equivalent traceable standards. These standards generally are between 2 and 100 µm. Analysts should verify the performance of the apparatus using the USP Particle Count RS dispersed in particle-free water at an appropriate volume. Care must be taken to avoid agglomeration of particles during dispersion in the calibration process.

General Precautions

Carry out the test under conditions that limit ingress of particulate matter, preferably in a laminar-flow cabinet. The glassware and equipment used should be properly cleaned and handled to remain particle free. Care should be taken to minimize shaking and other stresses to the preparations (for liquids or reconstituted powders) and to prevent introduction of air bubbles into the preparation under examination.

Preparatory Steps

Glassware selected for the method should accommodate the volume of blank and sample aliquots, allow easy mixing, and be amenable to repeated cleaning used to prevent any significant contribution to particle counts. Typically, separate vessels are utilized and retained specifically for this test only.

Two types of system controls are recommended: a Blank Test and System Suitability Verification.

  1. The Blank Test involves the following:
    • A. Run at the start of the day or analysis to ensure that proper care has been used in preparing the site and apparatus for use (Environmental Blank).
    • B. Any additional blanks run throughout the day (such as between product families) should also comply with the Blank Test requirements (Procedural Blank).
  2. The System Suitability Verification is run to verify proper count/mL and ratio for the current USP Particle Count RS or equivalent commercial standard. See general information chapter 〈1788〉.

Blank Test

In order to check that (a) the environment is suitable for the test, (b) the glassware is properly cleaned, and (c) the water or suitable solvent that will be used is particle free (b and c constitute the "blank"), the following test is carried out. Determine the count in five aliquots of degassed particle-free water or suitable solvent, representative of the product test volume (but NLT 1 mL). If the number of particles 10 µm or greater in size exceeds 1 particle per mL for the combined aliquot volume, the precautions taken for the test are not sufficient.

System Suitability Verification

To verify system suitability, Particle-Free Water and the USP Particle Count RS or equivalent commercial standard aqueous suspension are tested from the same or similar type of container as product samples, are degassed in the same manner, and generally are handled like the test article. Ensure that the system can meet the Blank Test requirement. Results from the Standard preparation should fall within the stated specifications of the Reference Standard.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: How Light Obscuration Works

Light Obscuration (LO): the sample liquid flows through a light-beam sensor. When a particle passes through the beam, it blocks part of the light; the sensor calculates the equivalent spherical diameter of the particle from the drop in light intensity and accumulates the particle count for each size range.

Key calibration parameters:

  • NIST-traceable standards: ensure particle size measurements are traceable to national standards, a basic GMP calibration requirement
  • USP Particle Count RS: the reference standard used to verify the counting accuracy of the instrument; it must pass before each use
  • Flow rate accuracy: the flow rate directly affects the calculation of counts per milliliter and must be verified periodically

Analogy: The Blank Test Is Like Sterility Verification Before Surgery

Before an operation begins, the surgeon does not simply start cutting; the team first confirms that the operating room and instruments meet sterility standards.

The logic of the blank test is exactly the same:

  • Environmental blank: confirms that the air environment (laminar flow hood) has no sources of contamination
  • Procedural blank: confirms that the glassware, dilution water and handling process do not introduce extraneous particles

If the blank test fails (>1 particle/mL ≥10 µm), no subsequent test result can be relied upon, and the environment must be cleaned again before continuing.

Key Point: Air Bubbles Are the "Ghosts" of the LO Method

A light obscuration instrument cannot distinguish solid particles from air bubbles: bubbles also block light and are wrongly counted as particles.

This matters especially for protein formulations because:

  • Protein solutions have low surface tension, so they easily entrain bubbles when shaken or pipetted
  • Reconstituted lyophilized products often contain large numbers of tiny bubbles
  • Degassing is needed before testing, by standing at atmospheric pressure or gentle vacuum (about 75 Torr)
  • Sonication is strictly prohibited because it damages protein structure

After degassing, the sample must be mixed gently again (slow manual swirling); mixing by inversion is not permitted.

Practical Application: CDMO Scenario

When taking on a new client's mAb (monoclonal antibody) product, the CDMO QC laboratory needs to develop a <787> method for it:

  • First confirm whether the product volume is sufficient for direct testing or whether several units need to be pooled
  • Assess the optical properties of the product (high protein concentration may affect the refractive index and lead to under-counting)
  • Perform method validation: repeatability and dilution linearity/recovery (if dilution is needed)
  • Establish system suitability acceptance criteria and incorporate them into the batch release specification
Method Detail: Test Procedure

Original Text

Method

Product samples are tested in the manner that most suitably represents delivery (e.g., expelled syringe contents). For parenterals that have a sufficient volume (i.e., volume large enough to facilitate testing), testing of individual units is often diagnostic. For parenteral products that do not have a sufficient volume, carefully and thoroughly mix each unit, then combine the contents of a suitable number of units in a separate container to obtain the volume required for a single test (generally 0.2–5.0 mL). If a dilution is performed, ensure that the blank container has sufficient volume for product and diluents and that few, if any, particles are introduced in the process.

Open each unit cautiously, remove the sealing closure, and avoid contamination of the contents before sampling, diluting, or, if necessary, pooling.

When a product solvent is specified, e.g., for lyophilized solids or powders for parenteral use, the reconstitution or dilution must be performed with the appropriate amount of specified solvent. In this case, the solvent itself may be tested to ensure that it is not a significant source of particles. Subtraction of the solvent particle count from the total count is not allowed.

Eliminating gas bubbles is a key step, especially for proteinaceous products that readily entrain gas. Two methods are recommended: either allowing the product fluid to stand under ambient pressure or applying a gentle (e.g., 75 Torr) vacuum. Other methods may be used when demonstrated to be suitable. Sonication should be avoided.

Once the samples have been degassed, they must be remixed gently to suspend all particles by mixing the contents of the sample gently but thoroughly by an appropriate means, e.g., slow swirling of the container by hand. Inversion to mix the product fluid is not recommended at any time.

Immediately after mixing, withdraw NLT four aliquots, each of a volume appropriate for the instrument's capacity (generally 0.2–5.0 mL). Count the number of particles over the selected size range, including particles equal to or greater than 10 and 25 µm. Disregard the result obtained for the first aliquot, and calculate the mean number of particles at each size range for the remaining aliquots of the preparation being tested.

General Considerations — Dilution

Dilution is allowed as long as the diluent and methods are demonstrated to be appropriate and the smallest level of dilution that allows for reproducible testing is used. Under certain circumstances dilution of samples may be required:

  • High-concentration products and/or high-viscosity products that cannot be drawn into the instrument properly
  • Products that cause saturation of the instrument sensor due to high counts (especially in smaller than 10-µm size ranges)
  • Cases where the high concentration results in very small differences in refractive index between the solution and the protein aggregates

In situations where sample dilution is conducted, the underlying rationale for the dilution as well as the suitability of the selected dilution scheme including choice of diluent must be demonstrated. Bridging studies with and without dilution at size ranges intended for particle counting should be considered. An orthogonal technique may be used to justify the suitability of the method by verifying the impact of dilution.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is the First Portion Discarded?

"Disregard the result of the first portion" is a specific requirement of <787>/<788>, for these reasons:

  • When the first portion is drawn, the tubing is not yet completely filled with sample, and it may pick up rinse water or residue from the previous sample trapped in dead spaces in the tubing
  • The flow rate has not yet stabilized during the first portion, so volume measurement may be biased

This is a conservative but reasonable design that ensures the results of the remaining portions are representative. NLT 4 portions means at least 4 portions are taken; after the first is discarded, the average is calculated from at least 3 portions.

Key Point: Solvent Particle Counts May Not Be Subtracted

USP states explicitly: "Subtraction of the solvent particle count from the total count is not allowed."

This is especially important for lyophilized products. The background particle count of the water for injection or buffer used for reconstitution may not be subtracted from the total count.

Practical implication: if a formulation developer wants to use a particular reconstitution solution, the solution itself must have a very low particle level; otherwise the overall test result will be high, causing unnecessary OOS results.

Formula and Calculation: Converting Results After Dilution

Converting counts after dilution:
  Actual particles/mL = instrument reading/mL × dilution factor (DF)

Example:
  Reading after 1:10 dilution of sample = 500 particles/mL (≥10 µm)
  Estimated neat sample = 500 × 10 = 5,000 particles/mL

Notes:
  - First confirm that dilution does not change the particle size distribution
  - Dilution linearity must be verified (at least 3 dilution levels)
  - A bridging study must be included, comparing
    diluted vs. undiluted (within the permitted volume range)

Analogy: The High Refractive Index Problem Is Like Taking Photos in Fog

When the protein concentration is very high (for example, a high-concentration antibody at 100–200 mg/mL), the refractive index of the protein solution is very close to that of the protein aggregates. It is like taking photos in fog: the camera (the light obscuration sensor) has difficulty telling the "air" (background solution) from the "fog droplets" (protein aggregates), so counts are low and real aggregates are missed.

The solution is to dilute to reduce the background concentration, or to use orthogonal techniques (such as dynamic light scattering (DLS) or nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)) to confirm that the dilution scheme is justified.

  2. A laboratory analyst performing the blank test finds a marked difference between the day 2 and day 1 results. Which factors could cause this difference?

Section Summary

  • <787> is the dedicated chapter on subvisible particulate matter in therapeutic protein injections; it allows smaller test volumes and takes into account the technical challenges specific to protein formulations (aggregation, silicone oil, high viscosity, high concentration)
  • Particles fall into three types: extrinsic (contamination), intrinsic (introduced by the process) and inherent (the protein itself). All three must be counted and controlled, and inherent particles are the core challenge for protein products
  • LO is the preferred method, but protein formulations need special attention: degassing (to remove false positives from bubbles), no inversion, no sonication, and disregarding the first portion
  • The blank test (≤1 particle/mL ≥10 µm) is a required preliminary step at the start of each day's testing; if it fails, the environment must be re-prepared
  • When dilution is needed, a thorough dilution scheme validation must be performed, including a bridging study and orthogonal technique confirmation, and the background solvent particle count may not be subtracted from the result

Evaluation: Acceptance Criteria

Original Text

Evaluation

The values below are derived historically from general chapters 〈788〉 and 〈789〉. If specifications are different from those stated below, they will be indicated in an individual monograph where available.

Small-Volume Parenterals (≤100 mL)

For parenteral products that are therapeutic protein injections for infusion or injection supplied in containers with a nominal content of less than or equal to 100 mL:

The average number of particles present in the units tested should not exceed:

  • 6000 per container equal to or greater than 10 µm
  • 600 per container equal to or greater than 25 µm

Large-Volume Parenterals (>100 mL)

For therapeutic protein injections supplied in containers with a nominal content of more than 100 mL, and parenteral infusion preparations or injections with a nominal content of more than 100 mL:

The average number of particles present in the units tested should not exceed:

  • 25/mL equal to or greater than 10 µm
  • 3/mL equal to or greater than 25 µm

Also, total particle load should not exceed 6000 per container equal to or greater than 10 µm and should not exceed 600 per container equal to or greater than 25 µm.

Exemptions

Products that are used with a final filter during administration (in-line) are exempt from these requirements, providing that scientific data are available to justify the exemption. However, filtrates are expected to comply with the guideline.

For products supplied or first reconstituted in <100 mL, and then diluted for infusion in a volume >100 mL, particle content should be assessed both before and after dilution and evaluated based on their final volume.

"If specifications are different from those stated below, they will be indicated in an individual monograph where available."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Specification Logic of SVP vs. LVP

Small-Volume Parenteral (SVP): an injection with a labeled volume of ≤100 mL, such as most mAb vials (typically 2–20 mL) or prefilled syringes.

Large-Volume Parenteral (LVP): a labeled volume of >100 mL, usually an intravenous infusion bag.

Why does an LVP have a "dual standard" (limits on both /mL and /container)?

  • A patient receives a large volume of infusion in a short time (for example, 250–500 mL). If only a per-container limit applied, a single 500 mL bag could deliver 12,500 particles ≥10 µm into the vein!
  • LVPs therefore need a stricter per-milliliter limit to control the total particle burden
  • At the same time, the per-container upper limit is retained so that particles cannot accumulate simply because the volume is large

Formula and Calculation: What the Dual Standard Means in Practice

Scenario: 250 mL LVP product (IV infusion bag)
─────────────────────────────────────
LVP per-milliliter limit ≥10 µm: 25/mL
  → 250 mL × 25/mL = 6,250 particles (exceeds the per-container limit of 6,000)

Wait! This exceeds the per-container limit:
  → Per-container limit = 6,000
  → The effective limit is therefore min(25/mL, 6,000/container)
  → For 250 mL: 6,000/250 = 24/mL (stricter than 25/mL)

Conclusion:
  For large-volume containers, the per-container limit is often stricter than the per-milliliter limit
  The specification must satisfy both conditions at the same time

Analogy: The In-Line Filter Exemption Is Like a Mask Strategy During a Pandemic

During the COVID-19 pandemic, some high-risk places (such as hospitals) required N95 masks even when the environment already had air filtration. But if a patient is using a high-efficiency air purifier connected directly to the airway, some external rules might be waived.

The in-line filter logic is the same:

  • If a 0.2 µm sterilizing filter is fitted in the infusion line, particles are retained before they reach the patient's vein
  • USP therefore permits an exemption, but scientific data must support the filter's retention efficiency, and the filtrate must still meet the specification

Key point: an exemption does not mean no testing; it moves the test point to after the filter, and the scientific justification cannot be omitted.

Key Point: The Dual Evaluation Obligation for IV Infusion After Dilution

Many mAb products are used clinically as follows: 5–10 mL of reconstituted product or drug solution is first prepared, then added to 100–250 mL of NS (normal saline) for IV infusion.

<787> explicitly requires: particulate matter must be evaluated both before and after dilution, with the final volume as the basis of evaluation.

The impact on a CDMO: when setting the specification, you need to set not only the particle limits for the drug solution but also provide particle assessment data under the final infusion conditions (usually this must be collected as early as the clinical study stage).

Microscopic Particle Count Test & Practical Considerations for Protein Biologics

Original Text

MICROSCOPIC PARTICLE COUNT TEST

As noted, the LO method is the preferred method for therapeutic protein injections and parenteral infusions. However, the microscopic method may be used when appropriate, such as determination of extrinsic and intrinsic particle types only. It should be demonstrated, however, that particular classes of particles (e.g., inherent) are also being counted when using this method.

For the determination of product acceptability, apply the limits for the membrane microscopic test in general chapter 〈788〉. Because of the interference of some protein particles and their physical characteristics (fragile or translucent), the results of the Microscopic Particle Count Test are not equivalent to those of the Light Obscuration Particle Count Test, and the two methods cannot be considered interchangeable.

For informational purposes only, analysts can refer to 〈1788〉, which may be a helpful but not mandatory resource.

Key Practical Considerations Summary for Protein Biologics

  • Allow for pooling of multiple units when individual unit volume is insufficient (generally 0.2–5.0 mL required)
  • Method verification (reproducibility, linearity) required for each product — especially when dilution is needed
  • Sampling plans must account for particle count variability between units — statistical justification required
  • Gas bubble elimination is critical: use vacuum (≈75 Torr) or ambient standing, never sonication
  • Protein aggregates (inherent particles) must still be counted and controlled even if they are product-related
  • Results from 〈787〉 testing of one batch cannot be extrapolated to other untested batches
"Because of the interference of some protein particles and their physical characteristics (fragile or translucent), the results of the Microscopic Particle Count Test are not equivalent to those of the Light Obscuration Particle Count Test, and the two methods cannot be considered interchangeable."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Can't Microscopy Replace LO?

The principle of microscopy (membrane filtration microscopic counting) is: the sample is filtered through a membrane with a pore size of 1 µm, and the particles on the membrane are counted directly under a microscope.

The core problems for protein formulations:

  • Fragility: protein aggregates may break up or deform under filtration pressure, leading to under-counting
  • Translucency: protein particles have a refractive index close to water and are almost transparent under the microscope, making them hard to distinguish from the background
  • Non-equivalent results: for the same sample, LO may count 5,000 particles while microscopy counts only 800, a huge difference

For this reason, <787> states explicitly that the two methods are not interchangeable. If the regulations require you to use LO, you cannot claim compliance because the microscopy result passes.

Key Point: <1788> Is Supporting Reference, Not a Mandatory Requirement

USP <1788> (Methods for the Determination of Subvisible Particulate Matter) is an informational chapter that provides more detailed method background, instrument operation and validation guidance.

Regulatory status: referencing <1788> is not a compliance requirement, but reading it is strongly recommended.

During a GMP inspection, regulators may ask: how did you establish the system suitability criteria for the LO method? How did you verify that the dilution scheme is appropriate? <1788> provides a best-practice framework that helps answer these questions.

Analogy: The Regulatory Dilemma of Inherent Particles

Imagine you are the QC head at a juice factory. Your "100% natural orange juice" naturally contains bits of pulp (inherent). You cannot remove the pulp entirely (it would no longer be orange juice), but you also need to make sure there is not so much that consumers feel they are drinking jam.

Protein inherent particles follow the same logic:

  • They are part of the product itself (such as oligomers and dimers), and eliminating them completely might require sacrificing potency or activity
  • But they must be controlled within an acceptable range, because the immunogenicity risk is real
  • Regulatory acceptance: a scientific justification is needed showing that these inherent particles pose no patient safety risk

Practical Application: CDMO Batch Release Decision Tree

When the <787> LO test result of a mAb batch exceeds the limit, how should CDMO QA proceed:

  1. Confirm test validity: did the blank test pass? Is system suitability met? Rule out instrument error
  2. Investigate the particle source: is it environmental particles (LFH malfunction?), a process abnormality (filter failure?), or increased protein aggregation?
  3. Evaluate resampling: if there are not enough samples, perform additional testing according to the sampling plan
  4. Batch disposition decision: if the out-of-limit result is confirmed, open a deviation report and follow the notification procedure in the client Quality Agreement (QAA)
  5. Root cause analysis: correlate with process parameters (compounding temperature, filtration pressure, storage conditions)

Practice Questions

  1. A high-concentration (150 mg/mL) mAb drug solution needs a 1:20 dilution before LO testing. How would you design the bridging study when validating this dilution scheme?
  2. For the same batch, the microscopy result passes the <788> limit but the LO result exceeds the <787> limit. How is this judged from a regulatory standpoint? Can the product be claimed to be compliant?
  3. If the particle counts of individual units are highly variable (CV >50%), how should the sampling plan be designed to represent the whole batch reasonably?

Section Summary

  • SVP (≤100 mL): 6,000 particles/container (≥10 µm), 600 particles/container (≥25 µm); calculated "per container"
  • LVP (>100 mL): 25 particles/mL and 6,000 particles/container (≥10 µm); 3 particles/mL and 600 particles/container (≥25 µm); both standards must be met
  • Products infused intravenously after dilution must be evaluated both before and after dilution, with the final volume as the basis of judgment
  • Because of the fragility and translucency of protein particles, microscopy results are not equivalent to or interchangeable with LO results, and microscopy serves only as a supplementary tool
  • Products with an in-line filter may apply for an exemption, but the exemption must be supported by scientific data, and the filtrate must still meet the specification
  • The <787> limits derive from the history of <788>, and individual monographs may specify stricter limits
