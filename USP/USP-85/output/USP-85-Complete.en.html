0.1 Bacterial Endotoxins Test Overview

Original Text

Portions of this general chapter have been harmonized with the corresponding texts of the European Pharmacopoeia and/or the Japanese Pharmacopoeia. Those portions that are not harmonized are marked with symbols (◊) to specify this fact.

The Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET) is a test to detect or quantify endotoxins from Gram-negative bacteria using amoebocyte lysate from the horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus or Tachypleus tridentatus).

There are three techniques for this test: the gel-clot technique, which is based on gel formation; the turbidimetric technique, based on the development of turbidity after cleavage of an endogenous substrate; and the chromogenic technique, based on the development of color after cleavage of a synthetic peptide-chromogen complex. Proceed by any of the three techniques for the test. In the event of doubt or dispute, the final decision is made based upon the gel-clot limit test unless otherwise indicated in the monograph for the product being tested.

The test is carried out in a manner that avoids endotoxin contamination.

"The Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET) is a test to detect or quantify endotoxins from Gram-negative bacteria using amoebocyte lysate from the horseshoe crab."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

What is endotoxin? Endotoxin is the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) component of the outer membrane of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria. LPS is released into the environment when bacteria die or divide. Even after sterilization has killed the bacteria, endotoxin can remain in the drug product because LPS is extremely heat stable.

Why is BET so important? An injectable product containing endotoxin above the limit can trigger a pyrogenic response in the patient, with symptoms including fever, chills, and hypotension, and in severe cases septic shock or even death. USP ⟨85⟩ is a mandatory release test for all injectable products.

Harmonization among the three pharmacopoeias: USP ⟨85⟩ is harmonized with the European Pharmacopoeia (EP 2.6.14) and the Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP 4.01), but some content still differs between them (marked with the ◊ symbol).

Analogy

The fire alarm analogy:

Imagine endotoxin as the toxic smoke left behind after a fire. Even though the fire is out (the bacteria have been killed), the smoke (endotoxin) still hangs in the air and is harmful to people. The BET is like an alarm designed specifically to detect this "residual toxin": even if you cannot see the fire (live bacteria), the alarm still sounds and tells you there is a hazardous substance in the environment.

The three test methods are like three different alarm models: the gel-clot method (a visible clotting reaction), the turbidimetric method (measures the change in turbidity), and the chromogenic method (measures the change in color). Each can detect the hazard, but they differ in sensitivity and quantitative capability.

Key Notes

Impact on CDMO operations: An endotoxin excursion is one of the most serious quality events in injectable manufacturing. Once a batch exceeds the endotoxin limit:

  • The batch must be rejected - endotoxin cannot be removed by routine filtration (LPS has a molecular weight of about 10-20 kDa, far smaller than the pore size of a sterilizing-grade filter)
  • A deviation investigation and CAPA must be initiated
  • It may force a line shutdown for cleaning and revalidation
  • It seriously affects customer delivery timelines

Referee method: When results are disputed, the gel-clot limit test is the final determination unless otherwise specified in the monograph. This is a globally harmonized consensus.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can a sterilized product still contain endotoxin above the limit? What is the fundamental difference between sterilization and depyrogenation?
  2. Your CDMO site receives a customer complaint that a batch of water for injection (WFI) gave an out-of-limit endotoxin result. List the three possible contamination sources you would check first.
0.2 Apparatus

Original Text

Depyrogenate all glassware and other heat-stable materials in a hot air oven using a validated process. A commonly used minimum time and temperature is 30 min at 250°. If employing plastic apparatus, such as microplates and pipet tips for automatic pipetters, use apparatus that is shown to be free of detectable endotoxin and does not interfere in the test.

[Note — In this chapter, the term “tube” includes any other receptacle such as a microtiter well.]

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Depyrogenation: The process of removing endotoxin from the surface of an item. The most common method is dry heat, at 250°C for at least 30 minutes. This is completely different from typical sterilization conditions (autoclave steam at 121°C for 15 min).

Why is a temperature as high as 250°C needed? The LPS (lipopolysaccharide) molecule is extremely stable. Ordinary steam sterilization (121°C) only kills microorganisms and cannot destroy endotoxin. Dry heat at 250°C is needed to cleave and inactivate the LPS molecule.

Plastic apparatus: Plastic materials that cannot withstand dry heat at 250°C (such as microplates and pipette tips) must be certified products that have been verified to be free of endotoxin and not to interfere with the test.

Analogy

The dishwashing analogy: Ordinary dishwashing (sterilization) removes food residue (microorganisms), but if a bowl has industrial glue (endotoxin) on it, you need a much stronger cleaning method: baking it at high temperature in an oven (depyrogenation) is the only way to truly remove it. This is why glassware used for BET must go through "oven cleaning" at 250°C.

Key Notes

Validation requirement: The depyrogenation process must be validated. USP ⟨1211⟩ Sterility Assurance specifies the details of depyrogenation validation, including challenge testing with a known amount of endotoxin to demonstrate at least a 3-log reduction (99.9% removal).

CDMO points to note: Heat distribution studies and heat penetration studies of the depyrogenation oven are items auditors frequently check. Make sure calibration records and validation reports are readily available.

Practice Questions

  1. If the temperature record of your depyrogenation oven shows a brief drop to 245°C at minute 15, how would you handle this deviation?
  2. Why might using single-use plastic apparatus for BET have supply chain advantages over depyrogenated glassware? Conversely, what are the risks?
0.3 Reagents and Test Solutions

Original Text

Amoebocyte Lysate

A lyophilized product obtained from the lysate of amoebocytes (white blood cells) from the horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus or Tachypleus tridentatus). This reagent refers only to a product manufactured in accordance with the regulations of the competent authority.

[Note — Amoebocyte Lysate reacts to some β-glucans in addition to endotoxins. Amoebocyte Lysate preparations that do not react to glucans are available: they are prepared by removing the G factor reacting to glucans from Amoebocyte Lysate or by inhibiting the G factor reacting system of Amoebocyte Lysate and may be used for endotoxin testing in the presence of glucans.]

Water for Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET)

Use Water for Injection or water produced by other procedures that shows no reaction with the lysate employed, at the detection limit of the reagent.

Lysate TS

Dissolve Amoebocyte Lysate in Water for BET, or in a buffer recommended by the lysate manufacturer, by gentle stirring. Store the reconstituted lysate, refrigerated or frozen, according to the specifications of the manufacturer.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL): A lyophilized lysate extracted from the blood cells (amebocytes) of the horseshoe crab. When horseshoe crab blood contacts endotoxin, it triggers a coagulation cascade, and LAL exploits this natural defense mechanism to detect endotoxin.

Worldwide, LAL currently comes mainly from two species:

  • Limulus polyphemus - the American horseshoe crab (Atlantic coast), the main source of LAL
  • Tachypleus tridentatus - the tri-spine horseshoe crab (Asia), also known as TAL (Tachypleus Amebocyte Lysate)

The β-glucan interference problem: Conventional LAL reacts not only to endotoxin but also to certain fungal cell wall components (β-1,3-glucan), causing false positives. Solutions include LAL with Factor G removed, LAL in which the Factor G reaction pathway is blocked, or switching to recombinant Factor C (rFC) technology.

Analogy

The sniffer dog analogy: LAL is like a trained drug-detection dog: it raises the alarm (gel-clot reaction) when it smells a specific substance (endotoxin). But the dog sometimes also reacts to certain other substances (β-glucan), just as a drug dog sometimes barks at legitimate medications. To improve accuracy, we can "retrain" the dog (LAL with Factor G removed) so that it reacts only to the true target (endotoxin).

Key Notes

LAL supply chain risk: Horseshoe crabs are a protected species, and access to LAL is increasingly limited by environmental regulations. This poses a major challenge to CDMO supply chain management:

  • LAL suppliers are limited (mainly Lonza, Associates of Cape Cod, and Fujifilm Wako)
  • Prices keep rising and lead times are unstable
  • This is also an important driver of the growing interest in rFC (recombinant Factor C) technology

BET water: Water for Injection (WFI) or equivalent endotoxin-free water must be used. Purified Water must never be used for BET testing.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO is testing a drug product that contains a cellulose derivative. The laboratory reports an abnormally high BET result. What would you suspect first? (Hint: β-glucan interference)
  2. If your LAL supplier notifies you that the lead time for the next lot of LAL will be delayed by three months, what is your contingency plan?
0.4 Preparation of Solutions

Original Text

Standard Endotoxin Stock Solution

A Standard Endotoxin Stock Solution is prepared from a USP Endotoxin Reference Standard that has been calibrated to the current WHO International Standard for Endotoxin. Follow the specifications in the package leaflet and on the label for preparation and storage of the Standard Endotoxin Stock Solution. Endotoxin is expressed in Endotoxin Units (EU). [Note — One USP Endotoxin Unit (EU) is equal to one International Unit (IU) of endotoxin.]

Standard Endotoxin Solutions

After mixing the Standard Endotoxin Stock Solution vigorously, prepare appropriate serial dilutions of Standard Endotoxin Solution, using Water for BET. Use dilutions as soon as possible to avoid loss of activity by adsorption.

Sample Solutions

Prepare the Sample Solutions by dissolving or diluting drugs using Water for BET. Some substances or preparations may be more appropriately dissolved, or diluted in other aqueous solutions. If necessary, adjust the pH of the solution to be examined (or dilution thereof) so that the pH of the mixture of the lysate and Sample Solution falls within the pH range specified by the lysate manufacturer, usually 6.0–8.0. The pH may be adjusted by use of an acid, base, or suitable buffer as recommended by the lysate manufacturer. Acids and bases may be prepared from concentrates or solids with Water for BET in containers free of detectable endotoxin. Buffers must be validated to be free of detectable endotoxin and interfering factors.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

USP Reference Standard Endotoxin (RSE): The USP RSE is a lyophilized endotoxin that has been calibrated and is traceable to the WHO International Standard. It is the basis of metrological traceability for all BET testing.

Control Standard Endotoxin (CSE): Each LAL manufacturer supplies its own CSE, but the CSE must be calibrated against the USP RSE. In routine testing, the CSE is normally used rather than the RSE directly.

Endotoxin Unit (EU): 1 EU = 1 IU (International Unit), a globally uniform unit of measurement. A useful reference point: about 5-10 EU can cause a pyrogenic response in humans.

Adsorption loss: Endotoxin molecules are amphipathic and readily adsorb onto container walls, especially at low concentrations. Standard solutions must therefore be vortexed thoroughly and used promptly.

Analogy

Traceability to the gold standard: The RSE is like the "International Prototype Kilogram" kept by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures: it is the ultimate reference for all endotoxin measurement worldwide. A CSE is like a country's own "national copy of the kilogram", which must be periodically traced back to the international prototype to ensure accuracy. The standard your laboratory uses every day (CSE) must be traceable to the RSE, and the RSE is traceable to the WHO International Standard.

Key Notes

pH control is critical: The LAL reaction is pH sensitive, and a pH of 6.0–8.0 is normally required. If the sample itself has an extreme pH (for example, some antibiotic solutions with pH < 4), the pH must be adjusted before testing. However, the acids, bases, and buffers used to adjust the pH must also be verified as endotoxin-free; otherwise you are introducing a new source of contamination.

CDMO challenge: The pH of different customers' products varies widely (acidic antibiotics vs. neutral protein drugs vs. alkaline buffers), which means your QC laboratory must prepare a different pH adjustment strategy for each product and validate each one individually.

Practice Questions

  1. Why must the standard endotoxin solution be "thoroughly vortexed" before use? What effect would it have on the result if you withdrew and diluted it without mixing?
  2. A colleague in your laboratory reports that the calibration of a lot of RSE against CSE deviates beyond the acceptance range. What action would you take?
0.5 Determination of Maximum Valid Dilution (MVD) & Endotoxin Limit

Original Text

The maximum valid dilution is the maximum allowable dilution of a specimen at which the endotoxin limit can be determined. Determine the MVD from the following equation:

MVD Formula

MVD = (Endotoxin Limit × Concentration of Sample Solution) / λ

Endotoxin Limit

The endotoxin limit for parenteral drugs, defined on the basis of dose, equals K/M, where K is a threshold pyrogenic dose of endotoxin per kg of body weight, and M is equal to the maximum recommended bolus dose of product per kg of body weight. When the product is to be injected at frequent intervals or infused continuously, M is the maximum total dose administered in a single hour period.

The endotoxin limit for parenteral drugs is specified in the individual monograph in units such as EU/mL, EU/mg, EU/Unit of biological activity, etc.

Concentration of Sample Solution

  • mg/mL: in the case of endotoxin limit specified by weight (EU/mg)
  • Units/mL: in the case of endotoxin limit specified by unit of biological activity (EU/Unit)
  • mL/mL: when the endotoxin limit is specified by volume (EU/mL)

λ: the labeled sensitivity in the Gel-Clot Technique (EU/mL) or the lowest concentration used in the standard curve for the Turbidimetric Technique or Chromogenic Technique.

K Values (Footnote 2)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The K/M formula - the core of the endotoxin limit:

  • K (threshold dose) = the threshold endotoxin dose that humans can tolerate (EU/kg body weight)
  • M (maximum dose) = the maximum recommended single dose of the drug per kg body weight
  • The logic of K/M: if a person can tolerate K EU/kg of endotoxin and the dose of the drug is M, then the maximum amount of endotoxin allowed per unit of drug is K/M

Why is the K value for intrathecal injection only 0.2 EU/kg? Because intrathecal injection goes directly into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), where immune defenses are much weaker than in blood, so even a trace of endotoxin can trigger severe neuroinflammation.

MVD Calculation Example

Scenario: An injectable product has a strength of 100 mg/mL, an endotoxin limit of 0.5 EU/mg, and the LAL used has a sensitivity λ = 0.125 EU/mL

MVD = (Endotoxin Limit × Concentration) / λ
MVD = (0.5 EU/mg × 100 mg/mL) / 0.125 EU/mL
MVD = 50 / 0.125
MVD = 400

Meaning: the sample can be diluted at most 400-fold and still allow endotoxin to be detected effectively.

Why is the MVD important? Some samples interfere with the LAL reaction (inhibition or enhancement). Dilution can reduce the interference, but too much dilution brings the endotoxin concentration below the detection limit. The MVD is the maximum allowable balance point between "eliminating interference" and "maintaining detection capability".

K/M Limit Calculation Examples

Example 1: General intravenous (IV) injection
Drug dose: 200 mg per administration, 70 kg adult
K = 5 EU/kg
M = 200 mg / 70 kg = 2.86 mg/kg
Endotoxin Limit = K/M = 5/2.86 = 1.75 EU/mg

Example 2: Intrathecal injection
Drug dose: 10 mg per administration, 70 kg adult
K = 0.2 EU/kg
M = 10 mg / 70 kg = 0.143 mg/kg
Endotoxin Limit = K/M = 0.2/0.143 = 1.4 EU/mg

Note: the K value for intrathecal injection is 1/25 of that for general administration!

Analogy

The dilution analogy - coffee and poison: Imagine you suspect a cup of coffee has been laced with a trace of poison, and you have a test strip that can detect the poison above a certain concentration. If the coffee is too strong (dark), the strip cannot develop color properly (this is the interference). You can dilute the coffee with water so the strip works, but if you add too much water, the poison is diluted below what the strip can detect (below λ) and the test fails. The MVD tells you "how much water you can add at most".

Key Notes

MVD management in CDMO practice:

  • Every customer product has a different endotoxin limit and concentration, so the MVD differs for each product
  • MVD calculation errors are a common 483 observation - make sure calculation sheets are independently double-checked
  • When a customer changes the dosage form strength (for example, concentration from 50 mg/mL to 100 mg/mL), the MVD must be recalculated
  • Using a more sensitive LAL (smaller λ) gives a larger MVD, which makes it easier to overcome interference

Practice Questions

  1. A customer's product has an endotoxin limit of 5 EU/mL and the LAL sensitivity λ = 0.03 EU/mL. Calculate the MVD. If testing still shows interference at the MVD dilution, what alternative strategies do you have?
  2. Why does the endotoxin limit of the same drug become extremely strict if it is changed to the intrathecal route? What impact does this have on CDMO process design?
  3. Your laboratory currently uses LAL with λ = 0.125 EU/mL. If you switch to a more sensitive LAL with λ = 0.03 EU/mL, by what factor does the MVD increase? What is the practical benefit?

Section Summary

  • The BET is a mandatory release test for all injectable products and uses Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) to detect the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria
  • Three test techniques: gel-clot, turbidimetric, and chromogenic - in a dispute, the gel-clot method is the referee method
  • Depyrogenation of apparatus: glassware must be treated with dry heat at 250°C / 30 min; plastic apparatus must be certified endotoxin-free products
  • LAL and β-glucan: conventional LAL cross-reacts with β-glucan, so LAL with Factor G removed or rFC technology is needed to avoid false positives
  • Endotoxin limit = K/M: K is the human threshold dose (generally 5 EU/kg, intrathecal 0.2 EU/kg), and M is the maximum dose per kg
  • MVD = (Endotoxin Limit × Concentration) / λ: determines the maximum allowable dilution of the sample and is the key parameter balancing "eliminating interference" and "maintaining detection capability"
  • RSE → CSE traceability chain: the basis of metrological traceability for all BET testing; 1 EU = 1 IU is the globally uniform unit of measurement

1.1 Gel-Clot Technique

Original Text

The gel-clot technique is used for detecting or quantifying endotoxins based on clotting of the lysate reagent in the presence of endotoxin. The minimum concentration of endotoxin required to cause the lysate to clot under standard conditions is the labeled sensitivity of the lysate reagent.

Preparatory Testing — Test for Confirmation of Labeled Lysate Sensitivity

Confirm in four replicates the labeled sensitivity, λ, expressed in EU/mL of the lysate prior to use in the test. The test for confirmation of lysate sensitivity is to be carried out when a new batch of lysate is used or when there is any change in the test conditions that may affect the outcome of the test.

Prepare standard solutions having at least four concentrations equivalent to 2λ, λ, 0.5λ, and 0.25λ by diluting the USP Endotoxin RS with Water for BET.

Mix a volume of the Lysate TS with an equal volume (such as 0.1-mL aliquots) of one of the Standard Endotoxin Solutions in each test tube. Incubate the reaction mixture for a constant period according to the directions of the lysate manufacturer (usually at 37 ± 1° for 60 ± 2 min), avoiding vibration.

To test the integrity of the gel, take each tube in turn directly from the incubator, and invert it through about 180° in one smooth motion. If a firm gel has formed that remains in place upon inversion, record the result as positive (+). A result is negative (−) if an intact gel is not formed.

The test is considered valid when the lowest concentration of the standard solutions shows a negative result in all replicate tests.

Geometric Mean Endpoint Concentration

Geometric mean endpoint = antilog (Σe / f)

where:
  Σe = sum of log endpoint concentrations of dilution series
  f  = number of replicate test tubes

The geometric mean endpoint concentration is the measured sensitivity of the lysate (in EU/mL). If this is not less than 0.5λ and not more than 2λ, the labeled sensitivity is confirmed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Principle of the gel-clot technique: When LAL reagent comes into contact with endotoxin, it triggers an enzyme cascade that ultimately converts a soluble protein (coagulogen) into insoluble coagulin, forming a gel visible to the naked eye. The threshold of this reaction is the labeled sensitivity of the lysate (λ).

Logic of the sensitivity confirmation:

  • Prepare four concentrations: 2λ, λ, 0.5λ and 0.25λ
  • Expected results: 2λ and λ should be positive (+), 0.25λ should be negative (−), and 0.5λ may be either + or −
  • Calculate the geometric mean endpoint of the four replicates; the result must fall between 0.5λ and 2λ

Why use the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean? Endotoxin concentrations are prepared by serial two-fold dilution, so the data are distributed logarithmically, and the geometric mean reflects the true central tendency more accurately.

Analogy

The pudding analogy: The gel-clot technique is like making pudding. You mix the LAL reagent and the sample, then "bake" it in a 37°C "oven" (the incubator) for 60 minutes. If there is enough endotoxin in the mixture (like enough pudding powder), it sets into a jelly. Turn the tube upside down: if the "pudding" stays firmly at the bottom of the tube, the reaction is positive; if it runs down as soon as you invert the tube, it is negative.

Confirming sensitivity is like checking that your "pudding recipe" is as sensitive as the label says. You test it with a known amount of "pudding powder" (reference standard endotoxin) and confirm that it sets at exactly the labeled concentration.

Key Notes

Critical operating details:

  • Avoid vibration: Any vibration during incubation can disrupt the gel as it forms and lead to false negatives
  • Inversion technique: The tube must be inverted through 180° in one smooth motion, not shaken back and forth. Beginners often make the mistake of inverting too slowly or too abruptly
  • 37°C ± 1°: Temperature control is extremely strict. The incubator must be calibrated regularly

CDMO note: The gel-clot technique is the most classic and lowest-cost method, but it is qualitative or semi-quantitative and depends on the operator's subjective reading. For high-throughput CDMO laboratories, the photometric quantitative techniques (turbidimetric/chromogenic) are more commonly used for routine release testing.

Practice Questions

  1. During a sensitivity confirmation in your laboratory, the endpoints of the four replicates are λ, λ, 0.5λ and 2λ. Calculate the geometric mean endpoint. Is the sensitivity confirmed?
  2. A new analyst reports a gel-clot result by saying, "After I inverted the tube, the clot moved slightly but did not fall." How would you coach the analyst to interpret this result?
1.2 Gel-Clot Limit Test (Method A)

Original Text

Table 2. Preparation of Solutions for the Gel-Clot Limit Test

Solution A and the positive product control Solution B use a dilution not greater than the MVD. The positive control Solutions B and C contain the Standard Endotoxin Solution at a concentration corresponding to twice the labeled lysate sensitivity (2λ). The negative control Solution D consists of Water for BET.

Interpretation

The test is considered valid when both replicates of Solutions B and C are positive and those of Solution D are negative.

When a negative result is found for both replicates of Solution A, the preparation under test complies with the test.

When a positive result is found for both replicates of Solution A, the preparation does not comply.

When a positive result is found for one replicate and a negative for the other, repeat the test. In the repeat test, the preparation complies if both replicates of Solution A are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The roles of the four solutions:

  • Solution A (sample) - the product you are testing, with no reference standard endotoxin added
  • Solution B (positive product control) - product + 2λ reference standard endotoxin. Verifies that the product does not inhibit the LAL reaction
  • Solution C (positive control) - water + 2λ reference standard endotoxin. Verifies that the LAL reagent itself works properly
  • Solution D (negative control) - water only. Verifies that there is no extraneous contamination

Three-way validity check: B and C must both be positive (demonstrating that the system can detect endotoxin) and D must be negative (demonstrating that there is no background contamination). If any one condition is not met, the whole test is invalid.

Analogy

The smoke detector analogy: Imagine you are testing whether a room contains smoke (endotoxin):

  • A = test the target room directly
  • B = light a test incense stick in the target room - the detector must sound (confirming that nothing else in the room masks the smoke signal)
  • C = light an incense stick in a clean room - the detector must sound (confirming that the detector itself works)
  • D = test a clean, empty room - the detector must not sound (confirming that the detector does not give false alarms)

Key Notes

Handling one positive and one negative: When the two replicates of Solution A disagree (one +, one −), you cannot simply take a "majority vote". The entire test must be repeated. In the repeat test, both replicates must be negative for the product to pass - a very strict standard.

CDMO batch release risk: The gel-clot limit test is a pass/fail determination. Once it fails, the entire batch may be rejected. In a CDMO setting, this can mean losses of millions and damage to the client relationship. It is therefore essential to ensure that sampling procedures, dilution calculations and operating technique are accurate.

Practice Questions

  1. In a limit test, Solution B gives a negative result. What does this mean, and what should you do next?
  2. In the initial test of your product, Solution A gave one positive and one negative result; in the repeat test, both were negative. The product passed. What follow-up investigation should you carry out?
1.3 Gel-Clot Quantitative Test (Method B)

Original Text

The test quantifies bacterial endotoxins in Sample Solutions by titration to an endpoint.

Table 3. Preparation of Solutions for the Gel-Clot Assay

Calculation and Interpretation

The test is considered valid when: (1) Both replicates of Solution D are negative; (2) Both replicates of Solution B are positive; and (3) The geometric mean endpoint concentration of Solution C is in the range of 0.5λ to 2λ.

To determine the endotoxin concentration of Solution A, calculate the endpoint concentration for each replicate by multiplying each endpoint dilution factor by λ. If none of the dilutions is positive, report as less than λ. If all dilutions are positive, report as equal to or greater than the greatest dilution factor × λ.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quantitative gel-clot vs. the limit test:

  • Limit test (Method A): asks only "Is the limit exceeded?" - pass/fail
  • Quantitative test (Method B): asks "How much is there?" - the endpoint is found through serial dilution and the actual endotoxin concentration is calculated

Principle of the endpoint dilution method: Dilute the sample 1-, 2-, 4- and 8-fold and observe which dilution is the last one that still forms a clot. That dilution factor × λ is the estimate of the endotoxin concentration in the sample.

Analogy

The wine taster's dilution test: Imagine a wine taster who wants to determine the capsaicin content of a bottle of wine. He dilutes the wine step by step (1-, 2-, 4- and 8-fold) and tastes each glass. The last dilution at which he can still detect heat is his "detection endpoint". Multiplying that dilution factor by his lowest detection threshold (λ) gives the capsaicin concentration in the original wine.

Key Notes

The reporting format matters:

  • All negative → report as "< λ" (or < λ × the lowest dilution factor)
  • All positive → report as "≥ the maximum dilution factor × λ" - this means the actual concentration may be higher and further dilution is needed to quantify it

CDMO application: The quantitative gel-clot test is particularly useful in stability studies and in-process control (IPC) monitoring because it provides trend data. For example, if the endotoxin level in a client's product gradually rises from 0.1 EU/mL to 0.3 EU/mL, the root cause needs to be investigated even though it is still within the limit.

Practice Questions

  1. A sample gives the following quantitative gel-clot results: 1-fold (+), 2-fold (+), 4-fold (+), 8-fold (−). λ = 0.125 EU/mL. Calculate the endotoxin concentration of the sample.
  2. Why does USP ⟨85⟩ require that dilutions in the quantitative gel-clot test not exceed the MVD? What happens if they do?
1.4 Photometric Quantitative Techniques

Original Text

Turbidimetric Technique (Method C)

This technique is a photometric assay measuring increases in reactant turbidity. On the basis of the particular assay principle employed, this technique may be classified as either an endpoint-turbidimetric assay or a kinetic-turbidimetric assay.

  • Endpoint-turbidimetric: based on the quantitative relationship between the concentration of endotoxins and the turbidity (absorbance or transmission) of the reaction mixture at the end of an incubation period
  • Kinetic-turbidimetric: measures either the time (onset time) needed to reach a predetermined absorbance, or the rate of turbidity development

Chromogenic Technique (Method D)

This technique is an assay to measure the chromophore released from a suitable chromogenic peptide by the reaction of endotoxins with lysate.

  • Endpoint-chromogenic: based on the quantitative relationship between endotoxin concentration and chromophore release at the end of an incubation period
  • Kinetic-chromogenic: measures either the onset time to reach a predetermined absorbance, or the rate of color development

Both techniques are carried out at the incubation temperature recommended by the lysate manufacturer (usually 37 ± 1°).

Preparatory Testing — Assurance of Criteria for the Standard Curve

Prepare at least three endotoxin concentrations within the range indicated by the lysate manufacturer to generate the standard curve. Perform the assay using at least three replicates of each standard. If the desired range is greater than two logs in kinetic methods, additional standards should be included to bracket each log increase. The absolute value of the correlation coefficient, r, must be ≥ 0.980.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The egg drop soup analogy:

  • The turbidimetric technique (Method C) is like watching how cloudy the egg drop soup gets - the more egg you add (the more endotoxin), the cloudier the soup (the higher the absorbance)
  • The chromogenic technique (Method D) is like adding a special color indicator to the soup - the stronger the reaction, the deeper the color (usually a change to yellow, using a pNA substrate)
  • The kinetic technique is like using a timer to record how long it takes from adding the egg until the soup turns cloudy - the more egg, the faster it clouds

Key Notes

Practical meaning of r ≥ 0.980: The correlation coefficient requirement reflects the linearity of the standard curve. In practice, most acceptable standard curves have r values above 0.990. If the r value is close to the 0.980 boundary, check the following:

  • Were the standard solutions prepared accurately?
  • Is the incubator temperature stable?
  • Is there a position (edge) effect on the microplate?

CDMO selection strategy: Most modern CDMO QC laboratories use the kinetic chromogenic or kinetic turbidimetric technique as the routine release method, because they give quantitative results, can be automated and offer complete data traceability. The gel-clot technique is mainly kept as the referee method or a backup method.

  1. Your standard curve has r = 0.978. Under USP ⟨85⟩, is this standard curve acceptable? What action should you take?
  2. A client requires your CDMO to use the gel-clot technique for endotoxin release testing, but your laboratory routinely uses the chromogenic technique. How do you respond to the client's request?
  3. Compare the kinetic turbidimetric and kinetic chromogenic techniques: what are the advantages and disadvantages of each? Which would you recommend as the primary release method in your CDMO laboratory, and why?

Key Concepts

Photometric vs. gel-clot techniques - the essential difference:

The core concept of kinetic techniques - onset time: In kinetic techniques, the instrument continuously monitors the progress of the reaction. The higher the endotoxin concentration, the faster the reaction, and the shorter the time to reach the threshold absorbance (the onset time). A standard curve plotted as log[endotoxin] vs. log[onset time] is linear with a negative slope.

1.5 USP ⟨85⟩ Methods at a Glance

Original Text

Summary of USP ⟨85⟩ Methods

“Proceed by any of the three techniques for the test. In the event of doubt or dispute, the final decision is made based upon the gel-clot limit test unless otherwise indicated in the monograph.”

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Decision framework for method selection:

  • Routine release → kinetic chromogenic/turbidimetric technique (Method C/D) - quantitative, high-throughput, traceable data
  • Referee/dispute → gel-clot limit test (Method A) - the referee method harmonized across the three pharmacopoeias
  • Trend analysis/stability → quantitative methods (Method B/C/D) - numerical values are needed to track trends
  • Rapid on-site screening → gel-clot technique (Method A) - no expensive instrument required

Key Notes

Method validation strategy for a CDMO: As a CDMO, you may need to maintain the validated status of several BET methods at the same time:

  • Primary method (e.g., Method D, kinetic chromogenic) for routine release
  • Backup method (e.g., Method A, gel-clot) for emergencies such as instrument failure, or for referee testing
  • Each method requires independent validation (interfering factors testing), performed separately for each client product

This means BET method validation cannot be overlooked as a prerequisite activity during technology transfer for a new product. Many CDMOs factor the cost and timeline of BET interfering factors testing into the quotation stage.

  1. Your CDMO is evaluating whether to upgrade its primary BET method from gel-clot to the kinetic chromogenic technique. List the advantages of upgrading and the risks to consider.
  2. Why does USP ⟨85⟩ allow any of the methods to be used but revert to the gel-clot technique in the event of a dispute? What regulatory philosophy does this reflect?

Section Summary

  • Gel-clot limit test (Method A): pass/fail determination; uses four solutions, A/B/C/D, with controls; the referee method across the three pharmacopoeias
  • Gel-clot quantitative test (Method B): finds the endpoint by serial dilution; semi-quantitative result, reported as endpoint × λ
  • Kinetic turbidimetric technique (Method C): measures the rate of turbidity change or the onset time; requires a standard curve (r ≥ 0.980)
  • Kinetic chromogenic technique (Method D): measures the rate of chromophore release or the onset time; the highest quantitative precision
  • Sensitivity confirmation: each new lot of LAL must be confirmed to have a λ within 0.5λ–2λ, calculated using the geometric mean
  • CDMO strategy: use photometric methods (C/D) for routine release, keep the gel-clot technique (A) as the referee/backup method, and validate interfering factors independently for each client product

2.1 Inhibition/Enhancement Test for Photometric Techniques

Original Text

Select an endotoxin concentration at or near the middle of the endotoxin standard curve.

Table 4. Preparation of Solutions for the Inhibition/Enhancement Test for Photometric Techniques

Validity Criteria

  1. The absolute value of the correlation coefficient of the standard curve (Solution C) is ≥ 0.980.
  2. The result with Solution D does not exceed the limit of the blank value, or is less than the endotoxin detection limit.

Recovery Calculation

Calculate the mean recovery of the added endotoxin by subtracting the mean endotoxin concentration in Solution A from that in Solution B. The measured concentration must be within 50%–200% of the known added endotoxin concentration.

When the endotoxin recovery is out of the specified range, the Sample Solution is considered to contain interfering factors. Repeat the test using a greater dilution, not exceeding the MVD.

Overcoming Interference

Interference may be overcome by suitable treatment such as filtration, neutralization, dialysis, or heating. To establish that the chosen treatment effectively eliminates interference without loss of endotoxins, perform the assay using the preparation to which Standard Endotoxin has been added and which has then been submitted to the chosen treatment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Core logic of the interference test: You add a known amount of standard endotoxin to the product (Solution B) and measure how much can be recovered. If recovery is within 50%-200%, the product does not significantly interfere with the test result.

Two types of interference:

  • Inhibition: recovery < 50% - some components of the product (e.g., antimicrobial agents, chelating agents, extreme pH) inhibit the LAL reaction and give falsely low results → risk of false negatives
  • Enhancement: recovery > 200% - some components of the product (e.g., β-glucan, certain proteins) promote the reaction and give falsely high results → risk of false positives

Why is 50%-200% acceptable? This is a range of ±1 two-fold step on a log scale (log ±0.3). It is mathematically equivalent to the 0.5λ-2λ acceptance criterion of the gel-clot method and reflects the inherent biological variability of the BET.

Analogy

A blood alcohol test analogy: Imagine you are developing a new blood alcohol test, but you are concerned that some drugs may interfere with the result. How would you verify it?

You spike a patient's blood sample (Solution B) with a known concentration of alcohol and measure it. If you add 0.08% and read 0.04%-0.16% (50%-200%), the interference is acceptable. If you read only 0.02% (25%), there is severe inhibition - in that case, using this method to test this patient's blood directly is unreliable.

Key Notes

Strategies for overcoming interference (in order of common use):

  1. Further dilution (most common) - dilution lowers the concentration of the interfering substance, but must not exceed the MVD
  2. Heat treatment - some interfering proteins can be inactivated by heating (typically 70-80°C / 10-15 min)
  3. Filtration - use a filter of appropriate pore size to remove interfering particles
  4. Neutralization - adjust the pH to the optimal range for the LAL reaction
  5. Dialysis - removes small-molecule interfering substances (less commonly used)

Key reminder for CDMOs: Any treatment used to overcome interference must be shown not to cause endotoxin loss. Some treatments (such as heating) may also destroy endotoxin and lead to false negatives - which is more dangerous than the interference itself.

Practice Questions

  1. In a client product's interference test, after spiking 5.0 EU/mL of standard endotoxin, Solution B measured 8.5 EU/mL and Solution A measured 0.3 EU/mL. What is the recovery, and does it pass?
  2. Your product fails the interference test at a 1:10 dilution (recovery 35%), but the MVD is only 1:20. How much room do you have to try a higher dilution? If 1:20 still fails, what is your next step?
  3. You choose heat treatment to overcome interference. How would you design experiments to demonstrate both (a) that the interference has been eliminated and (b) that the endotoxin has not been destroyed?
2.2 Test Procedure, Calculation & Interpretation

Original Text

Test Procedure

Follow the procedure described for Test for Interfering Factors under Preparatory Testing.

Calculation

Calculate the endotoxin concentration of each of the replicates of Solution A, using the standard curve generated by the positive control Solution C.

Validity Requirements

  1. The results of the control Solution C comply with the requirements for validation defined for Assurance of Criteria for the Standard Curve.
  2. The endotoxin recovery, calculated from Solution B after subtracting Solution A, is within 50%–200%.
  3. The result of the negative control Solution D does not exceed the limit of the blank value or is less than the endotoxin detection limit.

Interpretation

In photometric assays, the preparation under test complies with the test if the mean endotoxin concentration of the replicates of Solution A, after correction for dilution and concentration, is less than the endotoxin limit for the product.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three safeguards for a valid photometric result:

  • Standard curve verification (Condition 1): confirms the instrument and reagent system are working properly - r ≥ 0.980
  • Recovery verification (Condition 2): confirms the sample does not interfere with the test - 50%-200% recovery
  • Negative control verification (Condition 3): confirms there is no extraneous contamination - Solution D below the detection limit

All three conditions must be met for the test to be valid. If any one is not met, the result cannot be used, and the cause must be investigated and the test repeated.

Reporting results: For photometric methods the mean is reported, not the worst value. However, dilution and concentration corrections must be applied correctly - forgetting to multiply back by the dilution factor is the most common calculation error.

Worked Calculation Example

Product: injection solution 50 mg/mL
Endotoxin limit: 2.0 EU/mg
Test dilution: 1:100
LAL method: kinetic chromogenic (λ = 0.005 EU/mL)

MVD = (2.0 EU/mg × 50 mg/mL) / 0.005 EU/mL = 20,000
Actual dilution 1:100 < MVD 20,000 → acceptable

Test results (Solution A replicates):
  Replicate 1: 0.032 EU/mL
  Replicate 2: 0.028 EU/mL
  Mean: 0.030 EU/mL

Corrected back to the undiluted solution:
  0.030 EU/mL × 100 (dilution factor) = 3.0 EU/mL

Converted to EU/mg:
  3.0 EU/mL ÷ 50 mg/mL = 0.06 EU/mg

Limit = 2.0 EU/mg
Result 0.06 EU/mg < 2.0 EU/mg → PASS

Key Notes

Data integrity requirements for a CDMO:

  • All BET results (including standard curves, controls and replicate tests) must be recorded in a controlled system
  • The calculation process (dilution correction, unit conversion) must be traceable
  • Software-calculated results must be verified by CSV (Computer System Validation)
  • Abnormal results (OOS) must trigger an OOS investigation in accordance with the SOP

Link to hold time studies: BET results are also used to validate in-process hold times. For example, during the wait between compounding and filling, could endotoxin rise because of microbial proliferation? The BET time-point data in a hold time study directly affect the design of your process window.

Practice Questions

  1. In the calculation example above, if the analyst forgets to multiply by the dilution factor of 100 and reports 0.030 EU/mL directly, what would be the consequence? (Hint: both 0.030 EU/mL and 3.0 EU/mL are below the limit, but which one is correct?)
  2. In a batch BET, Solution C gives r = 0.992 and Solution D = 0.002 EU/mL (below the detection limit), but the recovery for Solution B is 45%. How would you handle this?
2.3 Inhibition/Enhancement Test for Gel-Clot Techniques

Original Text

Table 1. Preparation of Solutions for the Inhibition/Enhancement Test for Gel-Clot Techniques

Solution A: A Sample Solution free of detectable endotoxins.
Solution B: Test for interference.
Solution C: Control for labeled lysate sensitivity.
Solution D: Negative control of Water for BET.

Validity & Interpretation

The test is considered valid when all replicates of Solutions A and D show no reaction and Solution C confirms the labeled sensitivity.

If the sensitivity of the lysate determined in the presence of Solution B is not less than 0.5λ and not greater than 2λ, the Sample Solution does not contain factors that interfere. Otherwise, the Sample Solution interferes with the test.

If the sample does not comply at a dilution less than the MVD, repeat using a greater dilution, not exceeding the MVD. The use of a more sensitive lysate permits a greater dilution and may contribute to elimination of interference.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

An allergy test analogy: The interference test is like an allergy test. You need to confirm that your "detective" (LAL) still works properly in a particular "environment" (the product). If the detective's sensitivity (its ability to find the target) does not change significantly in this environment (0.5λ-2λ), that shows the environment does not "allergically" affect the detective's performance.

Key Notes

Strategic significance of using a more sensitive LAL: The original text specifically notes that using a more sensitive LAL (smaller λ) gives a larger MVD, which allows a greater dilution to overcome interference. This is an important parameter for a CDMO to evaluate early in technology transfer:

  • LAL with λ = 0.125 EU/mL → smaller MVD → may be insufficient for interfering products
  • LAL with λ = 0.03 EU/mL → MVD about 4 times larger → more room for dilution
  • LAL with λ = 0.005 EU/mL → MVD about 25 times larger → interference can be overcome for almost all products

However, a more sensitive LAL is also more expensive and places stricter demands on keeping the working environment endotoxin-free.

2.4 Recombinant Factor C (rFC) Alternative

Original Text

USP ⟨85⟩ as currently written describes the use of amoebocyte lysate from the horseshoe crab. However, the pharmaceutical industry has developed alternative methods based on recombinant Factor C (rFC) technology that do not require animal-derived reagents.

rFC-based assays use a recombinant version of the Factor C enzyme — the initial endotoxin-sensing protein in the LAL cascade — produced by genetic engineering. When rFC contacts endotoxin, it activates and cleaves a fluorogenic substrate, producing a measurable fluorescent signal proportional to endotoxin concentration.

Regulatory acceptance:

  • European Pharmacopoeia (EP 2.6.32) — General Chapter for rFC adopted in 2021
  • USP ⟨85.1⟩ — Proposed chapter for alternative endotoxin testing methods (under development)
  • FDA — Has accepted rFC in multiple product filings; requires case-by-case comparability demonstration

[Note — USP Endotoxin RS is calibrated for use with amoebocyte lysate. When using rFC, ensure proper calibration and validation per the applicable regulatory framework.]

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Natural vs. recombinant insulin: The relationship of rFC to LAL is like that of recombinant human insulin (Humulin) to porcine insulin. Early diabetes treatment relied on insulin extracted from animal (porcine/bovine) pancreas; genetic engineering later made it possible to produce identical human insulin in the laboratory - with more stable supply, more uniform quality and no dependence on animal sources. rFC follows the same path of evolution.

Key Notes

rFC strategy considerations for a CDMO:

  • Supply chain advantage: LAL depends on the harvesting of horseshoe crab blood and is limited by seasonality, environmental regulations and geography. The supply of rFC is more stable and its price more predictable
  • Regulatory risk: Some markets (e.g., Japan PMDA) are still conservative about accepting rFC, so the regulatory requirements of the target market must be confirmed before use
  • Transition strategy: Many CDMOs adopt a "parallel validation" strategy - testing with LAL and rFC side by side, accumulating comparative data, and then transitioning step by step
  • ESG benefit: Using rFC supports corporate social responsibility (ESG) goals on animal welfare and sustainability, which is a plus with European and US clients

Important: When using rFC, calibration of the USP RSE may require additional verification steps, because the RSE was originally calibrated against LAL. Confirm the calibration scheme provided by the rFC reagent supplier.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO is considering a full switch from LAL to rFC. List the types and amount of comparative data you would need to submit to regulatory authorities.
  2. A client's product contains a large amount of β-glucan (e.g., some glycoprotein drugs). The interference test repeatedly fails with standard LAL. How does rFC solve this problem?
  3. From a supply chain management perspective, analyze the long-term value of rFC for a CDMO. If horseshoe crab conservation regulations tighten further within five years (restricting LAL production), what should your CDMO be preparing now?

Key Concepts

How rFC works: In the natural LAL cascade, Factor C is the first protein to contact endotoxin. rFC technology produces this key protein in quantity by genetic engineering (usually expressed in insect or mammalian cells), with no need to harvest horseshoe crab blood.

Original Text

USP Reference Standards ⟨11⟩

USP Endotoxin RS

Footnote 1

For a validity test of the procedure for inactivating endotoxins, see Dry-Heat Sterilization under Sterility Assurance ⟨1211⟩. Use Lysate TS having a sensitivity of not less than 0.15 Endotoxin Unit per mL.

Footnote 2 — K Values

K = 5 EU/kg for any route other than intrathecal.

K = 0.2 EU/kg for intrathecal administration.

For radiopharmaceutical products not administered intrathecally: endotoxin limit = 175 EU/V (V = maximum recommended dose in mL).

For intrathecally administered radiopharmaceuticals: 14 EU/V.

For formulations administered on a per square meter of body surface (usually anticancer products): K = 100 EU/m² and M = maximum dose/m².

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The full K value system:

  • K = 5 EU/kg - the standard threshold for the vast majority of injectables (IV, IM, SC, etc.), based on the maximum endotoxin dose a human can tolerate
  • K = 0.2 EU/kg - the stricter threshold for intrathecal injection (1/25 of the usual value), because the central nervous system is extremely sensitive to endotoxin
  • 175 EU/V - a special provision for radiopharmaceuticals, because radiopharmaceuticals usually have small doses and urgent use; it is derived in simplified form from 70 kg body weight × 5 EU/kg / 2 (safety)
  • 100 EU/m² - used specifically for anticancer drugs dosed by body surface area

Significance of Footnote 1: When validating depyrogenation processes, an LAL with sensitivity ≥ 0.15 EU/mL is required. This ensures the validation test has enough sensitivity to detect the residual endotoxin challenge.

Endotoxin Limit Calculation for Radiopharmaceuticals

Example: a Tc-99m labeled diagnostic agent
Maximum injection volume V = 5 mL

Non-intrathecal administration:
  Endotoxin Limit = 175 / V = 175 / 5 = 35 EU/mL

Intrathecal administration (if applicable):
  Endotoxin Limit = 14 / V = 14 / 5 = 2.8 EU/mL

Note: radiopharmaceuticals usually have a short shelf life,
so the BET must be completed before decay - this places
very high demands on test turnaround time.

Key Notes

K value management in practice for a CDMO:

  • The endotoxin limit for each client product must be clearly defined in the technology transfer documents
  • The K value is determined by the route of administration - if the client changes the route, the endotoxin limit must be recalculated
  • Some products may have more than one route of administration (e.g., the same drug given IV or IT) - the strictest limit must apply
  • If a monograph specifies an endotoxin limit, the monograph limit applies, rather than recalculating with the K/M formula
