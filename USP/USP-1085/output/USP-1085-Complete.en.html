Introduction

Original Text

The first version of Bacterial Endotoxins Test 〈85〉 appeared in USP 20–NF 15 (1980). The chapter was subsequently harmonized with the Japanese and European Pharmacopeias, and the first harmonized chapter appeared in USP 25–NF 20 (2002). Since its first publication, the content has changed very little, but years of experience, increasing knowledge, and more complex parenteral formulations suggest that the basic methodologies described herein could benefit from additional supporting information.

The purpose of this information chapter is to provide additional background information and guidance for the performance and proper application of the compendial bacterial endotoxins tests.

"The purpose of this information chapter is to provide additional background information and guidance for the performance and proper application of the compendial bacterial endotoxins tests."

Mentor Notes

Core Concept: How <85> and <1085> Are Positioned

USP chapter numbering follows an important rule:

  • <85> (number < 1000) - a mandatory, compendial test method that all marketed drug products must follow
  • <1085> (number ≥ 1000) - an informational guidance chapter that provides detailed background and recommendations for practical application

The relationship is like "exam rules" versus "exam strategy." <85> tells you how the test is done; <1085> teaches you how to do it well.

Analogy

Driving test rules vs. the instructor's handbook:

<85> is like the traffic-law exam - everyone taking the driving test must follow these rules, with no exceptions. <1085> is like the driving school instructor's handbook - it is not law, but it is full of experience and techniques that help you drive safely in real traffic.

In day-to-day QC work at a CDMO, analysts run the tests specified in <85>, but when problems arise (for example interfering factors, limit calculations, or OOS investigations), you need to turn to <1085> to find solutions.

Key Points

Harmonization among the three pharmacopeias: Since 2002, <85> has been harmonized with the Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP) and the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur.). This means one BET methodology is used worldwide, which is good news for a CDMO - you do not need to prepare a different BET method for each client.

  2. If your CDMO site supplies the European, US, and Japanese markets at the same time, how does harmonization among the three pharmacopeias affect your BET validation strategy?
Background: Pyrogens and Endotoxins

Original Text

Pyrogens, or fever-causing agents, are possible contaminants in parenteral products. Many substances can cause fevers when injected, infused, implanted, or when they come in contact with the bloodstream or cerebrospinal fluid of mammals.

Although some biologics, vaccines, and cell and gene therapies may, by their nature, elicit pyrogenic responses in patients, the predominant and most potent pyrogenic contaminants in the manufacturing of parenteral drugs and medical devices are bacterial endotoxins, which are components of the cell walls of Gram-negative bacteria (GNB).

Endotoxins are integral with the outer cell membrane of GNB. If GNB cannot grow, endotoxins cannot be generated. However, endotoxins may remain active in cell wall fragments after cells die, so a material may be sterile but may still contain quantifiable levels of endotoxins activity.

Endotoxins can be introduced into the process stream by pharmaceutical ingredients including water, raw materials (particularly from natural sources), the active pharmaceutical ingredients (API), drug product formulation excipients, primary (product contact) packaging components, improperly cleaned or stored manufacturing equipment, and/or ineffective microbial contamination control practices.

Mentor Notes

Core Concept: Pyrogen vs. Endotoxin

Pyrogen: a collective term for any substance that can cause a fever response. The range is broad and includes:

  • Bacterial endotoxin (the most common and most potent)
  • Certain proteins (such as cytokines)
  • Certain chemical substances

Endotoxin: specifically a component of the outer membrane of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria (GNB). It is a subset of pyrogens and the type of pyrogen the pharmaceutical industry is most concerned about.

Analogy

"Sterile" does not mean "endotoxin-free":

Imagine a house that caught fire (bacterial growth). After the fire is put out (sterilization), the house is safe (sterile). But the ash and debris left by the fire (endotoxin) are still scattered everywhere. Walking into the house will not burn you, but you may choke on the dust and debris.

This is why, at a CDMO, sterilization and depyrogenation are two separate control steps. A product that passes the sterility test is not necessarily free of endotoxin contamination.

Key Points: Map of Endotoxin Sources

The original text lists six major sources of endotoxin in the process, which are also the focus areas when investigating an OOS:

  • Water systems - the most common source; WFI quality is critical
  • Active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) - especially materials of biological origin
  • Excipients - sugars, heparin, and other natural-source materials carry a higher risk
  • Raw materials (natural origin) - require supplier control
  • Packaging materials - containers/stoppers in direct contact with the product
  • Improper equipment cleaning/storage - an important element of process control

  2. At your site, which endotoxin source is most likely to cause a batch failure? How would you prioritize the investigation?
Endotoxins: The Science Behind Them

Original Text

The terms "bacterial endotoxins" and "endotoxins" refer to a complex component of the outer cell membrane of GNB. Natural contaminants in sterile parenteral drug products or medical devices are thought to include outer membrane vesicles (OMV), which are mini-spheres of outer membrane material released from actively proliferating cells as part of the normal bacterial growth cycle, as well as outer membrane fragments that come from disrupted or dead GNB cells.

The biologically active lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is embedded in or associated with other Gram-negative outer membrane components, including various proteins, phospholipids, and lipoproteins. The hydrophobic lipid A portion of the LPS molecule is embedded in the membrane and the hydrophilic polysaccharide portion is exposed to the external environment.

The current USP Endotoxin Reference Standard (RSE) and commercially prepared control standard endotoxins (CSE) are extracted from the GNB cell membrane, generally by the Westphal hot phenol method, and are purified to remove any surrounding cell membrane components. As such, LPS prepared using Westphal, or other extraction methods, cannot contaminate pharmaceutical products because they do not exist in this form in nature.

Endotoxin standards, attributed to differences in source and manner of preparation, could "react differently from native sources of endotoxins." It is possible that standards extracted by the Westphal method or other denaturing methods and then formulated may not always be representative surrogates for modeling the behavior of natural endotoxins in some pharmaceutical, biopharma, or medical device extraction experiments.

Mentor Notes

Core Concept: LPS Structure and How Standards Differ

Natural endotoxin vs. purified LPS standards:

  • Natural endotoxin - present in the outer membrane of GNB, forming complexes with proteins, phospholipids, and other components. It is released as outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) or fragments
  • RSE/CSE standards - LPS extracted and purified by the Westphal hot-phenol method, with proteins and other components removed. This form does not exist in nature

This difference is critical: the standards you use for calibration and validation may differ significantly in physicochemical properties from the contaminants you actually need to detect.

Analogy

Training mannequin vs. a real person:

RSE/CSE are like first-aid training mannequins - they are designed to simulate a real person so you can practice and test in a standardized way. But a mannequin is not a real person, and some techniques that work well on a mannequin may need adjustment on a real patient.

Likewise, excellent recovery results from a CSE spike do not mean your method detects natural endotoxin contamination with the same efficiency. This is the core of the controversy over the "low endotoxin recovery" (LER) phenomenon.

Key Points: Practical Impact for a CDMO

Impact on biologics: Biological drug products (monoclonal antibodies, protein drugs, etc.) have complex matrices, and natural endotoxin may behave very differently in these matrices from purified LPS standards. This is the root of the recent LER (Low Endotoxin Recovery) controversy, discussed in depth in Section 3.

Day-to-day takeaway: When performing BET suitability tests, do not settle for "passing CSE spike recovery." Consider whether your product matrix could affect how natural endotoxin is detected.

Discussion Questions

  1. Why does USP specifically note in <1085> that standards may "react differently from natural endotoxin"? How does this affect your BET validation strategy?
  2. If a client asks you to use "natural endotoxin" to spike a hold time study, how would you design the experiment? What challenges would you face?
RSE: CSE Calibration

Original Text

It is well known that purified LPS from different genera/species/strains of GNB, when experimentally administered to rabbits on a uniform weight or mass basis, can produce significantly different pyrogenic reactions. However, by relating these effects to the administered activity — meaning their ability to elicit a fever in the rabbit or initiate an LAL reaction — rather than weight or mass, the structural variability of LPS molecules can be normalized to a defined unit of activity called the endotoxin unit (EU).

One USP EU is equivalent to one International Unit (IU) as indicated in 〈85〉.

The RSE is the purified LPS primary standard formulated from a common bulk preparation of E. coli 0113:H10:K LPS that is shared among the WHO, European Pharmacopoeia (Ph.Eur.), Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP), and U.S. Pharmacopeia (USP).

Although RSE is available from USP for routine use, most BET assays are performed using a CSE, which is a secondary calibration analyte that may be included in LAL test kits purchased from reagent manufacturers.

All the reagents used in BET assays are biological in nature and, therefore, can exhibit some variability in sensitivity and potency, making calibration against the RSE an important task. Calibration against RSE is necessary for each unique combination of lysate lot and CSE lot.

"Calibration against RSE is necessary for each unique combination of lysate lot and CSE lot."

CSE Potency by Gel-Clot Method

CSE potency determination is accomplished by comparing the geometric mean (GM) endpoints of separate dilution series of RSE and CSE, tested in quadruplicate. The potency is calculated as:

Potency (EU/ng) = GM endpoint of RSE (EU/mL) ÷ GM endpoint of CSE (ng/mL)

Example: RSE GM = 0.125 EU/mL, CSE GM = 0.00625 ng/mL → Potency = 20 EU/ng

CSE Potency by Quantitative Assays

For quantitative assays: EU/ng = (EU/mL) ÷ (ng/mL). CSE dilutions are tested as unknowns against an RSE standard curve.

Mentor Notes

Core Concept: EU Units and the Calibration Chain

Why use EU rather than mass units?

Even at the same mass, LPS from different bacterial strains can cause very different fever responses. The EU is an "activity unit" that brings LPS from different sources onto a common basis of "causing the same biological response."

Levels of the calibration chain:

  • WHO International Standard → the top level
  • RSE (USP Reference Standard Endotoxin) → primary standard, prepared from E. coli 0113:H10:K
  • CSE (Control Standard Endotoxin) → secondary standard, supplied by the reagent manufacturer

Formula Breakdown: Calculating CSE Potency

Gel-clot method:

Potency (EU/ng) = GM(RSE) in EU/mL ÷ GM(CSE) in ng/mL

Quantitative methods (kinetic/endpoint):

EU/ng = EU/mL (interpolated from RSE curve) ÷ ng/mL (known CSE conc.)

Key rule: Every combination of lysate lot × CSE lot requires its own calibration. If either lot number changes, calibration must be repeated.

Analogy

Calibrating a scale:

Imagine you have an electronic scale (the lysate) and a bag of reference weights (the CSE). You first calibrate your scale with nationally certified weights (the RSE) to make sure it measures accurately. Then you use your scale to "weigh" that bag of weights to confirm their true weight.

But if you switch to a new scale (a new lysate lot), you must recalibrate even if the weights have not changed - because every scale has a different sensitivity.

Key Points

Interpreting the Table 1 data: Note that the EU/ng results for the four CSE dilutions are 15, 12, 12, and 14. These values show some scatter, but the mean of 13.25 is "rounded down" to 13 EU/ng - because the rounding rule calls for a conservative estimate (not overstating potency).

CDMO daily practice: Most CDMO laboratories use kits from reagent manufacturers (such as Charles River or Lonza), and the CSE calibration data are provided on the CoA. But you must understand the principle behind it so that, during an OOS investigation, you can judge whether the reagent is the problem.

Calculating Endotoxin Limits

Original Text

An endotoxin limit specification for a compendial article is the allowable amount of endotoxin activity that can be safely contained in a parenteral product. The endotoxin limit calculation for any drug product is dependent on three variables:

  1. The route of administration, which largely defines K
  2. The dose of the product per kilogram of body weight
  3. The duration (time) of administration

Endotoxin Limit Formula

Endotoxin Limit = K / M

Where:

  • K = threshold pyrogenic dose (EU/kg)
  • M = maximum recommended dose per kg of body weight per hour

When calculating the endotoxin limit specification, body weight is defined according to the intended patient population. For example, the average adult in the United States is assumed to weigh 70 kg, whereas the average adult in Japan is assumed to weigh 60 kg.

If the pediatric dose per kilogram per hour is higher than the adult dose, the pediatric dose must be used for the calculation.

Table 2. Defined Values for K by Route of Administration

Where the product package insert describes multiple patient populations, indications, and routes of administration, it is suggested that the laboratory calculates the limit specification for each administration and chooses the most conservative one as the endotoxin limit specification.

It is important that the endotoxin limit specification, as a critical quality attribute for a new product, be calculated early in development and monitored throughout development and early-stage clinical trials.

Mentor Notes

Core Concept: What the K/M Formula Means

The logic of the endotoxin limit formula is actually quite intuitive:

  • K (numerator) - the maximum amount of endotoxin the human body can safely tolerate. It is 5 EU/kg for intravenous injection, but only 0.2 EU/kg for intrathecal injection (because cerebrospinal fluid is extremely sensitive to endotoxin)
  • M (denominator) - the maximum dose of drug given per kilogram of body weight per hour. The larger the dose, the less endotoxin is allowed per unit of drug

Core logic: The larger the dose → the larger M → the stricter the limit. The more sensitive the route of administration → the smaller K → the stricter the limit.

Worked Example of the Formula

Case: An IV-injected protein drug with a maximum adult dose of 10 mg/kg/h

K = 5 EU/kg
M = 10 mg/kg/h
Endotoxin Limit = 5 / 10 = 0.5 EU/mg

Converting to EU/mL: if the product concentration is 50 mg/mL

0.5 EU/mg × 50 mg/mL = 25 EU/mL

This means each mL of product may contain at most 25 EU of endotoxin.

Analogy

The concept of a drinking limit:

The endotoxin limit is like a "safe amount" of alcohol. K is the total amount of alcohol your body can tolerate (the heavier you are, the more you can handle). M is how "strong" the drink is (the larger the dose, the lower the alcohol content allowed per glass).

Intrathecal injection is like "drinking hard liquor on an empty stomach" - the body's tolerance for the toxin is only 1/25 that of intravenous injection (0.2 vs. 5 EU/kg), so the limit is extremely strict.

Key Points: Practical Details Every CDMO Must Know

  • Most conservative principle: With multiple indications/routes of administration, choose the strictest limit
  • Pediatric dose takes priority: If the pediatric mg/kg dose is higher than the adult dose, the pediatric dose must be used in the calculation
  • Body weight differences: The US uses 70 kg and Japan uses 60 kg - the limit for a product exported to Japan will be stricter
  • Early development: The endotoxin limit should be calculated at the start of new drug development; even if the dose has not yet been determined, calculate it using the expected highest dose

Discussion Questions

  1. A product can be given both IV and IT, with an IT dose of 0.5 mg/kg/h. Calculate the endotoxin limit for both routes and explain which one should be used.
  2. Your CDMO client manufactures an intraocular injection and is also developing an IV indication. How do you determine the product's endotoxin limit?
  3. A Japanese client and a US client commission you to manufacture the same product. Why might the Japanese endotoxin limit be stricter?
Special Limit Considerations

Original Text

Compounded Sterile Preparations

When sterile compounding pharmacies prepare therapies for injection or infusion, care must be taken to avoid the addition of endotoxins to the preparations. The compounding pharmacy should use only product contact materials that they have depyrogenated in house or that they have received as sterile and free of detectable endotoxins.

When diluents or IV solutions are used, the diluents should be commercially obtained and should meet the compendial limits, which most commonly are 0.5 EU/mL.

Active Substances and Excipients

Suppliers and drug manufacturers should perform risk assessments of these substances based on raw material origins, production methods, representative sampling and testing, and storage conditions. Materials of natural origin such as sugars, heparins, and enzymes may contain significant levels of endotoxins and/or glucans.

Consideration should be given to using a glucan blocker for products or materials that may contain glucans in addition to endotoxins.

Combination Products

A combination product is defined as a product comprised of two or more regulated components (drug/device, biologic/device, drug/biologic, or drug/device/biologic). Examples include prefilled syringes and kits with lyophilized drug + diluent + syringe.

Medical Devices

Chapter 〈161〉 assigns the endotoxin limit for medical devices as 20 EU/device except for those contacting cerebrospinal fluid (2.15 EU/device) or the anterior segment of the eye (0.2 EU/device).

Device Endotoxin Limit (Pooled Extract)

Endotoxin Limit = K / (N × V)

Example (IT devices):
= 2.15 EU/device × 10 devices / 500 mL
= 0.043 EU/mL

Mentor Notes

Core Concept: The Logic of "Apportioning" Limits

The core thinking behind endotoxin limits is "back-allocation":

  • First calculate the limit for the finished product
  • Then work backward to allocate an individual limit to each raw material/component
  • Ensure that even if every component is at the edge of its limit, the finished product still does not exceed its limit

If the same raw material is used in multiple product formulations, the strictest limit should be applied to control that raw material.

Analogy

Household budget allocation:

Imagine your monthly income is fixed (= the K value). You need to allocate this money to rent, food, transportation, and other expenses (= the endotoxin contribution of each raw material/component). You cannot let any single expense exceed its budget cap, or you will "overspend" overall (= exceed the endotoxin limit).

A prefilled syringe is like a "set meal" - the endotoxin contributions of the drug and the syringe must be counted together within the product limit.

Key Points: Glucan Interference

The importance of glucan blockers: Materials of natural origin (sugars, heparin, enzymes) may contain β-glucan, which activates the Factor G pathway in the LAL reaction and causes "false positives." A glucan blocker blocks this non-specific pathway so that the test measures only true endotoxin.

CDMO reminder: If your client's product contains naturally sourced excipients (such as sucrose or trehalose), always consider whether a glucan blocker is needed in the method suitability test.

Discussion Questions

  1. Your CDMO receives an order for a prefilled syringe product. How do you design the endotoxin testing strategy? Do the syringe and the drug solution need to be tested separately?
  2. A kit contains a lyophilized drug product + diluent + syringe. How do you combine the endotoxin of the three in the calculation?

Maximum Valid Dilution (MVD)

Original Text

As product-associated interferences are diluted, so will any endotoxins in the sample be diluted. Therefore a calculation called the MVD is included in the compendial chapter to define the upper bound of allowable product dilution.

MVD Formula

MVD = (Endotoxin Limit × Product Concentration) / λ

Where:

  • Endotoxin Limit = calculated limit for the product or device
  • Product Concentration = concentration of active ingredient (units/mL). For products dosed in mL/kg or device extracts, concentration = 1
  • λ = confirmed label claim sensitivity for gel-clot, or lowest point on the standard curve for quantitative tests

Additional information on the MVD:

  • The endotoxin limit is constant for any given formulation/dose/administration
  • The MVD is directly related to the starting concentration of active ingredient — higher concentration means higher MVD
  • The MVD is inversely related to the test method sensitivity — more sensitive test (λ lower) means higher MVD
  • The resulting calculated value is a dilution factor with no units. An MVD of 100 means maximum dilution is 1:100
  • The MVD does not limit necessary product dilution when determining the true amount of contamination in a sample that fails to meet the endotoxin limit — in that case, dilute to extinction for trending purposes

Mentor's Analysis

Core Concept: Two Sides of the MVD

The MVD formula is essentially a "balance-point calculation" - the question it answers is: "What is the most I can dilute the sample and still detect endotoxin if it sits exactly at the limit at that dilution?"

  • The benefit of dilution - it reduces the interfering effect of the product matrix
  • The drawback of dilution - it dilutes the endotoxin too, possibly below the lower limit of detection

The MVD is the critical point between the two. Diluting beyond the MVD means you may "miss" endotoxin that is truly at the limit.

Worked MVD Calculation

Case: IV protein drug, limit = 0.5 EU/mg, concentration = 50 mg/mL, lysate λ = 0.05 EU/mL

MVD = (0.5 EU/mg × 50 mg/mL) / 0.05 EU/mL
    = 25 / 0.05
    = 500

This means the sample can be diluted at most 1:500. If the product's interference is eliminated at 1:200, use 1:200 for routine testing.

Ways to increase the MVD: use a more sensitive reagent (lower λ), for example switching from 0.125 EU/mL to a kinetic method at 0.005 EU/mL.

Analogy

Diluting ink with mineral water:

Imagine a glass of water mixed with ink (= product containing interfering substances). You want to dilute it with clean water until it looks clear (= eliminating interference). But the water may also contain gold flakes you are looking for (= endotoxin), and if you dilute too much, you can no longer see the gold.

The MVD tells you: "Add this much clean water at most - any more, and even if the gold is there, you will not see it."

Key Point: The Exception for OOS Results

The MVD does not limit an OOS investigation: the source text specifically notes that when a product already fails at the MVD, you can (and should) continue diluting to the endpoint (the last positive tube in the gel-clot method, or the last valid reading in quantitative methods). Results from dilutions beyond the MVD cannot be used to release the product, but they are very valuable for trend analysis and root cause investigation.

Practice Questions

  1. For the same product, what is the MVD with the gel-clot method (λ = 0.125 EU/mL) and with the kinetic chromogenic method (λ = 0.005 EU/mL)? Which method gives you more "room for dilution"?
  2. Your client's product has an MVD of only 8 (i.e., a maximum dilution of 1:8). Interference is still severe at 1:8. What should you do?
Screening and Qualification of Consumables

Original Text

The harmonized compendial chapter on the BET requires that the laboratory screen consumable plastics for test interferences. From 〈85〉, Apparatus:

"If employing plastic apparatus, such as microplates and pipet tips for automatic pipettes, use apparatus that is shown to be free of detectable endotoxins and does not interfere in the test."

Plastics used in the performance of the test are molded at very high temperatures that will destroy bacterial endotoxins, so molded plastics are free of detectable endotoxins when released from the molds. However, recontamination is possible depending on subsequent handling.

Two examples of vendor CoA pitfalls:

  • If labeled "non-pyrogenic" — was it tested by rabbit pyrogen test, monocyte activation test, or standard 〈85〉? At what level does the vendor consider it "non-pyrogenic"?
  • If labeled "<0.5 EU/mL" — with what extraction volume? A 1-mL extraction = <0.5 EU/tube, but a 10-mL extraction = <5 EU/tube.

A default method to confirm the CoA is to treat the plastic disposable as if it were a medical device per 〈161〉.

Some materials could contain extractable or leachable substances that could inhibit the LAL-bacterial endotoxins assay. If a laboratory is considering use of disposable plastic containers for long-term storage, testing should confirm no inhibitory or enhancing factors.

Mentor's Analysis

Core Concept: The CoA Trap

The source text uses two excellent examples to show the possible gap between the CoA you assume and the CoA you actually have:

  • "Non-pyrogenic" - this label does not tell you the test method or the standard. It may have been tested with the rabbit pyrogen test (far less sensitive than LAL), or may simply reflect a high-temperature process assurance during manufacturing
  • "<0.5 EU/mL" - the extraction volume is the key! For the same result, if the extraction volume differs, the actual endotoxin amount per device can differ by 10-fold

Analogy

Interpreting a water quality report:

It is like receiving a report saying "water quality acceptable" without knowing whether they tested tap water or swimming pool water, or whether they used visual inspection or chemical analysis. The number is meaningless unless you know the full test method and conditions.

In a CDMO environment, before accepting consumables from a new supplier, proactively ask: (1) What is the test method? (2) What are the extraction conditions? (3) What are the acceptance criteria?

Key Point: Everyday Risks in a CDMO

Storage is also a risk source: even if plastic consumables are endotoxin-free when shipped, they can be re-contaminated if stored in a humid environment or if they contact materials that may contain GNB during handling.

Leachable interference: some plastic materials may release substances that inhibit the LAL reaction (leachables). Such interference is usually detected in the PPC (positive product control) - if PPC recovery is low, consider whether the consumables are the cause.

Practice Questions

  1. Your laboratory is changing its dilution tube supplier. The new supplier's CoA states "Endotoxin: <0.25 EU/mL". What key questions should you ask them?
  2. If you plan to use single-use plastic containers for long-term storage of samples awaiting testing, what additional validation is needed?
Analyst Qualification

Original Text

Training for performing any BET involves demonstration of acceptable proficiency for both sample preparation and assay method(s). Training should be divided into two parts:

  1. Classroom training delivered by an SME: understanding principles and limitations, effects of analyst technique on results. Poor or inconsistent techniques may significantly affect accuracy. Analysts must be retrained if a new test method is introduced.
  2. Competency training divided into:
    • Part A: Confirmation of assay sensitivity — for gel-clot, confirm labeled lysate sensitivity; for quantitative tests, construct a linear standard curve
    • Part B: "On the job" training — demonstrate ability to calculate endotoxin limits, prepare samples per suitability testing, and execute controls per 〈85〉

Key training points include:

  • Appropriate aseptic technique to avoid contamination
  • Use of vortex mixer is important for optimizing endotoxin distribution; but vortexing lysate is not recommended (causes bubbles)
  • Ensure open date and expiration date are marked on reagent containers
  • Do not store RSE or CSE dilutions without a hold time validation study
  • When reading gel-clot results, pick up tubes one at a time and invert 180°. Once a gel is broken, it will not re-form
  • Avoid bubble formation in microplates, tubes, or cartridges
  • Use larger volumes (mL) rather than smaller volumes (µL) to reduce variability

An analyst requires additional training if: failure in initial training, frequent invalid tests, erratic standard curve parameters, or adverse OOS/OOT trends.

Mentor's Analysis

Core Concept: Two-Track Training System

The analyst training required by USP runs two tracks in parallel, "knowing" and "doing":

  • Track 1 (knowing) - classroom training: understanding the method principles, limitations, and how technique affects results
  • Track 2 (doing) - practical competency demonstration: divided into Part A (system suitability operations) and Part B (routine testing operations)

Of particular note, if the laboratory introduces a new method (for example, switching from gel-clot to a kinetic method), all analysts must be retrained, even very experienced ones.

Analogy

A chef's knife skills and palate:

Training a BET analyst is like training a chef. Classroom training teaches the "palate" - understanding ingredients and cooking principles. Practical training tests the "knife skills" - pipetting accuracy, dilution consistency, and the feel for reading results.

A chef may have a sensitive palate (understands the principles), but uneven cutting (inconsistent technique) will affect the final dish. The same applies to BET: even if you know the method well, a 5% pipetting deviation can shift the standard curve.

Key Point: Common Mistakes

  • Reading gel-clot results: pick up and invert only one tube at a time by 180°. If you pick up several tubes and shake them together, a formed clot can break, causing a false negative - and the clot will not re-form!
  • Vortex precautions: the CSE needs adequate vortexing (follow the manufacturer's instructions), but the lysate must not be vortexed, as bubbles will affect the results
  • Volume effect: dilutions made with mL-range volumes are more accurate than those made with µL-range volumes, because pipetting error is proportionally larger at small volumes
  • Storage of standards: CSE/RSE dilutions must not be stored casually! A complete hold time validation is needed (container material, concentration, temperature, volume)

  1. An analyst in your CDMO laboratory has failed PPC recovery three times in a row. Could this be an analyst technique problem? How would you investigate?
  2. If your laboratory switches from the gel-clot method to the kinetic turbidimetric method, what should the training plan include?
Equipment, Software & Lab Conditions

Original Text

Equipment Calibration and Qualification

All instrumentation and equipment used in BET, including mechanical pipettors, water baths, heat blocks, and incubating plate readers, should be qualified using proper scientific standards and according to approved protocols. Where appropriate, URS, IQ/OQ/PQ protocols and reports should be written and approved by the quality unit. See 〈1058〉 for additional guidance.

Because BET incubation temperatures are critical, equipment such as incubating plate readers, heat blocks, and water baths should be evaluated for uniformity of heat distribution.

If a laboratory or production dry heat oven is used to depyrogenate glassware, it must be validated to ensure appropriate time/temperature exposure and load pattern (see 〈1228.1〉).

Computer Software

Computer software must be compliant with all federal regulations and standards (21 CFR Part 11 in the United States). It must allow for individual user passwords and audit trails. QC should understand how vendors have programmed their calculations — e.g., whether the software averages onset times or averages replicate results.

Laboratory Environmental Conditions

The BET can be performed in most modern laboratories under controlled conditions. Aseptic technique is important. Gowning beyond normal PPE is not required unless the product demands safety considerations. Gloves should be talc-free, as talc may contain significant levels of endotoxins.

Plate readers, water baths, and heat blocks should be on a bench away from HVAC ducts, vibration, and laboratory traffic. Sample hold times and conditions should be determined and documented.

Prior to testing, it is recommended that primary sample containers be adequately mixed before removing test aliquots.

Mentor's Analysis

Core Concept: Three Layers of BET Equipment Control

Equipment control in a BET laboratory can be divided into three layers:

  • Qualification - URS/IQ/OQ/PQ, ensuring the equipment is installed correctly, operates to specification, and produces reliable results
  • Calibration - periodic calibration (pipettors, temperature devices) at the frequency recommended by the manufacturer
  • Maintenance - preventive maintenance, especially water baths, which need their water changed at least weekly

Analogy

Temperature uniformity in an oven:

Your oven may be set to 180°C, but if the left side is 175°C and the right side is 185°C, the cake comes out undercooked on the left and burnt on the right. BET heating equipment is the same - if the temperature across the four corners of a microplate is uneven, wells at the edge and wells in the middle will react at different rates, causing deviations in the standard curve.

Key Point: Details a CDMO Can Easily Overlook

  • 21 CFR Part 11 compliance: BET software must have individual user accounts with passwords and an audit trail. Many CDMOs overlook this, especially when using older versions of BET reader software
  • Software calculation logic: QC must understand how the software calculates results - does it average the onset times first and then calculate the result, or calculate results separately and then average? The two approaches can give different answers
  • Powder-free gloves: talc can contain large amounts of endotoxin. This seems like a minor detail but can cause false positives
  • Sample holding time: after receiving a WFI or in-process intermediate sample, does it need refrigeration? How long can it be held? These all need to be validated and documented

  1. What key test items should the IQ/OQ/PQ of your BET plate reader include?
  2. Your laboratory receives a WFI sample for BET but cannot test it today. What information do you need to decide on the storage conditions?
  3. An auditor asks you: "How does your BET software calculate the results of replicate samples?" How should you answer?
Method Suitability Testing

Original Text

Interference Screening for Drug Products

Although historically suitability tests utilizing three consecutive lots of drug product were considered sufficient, it is recommended that an appropriate number of lots be determined prospectively based on the potential for lot-to-lot variability.

Products with greater variability in starting material, API, and manufacturing process will typically require more than three lots for suitability testing. The number of lots should be supported by a risk assessment including life cycle stage, known sources of variability, and material testing history.

All materials tested using methods in 〈85〉, including excipients and raw materials, should have a suitability study.

Common Test Interferences (Table 3)

A majority of test interferences are overcome by simple dilution. All BET assays must be conducted at neutral pH (6.0–8.0).

If significant product-specific interference cannot be overcome by dilution to within the MVD or by standard mitigations, the laboratory should consider a different lysate formulation or test method.

Methods Other than Dilution

For sample preparation requiring manipulation other than simple dilution (e.g., heating or ultrafiltration), the analyst must demonstrate that the treatment eliminates interference without loss of endotoxins.

Mentor's Analysis

Core Concept: Two Targets of Interference

Looking closely at Table 3, the factors that cause interference act on two kinds of targets:

  • LAL cascade (enzymatic reaction chain) - the interfering substance directly affects the LAL enzymatic cascade, causing the reaction to be inhibited or enhanced
  • LPS aggregation (aggregation state of endotoxin) - the interfering substance changes the physical state of the LPS so that LAL cannot recognize it effectively

Understanding these two targets is what allows you to choose the right mitigation strategy. For example, chelating agents affect both targets, so besides dilution, Mg2+ supplementation may also be needed.

Analogy

Interference with a sniffer dog:

The LAL reagent is like a well-trained sniffer dog (trained specifically to detect endotoxin). Interfering substances act in two ways: (1) they make the dog sneeze (LAL cascade inhibited), so it cannot sniff normally; (2) they wrap the drugs in a sealed bag (LPS aggregation), so the dog cannot smell them.

Mapped to mitigation strategies: dilution is like freshening the air so the dog's sense of smell recovers; adding Mg2+ is like opening the sealed bag so the scent can escape.

Key Point: Special Challenges of Biologics

Three lots vs. multiple lots: traditionally, a three-lot product suitability test was considered sufficient, but <1085> explicitly recommends that for products with high lot-to-lot variability, such as biologics, three lots may not be enough. A risk assessment is needed to decide the appropriate number of lots.

Glucan interference: glucan interference is common in biologics. If your CDMO handles biologic drug products, assess during method development whether a glucan blocker is needed.

pH range: although it is generally defined as 6.0–8.0, the optimal pH range may differ between lysates - follow the manufacturer's package insert.

Practice Questions

  1. Your client's product contains EDTA as an excipient. EDTA is itself a chelating agent - how would this affect BET? How would you design the method suitability test?
  2. The product still shows severe interference at the MVD, and neither dilution nor pH adjustment helps. What should you consider next?
  3. If you use heat treatment to eliminate protein interference, how do you prove that the heating "did not destroy the endotoxin in the sample"?

  • MVD formula: MVD = (Endotoxin Limit × Product Concentration) / λ - the balance point between eliminating interference and retaining detection capability
  • Consumables control: do not blindly trust the CoA - both the "non-pyrogenic" label and the "<0.5 EU/mL" label require follow-up questions about test details
  • Analyst training: two tracks (theory + practical), with retraining required whenever the method changes
  • Equipment qualification: temperature uniformity is key, and BET software must comply with 21 CFR Part 11
  • Interfering factors: most can be resolved by dilution, but biologics may need a glucan blocker, a different lysate, or a different method
  • Risk-based approach: the number of lots for suitability testing is no longer fixed at three and must be decided based on a risk assessment

Sampling

Original Text

All materials used to sample materials for endotoxin content must be inert, sterile, and free of detectable endotoxins. Even though samples are often taken in the field without a laminar flow hood, samplers must take precautions that a) they do not contaminate the sample, and b) in taking the sample, they do not contaminate the rest of the material.

Historically, the sampling scheme for finished drug products is to take at least 3 units representing the beginning, middle, and end of the batch. However, for a standard run of a small-volume parenteral, biological therapy, or large-volume parenteral, 3 units may not be a representative sample and may identify only those lots that are uniformly and highly contaminated.

Sampling schemes should be justified and based on the known variability of the process, unit operations, historical knowledge, and materials used in manufacture.

Bacteria and endotoxins are generally not homogeneously distributed in any product or material. Care must be taken to flush ports in a circulating water loop before sampling and use the same equipment for sampling that is used for manufacture.

For example, if a hose is attached to a port for transferring water from a circulating loop, the water sample must be taken from the same hose with the same flush times as used by manufacturing.

For highly viscous materials, a suitable inert rod free of detectable endotoxins may be used to mix prior to sampling. Samplers should have training on how to spot signs of non-uniformity — differences in shape, size, color, moisture, stratification, viscosity, or particulate contamination.

For a new vendor of critical materials, a site audit may be in order. It is highly suggested that in-house confirmation of the endotoxin level on the CoA be performed. Quality agreements should require manufacturers to inform of changes in processes, controls, or raw materials.

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: Representativeness of Sampling

USP challenges a deeply rooted industry habit here: "beginning, middle, end" three-vial sampling. This approach rests on two fundamental assumptions:

  • Assumption 1: Endotoxin is uniformly distributed across the batch - wrong! Endotoxin is usually not uniformly distributed in liquids and powders
  • Assumption 2: Three vials are enough to represent the whole batch - possibly not! Three-vial sampling will only catch batches that are highly and uniformly contaminated

The correct approach is to design the sampling plan based on process knowledge and risk assessment.

Analogy

Pest inspection in an orchard:

Imagine you need to check a large orchard for pests. If you only look at the first, middle and last trees, you may miss a row of trees right next to a drainage ditch (= the contamination source). A better approach is to design the inspection route according to the terrain, the water sources and the history of past infestations.

Likewise, if one filling needle on a filling line is not cleaned properly, only the vials it filled may be contaminated. Three-vial sampling may well miss exactly those vials.

Key Point: Critical Details of Water System Sampling

"Sample the way you produce" - this principle is often overlooked but extremely important. If, in production, water is drawn from a sampling point on the recirculating loop through a hose, then your BET sample must be drawn with the same hose and the same flushing time.

Why? Because the sampling point itself may be a source of endotoxin. If you sample with different equipment or a different flushing method, the result may not represent the actual condition during production.

Exercises for Reflection

  1. Your CDMO site fills a small batch of injectable product (10,000 vials) using 8 filling needles. How would you design the BET sampling plan, and why?
  2. The CoA from a key raw material supplier shows an endotoxin result of <0.5 EU/mL. As the new QC manager, how should you establish a supplier control mechanism?
Pooling

Original Text

Pooling refers to creating a composite sample preparation that includes the total contents of several individual units or equal aliquots from units taken from the same lot. Pooling is an acceptable option for batch-release testing.

However, pooling has drawbacks: it will dilute endotoxins that may be in any one unit, potentially concealing variability in endotoxin content among units.

If units are pooled, the MVD must be adjusted:

Adjusted MVD for Pooled Samples

Adjusted MVD = Original MVD / Number of units pooled

Example: MVD = 240, pooling 3 vials → Adjusted MVD = 240/3 = 80

Points to consider when pooling:

  • Pooling may obscure non-uniformity in endotoxin content — information valuable for troubleshooting
  • Aliquots should be taken using aseptic technique with individual units vigorously mixed
  • Original containers should be retained for investigation in event of OOS
  • The concept of adjusted MVD does not apply to medical devices (commonly pooled by convention)
  • Sampling must represent beginning, middle, and end of batch
  • Products with low calculated MVDs or suspensions may not be good candidates for pooling
  • Pooling is not appropriate for in-process samples representing different stages

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: The Mathematical Cost of Pooling

Pooling appears to save money and time, but it carries a hidden mathematical cost:

Suppose only one of three vials is contaminated (5 EU/mL) and the other two are clean (<0.05 EU/mL). After pooling, the contamination concentration becomes about 1.7 EU/mL - a result that could change from "out of specification" to "passing".

To compensate for this dilution effect, the MVD must be divided by the number of vials pooled. This effectively makes the endotoxin limit stricter, because your test "starting point" is more dilute.

Analogy

Quality testing of mixed juice:

Imagine you have three bottles of juice and one of them may have gone bad. If you mix all three and taste the blend, the bad taste is diluted and you may not detect it. So your "tasting standard" (= MVD) has to become more sensitive (divided by 3) to detect the problem bottle in the mixture.

A better approach may be to taste each bottle separately. It takes more time, but it pinpoints the source of the problem precisely.

Key Point: Pooling Decisions at a CDMO

  • Products with an already low MVD are not suitable for pooling: if the MVD is only 20, pooling 3 vials reduces it to ~7, which may leave no room even to overcome interference
  • Retain the original containers: if a pooled sample gives an OOS result, you need to go back and test the individual vials. If they have been discarded, the investigation reaches a dead end
  • Do not pool in-process intermediates: samples from different process stages represent different process states, and pooling them loses traceability
  • Medical device exception: pooling of devices does not require MVD adjustment, because the limit is already expressed "per device" rather than "per mL"

Exercises for Reflection

  1. Your product has an MVD of 50. If you pool 5 vials, the adjusted MVD becomes 10. Interference is still present at the 1:10 dilution. How would you handle it?
  2. Under what circumstances is pooling a reasonable choice? When would you strongly recommend testing individual units?
Calculation of Endotoxin Content

Original Text

All BET results are provided in EU/mL. If the product has a limit expressed in EU per unit of weight or activity (e.g., EU/mg), a calculation must be made to convert EU/mL to EU/mg.

Endotoxin Content Conversion

Example:
Product concentration = 10 mg/mL
Test result (adjusted for dilution) = 5 EU/mL

Endotoxin content = 5 EU/mL ÷ 10 mg/mL = 0.5 EU/mg

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: Unit Conversion

The result a BET instrument reports is always in EU/mL. But the product limit may be expressed in different units:

  • EU/mL - compare directly, no conversion needed
  • EU/mg - divide by the product concentration (mg/mL)
  • EU/unit - divide by the product potency (units/mL)
  • EU/device - multiply by the extraction volume

Do not forget to factor the dilution back in before converting!

Key Point: The Dilution Factor Trap

If your sample was diluted 1:100 and the instrument reads 0.05 EU/mL, the actual endotoxin content is 0.05 × 100 = 5 EU/mL. Many BET software packages enter the dilution factor automatically, but the analyst must confirm that the software settings are correct.

Exercises for Reflection

  1. The product concentration is 25 mg/mL, the limit is 2 EU/mg, and the gel-clot method gives an endpoint (last positive tube) at the 1:50 dilution. What is the actual result in EU/mg? Does it pass?
Standard Curve Control

Original Text

Standard curves for endpoint chromogenic assay evaluate the direct relationship between color intensity and standard concentration. The resulting curve has a positive slope, and ⟨85⟩ indicates the correlation coefficient must be ≥ 0.980.

For kinetic assays (turbidimetric or chromogenic), the standard curve plots log10 of onset/reaction time vs. log10 of concentration. This results in a negative slope, and the correlation coefficient must be ≤ −0.980.

Therefore: the linearity requirement for all compendial quantitative BET assays is |r| ≥ 0.980. The last "0" is important — it is inappropriate to have |r| = 0.979 and round up.

All standard curves have a corresponding linear equation y = mx + b where m is the slope and b is the y-intercept. Because log10 of 1 is 0, the y-intercept is at the 1 EU standard.

Accuracy of test results depends on accuracy of the standard curve. Onset times for standards from unique combinations of lysate/CSE lots should all be monitored from run to run, instrument to instrument, analyst to analyst.

An analyst can make a twofold or tenfold dilution error in standards, yet still produce a linear curve meeting |r| ≥ 0.980. The error would be evident in the onset times and y-intercept values, but not in the correlation coefficient alone.

"An analyst can make a twofold or tenfold dilution error in the dilution of the standards, yet still produce a linear standard curve that meets the requirement |r| ≥ 0.980."

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: |r| Is Not a Cure-All

This is one of the most instructive warnings in ⟨1085⟩: a passing correlation coefficient does not mean the standard curve is correct!

Why? Because r only measures "the strength of the linear relationship", not "whether the position is correct". You could dilute all the standards wrongly by a factor of two and the curve would still be a beautiful straight line (r ≥ 0.980), yet the whole curve would be shifted.

  • Over-dilution → onset time too long → curve shifts upward → sample results overestimated
  • Under-dilution → onset time too short → curve shifts downward → sample results underestimated

Analogy

A map with the wrong scale:

Imagine a map on which the relative positions of all the roads are correct (= a high r value) but the scale is labeled wrongly (= the standard concentrations are wrong). You measure the distance between two points and get a plausible-looking number, but the real distance is completely off.

This is why USP recommends monitoring onset time and y-intercept, not just the r value. Onset times are the "landmarks" on the map: they should remain consistent every time the same reagent lots are used.

Key Point: Standard Curve Monitoring at a CDMO

  • Build an onset time database: for each lysate lot × CSE lot combination, record the onset times of the standards at each concentration in every run
  • Set trend alert limits: raise an alert when onset time or y-intercept falls outside the historical range, even if the r value still passes
  • Compare across analysts: different analysts using the same reagent lots should obtain consistent onset times. If one analyst's onset times are systematically longer or shorter, it may be a technique issue
  • 0.980 cannot be rounded up: 0.979 fails, and this point is frequently cited in audits

Exercises for Reflection

  1. Your analyst reports a standard curve with r = 0.998, which looks good. But you notice that this run's y-intercept is 15% higher than the average of the last 20 runs. What might this indicate? How would you investigate?
  2. Why is a log10-transformed kinetic standard curve particularly sensitive to small changes?

Section Summary

  • Sampling strategy: "beginning, middle, end" three vials may not be enough - design the sampling plan based on process knowledge and risk assessment
  • Sampling equipment: sample with the same equipment and flushing procedure used in production, especially for water systems
  • Cost of pooling: Adjusted MVD = Original MVD / number of vials pooled. Pooling can mask contamination in a single vial and reduces the value of an investigation
  • Unit conversion: BET outputs EU/mL; convert appropriately according to the unit of the limit (EU/mg, EU/device)
  • Standard curve control: |r| ≥ 0.980 is necessary but not sufficient - onset time, slope and y-intercept must be monitored as well
  • Dilution errors in disguise: an incorrectly diluted standard can still give a passing r value yet cause systematic bias in all results

Out-of-Specification Results

Original Text

An endotoxins test result that does not meet product specifications necessitates an investigation. The suspect result is considered valid unless a comprehensive investigation clearly demonstrates otherwise.

Invalid tests are not OOS results. Invalid tests are those where system suitability parameters (negative controls, PPCs, confirmation of label claim, or generation of a linear standard curve) do not function as expected. Although invalid tests should be tracked and trended, a true OOS test failure only exists when a valid assay has been performed and generates results exceeding the specification.

Phase I: Laboratory Investigation

The FDA OOS guidance terms this a Phase I investigation. The checklist should include:

  • Testing history of the material under test
  • Sampling procedures, container integrity, sample preparation
  • Reagent preparation, storage, and use
  • Calibration and maintenance status of instrumentation
  • Appropriateness of contact equipment and disposables
  • Test procedure consistency with suitability data
  • All controls and system suitability indicators
  • Calculation accuracy (limits, MVD, contamination levels)
  • Analyst training and OOS history

Two important directions to analysts in the SOP:

  1. If an analyst realizes a mistake during testing, the test should be stopped and the reason documented at the time the error was identified
  2. Analysts should not discard the original sample, dilution tubes, or reagents until results are evaluated against specification

Phase II: Production Investigation

Assessment of all manufacturing activities that could have impacted endotoxin content. A product manufactured under a state of control should not fail endotoxins testing, so Phase II should focus on anything different about the lot in question.

Investigational tests are not re-tests and should not be used to release material. They are used to identify conditions contributing to inaccuracy.

Trainer's Analysis

Analogy

A broken thermometer vs a real fever:

An invalid test is like a broken thermometer: it reads 39°C, but you know the thermometer has a problem (= system suitability failed), so the number does not count. You simply take a new reading with a good thermometer.

An OOS is like confirming the thermometer works (= system suitability passed) and it still reads 39°C. You cannot declare the thermometer broken just because you dislike the result. You need to investigate seriously why there is a fever.

Key Point: The Golden Rules of the Phase I Checklist

  • "Don't throw anything away!": After an OOS, all original samples, dilution tubes, and reagents must be retained. They are the key evidence for the investigation
  • "Record errors in real time": If an analyst realizes during the procedure that they made a mistake, they should stop the test immediately and document the reason. An error "discovered" after the fact has very little credibility in the eyes of an auditor
  • Investigative testing ≠ retesting: Additional testing performed during Phase I/II is meant to test hypotheses. It cannot be used to overturn the original OOS result or to release the product

Questions to Consider

  1. Your analyst ran a BET. The PPC recovery was normal (80%), but the product result exceeded the limit. Is this an invalid test or an OOS? Why?
  2. Which departments should take part in a Phase II investigation? Why does <1085> recommend including a microbiologist?

Core Concept: Invalid vs OOS, Two Completely Different Paths

This is the most commonly confused concept in BET investigations:

The Gel-Clot "Dispute" Clause

Original Text

The statement in 〈85〉 that reads "In the event of doubt or dispute, the final decision is made based upon the gel-clot limit test unless otherwise indicated in the monograph" has been widely misinterpreted.

It is relevant only to the specific case of a conflicting result between a regulatory agency using the gel-clot method for lot release testing and a testing laboratory using a different LAL-bacterial endotoxins test platform.

It is not intended to allow a laboratory to use a gel-clot result that provides passing results when the laboratory's customary assay method for the product shows an OOS test result.

In the event of a product failure, the total level of endotoxins activity in the failed sample(s) should be determined. For gel-clot assays, this may involve testing of dilutions beyond the MVD until an endpoint is reached. These values may provide a clue as to the source of potential contamination, a case of mishandling of a positive control, or a tube mix-up.

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: The Misunderstood "Final Arbiter"

This may be the most seriously misused clause in <85>. Many laboratories wrongly believe that:

Incorrect understanding: "If my kinetic method result is OOS, I can switch to the gel-clot method, and if gel-clot passes, the product is acceptable."

Correct understanding: This clause applies only to one very specific scenario: FDA uses gel-clot for release confirmation testing, your laboratory uses another method, and the two results conflict. It is not an excuse for "method shopping."

Analogy

The exam appeal process:

This clause works like the appeal process for a university exam: if the student (the laboratory) and the teacher (FDA) dispute the grading of the same paper, the teacher's "answer key" (gel-clot) prevails. But that does not mean you can sit the exam with paper A (the kinetic method), fail, and then ask to retake it with paper B (gel-clot).

Key Point: Practical Actions After an OOS

Find the endpoint: After a product OOS, continue testing dilutions (beyond the MVD) until the endpoint is found. Knowing the actual endotoxin level (for example 50 EU/mL vs 500 EU/mL) is very valuable for root cause analysis: the former may point to an equipment cleaning problem, the latter to raw material contamination.

Questions to Consider

  1. Your customer insists on using the gel-clot method to "confirm" a kinetic method OOS result. How would you explain the position of <1085>?
  2. A product is OOS at the MVD. You continue diluting and find the actual level is 200 EU/mL (the limit is 5 EU/mL). How does this information help direct the investigation?
Low Endotoxin Recovery (LER) & Hold Time Studies

Original Text

The original text addresses several points related to endotoxin behavior in product matrices:

Endotoxin standards, attributed to differences in source and manner of preparation, could "react differently from native sources of endotoxins." Standards extracted by the Westphal method may not always be representative surrogates for modeling the behavior of natural endotoxins in some pharmaceutical, biopharma, or medical device extraction experiments.

Do not store RSE or CSE dilutions without a validation study that includes:

  • Vessel type and materials of manufacture
  • Concentrations of RSE or CSE to be held
  • Hold temperature
  • Volume of the dilutions to be held

Sample hold times and conditions should be determined and subsequently documented to ensure that accurate test results can be generated within the qualified time.

References cited in the chapter include key publications on endotoxin masking and hold-time studies by Bolden et al. (2017) on harmonized endotoxin recovery protocol evaluation by 14 BioPhorum companies, and Bolden et al. (2014) on evidence against a bacterial endotoxin masking effect.

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: LER (Low Endotoxin Recovery)

What is LER?

LER is the phenomenon in which, in certain product matrices (especially biologics containing components such as citrate and polysorbate), the recovery of an endotoxin spike made with purified LPS (CSE) drops significantly after a period of hold time.

The core of the controversy:

  • The "masking effect" hypothesis: Certain formulation components "mask" endotoxin so that LAL cannot detect it, even though the endotoxin is still present and biologically active
  • The opposing view: Bolden et al. (2014) presented evidence that endotoxin masking does not occur in biological drug products. The BioPhorum multi-company study (2017) explored the issue further

The position of <1085>: Cautious but not alarmist. It acknowledges that standard preparations and native endotoxin may behave differently, but it does not reject current BET methodology wholesale.

Analogy

The nail covered by paint:

The LER "masking effect" hypothesis is like a wall with a protruding nail (= endotoxin). You simulate it by driving in a nail of the same size using CSE. A few days later, paint (= formulation components) has covered the nail and you can no longer feel it (= LAL cannot detect it). But the nail is still there (= the endotoxin is still biologically active).

Skeptics respond: "The nail you drove in is a fake nail (purified LPS). It is made of a different material from a real nail (native endotoxin), so the way paint covers it is different too."

Key Point: Hold Time Study Strategy for CDMOs

Direct impact on CDMOs: If you take on biologics customers, hold time studies for bulk drug substance must include endotoxin spike recovery.

Study elements (four parameters):

  • Container material: Different materials adsorb LPS differently (glass vs plastic vs stainless steel)
  • CSE concentration: Spikes at different concentrations may show different recovery behavior
  • Hold temperature: 2-8°C vs room temperature vs -20°C
  • Hold volume: The surface area to volume ratio affects the degree of adsorption

Questions to Consider

  1. Your customer's product contains polysorbate 80 and citrate buffer. How would you design the endotoxin spike recovery study within the hold time study?
  2. If spike recovery is 95% at T=0 but drops to 40% at T=24h, how would you interpret this result? What does it mean for routine BET operations?
Alternate Test Methods & Glossary

Original Text

Alternate Test Methods

The methods listed in 〈85〉 (gel-clot limits test, gel-clot assay, kinetic chromogenic, endpoint chromogenic, kinetic turbidimetric) are considered to be validated.

A laboratory may choose to use an assay methodology not listed in 〈85〉. Key Glossary Terms

Trainer's Analysis

Core Concept: rFC, the Rising Star of Alternate Methods

Although <1085> does not mention recombinant Factor C (rFC) directly, it is currently the most closely watched alternate method. Understanding the framework for validating alternate methods is essential when evaluating rFC:

  • Advantages of rFC: It does not depend on horseshoe crab blood, which addresses animal welfare and supply chain concerns; it is not subject to glucan interference (because there is no Factor G pathway)
  • Validation requirements: You must demonstrate that it is "equivalent or better" than the USP method; regulatory approval is required
  • Current status: The European Pharmacopoeia has accepted rFC as an alternate method (Ph. Eur. 2.6.32), and USP is also evaluating it

Analogy

From horse carriage to automobile:

The LAL method is like the horse carriage: used for decades, familiar to everyone, with regulations built around it. rFC is like the automobile: technically possibly better (more specific, more stable, more environmentally friendly), but replacing the carriage requires rebuilding the roads (regulatory updates), training the drivers (analyst training), and building the fuel stations (supply chain).

We are currently at the stage where "carriages and cars coexist." You may choose to use rFC, but you must first pass a rigorous "road test" (equivalence validation).

Key Point: Alternate Method Strategy for CDMOs

  • Do not rush to switch: Unless there is a clear commercial or scientific reason (such as severe glucan interference), continuing with the LAL method is currently the safest choice
  • Watch regulatory developments: USP, Ph. Eur., and JP differ in how far they accept rFC. Your customers' target markets determine which methods you can use
  • Prepare for dual validation: If you plan to move to rFC in the future, you can start "bridging studies", testing the same batch of product with both LAL and rFC to accumulate comparative data
  • Monocyte Activation Test (MAT): Do not forget that MAT is also an alternate method option, particularly suitable for products that may contain non-endotoxin pyrogens

Key Point: Key Insights from the Glossary

CSE is not a "standard": The source text specifically emphasizes that although CSE is called "control standard endotoxin," it does not have third-party certified purity and potency the way RSE does. CSE is more accurately described as a "calibration analyte." You may be asked about this distinction during an audit.

Questions to Consider

  1. If your CDMO wants to adopt an rFC method in place of LAL, which validation steps would you need to complete under the framework of <1085>?
  2. A European customer asks you to use the rFC method, but your FDA customer requires the <85> method. How would you manage this difference?
  3. CSE has been redefined as a "calibration analyte" rather than a "standard." What impact does this have on the laboratory's quality system documentation?

Section Summary

  • Invalid ≠ OOS: A system suitability failure is an invalid test and requires a retest; a result that exceeds the limit with system suitability passed is an OOS and requires a formal investigation
  • Phase I + Phase II: An OOS investigation has two phases: first the laboratory (analytical error), then manufacturing (process deviation)
  • Do not discard evidence: After an OOS, retain all original samples, dilution tubes, and reagents. If an analyst notices a procedural error, they should document it in real time and stop the test
  • Gel-clot dispute clause: It applies only to a result dispute between a laboratory and a regulatory agency; it is not a license for "method shopping"
  • The LER phenomenon: Recovery of purified LPS falls after hold time in certain product matrices, which has an important impact on the design of hold time studies
  • Alternate methods: Non-<85> methods such as rFC must be fully validated and receive regulatory approval. LAL remains the mainstream choice today
