USP ⟨62⟩ Tests for Specified Microorganisms | p1-p3

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the positioning of USP ⟨62⟩: test methods for specified microorganisms in nonsterile products (⟨61⟩ enumerates; ⟨62⟩ detects and identifies)
  • Master the pre-test preparation steps: sample preparation, neutralization of antimicrobial activity, and confirmation that surfactants are not toxic to the test organisms
  • Understand the requirements for the source, culture conditions and maximum age of test strains
  • Master the three properties used to qualify culture media: growth promoting, inhibitory and indicative
  • Understand the purpose of the negative control and the investigation required when it fails
  • Master the logic and timing of the Suitability of the Test Method
0.1 Introduction

Original Text

The tests described hereafter will allow determination of the absence of, or limited occurrence of, specified microorganisms that may be detected under the conditions described.

The tests are designed primarily to determine whether a substance or preparation complies with an established specification for microbiological quality. When used for such purposes, follow the instructions given below, including the number of samples to be taken, and interpret the results as stated below.

Alternative microbiological procedures, including automated methods, may be used, provided that their equivalence to the Pharmacopeial method has been demonstrated.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Positioning of USP ⟨62⟩: This is a qualitative/semi-quantitative chapter whose purpose is to confirm whether specified "objectionable organisms" are present in the product. It is a sister chapter to ⟨61⟩ (microbial enumeration):

  • ⟨61⟩ answers "How many?" (Total Aerobic Microbial Count, TAMC / Total Yeast & Mold Count, TYMC)
  • ⟨62⟩ answers "Are these specific bad actors present?" (E. coli, Salmonella, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, Clostridia, C. albicans)

USP ⟨62⟩ is a mandatory chapter (numbered below 1000) and applies to all nonsterile drug products. This is not a recommendation; it is a legal requirement.

Analogy

Two checkpoints at an airport:

Think of ⟨61⟩ as the people counter at the airport entrance: it tells you how many people came into the terminal. ⟨62⟩ is like the system behind the security gate that identifies wanted criminals: it looks only for specific "dangerous persons". Even if the crowd size (the ⟨61⟩ count) is within specification, finding a single wanted criminal (a ⟨62⟩ specified microorganism) means the whole batch fails.

Key Notes

Alternative methods may be used, but equivalence must be demonstrated: The text explicitly allows alternative microbiological methods (such as rapid microbial methods, RMM), provided they are shown to be equivalent to the compendial method. This matters a great deal in a CDMO setting, where a client may ask for a specific rapid method to shorten release time.

CDMO practice: As a fill-finish contract manufacturer, your incoming material testing usually needs to cover ⟨61⟩ + ⟨62⟩. If a raw material supplier's CoA does not include ⟨62⟩ results, you may need to test the material yourself or ask the supplier to provide them.

Practice Questions

  1. Why do nonsterile products also need testing for specified microorganisms? Aren't sterile products already held to a stricter standard? (Hint: think about the Salmonella risk in oral solid dosage forms.)
  2. Your CDMO receives a raw material batch that passes the ⟨61⟩ count but shows E. coli in ⟨62⟩. How would you handle it? Reject? Accept under concession?
0.2 General Procedures

Original Text

The preparation of samples is carried out as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩.

If the product to be examined has antimicrobial activity, this is insofar as possible removed or neutralized as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩.

If surface-active substances are used for sample preparation, their absence of toxicity for microorganisms and their compatibility with any inactivators used must be demonstrated as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

⟨62⟩ shares sample preparation with ⟨61⟩: This is a smart design. You do not need to develop a completely new sample preparation method for ⟨62⟩. The ⟨61⟩ sample preparation procedures (dilution, dissolution, homogenization) apply equally to ⟨62⟩.

Three key prerequisites:

  • Sample preparation — prepare a 1:10 dilution following the ⟨61⟩ method
  • Neutralization of antimicrobial activity — if the product itself is bactericidal or bacteriostatic (for example, a formulation containing a preservative), this must be neutralized first
  • Confirmation that surfactants are nontoxic — if a surfactant is used to help dissolve the sample, you must confirm that it does not kill or inhibit the target microorganisms

Analogy

Evidence collection at a crime scene:

Imagine you are a forensic scientist looking for a specific suspect's DNA at a crime scene. First, you must make sure your collection tools do not destroy the DNA (= the surfactant must not be toxic). Second, if bleach is present at the scene (= antimicrobial ingredients), you must deal with it first, or it will degrade the evidence you are looking for. Finally, you use a standardized collection procedure (= the ⟨61⟩ sample preparation method) so that the results are reliable and reproducible.

Key Notes

Insufficient neutralization of antimicrobial activity is the most common cause of validation failure: In a CDMO setting, many topical products (creams, gels) contain preservatives (such as methylparaben or phenoxyethanol). If these are not adequately neutralized, the test gives a "false negative": it looks as if the specified microorganisms are absent when in fact the product killed them.

This is also why USP requires method suitability testing — you must show that the target microorganisms can still be detected in the presence of the product.

Practice Questions

  1. If an ophthalmic product containing 0.1% benzalkonium chloride needs ⟨62⟩ testing, how would you deal with its antimicrobial activity? List possible neutralizing agents.
  2. Why does ⟨62⟩ refer to ⟨61⟩ for sample preparation instead of defining its own method? What are the benefits and risks of this design?
0.3 Preparation of Test Strains & Negative Control

Original Text

Use standardized stable suspensions of test strains as stated below. Seed-lot culture maintenance techniques (seed-lot systems) are used so that the viable microorganisms used for inoculation are not more than five passages removed from the original master seed-lot.

Aerobic Microorganisms

Grow each of the bacterial test strains separately in containers containing Soybean–Casein Digest Broth or on Soybean–Casein Digest Agar at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours. Grow the test strain for Candida albicans separately on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar or in Sabouraud Dextrose Broth at 20° to 25° for 2 to 3 days.

Use Buffered Sodium Chloride–Peptone Solution pH 7.0 or Phosphate Buffer Solution pH 7.2 to make test suspensions. Use the suspensions within 2 hours or within 24 hours if stored at 2° to 8°.

Clostridia

Use Clostridium sporogenes such as ATCC 11437 (NBRC 14293, NCIMB 12343, CIP 100651) or ATCC 19404 (NCTC 532 or CIP 79.3). Grow the clostridial test strain under anaerobic conditions in Reinforced Medium for Clostridia at 30° to 35° for 24 to 48 hours. As an alternative to preparing and then diluting down a fresh suspension of vegetative cells of Cl. sporogenes, a stable spore suspension is used for test inoculation. The stable spore suspension may be maintained at 2° to 8° for a validated period.

Negative Control

To verify testing conditions, a negative control is performed using the chosen diluent in place of the test preparation. There must be no growth of microorganisms. A negative control is also performed when testing the products as described under Testing of Products. A failed negative control requires an investigation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Seed-lot system: This is the cornerstone of quality management in a microbiology laboratory. Just like batch control in drug manufacturing, test strains also need strict "generation control":

  • Master seed lot — the original strain, purchased from a recognized culture collection such as ATCC
  • Working seed lot — the working strain used day to day
  • No more than 5 passages — the working strain must be no more than 5 passages removed from the original seed lot, to avoid genetic drift

Suspension shelf life: 2 hours at room temperature, or 24 hours refrigerated (2-8°C). This limit ensures the viability of the strain.

Analogy

A photocopy of a photocopy:

Imagine you have an original document. Every time you photocopy it, the quality drops slightly. If you photocopy a 10th-generation copy, the text may already be blurred beyond reading. Subculturing strains works the same way: with each passage, the characteristics of the strain may change slightly. USP limits you to 5 passages so that the "suspect's photo" you test with stays clear enough that you do not identify the wrong person.

Key Notes

Negative control failure = investigation required: This is not a matter of "just repeat it". Growth in the negative control means your whole test environment may be contaminated, and the results of every test that used the same equipment, media and buffers become unreliable.

Common CDMO problems:

  • Poor strain management — passage numbers are not tracked
  • Suspensions used past their time limit — left at room temperature for more than 2 hours
  • Inadequate anaerobic incubation capability for Clostridia — no anaerobic workstation or anaerobic jars

Practice Questions

  1. Why does USP allow a spore suspension of Clostridia instead of fresh vegetative cells? What practical advantages does a spore suspension have?
  2. If your negative control shows one colony after 48 hours of incubation, what items should your investigation plan include?
  3. Why is the incubation temperature for Candida albicans (20-25°C) different from that for bacteria (30-35°C)? What microbiological principle does this reflect?
0.4 Growth Promotion, Inhibitory & Indicative Properties of Media

Original Text

Test each batch of ready-prepared medium and each batch of medium prepared either from dehydrated medium or from ingredients. Verify suitable properties of relevant media as described in Table 1.

Test for Growth-Promoting Properties, Liquid Media

Inoculate a portion of the appropriate medium with a small number (not more than 100 cfu) of the appropriate microorganism. Incubate at the specified temperature for not more than the shortest period of time specified in the test. Clearly visible growth of the microorganism comparable to that previously obtained with a previously tested and approved batch of medium occurs.

Test for Inhibitory Properties

Inoculate the appropriate medium with at least 100 cfu of the appropriate microorganism. Incubate at the specified temperature for not less than the longest period of time specified in the test. No growth of the test microorganism occurs.

Test for Indicative Properties

Perform Surface-Spread Method, inoculating each plate with a small number (not more than 100 cfu) of the appropriate microorganism. Incubate at the specified temperature for a period of time within the range specified in the test. Colonies are comparable in appearance and indication reactions to those previously obtained with a previously tested and approved batch of medium.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three properties used to qualify media: This is one of the most important foundational concepts in ⟨62⟩. Each selective medium must combine several functions:

  • Growth promoting — the target organism can grow (inoculum of ≤ 100 cfu, visible growth within the shortest incubation time)
  • Inhibitory — non-target organisms cannot grow (inoculum of ≥ 100 cfu, no growth after the longest incubation time)
  • Indicative — colonies of the target organism show distinguishing characteristics (for example, a specific color or shape)

The clever design of the inoculum levels: Note that the growth promotion test uses ≤ 100 cfu (even a small number must grow), while the inhibition test uses ≥ 100 cfu (even a large number must not grow). This ensures that the medium is sufficiently selective.

Analogy

A talent-selection interview system:

Think of each medium as a specially designed interview stage:

  • Growth promoting = even if only one or two target candidates show up, they pass the interview and perform well
  • Inhibitory = unqualified people are all screened out, even if a whole crowd of them arrives; not one gets through
  • Indicative = people who pass the interview wear a badge of a specific color, so you can recognize them at a glance

For example, MacConkey Agar: E. coli grows (growth promoting), S. aureus does not (inhibitory), and E. coli colonies show a characteristic pink color (indicative).

Key Notes

Every batch of medium must be tested: Whether the medium is ready-made or prepared in-house, every batch must undergo the tests above. This is one of the heaviest QC workloads in a GMP laboratory.

CDMO practical considerations:

  • Use commercial ready-to-use media (such as bioMerieux, BD, Merck) where possible, to reduce preparation variability
  • Keep the growth promotion test records for each medium batch, as a traceability basis for OOS/OOT investigations
  • Table 1 is one of the tables inspectors look at most often; make sure your test records correspond exactly to it

Practice Questions

  1. Why does the growth promotion test use the "shortest incubation time" while the inhibition test uses the "longest incubation time"? What would happen if it were the other way around?
  2. A batch of MacConkey Broth passes the growth promotion test but fails the inhibition test (S. aureus grew). How would you handle it? Can this batch of medium be used?
  3. In Table 1, why do some media require only "Growth promoting" while others require "Growth promoting + Indicative"? What is the difference?
0.5 Suitability of the Test Method

Original Text

For each new product to be tested perform sample preparation as described in the relevant paragraph under Testing of Products. At the time of mixing, add each test strain in the prescribed growth medium. Inoculate the test strains individually. Use a number of microorganisms equivalent to not more than 100 cfu in the inoculated test preparation.

Perform the test as described in the relevant paragraph under Testing of Products using the shortest incubation period prescribed.

The specified microorganisms must be detected with the indication reactions as described under Testing of Products.

Any antimicrobial activity of the product necessitates a modification of the test procedure (see Neutralization/Removal of Antimicrobial Activity under Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩).

For a given product, if the antimicrobial activity with respect to a microorganism for which testing is prescribed cannot be neutralized, then it is to be assumed that the inhibited microorganism will not be present in the product.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Method suitability: This is the "entry ticket" to ⟨62⟩ testing. You must show that your test method can still detect the target microorganisms in the presence of the specific product. The core logic:

  • Add ≤ 100 cfu of the target organism to the product + medium mixture
  • Carry it through the normal test procedure (using the shortest incubation time)
  • If the target organism is detected → the method is suitable
  • If it is not detected → the product has antimicrobial activity and the method must be modified

The special rule for "cannot be neutralized": If, after every effort, the product's inhibitory effect on an organism cannot be neutralized, USP allows the assumption that "the organism is unlikely to be present in the product". This is a pragmatic arrangement in practice.

Analogy

A detective's detection-capability test:

Before a detective starts a real case, you first test whether he can find a suspect at the "crime scene" (= your product environment). So you deliberately plant a known "fake suspect" (= 100 cfu of test organism) at the scene and see whether the detective can find him. If he cannot even find the fake suspect, this detective (= the test method) is not suitable for this case.

And if the scene itself would "eliminate the suspect" (= strong antimicrobial activity), the suspect could not have survived in that environment in the first place, so there is nothing to worry about.

Key Notes

When revalidation is needed: The text says "for each new product", so every new product needs method suitability testing. In addition, if the product formulation changes (for example, a different preservative) or the test conditions change (for example, a different medium supplier), revalidation is also required.

The special challenge for CDMOs: As a contract manufacturer, you take on multiple clients and many products. Every new product means another round of method suitability testing. It is advisable to build a "product grouping system": group products with similar formulations and use the "most challenging product" as the representative for validation.

Practice Questions

  1. A mouthwash containing 0.5% chlorhexidine fails to show S. aureus in the ⟨62⟩ method suitability test. What neutralization strategies could you try?
  2. If it is finally confirmed that a product's antimicrobial activity against P. aeruginosa cannot be neutralized, how should you document this in the test report? Does this conclusion require additional scientific data to support it?
  3. Why does method suitability testing use the "shortest incubation time" rather than the "longest"? Is this consistent with the logic of the growth promotion test?

Section Summary

  • USP ⟨62⟩ is a mandatory chapter (numbered below 1000) that provides qualitative/semi-quantitative testing for 6 specified microorganisms in nonsterile products
  • Sample preparation follows ⟨61⟩, including the 1:10 dilution, neutralization of antimicrobial activity, and confirmation that surfactants are not toxic
  • Strain management uses a seed-lot system with working strains of no more than 5 passages; suspensions are valid for 2 hours at room temperature or 24 hours refrigerated
  • Media qualification covers three properties: growth promoting (≤100 cfu, shortest time), inhibitory (≥100 cfu, longest time) and indicative (colony morphology comparison)
  • A negative control failure must trigger an investigation; it cannot be dealt with by simply retesting
  • Method suitability must be demonstrated for each new product; when antimicrobial activity cannot be neutralized, it may be assumed that the organism is not present in the product
  • CDMO link: incoming material testing, media QC and a multi-product method validation strategy are core challenges of day-to-day operations

1.1 Bile-Tolerant Gram-Negative Bacteria: Sample Preparation & Pre-Incubation

Original Text

Prepare a sample using a 1 in 10 dilution of not less than 1 g of the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩, but using Soybean–Casein Digest Broth as the chosen diluent, mix, and incubate at 20° to 25° for a time sufficient to resuscitate the bacteria but not sufficient to encourage multiplication of the organisms (usually 2 hours but not more than 5 hours).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

"Resuscitate but do not multiply" is the core principle of pre-incubation:

  • Why resuscitate? Microorganisms in a product may be sublethally injured, for example by preservatives or desiccation stress. If inoculated directly onto a selective medium, these injured cells may fail to grow, leading to false negatives.
  • Why not allow multiplication? If the organisms are allowed to proliferate, you can no longer judge accurately how contaminated the original sample was.
  • Time window: Typically 2 hours, and no more than 5 hours. This is an empirical value: long enough for injured cells to regain metabolic activity, but not long enough to enter the logarithmic growth phase.

Use SCDB rather than a general diluent: Note that Soybean-Casein Digest Broth (TSB) is specified as the diluent here, rather than a general buffer. This is because TSB supplies the nutrients that support resuscitation.

Analogy

Initial stabilization in the emergency room:

Imagine the injured bacteria as patients who have just been brought into the emergency room. First you give them fluids and oxygen (= nutrient medium + suitable temperature) so they stabilize and regain consciousness (= resuscitation). But you cannot let them stay so long that they start having children (= multiplication), or you will lose track of how many patients arrived originally. The 2-5 hours is this "stabilize but do not multiply" golden window.

Key Notes

20-25°C, not 30-35°C: Note that the pre-incubation temperature is in the room-temperature range, not the optimal growth temperature. This is precisely to prevent rapid multiplication.

CDMO practice: Many laboratories set a fixed 2-hour pre-incubation time, but if you are handling a particularly "harsh" product (such as a high-viscosity cream or a liquid containing preservatives), you may need to extend it to 5 hours. The key is to confirm the optimal time during method validation.

Practice Questions

  1. If you set the pre-incubation time to 6 hours (exceeding the 5-hour upper limit), what effect would it have on the test result? Would the result be biased high or low?
  2. Why does USP choose a nutrient medium (TSB) rather than a plain buffer for pre-incubation? What risks would there be if you used phosphate buffer?
1.2 Bile-Tolerant Gram-Negative Bacteria: Test for Absence

Original Text

Unless otherwise prescribed, use the volume corresponding to 1 g of the product, as prepared in Sample Preparation and Pre-Incubation, to inoculate Enterobacteria Enrichment Broth Mossel. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 24 to 48 hours. Subculture on plates of Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours.

The product complies with the test if there is no growth of colonies.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two-step selective isolation workflow:

  • Step 1 - Selective enrichment: Use Enterobacteria Enrichment Broth Mossel (EEB-Mossel). This medium contains bile salts and brilliant green, which allow bile-tolerant gram-negative bacteria to grow while inhibiting gram-positive bacteria.
  • Step 2 - Selective plate isolation: Subculture onto Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar (VRBGA). This plate contains bile salts and crystal violet dye, which further confirms selectivity.

The interpretation criterion is extremely simple: "No colonies = pass." Note that this is not a count: a single colony means failure.

Analogy

A two-gate security system:

EEB-Mossel is like the first security gate: only gram-negative bacteria carrying a "bile-tolerant" pass can get in and survive. VRBGA is the second gate, which checks identity again. If the lobby is empty after both gates (= no colonies), your product is safe. Even if just one person slips in, you must re-investigate the security system.

Key Notes

EEB-Mossel must not be autoclaved: Looking at the medium formulation (Section 2), EEB-Mossel is prepared by "heating to 100°C for 30 minutes and then cooling immediately," with no autoclaving. This is because brilliant green dye loses its selectivity under autoclave conditions. This is a common source of laboratory error.

CDMO audit focus: FDA/EMA inspectors will check whether your BTGNB test records are consistent with the product's microbial limits specification. For oral products, the limit set out in USP ⟨1111⟩ is typically BTGNB ≤ 102 cfu/g.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does the BTGNB test use bile salts as the selective component? What is the mechanism by which bile salts act on microorganisms?
  2. If several pink to red colonies appear on your VRBGA plate, what is your next action?
1.3 Bile-Tolerant Gram-Negative Bacteria: Quantitative Test

Original Text

Selection and Subculture

Inoculate suitable quantities of Enterobacteria Enrichment Broth Mossel with the preparation as directed under Sample Preparation and Pre-Incubation and/or dilutions of it containing respectively 0.1 g, 0.01 g, and 0.001 g (or 0.1 mL, 0.01 mL, and 0.001 mL) of the product to be examined. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 24 to 48 hours. Subculture each of the cultures on a plate of Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours.

Interpretation

Growth of colonies constitutes a positive result. Note the smallest quantity of the product that gives a positive result and the largest quantity that gives a negative result. Determine from Table 2 the probable number of bacteria.

Table 2. Interpretation of Results

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

MPN (Most Probable Number) logic: This quantitative test uses a simplified most probable number (MPN) method. The core idea:

  • Inoculate three different quantities of the product (0.1 g, 0.01 g, 0.001 g) into the enrichment medium
  • Based on which quantities show growth and which do not, estimate roughly how many bacteria were in the original sample
  • This is not an exact count but an order-of-magnitude estimate

Logical deduction: If 0.1 g shows growth but 0.01 g does not, the number of bacteria per gram of product is between 10 and 100. The 0.1 g portion contained enough organisms to be grown out by enrichment, but 0.01 g did not.

Analogy

Finding goldfish by dilution:

Imagine a very large pond and you want to know roughly how many goldfish are in it. You scoop water with three buckets of different sizes: a large bucket (equivalent to 0.1 g), a medium bucket (0.01 g), and a small bucket (0.001 g). If all three buckets catch goldfish, the pond has many fish (>1000 per unit). If only the large bucket catches fish, there are fewer fish (10-100 per unit). If all three buckets are empty, there are very few fish (<10 per unit).

Key Notes

When to use the Quantitative Test vs the Test for Absence:

  • Test for Absence - for products whose specification requires "not detected" (such as products for certain specific routes of administration)
  • Quantitative Test - for products whose specification has a numerical limit (such as oral products with BTGNB ≤ 102 cfu/g)

CDMO practice: Most oral solid dosage forms only need the BTGNB quantitative test (against the limits in USP ⟨1111⟩). However, some special routes of administration (such as vaginal use or topical wound application) may require complete absence. Always choose the correct test method according to the client's product specification.

Practice Questions

  1. If your quantitative test result is: 0.1 g (+), 0.01 g (+), 0.001 g (-), what is the bacterial count range according to Table 2? If the product specification is BTGNB ≤ 102 cfu/g, does this batch pass?
  2. Why does USP use three dilution levels rather than more? If you wanted to improve precision, how would you design the test?
  3. Compare the BTGNB quantitative method in ⟨62⟩ with the plate count method in ⟨61⟩. What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?
1.4 Escherichia coli: Sample Preparation & Pre-Incubation

Original Text

Prepare a sample using a 1 in 10 dilution of not less than 1 g of the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩, and use 10 mL or the quantity corresponding to 1 g or 1 mL, to inoculate a suitable amount (determined as described under Suitability of the Test Method) of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth, mix, and incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The first step of the E. coli test - non-selective enrichment:

  • Note that a non-selective medium (TSB) is used here, not a selective medium
  • The incubation temperature is 30-35°C (the general incubation temperature for aerobic bacteria), not the optimum temperature for E. coli of 37°C
  • Incubation time of 18-24 hours - allows all aerobic bacteria (including injured E. coli) to resuscitate and multiply fully

Difference from BTGNB: BTGNB pre-incubation is only to "resuscitate without multiplying" (2-5 hours), whereas the first step of the E. coli test is a full 18-24 hour enrichment. This means the E. coli test is more sensitive: even if the sample contains only a very small number of E. coli, overnight enrichment can amplify it to a detectable level.

Analogy

Gather everyone first, then screen the suspects:

The BTGNB test is like searching directly with special screening conditions (bile salt medium). The E. coli test is more refined: first invite all the suspects into a comfortable room to rest and recover overnight (non-selective enrichment), making sure no one is missed because of injury, and only then carry out the next, precise screening step.

Key Notes

Flexibility of "suitable amount": The original text says the quantity of TSB is determined by "Suitability of the Test Method." This means that if your product has antimicrobial activity, you may need to increase the TSB volume to dilute the inhibitory effect. For example, 100 mL of TSB may be used normally, but if the product is strongly inhibitory, 200 mL or more may be needed.

Practice Questions

  1. The temperature and time of E. coli pre-incubation differ from those of BTGNB pre-incubation. Why did USP design different pre-incubation strategies for these two tests?
  2. If your product contains a high concentration of preservative, how would you adjust the TSB volume in the E. coli pre-incubation step?
1.5 Escherichia coli: Selection, Subculture & Interpretation

Original Text

Selection and Subculture

Shake the container, transfer 1 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth to 100 mL of MacConkey Broth, and incubate at 42° to 44° for 24 to 48 hours. Subculture on a plate of MacConkey Agar at 30° to 35° for 18 to 72 hours.

Interpretation

Growth of colonies indicates the possible presence of E. coli. This is confirmed by identification tests.

The product complies with the test if no colonies are present or if the identification tests are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

42-44°C - the "fingerprint temperature" of E. coli: This is the most critical technical feature of the E. coli test. Most bacteria cannot grow at this high temperature, but E. coli and a few coliforms can. This temperature choice provides very high selectivity.

Integrating the three-step workflow:

  1. Non-selective enrichment (TSB, 30-35°C, 18-24 hr) - resuscitates all bacteria
  2. Selective enrichment (MacConkey Broth, 42-44°C, 24-48 hr) - selects heat-tolerant coliforms
  3. Selective plate isolation (MacConkey Agar, 30-35°C, 18-72 hr) - isolates organisms and allows observation of colony morphology

The wording "possible presence": Note that USP carefully uses the phrase "possible presence." Colonies on MacConkey Agar are not necessarily E. coli; other lactose-fermenting bacteria can also produce similar pink colonies. Confirmatory identification tests must therefore be performed.

Analogy

A three-round elimination contest:

It is like the three screening rounds of a talent show:

  • Round 1 (TSB): Let all contestants onto the stage to warm up - no one is eliminated
  • Round 2 (MacConkey Broth, 42-44°C): Turn the air conditioning up to extreme heat - only the heat-tolerant contestants survive
  • Round 3 (MacConkey Agar): Have the survivors put on special costumes (indicator reaction) - see who is wearing the E. coli uniform (pink colonies)

Finally, check ID (confirmatory identification) to make sure that whoever is wearing the pink uniform really is E. coli and not an impostor.

Key Notes

Precise control of the 42-44°C temperature is critical: The permitted range is only 2°C! Your incubator must be calibrated and qualified, and have temperature records. If the temperature is too low (such as 40°C), other non-E. coli bacteria may survive and produce false positives. If it is too high (such as 46°C), E. coli may be killed, producing false negatives.

Choosing the confirmatory identification method: USP does not specify which confirmatory identification method to use. Commonly used methods include:

  • Traditional biochemical identification (such as the IMViC reactions: Indole +, Methyl Red +, VP -, Citrate -)
  • Commercial identification systems (such as API 20E, VITEK 2)
  • Genotypic methods (such as 16S rRNA sequencing, MALDI-TOF MS)

CDMO tip: Many CDMOs now use MALDI-TOF MS as their primary microbial identification tool; it is fast (minutes) and highly accurate. This is equipment worth investing in.

Practice Questions

  1. A typical pink colony grows on MacConkey Agar, but the API 20E identification result shows it is Klebsiella pneumoniae rather than E. coli. Does this batch pass? Why?
  2. Why is the permitted incubation time range for MacConkey Agar 18-72 hours (wider than for other plates)? What practical considerations are involved?
  3. Your laboratory incubator temperature record shows that during the 48 hours of E. coli selective enrichment, the temperature fell to 38°C for a 3-hour period. How should you handle this deviation?

Section Summary

  • BTGNB pre-incubation uses TSB diluent at 20-25°C for 2-5 hours, with the aim of "resuscitating but not multiplying" injured bacteria
  • The BTGNB Test for Absence uses a two-step workflow of EEB-Mossel enrichment → VRBGA plate isolation; no colonies means pass
  • The BTGNB Quantitative Test uses an MPN method with three dilution levels (0.1/0.01/0.001 g) and estimates the order of magnitude of the count from Table 2
  • The E. coli test uses a three-step workflow: non-selective enrichment (TSB, 30-35°C) → selective enrichment (MacConkey Broth, 42-44°C) → plate isolation (MacConkey Agar)
  • 42-44°C is the fingerprint temperature of the E. coli test - the precision requirement is high, so incubator calibration is essential
  • Colonies on MacConkey Agar are only a "preliminary indication" - confirmatory identification is required before any conclusion can be drawn
  • CDMO link: Managing BTGNB limits for oral products, temperature control and equipment qualification for the E. coli test, and investment in microbial identification technology (MALDI-TOF) are core everyday QC topics

2.1 Salmonella Test

Original Text

Sample Preparation and Pre-Incubation

Prepare the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩, and use the quantity corresponding to not less than 10 g or 10 mL to inoculate a suitable amount (determined as described under Suitability of the Test Method) of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth, mix, and incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours.

Selection and Subculture

Transfer 0.1 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth to 10 mL of Rappaport Vassiliadis Salmonella Enrichment Broth, and incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours. Subculture on plates of Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate Agar. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 48 hours.

Interpretation

The possible presence of Salmonella is indicated by the growth of well-developed, red colonies, with or without black centers. This is confirmed by identification tests.

The product complies with the test if colonies of the types described are not present or if the confirmatory identification tests are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three special features of the Salmonella test:

  • Minimum test quantity of 10 g (not 1 g): This is the largest sample quantity required among all ⟨62⟩ tests. Why? Salmonella contamination in drug products is usually at very low levels, so a larger sample is needed to improve the detection rate. It also reflects the seriousness of Salmonella contamination, which poses a very high health risk to patients.
  • Rappaport Vassiliadis (RV) enrichment broth: This is one of the most elegant selective media in microbiology. It contains malachite green and a high concentration of MgCl2, creating an extremely selective environment. The pH is as low as 5.2, which prevents most enteric bacteria from growing, while Salmonella is resilient enough to survive.
  • Indicator reaction on XLD plates: Typical Salmonella colonies on XLD Agar are red (because of the pH change that follows non-fermentation of xylose). If the Salmonella produces H2S, a black precipitate (ferrous sulfide) appears in the center of the colony.

Analogy

Fishing for a rare deep-sea creature:

Salmonella is like a rare fish in the deep sea. You need a bigger net (a 10 g sample instead of 1 g) and a more specialized bait (the extreme environment of RV enrichment broth) to lure it out. Ordinary fishing gear (general enrichment media) is fine for common fish (common bacteria), but catching this rare and cunning fish takes a dedicated "fishing method". The XLD plate is then like an aquarium display tank: you can pick out the target at a glance by its red body and black spot.

Key Notes

Sterilization temperature limit for RV medium: Rappaport Vassiliadis Broth must not be sterilized above 115°C (not the usual 121°C), because malachite green degrades at high temperature and the medium loses its selectivity. A common laboratory error is to sterilize it with the standard medium cycle, which renders the medium ineffective.

Salmonella is the strictest "must be absent" organism in drug products: USP ⟨1111⟩ requires Salmonella absent in 10 g for drug products of all routes of administration. It is the only specified microorganism that must be absent from every product category.

CDMO practice: Naturally derived materials (such as plant extracts and animal-derived excipients) are a high-risk source of Salmonella contamination. We recommend 100% Salmonella testing of such materials, with no skip-lot testing.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does the Salmonella test inoculate 0.1 mL of TSB into 10 mL of RV medium (a 1:100 dilution) rather than using more? What is the scientific basis for this dilution ratio?
  2. Your XLD plate shows red colonies without black centers. Could this be Salmonella? Which other bacteria could produce a similar colony appearance?
  3. A batch of herbal capsule raw material tests positive for Salmonella. As a CDMO, what steps should your emergency response process include?
2.2 Pseudomonas aeruginosa Test

Original Text

Sample Preparation and Pre-Incubation

Prepare a sample using a 1 in 10 dilution of not less than 1 g of the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩, and use 10 mL or the quantity corresponding to 1 g or 1 mL to inoculate a suitable amount (determined as described under Suitability of the Test Method) of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth, and mix. When testing transdermal patches, filter the volume of sample corresponding to one patch of the preparation (see Transdermal Patches under Preparation of the Sample in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩) through a sterile filter membrane, and place in 100 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours.

Selection and Subculture

Subculture on a plate of Cetrimide Agar, and incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 72 hours.

Interpretation

Growth of colonies indicates the possible presence of P. aeruginosa. This is confirmed by identification tests.

The product complies with the test if colonies are not present or if the confirmatory identification tests are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The P. aeruginosa test flow is streamlined but effective:

  • Non-selective enrichment (TSB) → streak directly onto a selective plate (Cetrimide Agar)
  • Note that there is no "selective liquid enrichment" step here: the flow goes straight from TSB to a solid plate. It is simpler than the Salmonella and E. coli flows.

Selectivity principle of Cetrimide Agar: Cetrimide (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) is a quaternary ammonium cationic surfactant. It disrupts the cell membranes of most bacteria, but P. aeruginosa is naturally resistant to it. This is the source of the selectivity.

Special handling of transdermal patches: USP specifically addresses how to handle transdermal patches: they must be filtered through a sterile membrane filter. Patches are neither an ordinary solid nor a liquid dosage form, so a special extraction method is needed to detect the microorganisms in them.

Analogy

A chemical battlefield:

Cetrimide Agar is like a battlefield strewn with chemical weapons. Most soldiers (ordinary bacteria) are poisoned and fall as soon as they step onto it. P. aeruginosa, however, is like a special-forces soldier in a chemical protection suit: naturally immune to this "chemical weapon", it walks freely across the battlefield. If you see someone alive and moving around on this battlefield, it is almost certainly this special-forces soldier.

Key Notes

Importance of P. aeruginosa in the CDMO environment:

  • P. aeruginosa is one of the most common "problem microorganisms" in pharmaceutical water systems; it survives in low-nutrient environments and forms biofilms
  • For topical preparations (creams, gels, lotions) and ophthalmic preparations, P. aeruginosa is a specifically required test organism (USP ⟨1111⟩)
  • On a CDMO filling line, P. aeruginosa contamination of the purified water system can affect product batches for multiple clients

Typical features of P. aeruginosa on Cetrimide Agar: Colonies usually produce a green pigment (pyocyanin) or yellow-green fluorescein, which is more obvious under UV light.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does the P. aeruginosa test not need a "selective liquid enrichment" step as the E. coli or Salmonella tests do? Is the selectivity of Cetrimide Agar sufficient?
  2. P. aeruginosa is frequently detected in the water system monitoring of your CDMO production line. How might this affect ⟨62⟩ test results, and what corrective actions would you take?
2.3 Staphylococcus aureus Test

Original Text

Sample Preparation and Pre-Incubation

Prepare a sample using a 1 in 10 dilution of not less than 1 g of the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩, and use 10 mL or the quantity corresponding to 1 g or 1 mL to inoculate a suitable amount (determined as described under Suitability of the Test Method) of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth, and homogenize. When testing transdermal patches, filter the volume of sample corresponding to one patch of the preparation (see Transdermal Patches under Preparation of the Sample in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩) through a sterile filter membrane, and place in 100 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 24 hours.

Selection and Subculture

Subculture on a plate of Mannitol Salt Agar, and incubate at 30° to 35° for 18 to 72 hours.

Interpretation

The possible presence of S. aureus is indicated by the growth of yellow or white colonies surrounded by a yellow zone. This is confirmed by identification tests.

The product complies with the test if colonies of the types described are not present or if the confirmatory identification tests are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dual selection mechanism of Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA):

  • High-salt selectivity (7.5% NaCl): Most bacteria cannot grow at such a high salt concentration, but Staphylococci are naturally salt-tolerant. This screens out the vast majority of other bacteria.
  • Mannitol fermentation indicator: MSA contains mannitol and the phenol red indicator. S. aureus ferments mannitol to acid, turning the medium from red to yellow. You therefore see the classic reaction of "yellow colonies + yellow zone".

Note: Other staphylococci (such as S. epidermidis) can also grow on MSA (because they are salt-tolerant), but they usually do not ferment mannitol. Their colonies are white or pink with no yellow zone around them. This is the value of an indicative reaction.

Analogy

Special residents of a salt lake:

MSA is like a hypersaline lake. Ordinary freshwater fish (common bacteria) cannot survive in it at all; only special salt-tolerant fish (staphylococci) can swim there. Among these salt-tolerant fish, S. aureus has a distinctive habit: after it eats the mannitol in the lake, it dyes the water yellow (acidic metabolites). So if you see a fish in the salt lake and the water turns yellow wherever it swims, it is almost certainly S. aureus.

Key Notes

The S. aureus and P. aeruginosa test flows are almost identical: both use non-selective TSB enrichment followed by direct streaking onto a selective plate. The only difference is the plate type. The two tests can be run in parallel to save time and resources.

Special handling of transdermal patches: As with the P. aeruginosa test, the S. aureus test also specifically notes that transdermal patches must be handled by the membrane filtration method.

CDMO practice:

  • S. aureus is particularly important for topical products and transdermal products
  • Personnel hygiene is the main source of S. aureus contamination, as it is a common commensal of human skin and nasal passages
  • If Staphylococci are frequently detected in environmental monitoring (EM) of the production area, pay particular attention to personnel practices and gowning procedures

Practice Questions

  1. White colonies grow on an MSA plate but there is no yellow zone. What does this mean? Does the product pass? What further tests do you need to perform?
  2. Why is there a contradiction between the threat S. aureus poses to human health and its status as a skin commensal? What does this suggest for personnel management at a CDMO?
  3. If your MSA plate fails to fully inhibit E. coli in the inhibitory (suitability) test (where it is expected to be inhibited), what are the possible causes?
2.4 Clostridia Test

Original Text

Sample Preparation and Heat Treatment

Prepare a sample using a 1 in 10 dilution (with a minimum total volume of 20 mL) of not less than 2 g or 2 mL of the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩. Divide the sample into two portions of at least 10 mL. Heat one portion at 80° for 10 minutes, and cool rapidly. Do not heat the other portion.

Selection and Subculture

Use 10 mL or the quantity corresponding to 1 g or 1 mL of the product to be examined of both portions to inoculate suitable amounts (determined as described under Suitability of the Test Method) of Reinforced Medium for Clostridia. Incubate under anaerobic conditions at 30° to 35° for 48 hours. After incubation, make subcultures from each container on Columbia Agar, and incubate under anaerobic conditions at 30° to 35° for 48 to 72 hours.

Interpretation

The occurrence of anaerobic growth of rods (with or without endospores) giving a negative catalase reaction indicates the presence of Clostridia.

This is confirmed by identification tests. The product complies with the test if colonies of the types described are not present or if the confirmatory identification tests are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The Clostridia test is the most distinctively designed test in ⟨62⟩, with three special features:

  1. Duplicate samples (heated vs. unheated):
    • Heated portion (80°C, 10 min): Kills all vegetative cells, leaving only heat-resistant spores. If anaerobes grow from the heated portion, they are almost certainly spore-forming Clostridia.
    • Unheated portion: Detects all Clostridia, including vegetative cells.
    • Comparing the two shows whether the contamination is in spore form or vegetative form, which is important for root cause analysis.
  2. Anaerobic incubation throughout: Clostridia are strict anaerobes, and oxygen is toxic to them. All incubation steps must be performed under anaerobic conditions.
  3. Confirmation by negative catalase: This is a rapid test to distinguish Clostridia from other anaerobes. Clostridia do not produce the catalase enzyme, so adding H2O2 produces no bubbles.

Analogy

Searching for survivors at a fire scene:

Imagine you need to search a building for special "fire-resistant residents" (Clostridia spores). You split the building in two: one half is set on fire (80°C heating) and the other half is not.

  • In the half that was burned, anyone still alive must be wearing a fireproof suit (= spores)
  • In the half that was not burned, you can see all the residents, including ordinary people without fireproof suits (= vegetative cells)

You then take the survivors to a room with no oxygen (anaerobic incubation), where only true "anaerobic residents" can live. Finally, a catalase test confirms their identity, much like checking an ID card.

Key Notes

Anaerobic incubation equipment is the key bottleneck: The Clostridia test requires an anaerobic workstation or an anaerobic jar with a gas-generating pack (GasPak). Many small QC laboratories may not have this equipment and need to outsource to a qualified laboratory.

Why 2 g rather than 1 g? Because the sample is split into two portions (heated and unheated), and each portion must supply at least a 1 g equivalent of product for testing. The starting quantity is therefore at least 2 g.

CDMO practice:

  • The Clostridia test mainly applies to products for oral and rectal administration
  • Naturally derived materials (such as starch and gelatin) are a common source of Clostridia spore contamination
  • If your CDMO lacks anaerobic incubation equipment, consider setting up a contract testing arrangement with an external laboratory

Practice Questions

  1. If Clostridia grow from the heated portion but not from the unheated portion, how would you explain this result? Is this scientifically possible?
  2. Why does the Clostridia test use Columbia Agar rather than a more selective medium? What is the function of Columbia Agar?
  3. What is the principle of the catalase test? Why does it help distinguish Clostridia from other anaerobes?
2.5 Candida albicans Test

Original Text

Sample Preparation and Pre-Incubation

Prepare the product to be examined as described in Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests ⟨61⟩, and use 10 mL or the quantity corresponding to not less than 1 g or 1 mL, to inoculate 100 mL of Sabouraud Dextrose Broth, and mix. Incubate at 30° to 35° for 3 to 5 days.

Selection and Subculture

Subculture on a plate of Sabouraud Dextrose Agar, and incubate at 30° to 35° for 24 to 48 hours.

Interpretation

Growth of white colonies may indicate the presence of C. albicans. This is confirmed by identification tests.

The product complies with the test if such colonies are not present or if the confirmatory identification tests are negative.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

What is unique about the C. albicans test: incubation time is critical:

  • Enrichment incubation of 3-5 days: This is the longest enrichment time among all ⟨62⟩ tests. The reason is simple: fungi grow much more slowly than bacteria. Bacteria usually form visible colonies in 18-24 hours, whereas yeasts need several days.
  • Sabouraud Dextrose medium (SDB/SDA): The low pH (5.6) is its core feature. Most bacteria grow poorly at pH 5.6, whereas fungi favor a slightly acidic environment. The high sugar content (dextrose) also gives fungi ample carbon.
  • White colonies = preliminary indication: C. albicans forms smooth, white to cream-colored colonies on SDA. However, many other yeasts form similar colonies, so confirmatory identification is essential.

Analogy

A special track for the slow runner:

If bacteria are sprinters (dashing to the finish in 18-24 hours), C. albicans is a marathon runner who needs 3-5 days to reach the finish line. You must design a special track for this slow runner (SDB/SDA, pH 5.6): the acidic environment keeps the sprinters from crowding the lane and gives the marathon runner enough time and space to finish the race. If you blow the whistle and end the race at 24 hours, the marathon runner may still be halfway along, and you will think no runner entered (a false negative).

Key Notes

Scope of the C. albicans test: According to USP ⟨1111⟩, the C. albicans test mainly applies to vaginal preparations. Not every product needs this test, so always confirm from the route of administration whether it is required.

Confirmatory identification methods:

  • Germ tube test: Inoculate the suspect colony into serum and incubate at 37°C for 2-3 hours. C. albicans forms germ tubes, which is the most classic and fastest confirmation method.
  • CHROMagar Candida: A chromogenic medium on which C. albicans forms green colonies, while other Candida species form different colors.
  • MALDI-TOF MS / API 20C AUX: More accurate but requires more equipment.

CDMO practice: If your CDMO takes on filling of vaginal products, the C. albicans test is mandatory. During method transfer, pay particular attention to controlling incubation time, and do not shorten the enrichment period to meet the release schedule.

  1. Why is the C. albicans enrichment incubation temperature 30-35°C rather than its optimum growth temperature of 37°C? What considerations lie behind this choice?
  2. Cream-colored colonies grow on your SDA plate, but the germ tube test is negative. What does this mean? Does the product pass?
  3. Compare the enrichment incubation times of all six tests in ⟨62⟩. Why do they differ so much? What microbiological principle does this reflect?

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The design logic of USP ⟨62⟩ that can be drawn from the table:

  • Sample quantity is proportional to risk: Salmonella (10 g) > Clostridia (2 g) > others (1 g). The higher the risk, the larger the test quantity.
  • Incubation time is inversely related to growth rate: bacteria (18-48 hr) < fungi (3-5 days). The slower the growth, the longer the incubation.
  • Diverse selection mechanisms: bile salts (BTGNB), elevated temperature (E. coli), extreme pH (Salmonella via RV), chemical resistance (P. aeruginosa via Cetrimide), high salt (S. aureus via MSA), anaerobic conditions (Clostridia), low pH (C. albicans via SDB/SDA).

Key Notes

Test planning efficiency at a CDMO: Once you understand this summary table, you can maximize laboratory efficiency:

  • P. aeruginosa and S. aureus can be sampled at the same time from the TSB enrichment and streaked onto different plates
  • The non-selective enrichment step of the E. coli test (TSB, 30-35°C) can also be shared with P. aeruginosa / S. aureus
  • Clostridia and C. albicans usually run independently because they need special conditions (anaerobic / prolonged incubation)
  • The 10 g sample quantity required for Salmonella calls for separate sampling

In total, a complete set of ⟨62⟩ tests takes about 7-9 working days to finish all items (the longest path being the 3-5 day enrichment for C. albicans plus the subsequent plate incubation). This has a major impact on release time, and it is also why rapid microbiological methods (RMM) are increasingly popular in the industry.

Section Summary

  • The Salmonella test requires the largest sample quantity (10 g), uses RV enrichment broth (sterilization temperature ≤ 115°C), and the indicator reaction is red ± black-centered colonies on XLD plates
  • The P. aeruginosa test uses Cetrimide Agar (quaternary ammonium selectivity); transdermal patches require membrane filtration; it is a key indicator organism for water system monitoring
  • The S. aureus test uses Mannitol Salt Agar (7.5% NaCl + mannitol fermentation indicator); yellow colonies + yellow zone is the typical reaction; personnel hygiene is the main source of contamination
  • The Clostridia test has the most distinctive design, with three features: duplicate samples (heated/unheated), anaerobic incubation throughout, and confirmation by negative catalase
  • The C. albicans test has the longest enrichment time (3-5 days), uses low-pH Sabouraud medium, and mainly applies to vaginal products
  • A complete set of ⟨62⟩ tests takes about 7-9 working days; C. albicans incubation is the critical path, and rapid microbiological methods (RMM) can significantly shorten the time
  • CDMO linkage: determining the required tests by route of administration, parallelizing the tests, equipment investment (anaerobic incubation, MALDI-TOF), and water system control are the core of operations
