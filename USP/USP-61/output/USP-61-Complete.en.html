USP 〈61〉 Microbiological Examination of Nonsterile Products: Microbial Enumeration Tests | p1–p2

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the scope of USP <61>: microbial enumeration tests for nonsterile products
  • Distinguish a "mandatory chapter" (general chapter number below 1000) from an "informational chapter" (number 1000 or above)
  • Get an overview of the three enumeration methods (membrane filtration, plate count, MPN) and the logic for choosing among them
  • Understand the core requirements of the general procedures: avoiding extrinsic contamination, removing/neutralizing antimicrobial activity, and surfactant compatibility
  • Become familiar with the growth promotion test and the basic framework for suitability of the counting method
0.1 Introduction

Original Text

The tests described hereafter will allow quantitative enumeration of mesophilic bacteria and fungi that may grow under aerobic conditions.

The tests are designed primarily to determine whether a substance or preparation complies with an established specification for microbiological quality. When used for such purposes, follow the instructions given below, including the number of samples to be taken, and interpret the results as stated below.

The methods are not applicable to products containing viable microorganisms as active ingredients.

Alternative microbiological procedures, including automated methods, may be used, provided that their equivalence to the Pharmacopeial method has been demonstrated.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Positioning of USP <61>: This is a mandatory chapter (general chapter number below 1000), which means it must be followed strictly whenever a product monograph refers to it. This differs from informational chapters numbered 1000 or above (such as <1111> and <1116>), which only offer guidance.

Mesophilic: Refers to microorganisms that grow best in the range of 20–45°C. The incubation conditions of USP <61> (30–35°C for bacteria, 20–25°C for yeasts and molds) target exactly this group. The method will not detect thermophiles or psychrophiles.

Aerobic Conditions: These methods count only microorganisms that grow in the presence of oxygen. Anaerobes are outside the scope of this chapter.

Analogy

The standard for a restaurant hygiene inspection:

Think of USP <61> as the health department's standard hygiene checklist for restaurants. It is not meant to find every bug in the restaurant (that would take more specialized testing); it uses a standardized method to check for the common problems that can be found under "ordinary conditions." If the restaurant passes, its hygiene is within an acceptable range, but that does not mean there are no problems at all.

In the same way, USP <61> uses defined incubation conditions to detect "common mesophilic aerobic microorganisms," not every microorganism that might be present.

Key Notes

Not applicable to live-organism products: The original text states explicitly that products containing "live microorganisms as active ingredients" are not covered by these methods. This includes probiotic preparations, live vaccines and similar products. Because such products contain large numbers of live organisms by design, a standard counting method cannot distinguish the "product organisms" from "contaminants."

Openness to alternative methods: USP allows alternative methods (including rapid microbiological methods, RMM), provided they are shown to be equivalent to the pharmacopeial method. For a CDMO this is a significant opportunity to upgrade technology; for example, real-time microbial detection can shorten release times substantially.

CDMO Industry Application

CDMO scenario: As a contract manufacturer, USP <61> affects the following operations:

  • Incoming raw material testing — Nonsterile raw materials (such as excipients and some APIs) are tested for bioburden according to this chapter
  • Packaging material testing — Bioburden testing of packaging materials such as rubber stoppers and glass vials before sterilization
  • In-process monitoring — Bioburden data on the bulk solution before sterilizing filtration serve as a process control indicator
  • Supporting environmental monitoring — Counting of aerobic bacteria and yeasts/molds in environmental samples

Practice Questions

  1. Why does USP <61> choose "mesophilic aerobic" conditions as the standard test conditions? If a raw material might be contaminated with anaerobes, what additional testing would you need to do?
  2. If your CDMO wants to introduce a rapid microbiological method to replace the traditional plate count method, which validation steps must you complete according to USP <61>?
0.2 General Procedures

Original Text

Carry out the determination under conditions designed to avoid extrinsic microbial contamination of the product to be examined. The precautions taken to avoid contamination must be such that they do not affect any microorganisms that are to be revealed in the test.

If the product to be examined has antimicrobial activity, this is, insofar as possible, removed or neutralized. If inactivators are used for this purpose, their efficacy and their absence of toxicity for microorganisms must be demonstrated.

If surface-active substances are used for sample preparation, their absence of toxicity for microorganisms and their compatibility with any inactivators used must be demonstrated.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three principles of the procedure: This short passage condenses the three core principles of microbiology laboratory work:

  • Principle 1: Aseptic technique — Avoid extrinsic contamination. Work in a biological safety cabinet (BSC) or laminar flow workstation so that the microorganisms you detect come from the product itself and not from the handling process.
  • Principle 2: Neutralize antimicrobial activity — Many drug products contain preservatives (such as parabens), antimicrobial agents or other inhibitory components. If this activity is not neutralized, microorganisms are killed or inhibited during the test, leading to false negative results.
  • Principle 3: Surfactant safety — A surfactant used to help disperse the sample (such as polysorbate 80) must itself be non-toxic to microorganisms and must be compatible with the neutralizing agent.

Analogy

Rules of crime scene forensics:

Microbial enumeration is like a forensic investigation of a crime scene. You need to make sure that you:

  • Do not introduce new fingerprints (avoid extrinsic contamination) — investigators wear gloves and protective suits
  • Do not destroy existing evidence (neutralize antimicrobial activity) — if a chemical at the scene would degrade fingerprints, you must neutralize it first
  • Use collection tools that do not interfere with the evidence (non-toxic surfactant) — your collection swab must not contain substances that destroy DNA

If any one of these steps goes wrong, the "findings" of the investigation are unreliable.

Key Notes

The twofold demonstration of "must be shown": Note that the requirement for the neutralizing agent is twofold. You must demonstrate not only that it effectively neutralizes the antimicrobial activity (efficacy) but also that it is non-toxic to microorganisms (absence of toxicity). In other words, the neutralizing agent itself must not kill or inhibit microorganisms.

Example: If you use lecithin to neutralize the parabens in a product, you need to verify both that (1) lecithin effectively neutralizes the antimicrobial activity of the parabens, and (2) lecithin itself does not inhibit or kill the test microorganisms.

Practice Questions

  1. In a CDMO setting, which common pharmaceutical excipients have antimicrobial activity and need to be neutralized before USP <61> testing?
  2. If a product contains a high concentration of preservative and you cannot find an effective neutralization method, what does USP <61> allow you to do? (Hint: see the dilution approach later on.)
0.3 Enumeration Methods Overview

Original Text

Use the Membrane Filtration method or one of the Plate-Count Methods, as directed. The Most-Probable-Number (MPN) Method is generally the least accurate method for microbial counts; however, for certain product groups with very low bioburden, it may be the most appropriate method.

The choice of a method is based on factors such as the nature of the product and the required limit of microorganisms. The method chosen must allow testing of a sufficient sample size to judge compliance with the specification. The suitability of the chosen method must be established.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Three ways of "counting heads":

Imagine you need to count the people in a large square:

  • Membrane filtration = everyone passes through a gate that stops each person and counts them one by one — the most precise
  • Plate count = spread the crowd across several small areas, count each area and add up the totals — fairly precise
  • MPN method = split the crowd into several groups, record only "someone present / nobody present," and use a statistical table to estimate the total — an estimate, and less precise

Key Notes

Suitability of the method must be established: The last sentence of the original text is the key: "The suitability of the chosen method must be established." Whichever method you choose, its suitability must be demonstrated for your specific product. This is not optional; it is a mandatory requirement.

Sufficient sample amount: The method must be able to test a sample amount large enough to judge compliance with the specification. If a product's acceptance criterion is 102 cfu/g but you can only test 0.001 g of sample, the sensitivity of your method is not sufficient to judge compliance.

Practice Questions

  1. For a topical preparation containing a high concentration of alcohol (70% ethanol), which enumeration method would you choose, and why?
  2. Why does USP describe the MPN method as "the least precise" yet possibly "the most appropriate" for products with extremely low bioburden? What is the logic behind this seemingly contradictory statement?

Key Concepts: Comparison of the Three Enumeration Methods

Logic of method selection: Membrane filtration is preferred (highest precision, and inhibitory substances can be rinsed away), plate count is second choice, and the MPN method is the last resort.

0.4 Growth Promotion Test, Suitability & Negative Controls (Overview)

Original Text

General Considerations

The ability of the test to detect microorganisms in the presence of product to be tested must be established.

Suitability must be confirmed if a change in testing performance or a change in the product that may affect the outcome of the test, is introduced.

Preparation of Test Strains

Use standardized stable suspensions of test strains or prepare as stated below. Seed-lot culture maintenance techniques (seed-lot systems) are used so that the viable microorganisms used for inoculation are not more than five passages removed from the original master seed-lot. Grow each of the bacterial and fungal test strains separately as described in Table 1.

Negative Control

To verify testing conditions, a negative control is performed using the chosen diluent in place of the test preparation. There must be no growth of microorganisms. A negative control is also performed when testing the products as described under Testing of Products. A failed negative control requires an investigation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

"Growth promotion" and "method suitability" are two different things:

  • Growth Promotion Test: This tests the quality of the "culture medium itself": can this batch of medium support normal microbial growth? It is equivalent to checking that your "detector" works properly.
  • Suitability of the Counting Method: This tests the combination of "method + product": in the presence of the product, can the method you chose still detect microorganisms accurately? It is equivalent to checking that your "detector" is still reliable "under actual conditions of use."

Seed-Lot System: To keep the test strains stable and consistent, USP requires a seed-lot system, and the strains used for inoculation must not be more than five passages from the master seed-lot. Too many passages cause the strain's characteristics to drift.

Analogy

Quality control of a pregnancy test strip:

The growth promotion test is like running quality control on a pregnancy test strip before using it:

  • Growth promotion = use a known positive sample to check that the strip shows "two lines" properly — confirms that the tool itself is fine
  • Method suitability = mix the positive sample into the sample to be tested and see whether the strip detects it correctly "under real conditions of use" — confirms that components of the product do not interfere with the test result
  • Negative control = test the strip with pure water to make sure it gives no false positive — confirms that there is no contamination from the working environment

Key Notes

"Must be established" — mandatory language: Note that USP uses "must," not "should" or "may." This means that demonstrating method suitability is a mandatory requirement, not a recommendation.

When revalidation is needed: The original text identifies two situations that require suitability to be confirmed again: (1) a change in how the test is performed, and (2) a change in the product formulation that may affect the test result. In a CDMO this is especially important: method suitability must be repeated every time a new client's product is received or an existing product's formulation changes.

Negative control failure = investigation required: If microorganisms grow in the negative control, the test environment may be contaminated and all test results from the same batch are unreliable. A formal deviation investigation must be initiated.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO has just received a new client's product containing 0.1% benzalkonium chloride as a preservative. What preliminary validation work must you complete before performing USP <61> testing?
  2. Why does USP limit the test strains to no more than five passages? What problems could arise if you used a passage 8 culture of S. aureus?
  3. Your laboratory has had three consecutive negative control failures. Which areas would you examine in the investigation? (Hint: man, machine, material, method, environment.)

Section Summary

  • USP <61> is a mandatory chapter (number below 1000) that defines quantitative enumeration methods for mesophilic aerobic bacteria and molds/yeasts in nonsterile products
  • Three enumeration methods: membrane filtration (first choice), plate count (pour-plate or surface-spread), and the MPN method (last resort)
  • Three general principles: avoid extrinsic contamination, neutralize antimicrobial activity, and confirm that surfactants are non-toxic
  • Two layers of verification: the growth promotion test (verifies medium quality) plus method suitability (verifies the method's ability to detect microorganisms in the presence of the product)
  • Negative controls are a required quality assurance step, and a failure must trigger an investigation
  • Impact on a CDMO: incoming raw materials, packaging material testing and in-process bioburden monitoring must all follow this chapter

1.1 Growth Promotion of the Media & Table 1: Test Microorganisms

Original Text

Test each batch of ready-prepared medium and each batch of medium prepared either from dehydrated medium or from the ingredients described.

Inoculate portions/plates of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth and Soybean–Casein Digest Agar with a small number (not more than 100 cfu) of the microorganisms indicated in Table 1, using a separate portion/plate of medium for each. Inoculate plates of Sabouraud Dextrose Agar with a small number (not more than 100 cfu) of the microorganisms indicated in Table 1, using a separate plate of medium for each.

For solid media, growth obtained must not differ by a factor greater than 2 from the calculated value for a standardized inoculum. For a freshly prepared inoculum, growth of the microorganisms comparable to that previously obtained with a previously tested and approved batch of medium occurs. Liquid media are suitable if clearly visible growth of the microorganisms comparable to that previously obtained with a previously tested and approved batch of medium occurs.

Table 1. Preparation and Use of Test Microorganisms

Abbreviations: SCDA = Soybean–Casein Digest Agar; SCDB = Soybean–Casein Digest Broth; SDA = Sabouraud Dextrose Agar; SDB = Sabouraud Dextrose Broth

Use Buffered Sodium Chloride–Peptone Solution pH 7.0 or Phosphate Buffer Solution pH 7.2 to make test suspensions; to suspend A. brasiliensis spores, 0.05% of polysorbate 80 may be added to the buffer. Use the suspensions within 2 hours, or within 24 hours if stored between 2° and 8°.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why these five standard test microorganisms:

  • S. aureus (Staphylococcus aureus): representative Gram-positive coccus; a common environmental contaminant and a normal skin colonizer
  • P. aeruginosa (Pseudomonas aeruginosa): representative Gram-negative rod; common in water systems; an opportunistic pathogen
  • B. subtilis (Bacillus subtilis): representative spore former; highly resistant, often used to verify sterilization/disinfection efficacy
  • C. albicans (Candida albicans): representative yeast; a normal human commensal
  • A. brasiliensis (Aspergillus brasiliensis): representative mold; spore-forming and common in the environment

Together, these five cover Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, spore formers, yeasts and molds, which represent the most common groups of microorganisms.

Analogy

The "factor of 2" rule is like a quality control tolerance:

If you inoculate 80 cfu onto the medium, you expect 80 colonies. USP requires that the actual count not deviate by more than a factor of 2, so the count must fall within 40–160 (80 ÷ 2 = 40, 80 × 2 = 160).

This is like the tolerance concept in manufacturing: a part's dimensions may vary somewhat, but not beyond the specified range. For a medium, the "dimension" is its ability to support microbial growth.

Calculation: Factor of 2 Rule

Growth promotion criterion for solid media:

Inoculum = N cfu (standardized inoculum suspension)
Actual count = C cfu

Acceptance criterion: N/2 ≤ C ≤ N×2

Example: inoculum of 50 cfu
  Acceptable range = 25 to 100 cfu
  Count = 45 cfu → pass
  Count = 120 cfu → fail (more than 2 times the inoculum)

Key Notes

Special arrangement for TYMC media: note that the three bacteria (S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, B. subtilis) are not used for growth promotion or suitability testing for TYMC. This is because TYMC uses SDA incubated at 20–25°C, which targets mainly molds and yeasts.

The MPN method is not applicable to molds: Table 1 marks the MPN method as "not applicable" for C. albicans and A. brasiliensis. The growth pattern of molds (spreading hyphae) makes the growth/no-growth reading used in the MPN method highly unreliable.

Holding time of microbial suspensions: use within 2 hours at room temperature; storage at 2–8°C extends this to 24 hours. Beyond that, the microbial count may decline and affect test accuracy.

1.2 Preparation of the Sample

Original Text

The method for sample preparation depends on the physical characteristics of the product to be tested. If none of the procedures described below can be demonstrated to be satisfactory, a suitable alternative procedure must be developed.

Water-Soluble Products — Dissolve or dilute (usually a 1 in 10 dilution is prepared) the product to be examined in Buffered Sodium Chloride–Peptone Solution pH 7.0, Phosphate Buffer Solution pH 7.2, or Soybean–Casein Digest Broth. If necessary, adjust to a pH of 6 to 8.

Nonfatty Products Insoluble in Water — Suspend the product to be examined (usually a 1 in 10 dilution is prepared) in the same diluents. A surface-active agent such as 1 g per L of polysorbate 80 may be added to assist the suspension of poorly wettable substances.

Fatty Products — Dissolve in isopropyl myristate sterilized by filtration, or mix with the minimum necessary quantity of sterile polysorbate 80 or another noninhibitory sterile surface-active reagent heated, if necessary, to not more than 40° or, in exceptional cases, to not more than 45°. Add a sufficient quantity of the prewarmed chosen diluent to make a 1 in 10 dilution.

Fluids or Solids in Aerosol Form — Aseptically transfer the product into a membrane filter apparatus or a sterile container for further sampling. Use either the total contents or a defined number of metered doses from each of the containers tested.

Transdermal Patches — Remove the protective cover sheets ("release liners") of the transdermal patches and place them, adhesive side upwards, on sterile glass or plastic trays. Cover the adhesive surface with a suitable sterile porous material (e.g., sterile gauze) to prevent the patches from sticking together, and transfer the patches to a suitable volume of the chosen diluent containing inactivators such as polysorbate 80 and/or lecithin. Shake the preparation vigorously for at least 30 minutes.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Like choosing the right tube for a blood draw:

Sample preparation is like drawing blood at a hospital: different tests need different colored vacuum tubes (different anticoagulants/additives). Use the wrong tube and the result is unreliable. Likewise, a fatty product will not dissolve in an aqueous diluent, so you need a "special tube" (isopropyl myristate or polysorbate 80).

Key Notes

A 1 in 10 dilution is the default: the original text repeatedly says "usually a 1 in 10 dilution". This means 1 g (or 1 mL) of product dissolved/suspended in 9 mL of diluent. This dilution is the starting point for method suitability; if a 1:10 dilution is still antimicrobial, further dilution is needed.

Why the temperature limit: when fatty products are handled, the temperature is limited to 40°C (45°C in exceptional cases) to avoid high temperatures killing the microorganisms originally present in the product, which would lead to false-negative results.

Industry Application (CDMO)

Product types a CDMO commonly handles:

  • Injectable raw materials (water-soluble): direct dilution method, the simplest
  • Lyophilized powders (water-soluble): dissolve, then dilute; note that some excipients may be antimicrobial
  • Cream/ointment bases (fatty): emulsification is needed, which is more time-consuming
  • Packaging materials (e.g., rubber stoppers) (water-insoluble): immersion and shaking to extract microorganisms

1.3 Neutralization/Removal of Antimicrobial Activity & Table 2

Original Text

The number of microorganisms recovered from the prepared sample diluted as described in Inoculation and Dilution and incubated following the procedure described in Recovery of Microorganisms in the Presence of Product, is compared to the number of microorganisms recovered from the control preparation.

If growth is inhibited (reduction by a factor greater than 2), then modify the procedure for the particular enumeration test to ensure the validity of the results. Modification of the procedure may include, for example:

  1. An increase in the volume of the diluent or culture medium;
  2. Incorporation of a specific or general neutralizing agent into the diluent;
  3. Membrane filtration; or
  4. A combination of the above measures.

Table 2. Common Neutralizing Agents/Methods for Interfering Substances

If no suitable neutralizing method can be found, it can be assumed that the failure to isolate the inoculated organism is attributable to the microbicidal activity of the product. This information serves to indicate that the article is not likely to be contaminated with the given species of the microorganism.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Four-Step Neutralization Strategy

Escalation strategy for antimicrobial products:

  1. Step 1: Increase the dilution - the simplest approach; dilute the product to a concentration at which the antimicrobial activity is reduced
  2. Step 2: Add a neutralizing agent - use the specific chemicals in Table 2 to neutralize the antimicrobial components
  3. Step 3: Membrane filtration - retain the microorganisms on the filter, then rinse away the antimicrobial substances with a large volume of diluent
  4. Step 4: Combination strategy - a combination of the above methods

The principle is to start with the simplest method and escalate step by step until an effective neutralization scheme is found.

Analogy

The concept of detoxification:

Table 2 is like an "antidote chart". The antimicrobial components in a product are the "toxins" that kill or inhibit microorganisms (so you cannot count them correctly). The neutralizing agents are the "antidotes", and each toxin has its matching antidote:

  • Parabens (preservatives) are neutralized by lecithin, like snake venom neutralized by antivenom
  • QACs (disinfectants) are neutralized by polysorbate: the surfactant envelops the QAC molecules and inactivates them
  • Alcohol is neutralized by dilution: the most intuitive method, dilute to a safe concentration

Key Notes

The "factor of 2" appears again: USP uses the same criterion. If the microbial recovery from the product is lower than the control by more than a factor of 2 (that is, recovery <50%), antimicrobial activity is considered present and the method must be modified.

What to do if no neutralization method can be found: if no method can eliminate the antimicrobial activity, USP allows the assumption that the product is bactericidal against the given microorganism and is therefore unlikely to be contaminated by that species. Testing must still be performed at the highest practicable dilution.

A "neutralizer kit" kept in the lab: most microbiology laboratories prepare in advance a general-purpose neutralizing diluent containing lecithin + polysorbate 80 (such as Dey-Engley Neutralizing Broth), which neutralizes most common preservatives.

1.4 Recovery of Microorganisms in the Presence of Product

Original Text

For each of the microorganisms listed, separate tests are performed. Only microorganisms of the added test strain are counted.

Membrane Filtration

Use membrane filters having a nominal pore size not greater than 0.45 µm. The type of filter material is chosen in such a way that the bacteria-retaining efficiency is not affected by the components of the sample to be investigated. For each of the microorganisms listed, one membrane filter is used.

Transfer a suitable quantity of the sample (preferably representing 1 g of the product, or less if large numbers of cfu are expected) to the membrane filter, filter immediately, and rinse the membrane filter with an appropriate volume of diluent.

For the determination of total aerobic microbial count (TAMC), transfer the membrane filter to the surface of the Soybean–Casein Digest Agar. For the determination of total combined yeasts and molds count (TYMC), transfer the membrane to the surface of the Sabouraud Dextrose Agar. Incubate the plates as indicated in Table 1.

Plate-Count Methods

Perform plate-count methods at least in duplicate for each medium, and use the mean count of the result.

Pour-Plate Method — For Petri dishes 9 cm in diameter, add to the dish 1 mL of the sample and 15 to 20 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Agar or Sabouraud Dextrose Agar, both media maintained at not more than 45°. For each of the microorganisms listed in Table 1, at least two Petri dishes are used.

Surface-Spread Method — For Petri dishes 9 cm in diameter, add 15 to 20 mL of agar at about 45° to each Petri dish, and allow to solidify. Dry the plates. Spread a measured volume of not less than 0.1 mL of the sample over the surface of the medium.

Most-Probable-Number (MPN) Method

The precision and accuracy of the MPN Method is less than that of the Membrane Filtration method or the Plate-Count Method. Unreliable results are obtained particularly for the enumeration of molds. For these reasons, the MPN Method is reserved for the enumeration of TAMC in situations where no other method is available.

Prepare a series of at least three serial 10-fold dilutions of the product. From each level of dilution, three aliquots of 1 g or 1 mL are used to inoculate three tubes with 9 to 10 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Broth. Thus, if three levels of dilution are prepared, nine tubes are inoculated.

Incubate all tubes at 30° to 35° for not more than 3 days. From Table 3, determine the most probable number of microorganisms per g or mL.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: The Logic of the MPN Method

"Guess how many candies are in the jar":

The logic of the MPN method is indirect estimation. Imagine a jar of candies that you cannot open to count. Your approach is:

  1. Pour out 1/10 into three cups and see which cups contain candy (yes/no)
  2. Pour out 1/100 into three cups and see which cups contain candy
  3. Pour out 1/1000 into three cups and see which cups contain candy

From the pattern of "which of the 9 cups have candy and which do not", a statistical table (Table 3) gives an estimate of roughly how many candies are in the jar. This is a statistical estimation method, so naturally it is less precise than "opening the jar and counting" (membrane filtration or plate-count methods).

Key Notes

The key advantage of membrane filtration is rinsing: after the microorganisms are retained on the filter, a large volume of rinse fluid can be used to remove residual antimicrobial substances. This is the main reason it is the preferred method for testing antimicrobial products.

Pour-plate vs surface-spread:

  • Pour-plate: the sample is first added to the Petri dish, and then agar at 45°C is poured in. The microorganisms grow within the medium. Suitable for most products.
  • Surface-spread: the medium is poured first and allowed to solidify, and then the sample is spread on the surface. The microorganisms grow on the surface of the medium. Suitable when colony morphology needs to be observed.

0.45 µm, not 0.2 µm: note that the filter pore size required for microbial enumeration is ≤0.45 µm, which differs from the 0.2 µm used for sterilizing filtration. A 0.45 µm filter is sufficient to retain all bacteria and molds.

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: How to Read the MPN Table

Steps for reading the table:

  1. Record the number of tubes showing growth (out of 3) at each of the three dilutions (0.1 g/mL, 0.01 g/mL, 0.001 g/mL)
  2. This gives a combination of three numbers (e.g., 2-1-0)
  3. Find the corresponding MPN value and 95% confidence interval in Table 3

Example: the combination 2-1-0 means 2 of the 3 tubes at 0.1 g/mL show growth, 1 at 0.01 g/mL shows growth, and 0 at 0.001 g/mL show growth. The table gives MPN = 15 cfu/g (95% CI: 4–38).

Analogy: Why the MPN Is Imprecise

Estimating headcount by rolling dice:

Note the width of the 95% confidence interval: for many combinations the upper and lower limits differ by a factor of 10 or more. For example, the combination 3-0-2 has an MPN of 64, but the 95% CI is 16–181, spanning more than one log. It is like estimating how many people are in a classroom by rolling dice: you can give a rough range, but the precision is far below that of counting heads directly.

This is also why the MPN method is used only when no other method is feasible.

Key Notes

The meaning of the combination 0-0-0: when none of the nine tubes shows growth, the result is reported as <3 cfu/g (or /mL). Note that it is not reported as "0", because statistically the complete absence of microorganisms cannot be proven; one can only say it is below the detection limit.

The meaning of the combination 3-3-3: when all nine tubes show growth, the result is reported as >1100 cfu/g. This means the bioburden exceeds the measuring range of this dilution scheme, and the test must be repeated at higher dilutions.

Calculation Example: MPN

Scenario: TAMC test of a batch of excipient
Dilution scheme: 0.1 g/mL, 0.01 g/mL, 0.001 g/mL
3 tubes per dilution

Results:
  0.1 g/mL: 1 of 3 tubes with growth
  0.01 g/mL: 1 of 3 tubes with growth
  0.001 g/mL: 0 of 3 tubes with growth

Combination = 1-1-0
Table 3 → MPN = 7.4 cfu/g
95% CI = 1.3–20

Report: TAMC = 7.4 cfu/g (MPN method)

1.6 Results and Interpretation of Suitability Testing

Original Text

When verifying the suitability of the Membrane Filtration method or the Plate-Count Method, a mean count of any of the test organisms not differing by a factor greater than 2 from the value of the control defined in Inoculation and Dilution in the absence of product must be obtained.

When verifying the suitability of the MPN Method, the calculated value from the inoculum must be within 95% confidence limits of the results obtained with the control.

If the above criteria cannot be met for one or more of the organisms tested with any of the described methods, the method and test conditions that come closest to the criteria are used to test the product.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Acceptance Criteria

Two different sets of criteria:

  • Membrane filtration/plate-count methods: the recovery count for the product must not differ from that of the control by more than a factor of 2 (the factor of 2 rule), that is, recovery of 50–200%.
  • MPN method: the MPN value for the product must fall within the 95% confidence interval of the control result. Because the MPN method is inherently less precise, a wider statistical criterion is used.

Analogy

The "best effort" principle:

The last paragraph is USP's "safety net": if you have tried all available methods and none fully meets the factor of 2 criterion, you do not need to give up. Instead, choose the method that comes "closest" to the criterion. It is like an exam where no perfect answer can be found, so you choose the "best option". This reflects USP's pragmatism: having test data is better than having none.

Key Notes

Method suitability vs method validation: USP <61> requires "method suitability", not full "method validation". Suitability testing confirms that the compendial method works properly on your specific product; method validation is the full performance confirmation of a brand-new method. The former is less demanding, the latter more comprehensive.

Practice Questions

  1. In the method suitability test, the recovery of S. aureus is 45% (below 50%), but the recovery of P. aeruginosa is 85%. Does the suitability test pass overall? What is your next step?
  2. If you have used a combination of dilution, neutralizing agent and membrane filtration, but the recovery of B. subtilis is still only 35%, what can you do under USP's "best effort" principle?

Section Summary

  • Table 1 defines five standard test microorganisms: covering Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, spore formers, yeasts and molds, each with specified media and incubation conditions
  • The factor of 2 rule is the core criterion: both growth promotion and method suitability use this rule (recovery of 50–200%)
  • Sample preparation must be chosen according to the physical properties of the product; a 1:10 dilution is the default starting point
  • Table 2 provides guidance on neutralizing agents: it pairs interfering substances with the corresponding chemical neutralizers
  • The three recovery methods each have their own applications: membrane filtration (allows rinsing, the first choice), plate-count methods (simple, require duplicate plates), MPN method (statistical estimation, TAMC only)
  • Table 3 (MPN values) gives the estimated microbial count for every possible combination of the three-tube, three-dilution scheme
  • The "best effort" principle: if no method fully meets the criteria, use the method that comes closest

2.1 Amount Used for the Test

Original Text

Unless otherwise directed, use 10 g or 10 mL of the product to be examined taken with the precautions referred to above. For fluids or solids in aerosol form, sample 10 containers. For transdermal patches, sample 10 patches.

The amount to be tested may be reduced for active substances that will be formulated in the following conditions: the amount per dosage unit (e.g., tablet, capsule, injection) is less than or equal to 1 mg, or the amount per g or mL (for preparations not presented in dose units) is less than 1 mg. In these cases, the amount of sample to be tested is not less than the amount present in 10 dosage units or 10 g or 10 mL of the product.

For materials used as active substances where the sample quantity is limited or batch size is extremely small (i.e., less than 1000 mL or 1000 g), the amount tested shall be 1% of the batch unless a lesser amount is prescribed or justified and authorized.

For products where the total number of entities in a batch is less than 200 (e.g., samples used in clinical trials), the sample size may be reduced to two units, or one unit if the size is less than 100.

Select the sample(s) at random from the bulk material or from the available containers of the preparation. To obtain the required quantity, mix the contents of a sufficient number of containers to provide the sample.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The wine taster's sample volume:

The sample quantity is like the amount a wine taster needs to evaluate a barrel of wine. For an ordinary large barrel, you take enough (10 mL) for a full analysis. But for a bottle of rare vintage wine (a very small batch), you can't pour out half of it for testing, so USP allows you to use only 1% of the batch. For a small clinical trial sample (like a limited-edition collector's wine), you can only take 1-2 units.

The core principle is: the sample quantity must be sufficient to represent product quality, while also taking into account how much product is available.

Key Notes

Common scenarios in CDMO practice:

  • Raw material receipt: For bulk excipients (such as 20 kg of lactose), taking 10 g is no problem
  • High-potency API: Some biologic materials may contain only µg quantities per dosage unit, so the sample quantity must be calculated under the "10 dosage units" rule
  • Clinical trial batches: A Phase I study may produce only 50-100 injection vials, in which case the quantity can be reduced to 1-2 units

The "random sampling" requirement: The final sentence stresses that sampling must be done at random. In practice, this means you cannot sample only from the "most convenient" locations in the batch; you must make sure the samples are representative.

Industry Application

Impact of sample quantity on release testing: In commercial CDMO manufacturing, a 10 g or 10 mL sample is usually not a problem. However, note the following:

  • The sample quantity for microbial testing (10 g) is separate from the quantity for chemical testing
  • The actual sample quantity must be recorded in the batch record and retention samples must be kept
  • For a CDMO with multi-product shared equipment, the sampling strategy must be re-evaluated at every product changeover

  1. Your CDMO receives a 500 g lot of high-potency API (0.5 mg per dosage unit). Under the rules of USP <61>, how much sample should you test?
  2. Why does USP require you to "mix the contents of a sufficient number of containers"? What problems could arise if you sampled from only one container?
2.2 Examination of the Product

Original Text

Membrane Filtration

Use a filtration apparatus designed to allow the transfer of the filter to the medium. Prepare the sample using a method that has been shown to be suitable. Transfer the appropriate amount to each of two membrane filters, and filter immediately. Wash each filter following the procedure shown to be suitable.

For the determination of TAMC, transfer one of the membrane filters to the surface of Soybean–Casein Digest Agar. For the determination of TYMC, transfer the other membrane to the surface of Sabouraud Dextrose Agar. Incubate the plate of Soybean–Casein Digest Agar at 30° to 35° for 3 to 5 days and the plate of Sabouraud Dextrose Agar at 20° to 25° for 5 to 7 days.

Plate-Count Methods

Pour-Plate Method — Prepare at least two Petri dishes for each medium and each level of dilution. Incubate the plates of Soybean–Casein Digest Agar at 30° to 35° for 3 to 5 days and the plates of Sabouraud Dextrose Agar at 20° to 25° for 5 to 7 days. Select the plates corresponding to a given dilution and showing the highest number of colonies less than 250 for TAMC and 50 for TYMC. Take the arithmetic mean per culture medium of the counts, and calculate the number of cfu per g or per mL of product.

Surface-Spread Method — Prepare at least two Petri dishes for each medium and each level of dilution. For incubation and calculation of the number of cfu, proceed as directed for the Pour-Plate Method.

Most-Probable-Number Method

Incubate all tubes for 3 to 5 days at 30° to 35°. Subculture if necessary. Record for each level of dilution the number of tubes showing microbial growth. Determine the most probable number of microorganisms per g or mL from Table 3.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

"A Tale of Two Cities": two temperatures, two worlds:

TAMC and TYMC are like residents of two different climate zones:

  • TAMC (30-35°C) = residents of the warm city (mainly bacteria), who thrive in the "TSA tower" and are visible in 3-5 days
  • TYMC (20-25°C) = residents of the cool city (mainly molds/yeasts), who need 5-7 days to grow well in the "SDA apartments"

Molds and yeasts grow more slowly, so they need a lower temperature and a longer incubation time.

Key Notes

Colony count limits for the plate-count method: When choosing the dilution to count, for TAMC select the plate with the highest number of colonies less than 250, and for TYMC the plate with the highest number of colonies less than 50. These limits ensure counting accuracy, because too many colonies overlap and make counting imprecise.

TAMC <250 vs TYMC <50: Why such a big difference? Because mold colonies are much larger than bacterial colonies (a single mold colony can spread across an entire plate), so 50 mold colonies on a plate is already very crowded.

Incubation time for the MPN method: Note that the MPN incubation time for actual product testing is 3-5 days (slightly longer than the 3 days or less used in method suitability), to ensure that microorganisms still have enough time to grow under the inhibitory conditions the product may present.

Practice Questions

  1. If you use the pour-plate method for TAMC with three dilutions (1:10, 1:100, 1:1000), two plates per dilution, and the results are: 1:10 → (280, 295), 1:100 → (32, 28), 1:1000 → (3, 5). Which dilution should you use for reporting? What is the TAMC in cfu/g?
  2. Membrane filtration uses two filters, yet the plate-count method requires at least two plates. Why does membrane filtration not need "replicates"?

Core Concept: Summary of Incubation Conditions for Actual Product Testing

Two membrane filters: In actual product testing, membrane filtration requires two filters: one placed on SCDA (for TAMC) and the other on SDA (for TYMC). This differs from method suitability, where one filter is used per organism.

2.3 Interpretation of the Results

Original Text

The total aerobic microbial count (TAMC) is considered to be equal to the number of cfu found using Soybean–Casein Digest Agar; if colonies of fungi are detected on this medium, they are counted as part of TAMC.

The total combined yeasts and molds count (TYMC) is considered to be equal to the number of cfu found using Sabouraud Dextrose Agar; if colonies of bacteria are detected on this medium, they are counted as part of TYMC.

When the TYMC is expected to exceed the acceptance criterion due to the bacterial growth, Sabouraud Dextrose Agar containing antibiotics may be used.

If the count is carried out by the MPN Method, the calculated value is TAMC.

When an acceptance criterion for microbiological quality is prescribed, it is interpreted as follows:

  • 101 cfu: maximum acceptable count = 20;
  • 102 cfu: maximum acceptable count = 200;
  • 103 cfu: maximum acceptable count = 2000;
  • and so forth.

The recommended solutions and media are described in Tests for Specified Microorganisms 〈62〉.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Definitions of TAMC and TYMC

Key definitions (often misunderstood):

  • TAMC = all cfu on SCDA, including molds/yeasts that grow on SCDA. "Total Aerobic Microbial Count" covers all microorganisms that grow under these conditions, not just bacteria.
  • TYMC = all cfu on SDA; likewise, if bacteria also grow on SDA, they are counted in the TYMC.

This means: TAMC and TYMC are not mutually exclusive categories. Some microorganisms may appear on both media. Therefore, TAMC + TYMC does not equal the "total microbial count".

Calculation Note: The "Half-Log" Interpretation Rule for Acceptance Criteria

The USP acceptance criteria use a 0.5 log tolerance:

Specification    Actual limit    Calculation logic
10^1 cfu        20 cfu          10^1.3 ≈ 20 (rounded)
10^2 cfu        200 cfu         10^2.3 ≈ 200
10^3 cfu        2000 cfu        10^3.3 ≈ 2000

General formula:
  Specification = 10^n cfu
  Maximum acceptable count = 2 × 10^n

This means a specification of 102 cfu actually allows up to 200 cfu. This is a fairly large "buffer", reflecting the inherent variability of microbial enumeration methods.

Analogy

Speed limits and speeding tickets:

Microbial limit criteria are like road speed limits:

  • The sign says 102 cfu (equivalent to 100 km/h)
  • But you are not actually "speeding" until 200 cfu (like places where you are only ticketed above 120 km/h)
  • This "buffer" is not there because the regulation is lax, but because microbial counts vary naturally: testing the same sample twice can give results that differ by 30-50%

So USP allows a tolerance of roughly 0.3 log (log 20 = 1.3, which is 0.3 log above the specification's 1), which is reasonable in microbiology.

Key Notes

SDA with antibiotics: When bacteria grow heavily on SDA and make the TYMC result inaccurate (because you cannot tell which colonies are molds and which are bacteria), you can use SDA containing an antibiotic (usually chloramphenicol or chlortetracycline) to suppress bacterial growth so that only molds/yeasts grow.

Division of labor between USP <61> and <62>:

  • <61> = the "how many" question: how many microorganisms are there? (TAMC, TYMC)
  • <62> = the "which kinds" question: are specific pathogens present? (such as E. coli, Salmonella, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus)

The two are usually used together. A product may pass TAMC/TYMC (the numbers are fine) and still fail because a specified pathogen is detected.

Industry Application

Business impact of result interpretation:

  • Raw material release: If an incoming excipient has a TAMC of 150 cfu/g against an acceptance criterion of 102 cfu/g (maximum 200), it passes. But at 220 cfu/g it fails and must be rejected or handled through a deviation.
  • Trend analysis: Even when results pass, a trend that stays close to the limit must be tracked and investigated. For example, if three consecutive batches all have TAMC in the 150-180 cfu/g range, each batch passes but the trend is concerning.
  • Data integrity: The reported result must include the method, dilution, count values, calculation, and the final cfu/g or cfu/mL result.

2.4 Connection to USP <62>

Original Text

"The recommended solutions and media are described in Tests for Specified Microorganisms 〈62〉."

USP 〈61〉 references USP 〈62〉 for the descriptions of recommended solutions and media. The two chapters work together as a comprehensive microbiological quality assessment framework for nonsterile products.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The two "checkpoints" of a health check:

<61> is like measuring blood pressure: it gives you a "number" (the blood pressure reading, analogous to a microbial count). <62> is like a blood test: it tells you whether there is a "specific problem" in the blood (such as an antibody to a particular virus, analogous to a specified pathogen). You need both to get the full health picture. A product may have "normal blood pressure" (TAMC/TYMC pass) but "a virus in the blood" (a pathogen is detected) and still fail.

Key Notes

Media and reagent specifications: Although <61> mentions media such as SCDA, SDA, and SCDB in its main text, the detailed composition and preparation of these media are described in <62> and in the USP general reagents chapters. Laboratories must refer to these specifications when preparing media.

Practice Questions

  1. A raw material has TAMC = 50 cfu/g (specification 102 cfu/g) and TYMC = 10 cfu/g (specification 101 cfu/g), but the <62> test detects E. coli (specification: absence in 1 g). What is the microbial quality disposition of this material?
  2. Why does USP separate microbial enumeration (<61>) and tests for specified microorganisms (<62>) into two independent chapters instead of combining them into one?

Core Concept: <61> + <62> = Complete Microbial Quality Assessment

The USP microbial quality chapter system:

<61> and <62> are mandatory chapters (numbered below 1000), while <1111>-<1113> are informational chapters (they provide guidance but are not mandatory).

Section Summary

  • The standard sample quantity is 10 g or 10 mL, with clear reduction rules for special cases (low-content API, very small batches, clinical trials)
  • Incubation conditions for actual product testing: TAMC uses SCDA at 30-35°C for 3-5 days; TYMC uses SDA at 20-25°C for 5-7 days
  • Colony count limits for the plate-count method: TAMC <250 cfu/plate, TYMC <50 cfu/plate
  • TAMC = all cfu on SCDA (including molds); TYMC = all cfu on SDA (including bacteria)
  • Interpreting acceptance criteria: a specification of 10n cfu actually allows a maximum of 2×10n (0.3 log tolerance)
  • <61> + <62> = complete assessment: enumeration (how many) and identification (are pathogens present) are both indispensable
  • CDMO business significance: It affects raw material release, process monitoring, and batch release decisions, and trend analysis matters more than any single result
