Section 1: Purpose, Scope & Density Determination

Original Text

PURPOSE

The following tests are designed to provide assurance that oral liquids will, when transferred from the original container, deliver the volume of dosage form that is declared on the label.

SCOPE

These tests are applicable to products that are dispensed by pouring from the container. The tests apply whether the products are supplied as liquid preparations or liquid preparations that are constituted from solids upon the addition of a designated volume of a specific diluent. When the container includes a flow restrictor or orifice reducer (e.g., a coupler for an oral syringe), the volume for this test should be discharged according to the labeling advice.

These tests are not required for an article packaged in single-unit containers when the monograph includes the test for Uniformity of Dosage Units 〈905〉.

DENSITY DETERMINATION

Because of the tendency of oral liquids to entrain air when shaken or transferred, a more accurate method for determining the delivered volume is to first determine the delivered mass, and then, using the density of the material, to convert the mass to delivered volume.

The following is one method to determine density:

  1. Tare a 100-mL volumetric flask containing 50.0 mL of water.
  2. Add approximately 25 g of well-shaken product, and gently swirl the contents to mix.
  3. Reweigh the flask.
  4. From a buret, add an accurately measured amount of water to bring the flask contents to volume while gently swirling the contents of the flask. Record the volume taken from the buret.
  5. Calculate the density of the sample:

Result = W/V

W = weight of the material taken (g)
V = 50.0 minus the volume, in mL, of the water necessary to adjust the contents of the flask to volume

Other methods of determining the density may be employed depending on the formulation (e.g., substantially nonaqueous formulations).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Deliverable Volume is a basic quality attribute of oral liquids: does the amount the patient actually gets from the bottle match the labeled amount? The core questions of this test are:

  • How much liquid remains on the container wall when it is transferred out?
  • How can the volume of a viscous liquid, or one containing suspended particles or prone to entrained air, be measured accurately?

Exemption: if the product is packaged in single-unit containers and the uniformity of the individual units has been verified by the ⟨905⟩ test, the ⟨698⟩ test does not apply.

Density Determination: Formula and Calculation

Density formula:
Result = W/V

W = weight of the sample added (g)
V = 50.0 mL − volume of water added (mL)

Principle:
• Use a 100-mL volumetric flask pre-filled with 50.0 mL of water
• Add about 25 g of sample, then top up to the mark with water from a buret
• V = 50.0 − (volume of water added) = volume occupied by the sample (mL)
• Density = weight ÷ volume (g/mL)

Example: 25.3 g of sample added, 24.8 mL of water added
V = 50.0 − 24.8 = 25.2 mL
Density = 25.3 ÷ 25.2 = 1.004 g/mL

Analogy

Why convert weight to volume instead of measuring the volume directly? Imagine measuring a cup of milkshake: after shaking it is full of bubbles, so reading the scale directly is not accurate. Weighing it first and then converting (using density) is like a chef weighing ingredients, which is more precise than using a measuring cup. The density method eliminates the problem of air entrainment error.

Key Notes

For containers fitted with a flow restrictor (for example, bottles with an oral syringe adapter), the test must be performed by dispensing according to the label directions rather than pouring directly. This reflects the importance of actual use conditions. In addition, non-aqueous preparations (for example, those with an oil-based vehicle) may use other methods of density determination, because the displacement method is not suitable for immiscible liquids.

Practice Questions

  1. Why not measure the volume of an oral suspension directly with a graduated cylinder, instead of measuring density first and then converting from weight?
  2. If an oral liquid has a density of 1.20 g/mL and 60.0 g is taken, what is the deliverable volume in mL?
Section 2: Test Preparations & Procedure

Original Text

TEST PREPARATIONS

For the determination of deliverable volume, select NLT 30 containers, and proceed as follows for the dosage form designated.

Oral Solutions and Oral Suspensions: Shake the contents of 10 containers individually.

Powders That Are Labeled to State the Volume of Oral Liquid That Results When the Powder Is Constituted with the Volume of Diluent Stated in the Labeling: Constitute 10 containers with the volume of diluent stated in the labeling, accurately measured, and shake individually.

PROCEDURE

The deliverable volume can be determined by weight as follows:

  1. Discharge the container contents into a suitable tared container (allowing drainage for NMT 5 s for single-unit containers and NMT 10 min for multiple-unit containers).
  2. Determine the mass of the contents.
  3. Calculate the volume using the density.

Alternatively, the following by-volume procedure may be used:

  1. Under conditions of use or as instructed in the labeling, carefully discharge the contents of each container into separate dry graduated cylinders of a rated capacity not exceeding 2.5 times the volume to be measured and calibrated "to contain" (see Volumetric Apparatus 〈31〉). Care must be taken to avoid the formation of air bubbles during the process. In the absence of labeling instructions, support the containers at about a 30° angle to the horizontal, and gently discharge the contents into the graduated cylinder.
  2. Allow each container to drain for a period not to exceed 10 min for multiple-unit containers and 5 s for single-unit containers, unless otherwise specified in the monograph.
  3. When free from bubbles, measure the volume of each mixture.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Test Design Logic

⟨698⟩ uses a staged sampling strategy:

  • Total sample on hand: select NLT 30 containers (reserved for Stage 2)
  • Stage 1: test 10 containers
  • Stage 2 (if needed): test 20 more, for a total of 30

Comparison of the two measurement methods:

  • By weight: weigh first, then convert to volume using density; more accurate, particularly suitable for viscous or foaming liquids
  • By volume: measure directly with a graduated cylinder; more intuitive to perform, but you must ensure there are no air bubbles, and the cylinder capacity must be 1–2.5 times the volume to be measured

Analogy: Differences in Drain Time

The drain-time requirements reflect how each container type is actually used:

  • Single-unit (≤5 seconds): like a single-serve cup of yogurt, you pour it out quickly and almost all of the contents come out
  • Multiple-unit (≤10 minutes): like a jar of honey, a viscous liquid needs time to flow out completely, and 10 minutes is a reasonable upper limit to wait

The cylinder capacity is limited to no more than 2.5 times the volume so that the reading falls within the cylinder's effective range, avoiding readings in the lowest graduations (the imprecise region).

Key Notes

Graduated cylinder requirements: use a cylinder calibrated "to contain" (TC) rather than "to deliver" (TD). The two types have different calibration bases: a TC cylinder is calibrated to hold a specified volume and does not allow for wall retention. In this test we measure the volume of liquid transferred into the cylinder, so a TC-calibrated cylinder is used.

30° tilt angle: unless otherwise specified, the container is tilted at about 30° when dispensing. This simulates the typical pouring posture in normal use and keeps the test conditions consistent with actual use.

Constituted powder products: first add the exact amount of diluent as directed on the label and shake thoroughly before testing, simulating the complete procedure the patient actually follows.

Practice Questions

  1. For testing a 100-mL oral suspension, which size of graduated cylinder (maximum capacity) should be used? What is the upper limit for drain time?
  2. A pediatric oral liquid forms a lot of bubbles after shaking, and the reading is taken before the bubbles have dissipated. How does this affect the result, and how should it be handled?
Section 3: Acceptance Criteria

Original Text

ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA

Use the following criteria to determine compliance with this test.

For Multiple-Unit Containers

The average volume of liquid obtained from the 10 containers is NLT 100%, and the volume of no container is less than 95% of the volume declared in the labeling.

If (A), the average volume is less than 100% of that declared in the labeling, but the volume of no container is less than 95% of the labeled amount, or if (B), the average volume is NLT 100% and the volume of NMT 1 container is less than 95%, but is NLT 90% of the labeled volume, perform the test on 20 additional containers.

The average volume of liquid obtained from the 30 containers is NLT 100% of the volume declared in the labeling; the volume of liquid obtained from NMT 1 of the 30 containers is less than 95%, but NLT 90% of that declared in the labeling.

For Single-Unit Containers

The average volume of liquid obtained from the 10 containers is NLT 100%, and the volume of each of the 10 containers lies within the range of 95%–110% of the volume declared in the labeling.

If (A), the average volume is less than 100% of that declared in the labeling, but the volume of no container is outside the range of 95%–110%, or if (B), the average volume is NLT 100% and the volume of NMT 1 container is outside the range of 95%–110%, but within the range of 90%–115%, perform the test on 20 additional containers.

The average volume of liquid obtained from the 30 containers is NLT 100% of the volume declared in the labeling; the volume obtained from NMT 1 of the 30 containers is outside the range of 95%–110%, but within the range of 90%–115% of the volume declared on the labeling.

Tutorial Commentary

Formula and Calculation: Decision Flow

Multiple-unit containers (10 units):
Pass: average ≥100% and all ≥95%
→ Proceed to Stage 2 (A or B):
  A: average <100% but all ≥95%
  B: average ≥100% but 1 unit is 90%–95%
Fail:
  - average <100% and any unit <95% (immediate failure)
  - any unit <90% (immediate failure)

Single-unit containers (10 units):
Pass: average ≥100% and all within 95%–110%
→ Proceed to Stage 2 (A or B):
  A: average <100% but all within 95%–110%
  B: average ≥100% but 1 unit is within 90%–115%
Fail:
  - any unit outside 90%–95% or 110%–115% (immediate failure case)
  - any unit <90% or >115% (immediate failure)

Analogy: Why Does a Single-unit Container Have a 110% Upper Limit?

A multiple-unit container (such as a bottle of syrup) labeled 100 mL can contain a little extra without harm, because the patient simply takes the dose as directed. But a single-unit container (such as a prefilled syringe) labeled 5 mL that delivers 6 mL (120%) may lead the patient to inject all of it, resulting in an overdose. Single-unit containers therefore require a strictly controlled upper limit (≤110%).

The lower limit is considered from the opposite angle: if too little is delivered (<90%), the patient may actually receive an insufficient dose, compromising efficacy.

Key Notes

The core meaning of "average ≥100%": this ensures that the batch as a whole does not have a systematic underfill. If the average is below 100%, the filling process may have a problem, and an investigation is needed even if the individual differences are still within the permitted range.

Practical considerations in setting the fill volume: in production, the fill volume is usually set slightly above the labeled amount (for example, a 100-mL label with a fill target of 102 mL), so that after process variability is taken into account, the delivered volume still meets the ≥100% requirement.

Practice Questions

  1. A multiple-unit oral liquid (labeled 120 mL) is tested on 10 containers. Results: average 121 mL (≈100.8%), with 1 container at 113 mL (≈94.2%). Determine: pass, proceed to Stage 2, or fail outright?
  2. A single-unit oral liquid (labeled 5 mL) is tested on 10 containers. The average is 5.05 mL (101%), but 2 containers measure 4.6 mL and 5.6 mL respectively. What is the outcome?

Practical Application

On a fill-finish line, deliverable volume results are directly linked to fill volume adjustment of the filling machine. If initial testing leads to a Stage 2 situation, QA should start an investigation to confirm whether the cause is the filling equipment (for example, unstable pump pressure or worn needles) or a change in the viscosity of the product itself. This is also one of the core indicators for process capability (Cpk) assessment.

Chapter Summary

  • Test purpose: to ensure that an oral liquid delivers the labeled volume after being transferred out of its container; applies to liquid preparations dispensed by pouring (including constituted powder products)
  • Exemption: for single-unit containers that have been tested under ⟨905⟩ Uniformity of Dosage Units, the ⟨698⟩ test is not required
  • Density determination: Density = W/V (W = sample weight, V = 50.0 mL − volume of water added), used in the by-weight method to convert weight to volume and eliminate air entrainment error
  • Test methods: by weight (weigh, then convert) or by volume (measure directly in a graduated cylinder, 30° tilt, cylinder capacity ≤2.5× the target volume, read once bubbles have cleared)
  • Drain time: single-unit ≤5 seconds; multiple-unit ≤10 minutes
  • Multiple-unit acceptance criteria: average ≥100%, each unit ≥95%; Stage 2 (30 units): average ≥100%, at most 1 unit ≥90%
  • Single-unit acceptance criteria: average ≥100%, each unit 95%–110%; Stage 2 (30 units): average ≥100%, at most 1 unit 90%–115%
  • Production significance: the fill target is usually set slightly above the labeled amount to accommodate process variability and ensure a delivered volume of ≥100%
