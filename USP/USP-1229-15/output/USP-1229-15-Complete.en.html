Introduction

Original Text

Gases, such as compressed air, nitrogen, and atmospheric gases that contact sterile components, containers and closures, and product contact surfaces of processing equipment, such as tanks and piping, must be sterile to prevent contamination of pharmaceuticals and biopharmaceuticals.

The sterilization of gases is typically accomplished by passing the gas through one or more sterilizing-grade membrane filters. The filtration process must be designed to ensure that the filtered gas has been sterilized.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why do process gases need to be sterilized? In sterile pharmaceutical manufacturing, any gas that contacts sterile product, containers, closures, or equipment surfaces must be sterile. This includes:

  • Compressed air - used to drive pneumatic valves, blow-form containers, dry components, and so on
  • Nitrogen - used for tank overlay (nitrogen overlay) to protect oxygen-sensitive products
  • Atmospheric gas - air entering the sterile area

If these gases are not filtered, they may carry microorganisms or particles that directly contaminate the product or break the sterile state.

Key Point: CDMO Practice

In an aseptic fill-finish environment, gas filtration involves several critical points:

  • Tank vent filter - when the liquid level in a compounding tank changes, the vent filter prevents outside microorganisms from entering
  • Nitrogen overlay filter - overlay nitrogen contacts the product surface directly, so it must pass through a sterilizing-grade filter
  • Grade A air supply - filtration of compressed air in the filling area
Gas Versus Liquid Sterilizing Filtration

Original Text

Sterilizing Filtration of Liquids 〈1229.4〉 contains information that is applicable to this chapter, including but not limited to sterilizing-grade filters, retention mechanisms and factors affecting retention, validation, integrity-testing principles and methods, and prefiltration bioburden control.

The significant differences between liquid and gas filtration will be discussed in this chapter.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Relationship between USP 〈1229.4〉 and 〈1229.15〉: 〈1229.4〉 covers the general principles of sterilizing filtration (membrane rating, retention mechanisms, validation, integrity testing), while 〈1229.15〉 focuses on the specific features of gas filtration. The two are complementary, not substitutes.

Summary of key differences:

  • Gas filtration typically uses hydrophobic membranes, whereas liquid filtration uses hydrophilic membranes
  • Microbial retention efficiency in gases is higher than in liquids (because diffusional interception and inertial impaction are more pronounced in the gas phase)
  • There are additional options for integrity testing (such as the water intrusion test)
Membranes for Gas Sterilization

Original Text

Filters used for sterilization of gases are commonly hydrophobic membranes, although hydrophilic membranes can be employed where membrane wetting during use is not an issue.

Wetting may be a factor with filters used for autoclave venting and where vent filters are required to maintain the sterility of tank headspace.

Microporous filter membranes that have a pore-size rating of NMT 0.2 µm, which meets the ASTM International standard (see F838-15a Standard Test Method for Determining Bacterial Retention of Membrane Filters Utilized for Liquid Filtration), are suitable for the sterilization of gases; in fact, these filters are considerably more retentive for gases than for liquids (1).

Additionally, the filters must be chemically compatible with the gas to be filtered and physically compatible with the filtration conditions (e.g., pressure differentials and flow rates).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Hydrophobic vs. hydrophilic membranes:

  • Hydrophobic membrane: repels water and is not wetted by it. This is the first choice for gas filtration because gas lines may contain condensate, and a hydrophobic membrane will not be blocked by water in a way that affects airflow
  • Hydrophilic membrane: is easily wetted by water. Once the membrane is wetted, gas cannot pass through, and the filter fails

NMT 0.2 µm pore size rating: the same standard as for liquid filtration. The key difference is that the same membrane retains microorganisms in gas far better than microorganisms in liquid, because diffusional interception and inertial impaction are more pronounced in the gas phase.

Key Notes: CDMO Context

Common wetting scenarios in a CDMO:

  • Tank vent filter: when the liquid level in a compounding tank changes, steam may condense on the vent filter membrane. If the membrane is wetted, the tank cannot breathe properly, which may cause tank deformation or a vacuum condition
  • Autoclave venting: steam during the sterilization cycle will wet the membrane, so membrane selection requires particular attention

Analogy

How a Gore-Tex jacket works:

A hydrophobic membrane is like a Gore-Tex jacket for outdoor sports: it lets air (gas) pass through but blocks rain (liquid). This is why hydrophobic membranes are preferred for gas filtration: even if the gas contains moisture, the membrane will not be blocked.

Retention Mechanisms

Original Text

The primary retention mechanisms of membrane filters used in gas sterilization are size exclusion, impaction, diffusional interception, and electrostatic attraction.

Size exclusion is effective for particles (microorganisms) that are too large to pass through the pore structure of the filter membrane and is essentially independent of the velocity of gas flowing through the filter.

Retention mechanisms other than size exclusion can be effective for particles smaller than the pores they encounter.

Retention due to impaction, diffusional interception, and electrostatic attraction is dependent on particle size, flow rate, and relative humidity.

  • Impaction: Particle retention efficiency is proportional to the mass of the particle; heavier particles have a greater momentum than lighter ones and are therefore more likely to contact pore walls and subsequently be retained by the filter during tortuous flow.
  • Diffusional interception: Capture efficiency is inversely proportional to particle size and velocity. Smaller particles influenced by Brownian motion can exit the streamlines, contact the filter matrix, and be retained. Lower velocity increases potential contact time and improves capture efficiency.
  • Electrostatic attraction: Attraction of particles is reduced by a high relative humidity in the filtered gas.

Adsorption, which can be important in liquid filtration, is not a retention mechanism for gases.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

Key differences between gas and liquid retention:

  • Adsorption does not apply to gases: in liquid filtration, microorganisms may be adsorbed onto the membrane surface. In the gas phase, this mechanism does not exist
  • Diffusional interception is stronger in the gas phase: compared with liquids, the Brownian motion of particles in gases is more pronounced, so diffusional interception is more effective. This is why the same membrane retains better in gas

Effect of humidity: high humidity reduces electrostatic attraction. In a CDMO environment, special attention is needed for vent filters on lines after steam sterilization or in humid environments.

Validation

Original Text

Validation of the processes used in sterilizing filtration for gases, in general, may be divided into two parts:

  1. Sterilization of the filter and its housing
  2. The ability of the filter to remove microorganisms from the filtered gas

Sterilization of the filter and its housing has been addressed specifically in 〈1229.4〉 and Sterilization-in-Place 〈1229.13〉, and generally in Sterilization of Compendial Articles 〈1229〉.

Membrane filters meeting the requirements of ASTM F838-15a are suitable for sterilizing filtration of gases in terms of microbial retention.

Liquid bacterial challenge testing represents a worst-case condition for sterilizing gas filters because the retention efficiency in liquids is lower than in gases (2).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two aspects of validation:

  • Sterilization of the filter itself: the filter and housing must be sterilized before use (usually by steam sterilization or SIP). This ensures that the filtration system itself does not become a source of contamination
  • Microbial retention capability: the membrane must be able to effectively remove microorganisms from the gas

Worst-case principle: this is one of the most important validation concepts in this USP chapter. Because the bacterial retention test in liquid (ASTM F838) is already the worst case for gas filtration, a membrane that passes the liquid challenge test does not need a separate gas bacterial challenge test.

Key Notes: CDMO Validation Strategy

Simplified logic for gas filter validation:

  1. Select a 0.2 µm membrane that meets ASTM F838 (with supplier documentation)
  2. Validate the sterilization process for the filter and housing (SIP or steam sterilization)
  3. Perform an integrity test before and after each use

No separate gas bacterial challenge test is needed - the liquid test already covers the worst case.

Integrity Testing

Original Text

Integrity testing requirements and techniques are discussed in 〈1229.4〉.

The hydrophobic filters typically used for gas filtration can be integrity tested using:

  • Bubble-point test
  • Diffusive-flow test
  • Pressure-hold test

These tests use a liquid of low surface tension to completely wet the pore structure of the filter membrane.

Additionally, integrity testing can be performed by measuring the membrane’s resistance to wetting with water as a function of pressure. Water intrusion testing is an example of this type of integrity testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The challenge of integrity testing hydrophobic membranes:

Conventional integrity tests (bubble point, diffusive flow) require the membrane to be wetted with a liquid first. But a hydrophobic membrane is not wetted by water, so there are two strategies:

  • Wetting with a low-surface-tension liquid: use an alcohol/water mixture or a dedicated test fluid (such as IPA/water) to wet the hydrophobic membrane, then perform the bubble point or diffusive flow test
  • Water intrusion test (WIT): does not require wetting the membrane. Instead, it exploits the resistance of a hydrophobic membrane to water penetration, measuring the amount of water intruding at a specified pressure to judge membrane integrity

Practice Questions

  1. Why is integrity testing of hydrophobic membranes more complex than for hydrophilic membranes? Which integrity test method is typically used for gas filters in your facility?
  2. If a tank vent filter fails its post-use integrity test, how would you assess the potential impact on the product? What processes need to be initiated?
  3. In which scenarios does the water intrusion test (WIT) have an advantage over the traditional bubble point test?

Key Notes: CDMO Practical Points

Practical advantage of WIT: in the CDMO setting, the water intrusion test is the most convenient, because no alcohol or other organic solvent is needed to wet the hydrophobic membrane; WFI can be used directly to run the test. However, confirm that the instrument supplier supports this test mode.

Chapter Summary

  • Scope: all process gases (compressed air, nitrogen, atmospheric gas) that contact sterile product, containers, closures, and equipment surfaces require sterilizing filtration
  • Membrane selection: hydrophobic membranes (≤ 0.2 µm) are preferred to avoid loss of venting due to wetting. The same membrane retains better in gas than in liquid
  • Retention mechanisms: four mechanisms act together - size exclusion (independent of flow rate), inertial impaction, diffusional interception, and electrostatic attraction. Adsorption does not apply to gases
  • Simplified validation: the liquid ASTM F838 test is already the worst case, so no separate gas bacterial challenge test is needed
  • Integrity testing: bubble point/diffusive flow/pressure hold (requires wetting with a low-surface-tension liquid) or the water intrusion test (WIT, no wetting required)
  • Key CDMO applications: tank vent filters, nitrogen overlay filters, and Grade A compressed air filtration all fall within the scope of this chapter
