0.1 Introduction & Purpose

Original Text

〈661〉 PLASTIC PACKAGING SYSTEMS AND THEIR MATERIALS OF CONSTRUCTION

(If the option of early adoption of Plastic Materials of Construction 〈661.1〉 and Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use 〈661.2〉 is not used prior to December 1, 2025, the requirements under this chapter will apply.)

INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this chapter is to provide standards for plastic articles (materials, components, and systems) used to package medical articles (pharmaceuticals, biologics, and dietary supplements). Definitions that apply to this chapter are provided in Packaging and Storage Requirements 〈659〉. Standards and tests for the functional properties of containers and their components are provided in Containers—Performance Testing 〈671〉.

Plastics are composed of a mixture of homologous polymers, having a range of molecular weights. Plastics may contain other substances such as residues from the polymerization process and additives such as plasticizers, stabilizers, antioxidants, pigments, and lubricants.

Plastic articles are identified and characterized by infrared (IR) spectroscopy and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Standards are provided in this chapter for the identification and characterization of the different types of plastic, and the test procedures are provided at the end of the chapter. Furthermore, plastics are characterized to establish that they are suited for their intended use.

Plastics and their additives, regardless of their risk and application, must meet the requirements for food contact as provided in Title 21 of the US Code of Federal Regulations (CFR). Ingredients added to these items must conform to the requirements in the applicable sections of 21 CFR Part 174—Indirect Food Additives: General, or have been evaluated by the FDA and determined to be acceptable substances for the intended use.

Physicochemical tests are performed on plastics used in high risk applications (inhalation, parenteral preparation, and ophthalmics) to characterize the extracted components and identify possible migrants. To this end, resin-specific extraction tests are provided in this chapter for polyethylene, polypropylene, polyethylene terephthalate, and polyethylene terephthalate G used in such high risk applications. Test all other plastics as directed for Physicochemical Tests. Conduct the Buffering Capacity test only when the containers are intended to hold a liquid product.

Physicochemical Tests are not required for articles used in low risk applications (those intended for oral or topical administration). The degree or extent of biological testing is also dependent on the intended use of the plastic article and the degree of risk that extracted substances could adversely impact the efficacy of the compendial article.

Plastic articles used for high risk products, such as those intended for inhalation, parenteral preparation, and ophthalmics, are tested using Biological Tests. Plastic articles used for low risk products, such as those intended for oral or topical administration, do not require Biological Tests.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Plastic Articles: USP <661> covers three levels:
1. Materials: the raw resins, such as PE, PP and PET
2. Components: individual molded parts, such as bottles and caps
3. Systems: the complete container-closure system

Understanding these three levels matters because the testing requirements can differ from one level to another. <661.1> focuses on Materials, <661.2> focuses on Systems, and the current <661> integrates all three.

Analogy

Think of plastic packaging as choosing tableware for a restaurant. The material is the ceramic or glass raw stock used to make the plates, the component is the finished plate itself, and the system is the complete set (plate, bowl, cup, lid).

High-risk applications (injectables) are like the vessels used in a hospital operating room: they must pass strict chemical and biological compatibility testing. Low-risk applications (oral tablets) are like ordinary restaurant tableware: there are basic hygiene standards, but not operating-room grade.

Key Notes

Risk-based Approach:

  • High Risk: inhalation products, injectables, ophthalmic preparations → require Physicochemical Tests + Biological Tests
  • Low Risk: oral and topical preparations → exempt from Physicochemical Tests and Biological Tests

This reflects regulatory efficiency: there is no need to test every plastic bottle of vitamin C tablets to injectable-grade standards. However, if the packaging changes the efficacy or safety of the drug, the level of testing must be raised.

Chapter Architecture

USP <661> is an "umbrella chapter" with three sister chapters beneath it:

  • <661.1> Plastic Materials of Construction — material-level testing
  • <661.2> Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use — system-level testing
  • <1661> Evaluation of Plastic Packaging Systems — informational (guidance) chapter

From December 1, 2025, manufacturers may choose to apply <661.1>/<661.2> in place of the current <661>. If they do not adopt them early, <661> remains the valid standard.

0.2 Polyethylene Containers

Original Text

POLYETHYLENE CONTAINERS

Scope

The standards and tests provided in this section characterize containers and components, produced from either low-density polyethylene (LDPE) or high-density polyethylene (HDPE) of either homopolymer or copolymer resins that are interchangeably suitable for packaging dry oral dosage forms not meant for constitution into solution.

All polyethylene components are subject to testing by IR spectroscopy and DSC. Where stability studies have been performed to establish the expiration date of a particular dosage form in the appropriate polyethylene container, then any other polyethylene container meeting these requirements may be similarly used to package such a dosage form, provided that the appropriate stability programs are expanded to include the alternative container.

Background

High-density and low-density polyethylene are long-chain polymers synthesized under controlled conditions of heat and pressure, with the aid of catalysts from NLT 85.0% ethylene and NLT 95.0% total olefins. Other olefin ingredients that are most frequently used are butene, hexene, and propylene.

High-density polyethylene has a density between 0.941 and 0.965 g/cm³. Low-density polyethylene has a density between 0.850 and 0.940 g/cm³. Other properties that may affect the suitability of polyethylene include modulus of elasticity, melt index, environmental stress crack resistance, and degree of crystallinity after molding.

High-Density Polyethylene — Identification Tests

Infrared spectroscopy: Proceed as directed for Multiple Internal Reflectance. The corrected spectrum of the specimen exhibits major absorption bands only at the same wavelengths as the spectrum of USP High-Density Polyethylene RS.

Differential scanning calorimetry: Proceed as directed for Thermal Analysis. The melting peak temperature obtained from the thermal analysis curve of the specimen does not differ from that of the Reference Standard by more than 6.0°.

HDPE — Heavy Metals and Nonvolatile Residue

For each 20.0 mL of Extracting medium, the portion shall be 60 cm², regardless of thickness.

Nonvolatile residue limits:

  • Water at 70°: NMT 12.0 mg
  • Alcohol at 70°: NMT 75.0 mg
  • Hexanes at 50°: NMT 100.0 mg

Low-Density Polyethylene — Identification & Residue Tests

DSC: melting peak temperature shall not differ from USP Low-Density Polyethylene RS by more than 8.0°.

Nonvolatile residue limits:

  • Water at 70°: NMT 12.0 mg
  • Alcohol at 70°: NMT 75.0 mg
  • Hexanes at 50°: NMT 350.0 mg

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

HDPE vs LDPE (high-density vs low-density polyethylene):

The difference in density comes from the degree of branching in the polymer chains. HDPE has little branching, so its chains pack tightly → high density (0.941–0.965 g/cm³), high rigidity and good barrier properties. LDPE has more branching → low density (0.850–0.940 g/cm³), soft and flexible, suitable for squeeze bottles.

In pharmaceutical packaging, HDPE is commonly used for tablet bottles (e.g., OTC products), while LDPE is commonly used for eye-drop bottles and tubes.

Specification Comparison

Nonvolatile Residue Limits:

Extraction medium   HDPE limit    LDPE limit
────────────────────────────────
Water (70°)         ≤12.0 mg      ≤12.0 mg
Alcohol (70°)       ≤75.0 mg      ≤75.0 mg
n-Hexane (50°)      ≤100.0 mg     ≤350.0 mg

Key difference: the LDPE limit in n-hexane is 3.5 times the HDPE limit,
reflecting the fact that LDPE, with its looser structure, yields larger extracts in nonpolar solvents.

Analogy

The molecular structure of HDPE is like neatly aligned bamboo skewers (straight chains): high density, so foreign substances have difficulty penetrating. LDPE is more like a heap of bent wires (branched): lots of gaps, so solvents extract relatively more material.

This is why the nonvolatile residue limit for LDPE in n-hexane (a nonpolar organic solvent) is 3.5 times more lenient than for HDPE: USP recognizes that this is an inherent characteristic of LDPE material.

Key Notes

Interchangeability: Once the shelf life of a PE container has been established by stability testing, other PE containers that meet the same specification can be used interchangeably, provided that the substitute container is added to the stability program as an extended evaluation.

This is very practical for CDMO customer service: when the originally specified container is in short supply, you can switch quickly to an equivalent container, but you must have data to support it.

  2. A pharmaceutical company wants to change an oral solid dosage form from an HDPE container to an LDPE container. What evaluation work is needed?
0.3 Polypropylene Containers

Original Text

POLYPROPYLENE CONTAINERS

Scope

The standards and tests provided in this section characterize polypropylene containers, produced from either homopolymers or copolymers, that are interchangeably suitable for packaging dry solid and liquid oral dosage forms.

Background

Propylene polymers are long-chain polymers synthesized from propylene or propylene and other olefins under controlled conditions of heat and pressure, with the aid of catalysts. Examples of other olefins most commonly used include ethylene and butene. The propylene polymers, the ingredients used to manufacture the propylene polymers, and the ingredients used in the fabrication of the containers conform to the applicable sections of 21 CFR.

Factors such as plastic composition, processing and cleaning procedures, contacting media, inks, adhesives, absorption, adsorption and permeability of preservatives, and conditions of storage may also affect the suitability of a plastic for a specific use.

Polypropylene has a distinctive IR spectrum and possesses characteristic thermal properties. It has a density between 0.880 and 0.913 g/cm³. The permeation properties of molded polypropylene containers may be altered when reground polymer is incorporated, depending on the proportion of regrind in the final product.

Other properties that may affect the suitability of polypropylene: oxygen and moisture permeability, modulus of elasticity, melt flow index, environmental stress crack resistance, and degree of crystallinity after molding.

Identification Tests

Infrared Spectroscopy: The corrected spectrum exhibits major absorption bands only at the same wavelengths as USP Homopolymer Polypropylene RS or copolymer polypropylene standard.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry: The melting peak temperature does not differ from that of USP Homopolymer Polypropylene RS by more than 12.0°.

Heavy Metals and Nonvolatile Residue

For each 20 mL of Extracting medium the portion shall be 60 cm², regardless of thickness.

Nonvolatile residue limits:

  • Water at 70°: NMT 10.0 mg
  • Alcohol at 70°: NMT 60.0 mg
  • Hexanes at 50°: NMT 225.0 mg
[Note—Hexanes and alcohol are flammable. When evaporating these solvents, use a current of air with the water bath; when drying the residue, use an explosion-proof oven.]

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Polypropylene (PP): Polymerized from propylene monomer, with a density of 0.880–0.913 g/cm³. It is lighter than PE, has excellent chemical stability, and high thermal stability (it can withstand autoclave steam sterilization).

The DSC melting-point tolerance for PP (12.0°) is larger than for HDPE (6.0°) and LDPE (8.0°), because the crystallinity of PP is more strongly affected by the manufacturing process (the differences between homopolymer and copolymer are more pronounced).

Key Notes

Effect of regrind: Adding regrind (recycled, reprocessed PP) changes the permeation characteristics of the container and affects its barrier properties. USP explicitly points this out, because some manufacturers use regrind to cut costs, but it must be confirmed that compliance with the material specification is not affected.

This is directly related to the GMP requirement for material consistency: if the supplier is changed or the regrind proportion is adjusted, revalidation is required.

Analogy

A PP container is like the sealing ring of a pressure cooker: it withstands high temperature and pressure and is chemically inert, so it can be used both for dry solids (sealing tablets) and for liquid oral preparations.

The effect of "regrind" is like mixing leftover dough into fresh dough: a small proportion makes little difference, but when the proportion is too high the texture (the physical barrier properties) may change.

Density Reference

Plastic type   Density range (g/cm³)   DSC tolerance
──────────────────────────────────
HDPE          0.941–0.965         ±6.0°
LDPE          0.850–0.940         ±8.0°
PP            0.880–0.913         ±12.0°
PET           1.3–1.4             ±4.0°
PETG          ~1.27               ±6.0°

The higher the density, the more tightly the molecules are generally packed and the better the barrier properties.

0.4 PET and PETG Containers

Original Text

POLYETHYLENE TEREPHTHALATE BOTTLES AND POLYETHYLENE TEREPHTHALATE G CONTAINERS

Scope

The standards and tests provided in this section characterize polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and polyethylene terephthalate G (PETG) bottles that are interchangeably suitable for packaging liquid oral dosage forms.

Background — PET

PET resins are long-chain crystalline polymers prepared by the condensation of ethylene glycol with dimethyl terephthalate or terephthalic acid. PET copolymer resins may also contain a small amount of either isophthalic acid (NMT 3 mol%) or 1,4-cyclohexanedimethanol (NMT 5 mol%).

PET and PET copolymer resins exhibit a large degree of order in their molecular structure. Characteristic properties: glass transition temperature ~76°, melting temperature ~250°. Density between 1.3 and 1.4 g/cm³; minimum intrinsic viscosity 0.7 dL/g (corresponds to number average molecular weight of ~23,000 Da).

Background — PETG

PETG resins are prepared by condensation of ethylene glycol with dimethyl terephthalate or terephthalic acid and 15–34 mol% of 1,4-cyclohexanedimethanol (CHDM). PETG resins are clear, amorphous polymers, having a glass transition temperature of about 81° and no crystalline melting point as determined by DSC. Density ~1.27 g/cm³; minimum intrinsic viscosity 0.65 dL/g (~16,000 Da).

PET and PETG resins do not contain any plasticizers, processing aids, or antioxidants. Colorants, if used, do not migrate into the contained liquid.

Colorant Extraction Test

Fill 2 test bottles: 50% alcohol for PET; 25% alcohol for PETG. Incubate at 49° for 10 days. Determine absorbances at the wavelength of maximum absorbance: the absorbance values are less than 0.01 for both test solutions.

Extracting Media for PET/PETG

  • Purified Water
  • 50% alcohol (for PET) / 25% alcohol (for PETG)
  • n-Heptane

General procedure: fill bottles to 90% nominal capacity; incubate at 49° for 10 days.

Key Analyte Limits

  • Heavy metals: NMT 1 ppm in extract
  • Total terephthaloyl moieties: NMT 0.150 absorbance (≤1 ppm) in alcohol and n-heptane extracts
  • Ethylene glycol: NMT 1 ppm in Purified Water extract
  • Colorant extraction: absorbance <0.01

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Structural differences between PET and PETG:

PET (polyethylene terephthalate) is a semicrystalline polymer with a well-defined melting point (~250°) and glass transition temperature (Tg ~76°). It has good clarity and is widely used for beverage bottles (a Coca-Cola bottle is PET).

PETG incorporates a large amount of CHDM (15–34 mol%) into its structure, which disrupts crystallinity and makes it an amorphous transparent polymer with no crystalline melting point and a Tg of ~81°. PETG is easier to process into complex shapes and has higher clarity, but its thermal stability is slightly lower than that of PET.

Analogy

PET is like ordinary window glass (crystalline structure, clear but with a slight tint); PETG is like optical glass (amorphous, higher clarity, easier to shape into complex forms). Both are transparent, but PETG offers more processing flexibility, which suits pharmaceutical companies that need special bottle shapes.

Key Notes

Significance of the three specific analytes:

  • Total Terephthaloyl Moieties: direct products of PET/PETG degradation or hydrolysis, and an indicator of polymer integrity
  • Ethylene Glycol: one of the raw materials for PET/PETG synthesis; excessive residual levels are potentially toxic
  • Colorant Extraction: ensures that any added colorant does not migrate into the drug product

These three tests are specific to PET/PETG and reflect the particular chemical composition of these materials. The different extraction media (water, alcohol, n-heptane) simulate different food/drug matrix environments.

Practical Application

In a CDMO setting, a client may ask to use PET or PETG containers for liquid oral preparations (such as syrups and oral solutions). Key points for evaluation:

  • Confirm that the container supplier provides a CoA (Certificate of Analysis) showing compliance with USP <661>
  • If the product contains alcohol as a solvent, take care to use the corresponding extraction medium (50% alcohol for PET, 25% alcohol for PETG)
  • Assess the risk of colorant migration from labels/printing inks
  • For high-temperature storage conditions (such as markets in tropical regions), consider whether the Tg (~76–81°) has any practical impact

Practice Questions

  1. Why does the DSC thermal analysis curve of PETG not show a melting peak? What does this mean for identification testing?
  2. PET and PETG bottles use different alcohol concentrations (50% vs 25%) as the extraction medium. What is the reason?
  3. An intrinsic viscosity of 0.7 dL/g corresponds to a molecular weight of ~23,000 Da. How does this value affect the mechanical strength of PET?
0.5 New Scope (Dec 2025 Text)

Original Text

INTRODUCTION (Official 1-Dec-2025)

Packaging systems used for drug products (pharmaceuticals, biologics) are defined in Packaging and Storage Requirements 〈659〉 and such systems may be constructed from plastic materials and components. Plastics are made of polymers, which can have a range of molecular weights and may contain other substances such as residues from the polymerization process, plasticizers, stabilizers, antioxidants, pigments, and lubricants.

Drug products will be in direct contact with the primary packaging component and may result in an interaction between the product and the packaging system. Such an interaction should not adversely affect the drug product, and this will be facilitated by the use of well-characterized plastic materials of construction in components and packaging systems and by the appropriate testing of packaging systems.

SCOPE (Official 1-Dec-2025)

Establishing the suitability of plastic packaging systems for drug products involves multiple tests and testing procedures, as briefly outlined below:

  • Material screening—characterization of a packaging system's materials of construction to evaluate ingredients as probable extractables and potential leachables. Such a characterization facilitates the identification of materials that are suitable for use in packaging systems.
  • Controlled extraction (simulation) study—worst-case controlled extraction (simulation) study to determine the extent to which extractables may become probable leachables (for additional information, see Assessment of Extractables Associated with Pharmaceutical Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈1663〉).
  • Product assessment—actual-case measurement of confirmed leachables in the drug product in the pharmaceutical packaging/delivery system intended for the commercial market (for additional information, see Assessment of Drug Product Leachables Associated with Pharmaceutical Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈1664〉).

Additionally, information provided by the vendor(s) of plastic packaging systems and their associated materials or components of construction can facilitate suitability for use assessments.

The testing of materials of construction used in packaging systems is addressed in Plastic Materials of Construction 〈661.1〉. A product's packaging component or system is deemed suited for its intended use, if it meets the requirements in 〈661.2〉.

For more information on the scope of, applicability of, and other topics related to the 〈661〉 suite of general chapters, see Evaluation of Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use and Their Materials of Construction 〈1661〉.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three-tier Evaluation Framework:

The new December 2025 version of <661> introduces a more modern E&L (Extractables and Leachables) evaluation framework:

  • Material Screening: identifies potential Extractables in the material → substances that may become Leachables
  • Controlled Extraction Study: worst-case extraction to determine the range of Extractables (see <1663>)
  • Product Assessment: measurement of Leachables under actual commercial conditions (see <1664>)

Analogy

These three tiers are like the three checkpoints of food safety inspection: material screening is "raw ingredient inspection" (confirming the ingredients are safe), the controlled extraction study is a "cooking simulation test" (what is released under the harshest conditions), and product assessment is the "finished product inspection before market release" (what the actual food really contains).

Key Notes

The core difference between Extractables and Leachables:

  • Extractables: substances extracted from the packaging material under extreme/accelerated conditions; they represent the worst-case "list of potential threats"
  • Leachables: substances that actually migrate into the drug product under real storage conditions; they represent the true patient exposure risk

Regulators (FDA, EMA) focus their review on Leachables, but Extractables studies are a necessary prerequisite for predicting Leachables. ICH Q3E (the E&L guideline) describes this in detail.

Practical Application

For a CDMO, the new <661> framework means that the qualification and assessment of the container-closure system (CCS) must be more systematic:

  • Request the Material Screening report (including the Extractables profile) from the supplier
  • Decide whether a Controlled Extraction Study is needed based on the dosage form and route of administration
  • Injectables/inhalation products must complete a Product Assessment (determination of Leachables in the actual product)
  • Make good use of the data from <661.1>/<661.2>, which may serve as an alternative to <661> testing

Practice Questions

  1. For a PET container for an intravenous drug, which three tiers of evaluation must be completed under the new <661> framework? What is the main output of each tier?
  2. The supplier's CoA includes material screening data. Can these data directly replace a Controlled Extraction Study performed by the manufacturer? Explain the basis for your judgment.
  3. What is the role of each of the four chapters <661>, <661.1>, <661.2> and <1661>? How do you decide which chapter to cite?

Section Summary

  • USP <661> provides testing standards for plastic packaging materials and systems, and applies to the packaging of drugs, biologics and dietary supplements
  • Risk-based approach: high-risk applications (injectables, inhalation products, ophthalmic preparations) require physicochemical tests + biological tests; low-risk applications (oral, topical) may be exempt
  • Main plastic types: HDPE (0.941–0.965 g/cm³), LDPE (0.850–0.940 g/cm³), PP (0.880–0.913 g/cm³), PET (1.3–1.4 g/cm³), PETG (~1.27 g/cm³)
  • Identification methods: IR spectroscopy (Multiple Internal Reflectance) + DSC (melting point confirmation), with each resin compared against its USP Reference Standard (RS)
  • The December 2025 update introduces a three-tier evaluation framework: Material Screening → Controlled Extraction Study → Product Assessment, aligning with the modern E&L evaluation system
  • All plastics and additives must comply with the 21 CFR food-contact requirements; this is the baseline compliance threshold for pharmaceutical packaging materials

1.1 Multiple Internal Reflectance (IR Spectroscopy)

Original Text

TEST METHODS

Multiple Internal Reflectance

Apparatus: Use an IR spectrophotometer capable of correcting for the blank spectrum and equipped with a multiple internal reflectance accessory.

Specimen Preparation: Cut 2 flat sections representative of the average wall thickness of the container, and trim them as necessary to obtain segments that are convenient for mounting in the multiple internal reflectance accessory. Taking care to avoid scratching the surfaces, wipe the specimens with dry paper or, if necessary, clean them with a soft cloth dampened with methanol, and permit them to dry. Securely mount the specimens ensuring adequate surface contact. Prior to mounting the specimens on the plate, they may be compressed to thin uniform films by exposing them to temperatures of about 177° under high pressures (15,000 psi or more).

General Procedure: Place the mounted specimen sections within the multiple internal reflectance accessory, and place the assembly in the specimen beam of the IR spectrophotometer. Adjust the specimen position and mirrors within the accessory to permit maximum light transmission of the unattenuated reference beam. Determine the IR spectrum from 3500 to 600 cm⁻¹ for polyethylene and polypropylene and from 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹ for PET and PETG.

"The corrected spectrum of the specimen exhibits major absorption bands only at the same wavelengths as the spectrum of the USP Reference Standard."

Scanning Ranges by Resin Type

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Multiple Internal Reflectance (MIR), also known as ATR-FTIR: This is a surface analysis technique in which the IR beam is reflected multiple times (typically 2–25) at the interface between the sample and the crystal, accumulating the absorption signal so that even thin layers of material give a spectrum of sufficient intensity.

Compared with conventional transmission IR, MIR does not require the sample to be pressed into a film, so it is easier to perform, and it is the method of choice for identifying solid plastic containers.

PET/PETG use a wider scan range (4000–400 cm⁻¹ vs 3500–600 cm⁻¹) because some of the characteristic absorption peaks of their benzene ring structure and ester bonds fall in the low-frequency region of 600–400 cm⁻¹.

Analogy

An IR spectrum is like a "molecular fingerprint" of each plastic: each chemical bond (C-H, C=O, O-H, etc.) absorbs IR light at a specific wavenumber, producing a unique pattern of absorption peaks. Just as everyone's fingerprint is different, the IR spectrum of each polymer is unique.

The USP Reference Standard (RS) is the "standard fingerprint library": the spectrum of the test sample must agree with the major absorption bands of the RS in position before the identity of the material can be confirmed.

Key Notes

Key details of sample preparation:

  • Cut a section that represents the average wall thickness, to ensure the test is representative
  • Avoid scratching the surface (surface damage affects the quality of MIR contact)
  • The sample may be wiped clean with methanol, but it must be dried thoroughly
  • If necessary, press into a film at 177° / 15,000 psi or above to ensure intimate contact with the crystal

"Major absorption bands only at the same wavelengths": note that it is the major absorption bands that must be at the same wavelengths; small differences in minor peaks are tolerated (different batches/suppliers may differ slightly).

Practice Questions

  2. If the IR spectrum does not match the absorption band positions of the USP RS, what are the possible causes? A sample preparation problem, a material problem, or an instrument problem?

1.2 Thermal Analysis (DSC)

Original Text

Thermal Analysis

General Procedure: Cut a section weighing about 12 mg, and place it in the test specimen pan. [Note—Intimate contact between the pan and the thermocouple is essential for reproducible results.] Determine thermal analysis curve under nitrogen, using the heating and cooling conditions as specified for the resin type and using equipment capable of performing the determinations as specified under Thermal Analysis 〈891〉.

For Polyethylene

Determine the thermal analysis curve under nitrogen at temperatures between 40° and 200° at a heating rate between 2° and 10°/min followed by cooling at a rate between 2° and 10°/min to 40°.

For Polypropylene

Determine the thermal analysis curve under nitrogen at temperatures ranging from ambient to 30° above the melting point. Maintain the temperature for 10 min, then cool to 50° below the peak crystallization temperature at a rate of 10° to 20°/min.

For Polyethylene Terephthalate

Heat the specimen from room temperature to 280° at a heating rate of about 20°/min. Hold the specimen at 280° for 1 min. Quickly cool the specimen to room temperature, and reheat it to 280° at a heating rate of about 5°/min.

For Polyethylene Terephthalate G

Heat the specimen from room temperature to 120° at a heating rate of about 20°/min. Hold the specimen at 120° for 1 min. Quickly cool the specimen to room temperature, and reheat it to 120° at a heating rate of about 10°/min.

DSC Melting Point Tolerance Summary

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC): measures the changes in heat absorbed or released by a material as it is heated or cooled, and can identify:

  • Melting point (Tm): characteristic of crystalline polymers; HDPE, LDPE, PP and PET all have well-defined melting peaks
  • Glass transition temperature (Tg): an amorphous or semicrystalline material is glassy below Tg and rubbery above it
  • Crystallization temperature (Tc): the exothermic peak of crystal formation on cooling

Because of its amorphous structure, PETG has no melting peak, which is itself one of its identifying characteristics.

DSC Protocol Comparison

Resin   Temperature range   Heating rate   Special steps
──────────────────────────────────────────
PE      40–200°C        2–10°/min  Heating + cooling cycle
PP      RT → Tm+30°C   Variable   Hold 10 min, then cool rapidly
PET     RT → 280°C     20°/min initially  Quench, then rescan at 5°/min
                                    (eliminates thermal history)
PETG    RT → 120°C     20°/min initially  Quench, then rescan at 10°/min
                                    (Tg confirmation, no melting peak)

Note: the purpose of the two-step scan for PET is first to eliminate the thermal history from processing,
then to use a slow scan to obtain an accurate Tg and crystallization characteristics.

Analogy

DSC is like watching a piece of chocolate go from solid to liquid with a thermometer: pure dark chocolate (analogous to HDPE) has a well-defined melting temperature; white chocolate (analogous to PETG), because of its complex composition, has no clear melting point and simply softens gradually (the glass transition).

Quenching (rapid cooling) PET before rescanning is like melting the chocolate again and re-cooling it, erasing the earlier heat treatment history to ensure the test is reproducible.

Key Notes

Why test under nitrogen (N₂)? Nitrogen is an inert gas that prevents the sample from oxidizing at high temperature, so that the test result reflects the thermal properties of the material itself rather than thermal-oxidative degradation. This is especially important for PP (prone to oxidation) and PET (which may hydrolyze at high temperature).

12 mg sample size: too much → uneven heat transfer and broad peaks; too little → weak signal. 12 mg is the balance point between sensitivity and uniform heat transfer.

Meaning of the allowed deviation for each resin: the ±4.0° for PET is the strictest (high molecular regularity, so the melting point should be very precise); the ±12.0° for PP is the most lenient (large differences between homopolymer and copolymer PP).

Practice Questions

  1. The maximum temperature of the DSC program for PETG is only 120°, far below the 280° for PET. What is the reason?
  2. Why does the DSC of PET involve two heating runs (first at 20°/min, then at 5°/min after quenching)? What is the purpose of this "erasing thermal history" step?
  3. If a container labeled HDPE has a DSC melting point 10° higher than the USP HDPE RS, how should this be assessed and handled?
1.3 Physicochemical Tests — Testing Parameters

Original Text

Physicochemical Tests

The following tests, designed to determine physical and chemical properties of plastics and their extracts, are based on the extraction of the plastic material, and it is essential that the designated amount of the plastic be used. Also, the specified surface area must be available for extraction at the designated temperature.

Testing Parameters

Extracting medium: Unless otherwise directed in a specific test below, use Purified Water as the Extracting medium, maintained at a temperature of 70° during the extraction of the Sample preparation.

Blank: Use Purified Water where a blank is specified in the tests that follow.

Apparatus: Use a water bath and the Extraction Containers as described in Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vivo 〈88〉, Classification of Plastics, Apparatus. Proceed as directed in the first paragraph of Classification of Plastics, Preparation of Apparatus. [Note—The containers and equipment need not be sterile.]

Sample preparation: From a homogeneous plastic specimen, use a portion, for each 20.0 mL of Extracting medium, equivalent to 120 cm² total surface area (both sides combined), and subdivide into strips approximately 3 mm in width and as near to 5 cm in length as is practical. Transfer the subdivided sample to a glass-stoppered, 250-mL graduated cylinder of Type I glass, and add about 150 mL of Purified Water. Agitate for about 30 s, drain off and discard the liquid, and repeat with a second washing.

Sample preparation extract: Transfer the prepared Sample preparation to a suitable extraction flask, and add the required amount of Extracting medium. Extract by heating in a water bath at the temperature specified for the Extracting medium for 24 h. Cool, but not below 20°. Pipet 20 mL of the prepared extract into a suitable container. [Note—Use this portion in the test for Buffering Capacity.] Immediately decant the remaining extract into a suitably cleansed container, and seal.

Key Parameters Summary

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Design logic of the Physicochemical Tests: The core idea of these tests is "worst-case extraction", in order to assess the chemicals that a plastic container may release into the drug product under conditions of use.

Key design elements:

  • Standardized surface area (120 cm²/20 mL): ensures comparability between containers of different shapes
  • 70° extraction temperature: accelerates extraction and simulates more severe conditions (more stringent than room temperature, but not as extreme as autoclaving)
  • 24 h extraction time: reaches a near-equilibrium state
  • Two pre-washes: remove surface contamination, so that the test result represents the extractables of the material itself

Analogy

This extraction procedure is like brewing tea: the tea leaves (plastic sample) are steeped in hot water (extraction medium) for a set time, and what has been brewed out is measured.

Standardizing the surface area (120 cm²/20 mL) is like specifying "how many milliliters of water per gram of tea leaves", so that comparisons between different batches of tea leaves (containers of different shapes) are meaningful.

Pre-washing twice is like "waking up the tea leaves" with hot water before the actual brewing, to remove surface dust (surface contaminants picked up during manufacturing).

Key Notes

Understanding "120 cm², both sides combined": A plastic strip has two faces (front + back); 120 cm² is the combined area of both faces, so the actual area of a single face is 60 cm².

Conversion: if the container wall thickness is uniform, a double-sided area of 120 cm² is roughly equivalent to a 10 cm × 6 cm strip (both sides = 120 cm²).

"Do not cool below 20°": The extract must remain liquid for handling; cooling too far may cause some high-molecular-weight extractables to precipitate, affecting subsequent test results.

Sample Amount Calculation

Standard extraction setup:
  Extraction medium volume: 20.0 mL (basis)
  Required surface area: 120 cm² (both sides)

If 100 mL of extraction medium is needed:
  100 mL ÷ 20 mL × 120 cm² = 600 cm² (both sides)
  i.e., 300 cm² of plastic sample on a single side

Test strip size: 3 mm × 5 cm = 0.3 cm × 5 cm = 1.5 cm²/strip (both sides = 3 cm²/strip)
→ Extraction of 100 mL requires: 600 cm² ÷ 3 cm² = 200 strips

Practice Questions

  1. Why must pre-washing be performed twice? Is once enough? What is the scientific basis for the USP requirement?
  2. If the extraction temperature is raised from 70° to 121° (autoclaving), how would the composition and amount of extractables change? How is this relevant to the assessment of terminally sterilized packaging for injectables?
1.4 Nonvolatile Residue, Heavy Metals & Buffering Capacity

Original Text

Nonvolatile Residue

Transfer, in suitable portions, 50.0 mL of the Sample preparation extract to a suitable, tared crucible (preferably a fused-silica crucible that has been acid-cleaned), and evaporate the volatile matter on a steam bath. Similarly evaporate 50.0 mL of the Blank in a second crucible.

[Note—If an oily residue is expected, inspect the crucible repeatedly during the evaporation and drying period, and reduce the amount of heat if the oil tends to creep along the walls of the crucible.] Dry at 105° for 1 h: the difference between the amounts obtained from the Sample preparation extract and the Blank is NMT 15 mg.

Residue on Ignition 〈281〉

[Note—It is not necessary to perform this test when the Nonvolatile Residue test result is NMT 5 mg.] Proceed with the residues obtained from the Sample preparation extract and from the Blank: the difference between the amounts of residue on ignition is NMT 5 mg.

Heavy Metals

Lead nitrate stock solution: Dissolve 159.8 mg of lead nitrate in 100 mL of water to which has been added 1 mL of nitric acid, then dilute with water to 1000 mL.

Standard lead solution: On the day of use, dilute 10.0 mL of Lead nitrate stock solution with water to 100.0 mL. Each milliliter of Standard lead solution contains the equivalent of 10 µg of lead. A comparison solution prepared on the basis of 100 µL of Standard lead solution per gram of substance contains the equivalent of 1 ppm of lead.

pH 3.5 acetate buffer: Dissolve 25.0 g of ammonium acetate in 25 mL of water, and add 38.0 mL of 6 N hydrochloric acid. Adjust, if necessary, to a pH of 3.5, dilute with water to 100 mL.

Procedure: Pipet 20 mL of the Sample preparation extract into one of two matched 50-mL color-comparison tubes. Adjust to pH 3.0–4.0. Into the second tube pipet 2 mL of Standard lead solution, and add 20 mL of the Blank. To each tube add 1.2 mL of thioacetamide–glycerin base TS and 2 mL of pH 3.5 acetate buffer, dilute with water to 50 mL: any brown color produced within 10 min in the tube containing the Sample preparation extract is NMT that in the tube containing the Standard lead solution (1 ppm in extract).

Buffering Capacity

Titrate the previously collected 20-mL portion of the Sample preparation extract potentiometrically to a pH of 7.0, using either 0.010 N hydrochloric acid or 0.010 N sodium hydroxide, as required. Treat a 20.0-mL portion of the Blank similarly: if the same titrant was required for both, the difference between the two volumes is NMT 10.0 mL; and if acid was required for either the Sample preparation extract or the Blank and alkali for the other, the total of the two volumes required is 10.0 mL.

"Conduct the Buffering Capacity test only when the containers are intended to hold a liquid product."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Significance of the three key tests:

1. Nonvolatile Residue: quantifies the total amount of nonvolatile substances in the extract, indirectly reflecting the total amount of migratable chemical components in the plastic (including additives, residual monomers, etc.). The limit is ≤15 mg (per 50 mL), relative to 20 mL of extraction medium × 120 cm² of surface area.

2. Heavy Metals: uses a colorimetric method (thioacetamide–glycerin) to detect lead and other heavy metals that form sulfide precipitates. The limit is ≤1 ppm (as lead).

3. Buffering Capacity: measures the ability of the extract to change pH, to ensure that the plastic does not significantly affect the pH of liquid drug products. It applies only to containers for liquid preparations.

Key Notes

Difference between Nonvolatile Residue and Residue on Ignition:

  • Nonvolatile Residue (105°): contains organic + inorganic matter and is the sum of all nonvolatile extractables
  • Residue on Ignition: builds on the Nonvolatile Residue by igniting at high temperature to remove the organic components, leaving only inorganic ash (metal salts, catalyst residues, etc.)

When Nonvolatile Residue is ≤5 mg, Residue on Ignition is not required, because the ash content is then extremely low and the test would have little significance.

Analogy

The heavy metals colorimetric method is like a quick water-quality test kit: after the color reagent is added, the color deepens if the water contains heavy metals above the limit. "any brown color produced... is NMT that in the tube containing the Standard lead solution" is an intuitive comparison meaning "the sample color is no darker than the standard solution color".

The buffering capacity test is like checking the water's "resistance to pH change": if the extract is clearly buffered (a large amount of acid or base is needed to reach pH 7.0), the plastic has released components with buffering capacity (such as carbonates, phosphates or organic acids) that may interfere with the pH of the drug product.

Heavy Metals Standard Calculation

Preparation of lead standard solution:
  Stock solution: 159.8 mg Pb(NO₃)₂ / 1000 mL = 0.1598 mg/mL Pb
  Dilution: take 10.0 mL → 100.0 mL (10-fold dilution)
  = 0.01598 mg/mL = ~10 µg/mL Pb

In the test, 2 mL of standard solution + 20 mL of Blank are added → 50 mL
Pb concentration = 2 mL × 10 µg/mL ÷ 50 mL = 0.4 µg/mL

Sample side: 20 mL of extract → 50 mL
If the sample color ≤ the standard tube → sample Pb ≤ Pb concentration of the standard tube
i.e., Pb in the extract ≤ 1 ppm (calculated on 20 mL of extract)

Practical Application

When a CDMO onboards a new plastic container supplier, the Physicochemical Tests are the core of the qualification assessment:

  • Request the complete USP <661> test report from the supplier and confirm that nonvolatile residue, heavy metals and buffering capacity all meet specification
  • Note the results for the different extraction media (water, alcohol, n-hexane); residues from organic extractants (alcohol/hexane) are usually higher than from water extraction and need to be assessed comprehensively
  • Containers for liquid preparations specifically require the Buffering Capacity test, so that the plastic does not affect the buffer system of the drug product (e.g., phosphate-buffered injection)
  • Compare the heavy metals limit of ≤1 ppm with the heavy metals limits for injectables (ICH Q3D), and confirm that the container's contribution does not exceed its share of the permitted daily exposure (PDE)

Practice Questions

  1. In the Buffering Capacity test, if the sample extract needs acid (HCl) to reach pH 7.0 while the blank needs base (NaOH), what is the maximum allowed total of the two volumes, in mL? What is the significance of this limit?
  2. If the Nonvolatile Residue result is 8 mg, is it necessary to continue with the Residue on Ignition test? Explain the basis for your judgment.
  3. The water-extract heavy metals result for an HDPE container for intravenous injection is 0.8 ppm (as Pb), which meets the USP limit of ≤1 ppm, but the ICH Q3D maximum daily dose requirement for the customer's drug product is stricter. How should the suitability of this container be assessed?

Section Summary

  • IR spectroscopy (Multiple Internal Reflectance) is the main tool for plastic identification, comparing the major absorption band positions of each resin with the USP RS; PET/PETG must be scanned to 400 cm⁻¹
  • DSC (Differential Scanning Calorimetry) confirms resin identity through the melting point; the heating/cooling program differs for each resin, and the allowed deviation ranges from ±4° (PET) to ±12° (PP)
  • Standard extraction conditions for Physicochemical Tests: Purified Water at 70°, 120 cm² (both sides) / 20 mL, 24 h extraction, two pre-washes
  • Three core tests: Nonvolatile Residue (≤15 mg/50 mL), Heavy Metals (≤1 ppm as Pb), Buffering Capacity (difference ≤10.0 mL)
  • Buffering Capacity is performed only when the container is used for liquid products; its purpose is to ensure that the packaging material does not interfere with the pH system of the drug product
  • PET/PETG additionally require: colorant extraction (absorbance <0.01), Total Terephthaloyl Moieties (≤1 ppm), Ethylene Glycol (≤1 ppm)

2.1 Biological Tests — Overview & Regulatory Context

Original Text

Biological Tests

The in vitro biological tests are performed according to the procedures set forth under Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vitro 〈87〉.

Materials that do not meet the requirements of the in vitro tests are not suitable for containers for drug products.

Plastic articles used for high risk products, such as those intended for inhalation, parenteral preparation, and ophthalmics, are tested using Biological Tests. Plastic articles used for low risk products, such as those intended for oral or topical administration, do not require Biological Tests.

"Biological reactivity testing in support of plastic packaging materials, components, and systems used for final pharmaceutical product packaging/delivery systems (drugs and drug/device combination products) provides baseline information and will often not be sufficient to assess the final suitability for use expectations of regulatory authorities. Thus, it is important to work with the appropriate regulatory authority for guidance regarding a product specific application."

Risk-Based Application of Biological Testing

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Purpose of Biological Tests: The Physicochemical Tests (Section 1) quantify the "amount" of extractables, whereas the Biological Tests assess the effect of these extractables on "living systems", namely cytotoxicity, tissue reactivity and systemic toxicity.

Even if a container passes the Physicochemical Tests, it may still contain low concentrations of highly toxic substances (such as certain residual catalysts); only Biological Tests can detect such problems.

Key Notes

The "necessary but not sufficient" principle: USP states explicitly that biological reactivity tests are "often not sufficient to assess the regulatory expectations for final suitability". This means:

  • Passing USP <87>/<88> = a baseline, not the end point
  • For high-risk products such as injectables and inhalation products, regulators (FDA, EMA) usually also require a full E&L evaluation
  • The packaging compatibility section of a marketing application (NDA/BLA/MAA) needs data support that goes beyond USP <87>/<88>

The recommendation to "consult with the relevant regulatory authority" is a rare direct reminder from USP: the standard itself is not enough, so go and confirm with the regulators.

Analogy

Physicochemical Tests are like checking whether a chef's knives are rusty (a quantitative question); Biological Tests are like having the chef cut ingredients with that knife and then testing whether the ingredients are toxic to humans (a functional effect).

"Biological tests are necessary but not sufficient": it is like a kitchen that has passed the basic food safety test but still needs an on-site inspection by the health authority to get a business license. USP <87>/<88> is the "health department test", and the regulators' specific requirements are the "business license".

Practice Questions

  1. A new inhalation product uses a PP container. Which USP tests are required? Are both Physical-Chemical Tests and Biological Tests needed?
  2. Why does USP specifically emphasize that biological tests are "often not sufficient"? What does this mean for a CDMO's preparation of CMC documentation?
  3. PE containers for oral preparations do not require biological tests, but if the oral preparation includes pediatric patients (0-2 years old), should additional evaluation be considered? What is the reason?
2.2 Biological Reactivity Tests In Vitro — <87>

Original Text

Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vitro 〈87〉 — Referenced Procedures

USP 〈661〉 references the in vitro biological tests as performed according to the procedures set forth under Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vitro 〈87〉. This chapter covers three assay types:

1. Elution Test (Elution-based Cytotoxicity)

Extracts of the test article are prepared and added to a mammalian cell culture. The elution test involves adding extract directly to cell culture medium and observing cell response. Results are scored on a reactivity scale:

  • Grade 0: No reactivity
  • Grade 1: Slight reactivity (subtle morphological changes)
  • Grade 2: Mild reactivity (not more than 20% of cells show reactive changes)
  • Grade 3: Moderate reactivity (up to 50% of cells affected)
  • Grade 4: Severe reactivity (more than 50% cells destroyed)

Materials pass the elution test if the reactivity is Grade 2 or less (NMT mild reactivity).

2. Agar Diffusion Test

The agar diffusion test (also called agar overlay method) places the test article directly on a cell monolayer covered with a thin layer of agar. Any cytotoxic extractables will diffuse through the agar and cause a zone of cell lysis. The lysis zone is measured and scored.

3. MEM Elution Test

The MEM Elution Test (Minimum Essential Medium Elution Test) is an extract-based cytotoxicity assay using L-929 mouse fibroblast cells. Test sample extracts are prepared in complete cell culture medium (MEM) at 37° for 24 h. The extract is then applied to the cell culture.

Acceptance Criteria Summary

"Materials that do not meet the requirements of the in vitro tests are not suitable for containers for drug products."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Differences among the three in vitro cytotoxicity tests:

  • Elution Test: an extract is prepared first, and the cells are then treated with the extract. It evaluates the toxicity of the eluted components and is more sensitive to water-soluble toxins
  • Agar Diffusion Test: a direct contact method in which the material is placed on top of the cells and toxins diffuse through the agar layer to the cells. It is more sensitive to lipid-soluble toxins (which can be released directly from the material surface)
  • MEM Elution Test: uses complete culture medium (containing serum) as the extraction medium, which is closer to the physiological environment; L-929 cells are the standard cell line of ISO 10993

Each of the three methods has its own advantages and disadvantages; usually at least one of them must be completed (chosen according to manufacturer/regulatory requirements).

Analogy

The Elution Test is like "soaking food in water for 24 hours, then watering a flower with that water to see whether the flower wilts" — an indirect assessment of water-soluble toxins.

The Agar Diffusion Test is like "placing the suspect item directly next to the flower to see whether the flower is affected by volatiles or contact" — a direct contact assessment.

A Grade 4 reaction (>50% cell destruction) is like an entire flower bed dying — the material is clearly unsuitable. Grade 0–2 is like flowers that are only slightly wilted and still within the acceptable range.

Key Notes

Significance of L-929 cells: L-929 is the NCTC Clone 929 mouse subcutaneous connective tissue cell line, the standard cell line for ISO 10993 (biological evaluation of medical devices) and USP biological tests. Reasons for choosing it:

  • Stable proliferation and high batch-to-batch consistency
  • Sensitive to cytotoxic substances
  • Widely used in laboratories worldwide, so data are comparable

"Nonconformance = unsuitable": This is rare mandatory language in USP. If the in vitro test fails, the material is regarded as unsuitable for use in drug containers from a regulatory standpoint; the material must be replaced and cannot be "remedied" by other tests.

Practical Application

Strategic considerations for biological tests in a CDMO's Supplier Qualification:

  • First request the test reports for the three <87> tests from the supplier and confirm that they pass
  • If the supplier provides only an ISO 10993-5 (cytotoxicity) report, confirm that the cell line used and the acceptance criteria are compatible with USP <87>
  • For injectables, the customer's CMC documentation usually also requires <88> in vivo test data (see Section 2.3)
  • A new material or new process (such as the addition of a UV stabilizer) should be re-evaluated for biocompatibility; the test results of the old material cannot simply be carried over

Practice Questions

  1. If a plastic gives Grade 2 (Mild reactivity) in the Elution Test, can this material be used for injectable containers? Explain the basis for your judgment.
  2. Which type of toxin is each of the Agar Diffusion Test and the Elution Test more sensitive to (water-soluble vs lipid-soluble)? Why does ISO 10993 recommend performing several methods together?
  3. A material passed the USP <87> in vitro tests, but FDA requested additional in vivo data during CMC review. Does this contradict the belief that "complying with USP = complying with regulatory requirements"?
2.3 Biological Reactivity Tests In Vivo — <88> Classification

Original Text

Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vivo 〈88〉 — Plastics Classification

USP 〈88〉 provides the Classification of Plastics based on their performance in a series of in vivo biological reactivity tests. This classification (Class I through Class VI) is used as a measure of the biocompatibility of plastic materials.

USP Plastics Classification System (Class I–VI)

In Vivo Test Methods (〈88〉)

  • Systemic Injection Test: Inject extract (saline and cottonseed oil) intraperitoneally or intravenously into mice; observe for 72 h for systemic reactions.
  • Intracutaneous Test: Inject extract intradermally into rabbits; observe for 72 h for local tissue reactions (erythema, edema).
  • Implantation Test: Implant test article strips subcutaneously or intramuscularly in rabbits; observe for macroscopic and histologic tissue reactions at defined time points (7, 14, 30 days).

Extracting Media for In Vivo Tests

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Logic of USP Plastics Class I–VI: The higher the class (Class VI), the more stringent the testing and the wider the range of application. Class VI is the "super certification": having passed the most stringent complete set of tests, the material can be used for almost any application (including injectable packaging and long-term implants).

Important understanding: Class VI is not a regulatory requirement, but a "confidence assurance" that a material supplier gives to customers. Most packaging materials for pharmaceutical injectables should meet at least the Class VI standard, which is also a common requirement in purchasing specifications ("USP Class VI compliant").

Four Extract Types

Extraction medium   Type of substance simulated   Use
────────────────────────────────────
0.9% NaCl    Water-soluble, ionic substances    Containers for IV injection
Vegetable oil   Lipid-soluble substances        Containers for oily injectables
20% ethanol  Polar organic substances           Containers for alcoholic preparations
PEG 400      High-molecular-weight polar substances   Polyol-based preparations

The four extraction media cover the solubility characteristics of most drug product matrices,
ensuring a comprehensive assessment of the in vivo toxicity of each type of extractable.

Key Notes

Positioning of in vivo vs in vitro tests:

  • In vitro (<87>): rapid screening, low cost, no animals required; the minimum requirement of USP <661> for high-risk applications
  • In vivo (<88>): a more comprehensive assessment of systemic and local tissue reactions; Class VI classification requires the complete set of in vivo tests

FDA's Container Closure System Guidance (2000) requires that the biocompatibility evaluation of injectable packaging include both in vitro and in vivo data; passing USP <87>/<88> is the baseline, but regulatory review also considers the results of E&L studies.

Analogy

USP Class I–VI is like the fire-resistance ratings of building materials: Class I is an ordinary office (low risk), and Class VI is the protective material of a nuclear power plant (highest risk, most stringent testing).

Requiring "USP Class VI" for pharmaceutical packaging containers is like requiring "the highest fire-rated materials" when building a hospital — not mandated by law, but industry best practice and the expectation of customers/regulators.
