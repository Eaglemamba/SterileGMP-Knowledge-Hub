1. Introduction

Original Text

Total organic carbon (TOC) is an indirect measure of organic molecules present in pharmaceutical waters measured as carbon. Organic molecules are introduced into the water from the source water, from purification and distribution system materials, from biofilm growing in the system, and from the packaging of sterile and nonsterile waters.

TOC also can be used as a process control attribute to monitor the performance of unit operations comprising the purification and distribution system. A TOC measurement is not a replacement test for endotoxin or microbiological control. Although there can be a qualitative relationship between a food source (TOC) and microbiological activity, there is no direct numerical correlation.

Apparatuses commonly used to determine TOC in water for pharmaceutical use have in common the objective of oxidizing the organic molecules in the water to produce carbon dioxide followed by the measurement of the amount of carbon dioxide produced.

All technologies must discriminate between the inorganic carbon (dissolved CO₂, bicarbonate) and the carbon dioxide generated from the oxidation of organic molecules. The discrimination may be accomplished either by:

  • Determining inorganic carbon and subtracting from total carbon (TC − IC = TOC), or
  • Purging inorganic carbon from sample before oxidation

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

TOC is one of the most important chemical purity indicators for pharmaceutical waters (Purified Water, WFI, dialysis water, etc.). The measurement logic is:

  • TC (Total Carbon) = organic carbon + inorganic carbon
  • IC (Inorganic Carbon) = dissolved CO₂ + bicarbonate
  • TOC = TC − IC (an indirect measurement of organic contaminants)

TOC reflects the sum of all organic molecules. It cannot identify individual contaminants, and it cannot replace endotoxin or microbiology testing.

Analogy

TOC is like the PM2.5 air quality index: it tells you the total amount of suspended particles in the air, but not which pollutant they are (pollen? soot? industrial exhaust?). Likewise, TOC tells you how much organic carbon is in the water, but not whether it comes from residual cleaning agents, extractables, or microbial metabolites. For this reason, TOC is normally used together with microbial testing and endotoxin testing, each supplying different information.

Key Notes

Four sources of TOC contamination:

  • Naturally occurring organic matter in the source water
  • Extractables from the materials of the purification and distribution system
  • Metabolic products of biofilm in the system
  • Leachables from packaging materials (specific to Sterile Water)

Identifying these four sources is essential for deviation investigations in a water system.

2. Procedures: Bulk Water

Original Text

The following sections apply to tests for bulk Purified Water, Water for Injection, Water for Hemodialysis, and the condensate of Pure Steam.

1.1 Instrumentation requirements: On-line or off-line calibrated instrument. Must have manufacturer's specified limit of detection of 0.05 mg/L (0.05 ppm) or lower of carbon.

1.2 Reagent water: TOC level NMT 0.10 mg/L. [A conductivity requirement may be necessary to ensure method reliability.]

1.4 System suitability solution: USP 1,4-Benzoquinone RS, 0.75 mg/L (0.50 mg/L carbon) in Reagent water.

1.5 Standard solution: USP Sucrose RS, 1.19 mg/L (0.50 mg/L carbon) in Reagent water.

1.9 Limit response:

  • rL = rS − rW
  • rS = response to Standard solution
  • rW = response to Reagent water control

1.10 System suitability:

  • rC = rSS − rW
  • rE = 100 × (rC / rL)
  • Suitable if rE is NLT 85% and NMT 115%

1.11 Procedure: Water sample meets requirements if rU is NMT rL.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Two reference substances are used in Bulk Water testing, each with its own purpose:

  • Sucrose: the standard solution, used to establish the Limit response (rL). It is easily and completely oxidized by TOC instruments; 0.50 mg/L carbon is set as the WFI limit.
  • 1,4-Benzoquinone: the system suitability solution, used to verify that the instrument can oxidize hard-to-oxidize organic compounds. The aromatic ring structure of benzoquinone is harder to oxidize than sucrose, so it is a challenging test of the instrument's oxidation capability.

Formula

Limit response calculation:
rL = rS - rW
Where:
  rS = instrument response of the standard solution (sucrose, 0.50 mg/L carbon)
  rW = instrument response of the reagent water control (blank)
  rL = corrected limit response

System suitability calculation:
rC = rSS - rW
rE = 100 × (rC / rL)

Acceptance criterion:
  rE = NLT 85% and NMT 115%

Water sample decision:
  rU ≤ rL → meets requirements
  rU > rL → does not meet requirements

TOC limits for pharmaceutical bulk waters:
  Purified Water: ≤ 0.50 mg/L (500 ppb)
  WFI: ≤ 0.50 mg/L (500 ppb)

Analogy

Imagine a filter machine (the TOC analyzer) that must be verified to handle wastewater of different difficulty. Sucrose (easily decomposed) is the "basic test" and benzoquinone (hard to decompose) is the "advanced test". If the instrument passes the system suitability test with benzoquinone (85–115%), it has enough oxidation capability to handle the various hard-to-decompose organic compounds in real samples.

Key Notes

The logic of "NMT rL": This is a limit test, not a precise quantitation. The response of the water sample only needs to not exceed the limit response of the standard solution; the instrument reading does not have to be a concentration value. This differs from conventional HPLC quantitation and is a distinctive design of the TOC chapter.

Reagent water quality is critical: If the TOC of the reagent water itself is too high (> 0.10 mg/L), rW will be elevated and rL will be underestimated, increasing the risk of false positives.

Practical Application

In on-line TOC monitoring of a WFI system, a CDMO typically sets two thresholds: an Action Limit (consistent with USP, ≤ 0.5 mg/L) and an Alert Limit (for example, 0.3 mg/L). When on-line data triggers the Alert Limit, sampling frequency is increased immediately and an investigation is started, to prevent the problem from escalating to the Action Limit and affecting the release of production batches.

3. Procedures: Sterile Water

Original Text

Applies to: Sterile Water for Injection, Sterile Purified Water, Sterile Water for Irrigation, Sterile Water for Inhalation. The sterile waters are derived from Purified Water or Water for Injection, and therefore have been determined to be compliant with the Bulk Water requirements before being stored and sterilized in the container.

2.9 Procedure (10-step decision tree):

  1. Measure TOC of Water sample; record rU
  2. Compare rU to rL1 for appropriate container volume
  3. If rU ≤ rL1: meets requirements, test complete
  4. If rU > rL1: compare to rL2
  5. If rU > rL2: does not meet requirements
  6. If rL1 < rU ≤ rL2: identify/quantify individual impurities >0.20 mg/L carbon
  7. If no impurities >0.20 mg/L: meets requirements
  8. If impurities >0.20 mg/L: evaluate for patient safety
  9. If evaluation shows safe: meets requirements
  10. If evaluation shows safety risk: does not meet requirements

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The TOC limits for Sterile Water are much more lenient than those for Bulk Water (up to 32 mg/L vs 0.5 mg/L), for the following reasons:

  • Container leachables: After high-temperature sterilization, glass or plastic containers release trace amounts of organic matter. This is inherent and should not by itself be a reason to reject the product
  • Inverse volume effect: Small containers (≤5 mL) have a larger contact surface area to volume ratio with the water, so the leachable concentration is relatively higher and the limit is therefore wider

10-Step Decision Tree for Sterile Water

Steps 1-3: Initial screening
  rU ≤ rL1 → meets requirements, end

Steps 4-5: Second confirmation
  rU > rL2 → does not meet requirements, end

Steps 6-7: Identification of individual impurities
  rL1 < rU ≤ rL2 →
  identify all individual organic impurities > 0.20 mg/L
  If no individual impurity exceeds the limit → meets requirements

Steps 8-10: Safety assessment
  Individual impurity exceeds the limit → assess patient safety
  Safe → meets requirements
  Safety concern → does not meet requirements

Container volume and limits:
  ≤5 mL: L1=32, L2=48 mg/L
  5-100 mL: L1=24, L2=36 mg/L
  >100 mL: L1=8, L2=12 mg/L

Analogy

The 10-step procedure for Sterile Water is like tiered screening at airport security: most passengers (step 3) pass straight through; a few go to secondary inspection (steps 4-5); very few need detailed questioning (steps 6-7); and a security officer makes the final decision (steps 8-10). This tiered procedure avoids the over-control of rejecting a whole acceptable batch because of trace amounts of container leachables.

Key Notes

Execution requirements for Step 6: If the TOC falls between L1 and L2, an appropriate analytical method (such as HPLC or GC-MS) must be used to identify and quantify each individual organic impurity exceeding 0.20 mg/L carbon. This is a major analytical workload, which suggests that preventive controls (ensuring container material quality and appropriate cleaning and sterilization procedures) are more economical and effective.

Practical Application

On a CDMO aseptic filling line, TOC testing of Sterile Water for Injection (SWFI) is a mandatory test for every batch release. Bulk Water TOC testing is usually performed before filling (to ensure the source water meets ≤0.5 mg/L), and Sterile Water TOC testing is then performed on the finished containers after filling (to verify that container leachables are acceptable). Together, the two tests form a complete water quality assurance system.

Chapter Summary

  • TOC definition: an indirect measurement of organic molecules in units of carbon; the measurement principle is to oxidize organic matter to CO₂ and then measure it
  • TOC limitations: cannot identify individual organic compounds; cannot replace endotoxin testing; cannot replace microbial testing
  • Bulk Water TOC limit: 0.50 mg/L carbon (shared by Purified Water and WFI)
  • Instrument requirements: detection limit ≤0.05 mg/L for Bulk Water; ≤0.10 mg/L for Sterile Water
  • Two reference substances: Sucrose (easily oxidized, sets the limit); 1,4-Benzoquinone (hard to oxidize, verifies instrument capability)
  • System suitability: rE = 85–115%; reflects the instrument's oxidation efficiency for hard-to-oxidize organic compounds
  • Sterile Water limits: three tiers by container volume (≤5, 5-100, >100 mL); container leachables are the main consideration
  • 10-step decision procedure: passes at L1 → end; between L1 and L2 → identify individual impurities → safety assessment
  • USP reference standards: USP 1,4-Benzoquinone RS and USP Sucrose RS
