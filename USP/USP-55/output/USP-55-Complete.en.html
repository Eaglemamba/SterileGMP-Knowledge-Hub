Learning Objectives

  • Explain the definition of a biological indicator (BI) and its central role in sterilization process validation
  • Understand the concept of the D-value and how a BIER vessel is used to determine microbial resistance
  • Correctly describe the sample collection and recovery procedures for the three BI types: paper/fiber carriers, other substrates, and spore suspensions
  • Understand the purpose of heat shock in the viable spore count procedure and the incubation condition requirements
Introduction — Biological Indicator Overview

Original Text

A biological indicator (BI) is a well-characterized preparation of a specific microorganism with a known resistance to a specific sterilization process. The correct use of BIs in the development, validation, and control of sterilization processes requires that their population and resistance be accurately known.

The population and resistance can be selected to confirm the adequacy of individual sterilization process conditions for an article. The recommendations of Sterilization of Compendial Articles 〈1229〉 should be followed for effective BI usage. The methods described below can be used to establish population and resistance, such that the response of the BI to the subject sterilization process is appropriate.

Although the BI manufacturers are required to maintain rigorous control of population and resistance using the number of replicates as specified below, the end users are not required to use the same number of replicates for verification of those determinations.

"Conduct all of the tests described in this chapter under appropriate microbiological laboratory conditions (see Microbiological Best Laboratory Practices 〈1117〉)."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Biological Indicator (BI): A product containing a specific, known concentration of spores, used to challenge a sterilization process. A BI acts as a "worst-case surrogate": if even the spores on the BI are killed, the sterilization process is effective.

Population (spore count): The total number of spores on each BI, typically 105–106 CFU.

Resistance: Expressed as the D-value, i.e. the time required under defined sterilization conditions to reduce the spore count by 90% (one log).

Analogy

Think of a BI as the "hardest-to-clean dish" in a restaurant kitchen: if even that dish comes out clean, the dishwasher (the sterilization process) works. The BI spores are the stubborn stain deliberately designed to challenge the dishwasher.

Key Notes

Requirements differ for manufacturers and end users: the manufacturer must verify the BI population and resistance strictly with the specified number of samples, whereas the end user (e.g. a CDMO site) may use fewer replicates for acceptance verification. Auditors often probe this distinction.

Practice Questions

  1. Why must both the spore population and the resistance of a BI be "accurately known"? If one of them is inaccurate, how does it affect the sterilization validation result?
  2. What is the relationship between USP <55> and USP <1229>? Why does the introduction of <55> cross-reference <1229>?
Total Viable Spore Count — Sample Collection / Recovery

Original Text

paper/fiber indicators

For paper/fiber carrier BIs, remove at least four test samples from their individual containers. Disperse the indicator into component fibers by placing the test samples in a sterile vessel containing 100 mL of sterilized Purified Water chilled to 2°–8°, and mechanically disrupt to achieve a homogeneous suspension. For self-contained BIs, aseptically remove four BI carriers from their containers and proceed as directed above.

indicators on other substrates

For all other biological indicators, remove at least four samples from their individual containers. Place the test samples in a sterile vessel containing 100 mL of sterilized Purified Water chilled to 2°–8°, and mechanically disrupt to achieve a homogeneous suspension of the spores in the water.

spore suspensions

For spore suspensions of BIs, prepare an appropriate serial dilution of the original spore suspension in sterilized Purified Water chilled to 2°–8°, in a sterile container, and follow the viable spore count procedures as specified below. The requirements of the tests are met if the average number of viable spores is within 50%–300% of the labeled count of the spore suspension.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three forms of BI:

  • Paper/fiber carrier: spores are deposited on a paper strip; the most traditional form, widely used in steam sterilization validation
  • Other substrates: spores are deposited on metal discs, plastic or similar materials, for special applications
  • Spore suspension: a liquid spore preparation that can be used to simulate the sterilization challenge inside a specific container

Self-contained BI: The spore carrier and the growth medium are packaged in the same container, so after exposure the unit is simply crushed and incubated. It is easier to handle, reduces contamination risk, and is widely used on the manufacturing floor.

Key Notes

Cold water (2°–8°) prevents the spores from germinating prematurely during collection and counting. Spore metabolism is halted at low temperature, which keeps the count accurate. If this detail cannot be explained during an audit, it will draw the auditor's attention.

The acceptance criterion for a spore suspension is 50%–300% of the labeled value. This wide range reflects the inherent variability of spore counting itself; it is not a quality problem.

Analogy

Collecting a BI sample and dispersing the spores evenly is like steeping a tea bag in water: you need the spores uniformly suspended in the water, not clumped and settled, so that the later plate counts are representative. The cold water keeps the spores "on hold" so they do not activate early.

Practice Questions

  1. Why are the recovery methods different for the three BI types? What factors determine which method you use?
  2. The acceptance criterion for a spore suspension is "50%–300% of labeled count". Why is the upper limit 300% rather than 200%? What does this asymmetric range tell you?
Viable Spore Count — Procedure

Original Text

Transfer a 10-mL aliquot of the suspension to a sterile tube. For BIs using spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus, Bacillus coagulans, and other thermophilic spore formers, heat the tube containing the suspension in a water bath at 95°–100° for 15 min (heat shock), starting the timing when the temperature reaches 95°.

For BIs containing nonthermophilic spore formers, heat the tube containing the suspension in a water bath at 80°–85° for 10 min, starting the timing when the temperature of the spore suspension reaches 80°. Cool suspensions rapidly in an ice-water bath at 0°–4°.

Transfer two 1-mL aliquots to suitable tubes, and make appropriate serial dilutions in sterilized Purified Water. Calculate the dilutions to yield 30–300 colonies on each plate in a pair, when treated as described below.

Prepare a separate series of plates for each aliquot. Place 1.0 mL of each selected dilution in each of two 15 × 100-mm Petri dishes. Within 20 min, add to each plate 20 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Agar Medium that has been melted and cooled to approximately 45°.

Incubate the plates in an inverted position at 55°–60° for thermophilic spore formers and at 30°–35° for nonthermophilic spore formers or at the optimal recovery temperature specified by the manufacturer. Examine the plates after at least 48 h. When evaluating vendor-supplied BIs, the viable spore count shall be between 50% and 300% of the manufacturer's stated value.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Heat Shock: A brief high-temperature treatment given to the spores before counting, in order to:

  1. Kill non-spore contaminants (vegetative cells) and eliminate interference
  2. Activate the spores to germinate (synchronize germination), improving counting accuracy

The heat shock conditions differ for thermophilic and nonthermophilic organisms, reflecting the difference in heat tolerance between the two groups.

Geobacillus stearothermophilus: The standard test organism for steam sterilization BIs. Because of its high resistance to moist heat, it is regarded as the most demanding challenge organism.

Formula and Calculation — Target Colony Count Range

Target colony count: 30–300 CFU/plate

Dilution factor logic:
Labeled BI spore count = N CFU/unit
Each plate needs 30–300 CFU
→ Dilution factor = N / 150 (taking the midpoint of the target range)

Example: BI labeled 10^6 CFU
→ dilute to 10^-4 (final dilution factor)
→ expected per plate = 10^6 × 10^-4 = 100 CFU ✓

Key Notes

The medium must be added within 20 minutes; this prevents the spores from settling unevenly while the sample waits. The incubation temperature setting (55–60°C vs 30–35°C) must strictly match the strain type; a wrong temperature leads to a low count and compromises the validation conclusion.

Practical Application

When a CDMO site receives BIs from a supplier, it must perform incoming verification. Under USP <55> you do not need to fully replicate the manufacturer's number of replicates, but you must confirm that the spore count is within 50–300% of the labeled value. If the result falls outside this range, contact the supplier immediately and initiate a deviation investigation; the BIs must not be used for sterilization validation.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is the target colony count per plate set at 30–300 rather than a wider range (e.g. 10–1000)?
  2. If the spore count result for a BI you received is 320% of the labeled value, what would you do?

Section Summary

  • A BI is a microbiological product with a known spore population and resistance, used to verify the effectiveness of a sterilization process; it must meet both the population and D-value specifications
  • The three BI types (paper/fiber, other substrates, spore suspension) each have a different collection and recovery method, but all are handled in cold water (2–8°C) to prevent premature spore germination
  • The viable spore count requires heat shock first (thermophiles 95–100°C/15 min; nonthermophiles 80–85°C/10 min); the target is 30–300 CFU/plate, and the acceptance criterion is 50–300% of the labeled value
  • Test requirements differ for manufacturers and end users; a CDMO site may use fewer replicates for incoming acceptance, but the conclusion must still meet the specification

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the minimum experimental design for D-value determination: 20 replicates, at least 5 exposure conditions, 100 tests in total

  • Know the three methods of D-value calculation (LSK, Survival Curve, Stumbo-Murphy-Cochran) and the requirement to use the chosen method consistently
D-Value Determination — Apparatus

Original Text

The test equipment used for the determination of microbial resistance ("D-value") is described in substantial detail in ISO 18472, Sterilization of Health Care Products—Biological and Chemical Indicators—Test Equipment. The details of individual Biological Indicator Evaluation Resistometers (BIERs) vary with the specifics of their design and the particular sterilization process for which they are used.

Provided that the performance of the BIER vessel meets the requirements of the ISO standard for exposure of the BI, design differences are acceptable. For single-phase sterilization processes where an acceptable BIER has not been defined, the D-value determination can be accomplished by adapting a BIER design intended for a sterilization process operating in the same phase.

"There are no current methods available for D-value determination for multiple-phase sterilization processes."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

D-Value: The time required under defined sterilization conditions to reduce the spore count by 90% (one log cycle), usually expressed in minutes. The larger the D-value, the more resistant the microorganism.

BIER Vessel (Biological Indicator Evaluator Resistometer): A precision laboratory instrument that exposes BIs under tightly controlled conditions (temperature, pressure, time) in order to determine their D-value. ISO 18472 specifies the performance standards for BIER vessels.

Formula and Calculation — Meaning of the D-Value

D-value definition:
The time required, under constant sterilization conditions,
to reduce the initial spore count by 1 log (i.e. 90%)

Example:
Initial spore count = 10^6 CFU
D-value = 2 minutes
→ After 2 min exposure: 10^5 CFU (1 log reduction)
→ After 4 min exposure: 10^4 CFU (2 log reduction)
→ After 12 min exposure: <1 CFU (theoretical sterility)

SAL 12-log calculation:
Sterilization time = D-value × (log N₀ + log 1/SAL)
= D × (6 + 6) = 12D
(SAL = 10^-6)

Key Notes

"No D-value determination method is currently available for multiple-phase sterilization processes" is an important statement of limitation. It means that processes such as combined dry heat/moist heat, or VHP combined with heat, cannot currently be described by their sterilization kinetics using the conventional D-value. This has a major impact on the validation strategy for novel sterilization technologies.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can "design differences" in BIER vessels be accepted as long as they meet the ISO 18472 performance requirements? What regulatory philosophy does the relationship between the two reflect?
  2. If your facility uses a new combination sterilization process and USP <55> says there is no D-value method, how would you validate that process?
D-Value Determination — Procedure

Original Text

Carry out the tests for D-value at sterilization conditions consistent with those intended for use. Use 20 replicate test sample BIs in their original individual containers, subjected to at least five exposure conditions for a total of 100 tests.

The number of exposure conditions is chosen to provide a range of observations from NLT one labeled D-value below the expected survival time through NLT one labeled D-value above the expected kill time. Place each group on a separate suitable sample holder that permits each sample to be exposed to the prescribed sterilizing condition at a specific location in the sterilizing chamber of the BIER.

The differences in sterilizing times over the series are as constant as feasible, and the difference between adjacent times is NMT 75% of the expected D-value. Test procedures for the use of BIER vessels for the evaluation of microbial resistance are defined in a series of ISO standards under the 11138 series.

The D-value exposure conditions for alternative material carriers are the same as the conditions used to determine the D-value for paper carriers. If a manufacturer's label permits usage of the BI carrier with multiple sterilization methods, then data on D-value, survival time, and kill time will need to be provided by the manufacturer for each sterilization method.

For BIs that are spore suspensions, conduct D-value determinations for each of the microorganisms provided as a liquid spore crop suspension. Where the suspension is placed on or in a substrate such as an elastomeric closure or formulated product, its resistance may differ from that determined in Purified Water. That difference may be significant to the usage of the BIs and appropriate measurements made before use in sterilization validation activities.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The logic of 20 replicates × 5 conditions = 100 tests: D-value determination needs sufficient statistical confidence. Each exposure time point uses 20 BIs, spanning the range from "all survive" to "all killed", so that a reliable survival curve can be plotted and the D-value calculated.

Time interval NMT 75% of D-value: The spacing between adjacent exposure times must not exceed 75% of the expected D-value. This keeps the data points dense enough, avoids large gaps along the curve, and improves calculation precision.

Analogy

D-value determination is like testing the resistance of a city wall: you need impact tests of varying intensity and duration, covering the whole range from "wall intact" to "wall collapsed", to estimate the true breaking point accurately. The 20 BIs at each time point are your observation samples, and the 5 different time conditions ensure that you cover the entire collapse process.

Key Notes

"When spore suspensions are placed on substrates such as rubber stoppers, the resistance may differ from that determined in pure water" is a very important reminder on the manufacturing floor of a CDMO. If your drug product stoppers (elastomeric closures) come into contact with a BI spore suspension, their sterilization kinetics may change. This means that matrix effects must be considered in sterilization validation involving the container-closure system.

Practical Application

When performing autoclave validation, a CDMO site usually purchases BIs from a supplier with a labeled D-value (e.g. G. stearothermophilus, D121 = 1.5 min). The user does not need to re-determine the D-value, but must confirm that the spore count of the incoming BIs matches the label, and must perform acceptance verification using the same calculation method (LSK or the supplier-specified method).

Practice Questions

  1. If a BI supplier claims that its product is suitable for both steam and EO sterilization processes, what data should the supplier provide?
  2. Why is the time interval "NMT 75% of D-value"? If the interval is too large (e.g. twice the D-value), how does that affect the results?
Recovery, Calculation, and Survival/Kill Time

Original Text

Recovery

After completion of the sterilizing procedure for BIs and within a noted time (NMT 4 h), aseptically remove and add each BI to a suitable medium (see media in Sterility Tests 〈71〉) to submerge the BI completely in a suitable tube. For self-contained BIs, the paper strip is immersed in the self-contained medium according to manufacturers' instructions, within a noted time NMT 4 h.

Incubate each tube at the optimal recovery temperature appropriate for the BI. Observe each inoculated medium-containing tube at appropriate intervals for a total of 7 days after inoculation. Where growth is observed at any particular observation time, further incubation of the test sample(s) concerned may be omitted.

Where Clostridium sporogenes or another anaerobic microorganism is used as a BI, methods for preparation, inoculation, and recovery methods and media must be adapted to accommodate the use of these anaerobic spore formers.

Calculation

The determination of D-values of BIs can be performed using the Limited Spearman-Karber (LSK), Survival Curve Method, or Stumbo-Murphy-Cochran procedures. When the BI has been purchased, use the same method as that defined by the BI manufacturer to subsequently determine D-values. The use of an alternate method can result in differences that are more an artifact of the method than a variation in the performance of the BI.

Survival Time and Kill Time

Take two groups of BIs, each consisting of 10 test samples, in their original, individual containers. Expose the samples for the required survival time, then subject the second group similarly for the required kill time. Recover BIs as described above. The Survival Time and Kill Time should be provided by the BI manufacturer and verified by the end user.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Survival Time: After exposure for this time, all 10 BI samples must show microbial growth (i.e. the spores survive). This indicates that the sterilization process is still "insufficient".

Kill Time: After exposure for this time, all 10 BI samples must show no growth (i.e. all spores are killed). This indicates that the sterilization process is "sufficient".

Relationship between the two: Survival Time < actual kill < Kill Time; the D-value estimate must fall within this range.

Formula and Calculation — Comparison of the Three D-Value Calculation Methods

Method                  Characteristics
─────────────────────────────────────
Limited Spearman-       Based on quantal assay (all or none)
Karber (LSK)            No intermediate survivor counts needed
                        Relatively simple calculation

Survival Curve          Uses survivor/non-survivor data directly
Method                  Plot the curve and calculate the slope
                        Suited to continuous data

Stumbo-Murphy-          More complex statistical method
Cochran                 Accounts for counting uncertainty
                        More common in academic research

⚠ Important: for BIs purchased from a supplier,
you must use the calculation method specified by the supplier.
Do not switch methods on your own!

Key Notes

The NMT 4-hour window: After sterilization exposure, the BIs must be transferred to the growth medium within 4 hours. A delay may cause spore death (especially of spores that are damaged but not yet dead) or contamination, producing a false negative (i.e. wrongly concluding that sterilization was effective). This is a critical time point that must be explicitly specified in the SOP.

7-day incubation observation: Observation is required for 7 consecutive days, and may stop as soon as growth is seen. This duration is especially important for G. stearothermophilus (a thermophile), because its growth is slower.

Analogy

Survival Time and Kill Time are like temperature testing for a roast chicken: you first determine "at what temperature the chicken is still raw (survival)", then "at what temperature it is definitely cooked (kill)", and your roasting temperature must then fall between the two, close to the kill side. Likewise, the target exposure time of a sterilization process must be well beyond the Survival Time and fall after the Kill Time.

Practical Application

In sterilization cycle development at a CDMO site, the Certificate of Analysis (CoA) from the BI supplier must include the D-value (and the calculation method used), Survival Time, Kill Time and spore count. Your process validation protocol (PQ protocol) must clearly state that you will use the same calculation method specified by the supplier to confirm the D-value. Auditors easily spot inconsistencies such as "the supplier used LSK, but you used Survival Curve".

Practice Questions

  1. If, in a Survival Time test, 1 of the 10 BIs shows no growth, what does this mean? Is there a problem with your Survival Time setting?
  2. Why does USP require that "the calculation method must be consistent with the BI manufacturer"? What specific risks could switching methods bring?
  3. Clostridium sporogenes requires special anaerobic handling. For which type of sterilization process is this most relevant to BI validation?
USP <55> — Comparison with the ISO 11138 Series of Standards

Section Summary

  • D-value determination requires a BIER vessel meeting ISO 18472; minimum requirement: 20 replicate BIs × 5 exposure conditions = 100 tests, with time intervals NMT 75% of D-value
  • BI recovery must be carried out within 4 hours after sterilization exposure, with 7 days of incubation observation; the resistance of spore suspensions on different substrates may differ from that determined in pure water and must be confirmed before use
  • The D-value can be calculated by three methods: LSK, Survival Curve or Stumbo-Murphy-Cochran; when purchasing BIs from a supplier, you must use the same calculation method as the supplier
  • Survival Time (all growth) and Kill Time (all no growth) each require 10 BI samples, supplied by the manufacturer and verified by the user; no D-value determination method currently exists for multiple-phase sterilization processes
