Learning Objectives

  • Understand the central role of Water for Injection (WFI) in depyrogenation by rinsing and its quality requirement (<0.25 EU/mL)
  • Understand rinse water temperature management, in particular the "danger zone" (8°–55°), and what it means for rinsing process design
  • Recognize the risk of re-pyrogenation and the strategies to prevent it
  • Know the five critical process parameters specified by USP and be able to present them correctly in a validation protocol
Introduction

Original Text

INTRODUCTION

The production of parenteral products requires that products be sterile, but also that they are free from harmful levels of pyrogens, or fever causing agents. For the purposes of the 〈1228〉 series, the term "depyrogenation" refers to the destruction or removal of bacterial endotoxins, the most prevalent and quantifiable pyrogen in parenteral preparations.

Effective depyrogenation depends on the product and the method of removal and/or destruction. Although depyrogenation of heat-stable articles may be best accomplished by dry heat, heat-labile equipment, components, ingredients, or materials such as drug product containers and closures, and some medical devices may be depyrogenated through physical means such as rinsing.

"This chapter provides an overview of the depyrogenation process, its validation, and routine process control."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Why Is Depyrogenation by Rinsing Needed?

Two depyrogenation strategies:

  • Destruction: dry heat depyrogenation (e.g., 250°C / 30 min) for heat-resistant items (glass vials, aluminum caps); LPS is hydrolyzed and inactivated at high temperature
  • Removal: depyrogenation by rinsing, used for heat-labile materials such as elastomeric stoppers, certain plastic components and medical devices

Heat-labile: materials that deform, degrade or lose function at high temperature (e.g., 250°C dry heat). Elastomeric stoppers are the classic example: they cannot withstand dry heat, so rinsing is the only practical physical means of depyrogenating them.

Key positioning: <1228.4> focuses on the mechanism of "removal"; the core strategy is "wash away + dilute", not "destroy".

Analogy: Dry Heat vs. Rinsing

Imagine your tableware is covered in a harmful substance (endotoxin):

  • Dry heat depyrogenation is like putting the tableware in an oven: high heat destroys the contaminant directly. It suits ceramics and metal (glass vials, aluminum foil), but plastic tableware (rubber stoppers) would melt in the oven.
  • Depyrogenation by rinsing is like rinsing the tableware with a large flow of hot water: the tableware is not damaged, but the contaminant is washed away and diluted.

The challenge with rinsing is that you must make sure the rinse water itself is clean; otherwise, rinsing with dirty water simply leaves the contaminant on the tableware in a different form.

Key Point: The Role of <1228.4>

Depyrogenation by rinsing is the standard depyrogenation method for heat-sensitive items such as rubber stoppers, plastic components and medical devices, and it is very common in sterile filling facilities.

In a CDMO's aseptic process, almost every rubber stopper must be washed and depyrogenated before use, and rinsing is one of the regulatory bases for that step.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can't rubber stoppers be depyrogenated by dry heat? In day-to-day production at a sterile filling site, how is stopper depyrogenation usually integrated into the stopper washing process?
  2. <1228.4> uses the wording "destruction or removal". Which category does rinsing fall into, and how does this affect the design of validation studies?
Rinsing Process

Original Text

RINSING PROCESS

Rinsing is the most common means of reduction or removal of bacterial endotoxins on closures (such as elastomeric stoppers), medical devices, and other materials that are not compatible with the temperatures used in dry heat depyrogenation. The mechanism for this method of depyrogenation is removal of the endotoxin, followed by dilution.

The process of general rinsing to remove pyrogens is accomplished by using high-purity water such as Water for Injection preferably above 60°. Multiple rinses may be necessary with proper controls to ensure that the Water for Injection does not become contaminated with Gram-negative bacteria and bacterial endotoxins during processing.

The use of high-purity rinse water is often the key to successful reduction of bacterial endotoxin activity on the surfaces of the materials being processed. At a minimum, the rinse water quality should meet the bacterial endotoxin limit of Water for Injection, which is <0.25 USP Endotoxin Units (EU)/mL. Using water that risks having bacteria grow in it may result in the deposition of bacterial endotoxins onto the items, or in the case of depyrogenated articles, the re-deposition (re-pyrogenation) of endotoxins from the rinse water itself.

It is important to note that starting with Water for Injection, while essential, does not ensure that such water remains suitable for use through the duration of the depyrogenation process. When cooled from the elevated circulation temperatures used for its distribution, Water for Injection is vulnerable to microbial proliferation. Water for Injection held at temperatures below 55° and above 8° should be considered at risk for microbial contamination unless sterilized and held in a sterile vessel. Therefore, holding water for more than 3–4 h within this temperature danger zone is an unsuitable practice. Systems that recirculate Water for Injection can be effective but process conditions and water storage times and temperatures should be carefully controlled and validated.

Solvents other than Water for Injection, such as caustic alkali or detergents, have been used for depyrogenation by rinsing. The concern with these solvents is the removal of residuals that may ultimately be harmful to patients or the product, so care must be taken to test for residuals during validation of the method.

Critical Process Parameters for Rinsing

For any process that purports to depyrogenate by rinsing, there are a number of critical factors that must be defined and controlled during the validation study and beyond:

  1. Solvent description, including normality or concentration if caustic alkali or detergents are used; if high-purity water is used, the source should be clearly described
  2. Solvent temperature
  3. Solvent pressure, particularly where rinsing is used to depyrogenate glass or other articles that remain stationary during the process
  4. Solvent flow rate through the system
  5. A justification for the recirculation of solvents

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Rinsing Mechanism and WFI Quality Requirements

Mechanism of depyrogenation by rinsing: physical removal + dilution. Unlike the chemical destruction achieved by dry heat, rinsing simply carries surface LPS away, so the quality of the rinse water and the number of rinses directly determine the final residual endotoxin level.

WFI (Water for Injection) quality requirements:

  • Bacterial endotoxin limit: <0.25 EU/mL (USP <661> Water for Injection specification)
  • Recommended rinse temperature: >60°C (to suppress microbial growth)
  • USP recommends keeping WFI circulation systems at ≥70°C or refrigerated (≤8°C), avoiding the "danger zone" in which microorganisms multiply rapidly

Re-pyrogenation: if the rinse water itself contains Gram-negative bacteria, LPS is released when the bacteria die and is deposited onto the already cleaned items, causing depyrogenation to fail. This is the most hidden failure mode of rinsing.

WFI Temperature Management: The Danger Zone

WFI safe temperature ranges:
  Hot circulation:   ≥ 70°C (USP recommended) / ≥ 60°C (operating minimum)
  Refrigerated:      ≤ 8°C (must be sterilized and held in sterile containers)

Danger zone (rapid microbial growth):
  8°C < T < 55°C

Maximum water hold time within the danger zone:
  ≤ 3–4 hours

→ WFI held longer than this should not be used for depyrogenation rinsing
  (unless it has been sterilized and stored in sterile, closed containers)

WFI endotoxin limit:
  < 0.25 EU/mL (USP minimum requirement)
  Actual production control targets are usually tighter (e.g., <0.1 EU/mL)

Key Point: Rinse Water Temperature Management Is a Critical Control Point

"Using WFI is enough" is one of the most common misconceptions in CDMO facilities. The core warning from USP is:

The starting quality of WFI does not guarantee that quality is maintained during the process.

A typical risk scenario:

  • WFI drawn from an 80°C circulation loop and used at room temperature quickly cools to 30–40°C
  • If it sits at this temperature for more than 3–4 hours, Gram-negative bacteria can multiply rapidly
  • Rinsing rubber stoppers with this contaminated WFI results in "re-pyrogenation": new endotoxin is carried onto the stoppers

CDMO solution: when designing the water circuit of the washer, make sure WFI stays at ≥60°C throughout the rinsing process, or is used immediately after being drawn.

Analogy: The WFI Temperature Danger Zone

WFI temperature management is like the "danger zone" concept in food safety:

Food safety rules say cooked food must not be kept between 4°C and 60°C for more than 2 hours, because bacteria multiply exponentially in this range.

The same logic applies to WFI: 8°C–55°C is the microorganisms' "warm comfort zone". Think of WFI as a pot of hot soup: either keep it piping hot (≥60°C) or refrigerate it right away (≤8°C); the temperatures in between are the most dangerous.

Core Concepts: The Five Critical Process Parameters (CPPs)

The five key factors that USP <1228.4> says must be defined and controlled in validation correspond to Critical Process Parameters (CPPs) in the process validation framework:

  1. Solvent Description: source of the WFI and water quality standard; if caustic or detergent is used, its concentration must be specified
  2. Solvent Temperature: directly affects depyrogenation effectiveness and microbial control; ≥60°C is recommended
  3. Solvent Pressure: especially important for static-rinsed glassware or equipment, where pressure determines whether the water reaches all contact surfaces
  4. Flow Rate: affects rinse contact time and mechanical scrubbing action, and is directly related to the LRV
  5. Recirculation Justification: if recirculated rinse water is used, it must be demonstrated that recirculation does not cause endotoxin accumulation or recontamination

Practical Application

A CDMO's stopper washing validation protocol needs to be built on USP <1228.4>.

Design corresponding to the five CPPs:

  • Solvent: WFI (from the site WFI system, acceptance standard <0.25 EU/mL) + final rinse without detergent
  • Temperature: final rinse WFI ≥80°C (above the 60°C recommendation, for an added safety margin)
  • Pressure: stopper washer spray pressure [X] bar, ensuring that all stopper surfaces are effectively rinsed
  • Flow rate: WFI volume per kg of stoppers ≥[X] L, ensuring sufficient dilution
  • Recirculation: this protocol does not use recirculated water (fresh WFI is drawn each time), so no recirculation justification is needed

Practice Questions

  1. Why does USP specifically emphasize WFI temperature management, rather than simply requiring WFI that meets specification? What microbiological principle does this reflect?
  2. If a site uses caustic (NaOH solution) as the detergent for depyrogenation rinsing, what additional testing is required in validation, and why?
  3. The mechanism of depyrogenation by rinsing is "removal + dilution". Which variables therefore determine the final depyrogenation effect?

Section Summary

  • Depyrogenation by rinsing is the preferred method for heat-labile materials (rubber stoppers, plastics, medical devices); its mechanism is "physical removal + dilution"
  • Rinse water must be WFI (<0.25 EU/mL), and operation at ≥60°C is recommended to maintain microbial control
  • Re-pyrogenation is the biggest hidden risk of rinsing: microbially contaminated WFI may deposit endotoxin onto items that have already been cleaned
  • WFI should not be held for more than 3–4 hours in the 8°C–55°C danger zone; this is a hard constraint on process design
  • USP requires that five critical process parameters be defined and controlled in validation: solvent description, temperature, pressure, flow rate and recirculation justification

Validation

Original Text

VALIDATION

Validation of depyrogenation by physical means is not different, in principle, from the validation of other depyrogenation methods. Endotoxin indicators, which are articles representative of the material to be depyrogenated spiked with a known amount of endotoxin, are prepared in a laboratory (see Endotoxin Indicators for Depyrogenation 〈1228.5〉). Quantitation of endotoxin activity prior to and subsequent to the rinsing process will demonstrate the effectiveness of the process in removing endotoxins.

To prepare endotoxin indicators, inoculate each indicator with a known level of activity of Control Standard Endotoxin (CSE), USP Endotoxin RS (RSE), or Naturally Occurring Endotoxin (NOE), which is calibrated against RSE before processing. Generally speaking, a small volume (e.g., <100 µL) of a highly concentrated analyte (purified LPS or NOE) is inoculated onto a section of the article that is the most difficult for the rinse solvent to reach. For example, stoppers are generally inoculated onto the product contact portion of the stopper. A low volume of inoculum is used to ensure rapid drying. The inoculated indicators may be dried in a unidirectional air flow cabinet or other validated means to hasten drying and limit the possibility of extrinsic microbial contamination.

Historically, 1000 EU/article has been the target spike value, but depending on: 1) the level of endotoxins historically found on the material, 2) the amount of recoverable endotoxins observed after the drying process, and 3) the "safe" level of endotoxin activity that must be attained post-processing, an inoculum at a level other than 1000 EU may be justified.

Because the prepared endotoxin indicators may be mixed with many uninoculated articles (e.g., stoppers), it is essential to provide a means to identify the indicators. For example, if the stoppers used by the company are gray, an identical stopper that is a different color but has the same elastomeric material, or a stopper of the same composition but a different shape, could be used for the indicators. If a stopper of different color or other identifying mark is not available, the inoculated indicator stoppers might be placed in individual small mesh bags.

Once dry, the endotoxin indicators are analyzed for the recoverable level of endotoxins activity using the Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) assay. To determine the recoverable activity, follow the process for extracting endotoxin from medical devices (see Medical Devices—Bacterial Endotoxin and Pyrogen Tests 〈161〉). Submerge the article in Water for Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET) prewarmed to 37°. Allow the article(s) to remain in contact with the Water for BET for an hour. Agitation such as intermittent vortexing or sonication may be added to enhance recovery.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Design Logic of Endotoxin Indicators

Endotoxin Indicator: an item of the same material and shape as the item being validated, artificially inoculated with a known amount of endotoxin, used to simulate a "worst-case" depyrogenation challenge.

Three endotoxin standards that can be used:

  • RSE (Reference Standard Endotoxin): the official standard designated by USP, with the greatest regulatory credibility
  • CSE (Control Standard Endotoxin): an alternative standard calibrated against RSE, suitable for routine validation
  • NOE (Naturally Occurring Endotoxin): natural LPS collected from a real environment; more representative of real-world conditions, but its activity must first be calibrated against RSE

Strategic choice of inoculation site: choose the "hardest-to-rinse location" (worst-case location); for rubber stoppers this is usually the product contact portion. This ensures that depyrogenation capability is validated under the most stringent conditions.

Key Point: Indicators Must Be Identifiable

This detail is often overlooked but is critical: in an actual validation, the endotoxin-inoculated indicators are loaded into the stopper washer together with a large number of uninoculated stoppers.

After the run, you must be able to pick out exactly those few indicators from a pile of nearly identical-looking stoppers in order to carry out the subsequent extraction and LAL testing.

If indicators cannot be distinguished from ordinary stoppers, the entire validation study is invalid.

Identification methods suggested by USP (in order of practicality):

  1. Use stoppers of the same material but a different color
  2. Use stoppers of the same material but a different shape
  3. Place them in individual mesh bags

Analogy: Validation Logic Is Like a "Food Safety Challenge Test"

The logic of depyrogenation validation is like the "bacterial inoculation test" in a restaurant hygiene audit:

A food safety auditor does not wait for a restaurant to become contaminated before assessing risk; instead, they artificially inoculate a test surface with a known amount of bacteria, run the cleaning procedure, count the residual bacteria, and calculate the cleaning effectiveness.

Depyrogenation validation works the same way:

  • Artificially inoculate rubber stoppers with 1000 EU of LPS (simulating worst-case contamination)
  • Run the rinsing process
  • Measure the residual EU and calculate the log reduction

Controls & Log Reduction Calculation

Original Text

Controls

Two sets of controls are recommended for validation studies:

  1. Testing of uninoculated indicators — provide data on the resident level of endotoxin activity on the lot of stoppers to be tested.
  2. An appropriate number of endotoxin indicators should be retained by the laboratory and used as positive controls. After the depyrogenation process is completed, extract both the processed units and positive control indicators as described above and test using a qualified BET method.

Any activity detected on the retained positive controls is called "recoverable" activity and activity detected on the processed indicators is called "residual" activity. The following factors should be considered during the extraction of endotoxin activity:

  1. Inoculated units should not be pooled for analysis. The conceptual basis for this study design is to determine the depyrogenation capability of the process, and pooling extracts from a number of units would not allow evaluation of reproducibility and also bias the results.
  2. The test result must account for all sources of dilution. For example, the units for an LAL test are EU/mL. If the extraction volume is 1 mL/stopper, then the result of the test is really EU/stopper. But, for example, if the extraction volume is 5 mL/stopper, then each milliliter of the extract is a 1:5 dilution of any endotoxin activity that has been extracted from the indicator.

Log Reduction Calculation

The log reduction is calculated using the following formula:

Log reduction = (log10 recoverable activity) − (log10 residual activity)

For example, if a laboratory detects 4500 recoverable EU/stopper in the controls and 0.3 residual EU/stopper, the log reduction is calculated as:

Log reduction = (log10 4500 EU) − (log10 0.3 EU)
Log reduction = (3.65) − (−0.52) = 4.17

Historically, laboratories added sufficient inoculum to each endotoxin indicator unit so that at least 1000 EU could be recovered in the retained controls. However, there are factors that the analyst and the validation team should be aware of: CSE tends to adsorb to surfaces and therefore may be difficult to remove from some materials resulting in control recoveries of less than 50%. In some cases, the recovery may be substantially less than 50%. This is to be expected and history has taught us that this does not impact the usefulness or effectiveness of a depyrogenation study.

A 3-log reduction of the recoverable endotoxin is a common target for acceptable depyrogenation effectiveness. However, this may not be attainable in all test systems or under all test conditions nor may it be necessary. Where a 3-log reduction is not attainable it is important to carefully evaluate endotoxin risk in the process based extensively on inherent endotoxin burden.

The target should consider the use of the article and the potential contribution to the final endotoxin limit established for the drug product. Elastomeric closures are generally received with little or no detectable endotoxin activity. It is possible that a 2-log reduction by rinsing could be sufficient for some packaging components, excipients, or other materials.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Logic of the Two Control Groups

Uninoculated Indicators: measure the resident endotoxin naturally present on the stoppers themselves. This value confirms that the validation is carried out against a known baseline and that the inoculation level (1000 EU) is far above the natural background, so it does not confound the results.

Positive Controls (inoculated indicators that are not treated): represent "how many EU can be recovered if there is no rinsing", that is, the recoverable activity.

Key distinction:

  • Recoverable activity: the EU extracted from the positive controls, representing the actual quantifiable starting value after inoculation
  • Residual activity: the EU extracted from treated indicators after rinsing, representing the amount still remaining after rinsing
  • Log Reduction = log(recoverable) − log(residual)

Note: log reduction is not calculated from the "inoculated amount of 1000 EU" but from the "actual recoverable amount", because some CSE may be lost during drying or extraction, and using the recoverable amount is the fair basis.

Log Reduction Formula and Example

Log reduction = log₁₀(recoverable EU) − log₁₀(residual EU)

Example (numbers from the USP text):
  Recoverable activity:  4500 EU/stopper
  Residual activity:     0.3 EU/stopper

  log₁₀(4500) = 3.653
  log₁₀(0.3)  = −0.523

  Log reduction = 3.653 − (−0.523) = 4.18 ≈ 4.17

Interpretation: the rinsing process removed more than 4 logs of endotoxin activity
  (i.e., less than 1/10,000 of the original activity remained)

Common acceptance criterion: ≥ 3-log reduction
Special case: some materials may accept ≥ 2-log reduction
(requires scientific justification, supported by an endotoxin risk assessment of the final product)

Key Point: No Pooling, and the Dilution Factor Must Be Corrected For

No pooling: each inoculated indicator must be analyzed individually; extracts from several indicators must not be combined and tested together. Reasons:

  • Pooling dilutes the data of individual indicators and masks variability between units
  • If one indicator has a very high EU value, pooling can average out other anomalous "low-EU" results
  • The ability to assess reproducibility is lost

Dilution correction: LAL results are reported in EU/mL, but what we need is EU/stopper (EU on each stopper).

If one stopper is extracted with 5 mL of Water for BET and LAL reads 0.06 EU/mL, the residual activity = 0.06 EU/mL × 5 mL = 0.3 EU/stopper.

Analogy: Flexibility of 3-log vs. 2-log

3-log reduction (99.9% removal) is the industry convention, like "food safety requires a 99.9% kill rate of pathogens". But this standard is based on:

historical practice + safety margin design, not an absolute law of physics.

The logic behind USP's flexibility: if the stoppers originally carry almost no endotoxin (natural background <1 EU/stopper), then even a 2-log reduction leaves a final residual (<0.01 EU/stopper) that may fully meet the final endotoxin limit of the drug product.

This is why USP requires that the "acceptance criterion be determined based on a product risk assessment" rather than imposing 3-log on all materials across the board.

Practical Application

A CDMO QA engineer is reviewing the validation report of a stopper washer and finds the following data:

  • Inoculation level: 1000 EU/stopper
  • Positive control recoverable amount: 380 EU/stopper (38% recovery)
  • Residual after rinsing: 0.12 EU/stopper

Calculation:

Log reduction = log₁₀(380) − log₁₀(0.12) = 2.58 − (−0.92) = 3.50 LRV

Does it meet the acceptance criterion (≥3 LRV)? Yes.

Does the recovery of only 38% affect the validity of the validation? No. USP notes that CSE tends to adsorb to surfaces and that recovery below 50% is normal and does not affect the validity of the validation study. The key is to use the "actual recoverable amount", not the "inoculated amount", to calculate the LRV.

  3. Why is it more correct to use "recoverable activity" rather than the "inoculated amount (1000 EU)" when calculating log reduction? Explain from a statistical perspective.
Routine Process Control

Original Text

ROUTINE PROCESS CONTROL

Proactive control of Gram-negative bacterial contamination is essential to the production of safe parenteral products. The use of properly generated, stored, and distributed Water for Injection ensures that the most prevalent ingredient in most products is not a significant source of endotoxin.

Once a depyrogenation process has been effectively developed and validated, it must be maintained in that state to ensure continued acceptability. Chapter 〈1228〉 details the general practices that are appropriate for all depyrogenation systems. This is accomplished by a number of related practices that are essential for the continued use of the process over an extended period of time.

"The essential practices to maintain validated status include calibration, physical measurements, periodic endotoxin assessment on incoming materials, ongoing process control, change control, preventive maintenance, and periodic reassessment and training."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Seven Elements for Maintaining the Validated State

Validation is not "done once and valid forever"; it is a state that must be continuously maintained. USP lists seven essential practices for maintaining the validated state:

  1. Calibration: periodic calibration of measuring instruments such as thermometers, pressure gauges and flow meters, to ensure CPPs are measured correctly
  2. Physical Measurements: periodically confirm that process parameters (temperature, pressure, flow rate) remain within the validated ranges
  3. Periodic Endotoxin Assessment on Incoming Materials: periodic endotoxin monitoring of rubber stoppers, packaging materials and similar items, to confirm stable supplier quality
  4. Ongoing Process Control: batch monitoring and trend analysis
  5. Change Control: any change to the process, materials or equipment must be assessed for its impact on depyrogenation effectiveness
  6. Preventive Maintenance: scheduled maintenance of the stopper washer, water system and similar equipment
  7. Periodic Reassessment and Training: periodic process review and periodic training of operators

Key Point: Change Control Is the Critical Node for Maintaining the Validated State

In day-to-day CDMO operations, what most easily undermines a validated depyrogenation state is the "seemingly minor change":

  • Changing the stopper supplier (even with the same specification, surface characteristics may differ between suppliers)
  • Water pressure distribution changing after nozzle replacement or repair on the stopper washer
  • Fluctuations in water temperature or quality after WFI system maintenance
  • Revision of the stopper washing SOP (e.g., adjusting the number or duration of rinses)

Every change should trigger a formal change control process to assess whether partial or full revalidation is needed.

Analogy: Maintaining the Validated State Is Like Keeping a Car Roadworthy

A new car that has passed its factory tests (validation) does not stay "safe to drive" forever. Keeping it safe requires:

  • Regular servicing (preventive maintenance)
  • Regular roadworthiness inspections (periodic reassessment)
  • Re-evaluating safety whenever someone modifies the car or replaces parts (change control)
  • Drivers renewing their licenses (training)

The depyrogenation rinsing process is exactly the same: validation establishes "passed at factory", but staying compliant requires continuous, systematic management.

Practical Application

After annual maintenance, the nozzle assembly of a CDMO's stopper washer was replaced with a new model (the vendor says "same function, just more durable"). The QA engineer needs to assess whether this affects the validated depyrogenation state.

Assessment steps:

  1. Open a change control: record the change, the vendor's statement, and the differences in technical specifications between the old and new models
  2. Risk assessment: the CPPs that nozzle replacement may affect (water pressure distribution, flow rate) and the sensitivity of the corresponding validation
  3. Physical Measurements: after replacement, measure the pressure and flow rate at each nozzle of the washer to confirm they are still within the validated ranges
  4. Decision: if pressure and flow rate match the validated parameters, record "no impact on validated state"; if out of range, initiate partial revalidation

Section Summary

  • Core of the validation design: use endotoxin indicators (inoculated with a known amount of LPS) + two controls (uninoculated indicators + positive controls) to quantify endotoxin activity before and after the process
  • Log Reduction = log(recoverable) − log(residual), calculated from "recoverable activity" rather than the "inoculated amount" so as to reflect the actual extraction efficiency
  • The common acceptance criterion is ≥3 LRV, but it may be adjusted based on a scientific risk assessment (some materials may accept ≥2 LRV); CSE recovery of <50% does not affect the validity of the validation
  • Post-validation routine process control must include seven elements: calibration, physical measurements, incoming material endotoxin monitoring, ongoing process control, change control, preventive maintenance, and periodic reassessment and training
  • Change control is the most easily overlooked critical node for maintaining the validated state of depyrogenation by rinsing; any change involving solvent, equipment or procedure should be assessed for its potential impact on depyrogenation effectiveness
