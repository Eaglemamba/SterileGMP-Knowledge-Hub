Introduction

Original Text

It is widely recognized that the current growth-based sterility tests with an incubation period of at least 14 days (see Sterility Tests ⟨71⟩) are not suitable for products with a short shelf-life or for products prepared for immediate use, which are usually infused into patients before the completion of the test. These short-life products include compounded sterile preparations (CSPs), positron emission tomographic (PET) products, and cell and gene therapies, which require a new generation of risk-based approaches that include rapid microbial tests.

For a general discussion of the factors other than sterility testing that contribute to sterility assurance, see Sterility Assurance ⟨1211⟩. It should be noted that as with alternate test methods, the referee test in the event of a dispute is ⟨71⟩.

If a microbial test is conducted, patient safety is best served through the completion of a test that detects microbial contamination prior to product use.

The rapid microbial tests (RMTs) should be risk-based so the stakeholder can select the preferred technology for their intended use and balance user requirement specifications (URS) including time to result, specificity, limit of detection (LOD), sample size, and product attributes.

For example, many radiopharmaceuticals, due to the short half-life of radiotracers, would benefit most from a real-time microbial test, while CSPs and cell therapy products, due to their short beyond-use dating, would benefit from an overnight test or at least one that is completed within 48 h.

This general informational chapter discusses the needs of those who manufacture/prepare and test products with a short shelf life and their URS, and includes a brief discussion of some suitable methods for risk-based rapid microbial testing for the release of short shelf-life sterile products.

"If a microbial test is conducted, patient safety is best served through the completion of a test that detects microbial contamination prior to product use."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is a 14-Day Sterility Test Not Enough?

Think about it: the traditional ⟨71⟩ sterility test requires at least 14 days of incubation. But many short-shelf-life products have a life cycle far shorter than that:

  • PET radiopharmaceuticals - the half-life can be anywhere from a few minutes to a few hours (for example, 18F-FDG has a half-life of about 110 minutes)
  • Compounded sterile preparations (CSPs) - the beyond-use date (BUD) is usually only a few hours to a few days
  • Cell and gene therapies - living-cell products cannot be stored for long, and patients may need treatment urgently

This means the product has already been injected into the patient before the sterility test is finished. That is why we need rapid microbial tests (RMT).

Analogy: Fresh Sushi vs. Canned Food

The traditional ⟨71⟩ sterility test is like a food testing lab sending canned goods for a 14-day incubation test - the cans have a shelf life of years anyway, so waiting is fine.

But if you run a sushi restaurant, the sashimi sushi made fresh each day has only a few hours of peak freshness. You cannot wait 14 days to find out whether today's ingredients had a problem - the customers have long since finished eating. What you need is a rapid test that tells you the result before the food goes out.

USP ⟨1071⟩ tells you how, for these "fresh sushi" products, to choose a suitable rapid test technology using a risk-based approach.

Key Point: The Status of an Informational Chapter

USP chapters numbered >1000 are informational chapters, unlike the mandatory chapters numbered <1000 (such as ⟨71⟩ Sterility Tests). This means ⟨1071⟩ provides guidance and recommendations, not legal requirements.

Note, however, that even though ⟨1071⟩ is not mandatory, the referee test is still ⟨71⟩. When results are disputed, the final decision still rests on the traditional 14-day sterility test. RMT supplements the compendial test; it does not replace it.

User Requirement Specifications (URS)

Original Text

Selection of an appropriate technology for the rapid microbial testing for the release of a short-life product should be a risk-based decision. The URS of different technologies include:

  • As short as possible time to result, ideally in real time or less than 24 hours, preferably prior to the administration of the product
  • Ability to detect, preferably less than 100 colony-forming units (cfu) in the test sample
  • Ability to detect a wide range of viable microorganisms in a product
  • Sample quantity, i.e., minimum number of articles tested and quantity per container tested that does not consume a large proportion of the available product; whenever feasible, manufacturers should consider assay requirements during process design
  • Aseptic test material handling, i.e., closed systems to reduce inadvertent contamination during testing
  • Availability of instruments and reagents from multiple vendors
  • Availability of reference standards, negative and positive controls, appropriate for technologies that use signals other than the colony-forming unit
  • Ease of use/simplicity of test and data interpretation
  • Low rates of false positive and false negative results
  • A method suitability testing strategy for each specific product
  • Improved patient safety arising from:
    • Completion of the test prior to administration
    • Tests that provide progressive monitoring and reports of a detection of sterility test failure
    • Ability to identify the detected microorganisms
  • Robustness and reliability of equipment and reagents used in the testing
  • Sample preparation suitable for both manual and automated methods

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Five Key Dimensions of a URS

When selecting an RMT technology, you need to balance risk across the following five dimensions:

  • Time to Result - the faster the better; ideally real-time or <24 hours
  • LOD (limit of detection) - the more sensitive the better; the target is <100 cfu
  • Specificity - the broader the range of detectable microorganisms, the better
  • Sample Size - the less product consumed, the better (especially for precious cell therapy products)
  • Reliability - low false-positive and false-negative rates

Key insight: there is often a trade-off among these five dimensions. For example, a rapid method may sacrifice sensitivity, and a highly sensitive method may need a larger sample size.

Analogy: Choosing a Security System

Imagine you are buying a security system for a factory. Your requirements list (URS) might include: fast detection (real-time alarms), the ability to detect all kinds of intruders (distinguishing people from animals), not too many cameras (cost control), a low false-alarm rate (so you are not woken up all night), and availability from several vendors (so you are not locked in).

No single system is best on every dimension - you need to make a risk-based choice according to your factory's specific risks (location, valuable assets, how often people come and go). The selection of an RMT follows the same logic.

Key Point: Practical Considerations for CDMOs

The "closed system" requirement deserves special attention. In a CDMO environment, the sterility test itself carries a risk of introducing contamination. Using a closed sampling and testing system can:

  • Reduce the risk of false positives during sampling
  • Meet the expectations of EU GMP Annex 1 for isolator technology
  • Reduce reliance on cleanroom grade

Availability from multiple suppliers is also an important consideration for a CDMO - you do not want to be forced to stop release because of equipment failure or a reagent shortage at a single supplier.

  2. The URS mentions "progressive monitoring and reports of sterility test failure". How does this differ from the traditional single reading at the end of incubation? What are the implications for patient safety?
The Concept of Risk-Based Microbiological Monitoring and Release Testing

Original Text

A review of the URS for an RMT shows that some risk-based decisions would need to be made especially in terms of time to results, LOD, sample size, and range of microbes detected to allow the use of such tests prior to administration. The stakeholders should carry out a risk assessment for choosing an RMT in cases where the current compendial sterility test is unsuitable.

Benefits may include, for instance:

  • Use of RMT in cases where a substantial risk to patient survival exists when the product is administered too late. One striking example from the clinical literature is that of bloodstream infections that are rapidly progressive infections with mortality rates of up to 40% and in which each day of delay in administrating antibiotics leads to a 10% increase in mortality.
  • Use of growth-based RMTs with continuous reading applied for “negative to date” on risk release because fast growing microorganisms can be detected earlier, and if a failure is detected this would enable the clinician to intervene sooner with the patient.
  • Use of non-growth based RMTs with LOD above 1 cfu that is amplified by growth in microbiological media but very fast time to results. The ability to detect contamination, in real time, prior to the administration of the short-life product may be considered more important than detection of a single colony-forming unit in the product.
  • Other advantages of nongrowth-based RMT methods also may include the inability to be affected by antibiotics in the test sample and the detection of culture-negative infectious agents.

In addition, RMTs or other rapid microbiological methods (RMMs) may be used as in-process controls prior to the final product release sterility test to provide faster information on the effectiveness of microbial controls and the early detection of gross contamination.

For the risk assessment, one consideration that may be overlooked is the relative risk to the patient based on the volume of the product injected or infused and the site of administration. The greater the volume and the more invasive, the greater the risk of blood stream infection for the patient.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Risk Trade-Off Between Sensitivity and Speed

The core idea of this section is: the perfect is the enemy of the good.

Traditional thinking pursues the ultimate sensitivity of LOD = 1 cfu (which ⟨71⟩ can theoretically achieve). But ⟨1071⟩ offers a more pragmatic view:

  • An imperfect test that can be completed before administration is more valuable than a perfect test whose result arrives 14 days after administration
  • Even if the LOD is 100 cfu rather than 1 cfu, as long as the result is available before administration, the patient is meaningfully protected
  • A progressive release strategy based on "Negative to Date" lets you take action while you wait for the final result

Analogy: Rapid Test in the ER vs. Precision Testing in the Lab

A patient with suspected influenza comes to the emergency room. You can choose:

  • Option A: a rapid test kit, result in 15 minutes, 80% sensitivity
  • Option B: PCR nucleic acid testing, result in 2 days, 99% sensitivity

Which would the ER physician choose? Option A, of course. A "roughly correct" result obtained quickly can save more lives than waiting for a "perfect result". The risk-based logic of RMT is exactly the same.

Key Point: Route of Administration and Risk Classification

Table 1 reveals a dimension that is often overlooked: not all injectables carry the same risk.

  • Intradermal: very small volume (≤0.1 mL), lowest risk
  • Intrathecal: goes directly into the spinal fluid, so even a small volume is high risk
  • Large-volume intravenous infusion (IV >250 mL): large volume delivered directly into the bloodstream, highest risk

For a CDMO, this means that when you build an RMT strategy for different clients' different products, you should consider the final route of administration when deciding the acceptable LOD and speed requirements.

Key Point: The Clinical Urgency of Bloodstream Infection

The source text cites a striking figure: for bloodstream infections, each day of delay in starting antibiotics increases mortality by 10%, and overall mortality can reach 40%.

This is why "progressive monitoring" matters so much - if an RMT can automatically detect an abnormality early in incubation (rather than waiting until the 14-day incubation ends to read the result), clinicians can intervene sooner, giving the patient antibiotics and fluid resuscitation and avoiding septic shock.

Core Concept: The Three-Way Trade-Off in Technology Selection

Table 2 is the most operationally valuable table in all of ⟨1071⟩. It clearly shows how each technology performs on three dimensions:

  • Speed champions: Gram stain (30 min), direct ATP detection (30 min), nucleic acid methods (2–4 h) - but with a poorer LOD
  • Sensitivity champion: ⟨71⟩ (theoretically 1–3 cfu) - but it takes 14 days
  • Balanced: ATP bioluminescence (with amplification), respiration methods, solid-phase cytometry - LOD down to 1–10 cfu, but takes several days
  • Fast with moderate sensitivity: solid-phase cytometry (SPC) - reaches 1–10 cfu in 2–3 h, with a wide sample size range

No single technology is best on every dimension - which is why a risk-based selection is needed.

Analogy: Choosing a Means of Transport

Choosing an RMT technology is like choosing a means of transport:

  • High-speed rail (nucleic acid methods) = fast, but small passenger capacity and expensive tickets
  • Cargo ship (⟨71⟩) = large capacity and low cost, but extremely slow
  • Truck (ATP with amplification) = moderate speed, moderate capacity, the most versatile
  • Bicycle (Gram stain) = extremely fast but very limited carrying capacity (LOD too poor)

You would not move house by bicycle, nor wait for a cargo ship to take you to work. The choice depends on your specific scenario.

Key Point: Technology Evaluation Strategy for CDMOs

If your CDMO is evaluating the introduction of an RMT system, Table 2 should be your starting point. Suggested steps:

  1. Take stock of the product portfolio: how many of your clients' products are short-shelf-life products?
  2. Confirm the route of administration: combine this with the risk classification in Table 1
  3. Define the URS: determine the acceptable LOD and time to result based on product characteristics
  4. Screen the technologies: use Table 2 to match candidate technologies
  5. Evaluate suppliers: confirm multi-supplier availability of equipment and reagents

Situations When ⟨71⟩ Is Unsuitable for Product Release Testing

Original Text

Sample Size Consideration

The sample size tested may need to be reduced based on either the sample processing capability of the technology or the need to conserve the much-needed product.

The minimum quantity of product used for each medium and the minimum of units to be tested relative to batch size is found in ⟨71⟩, Table 2 and Table 3. It is widely understood that for large pharmaceutical product batch sizes, the amount of units tested are not statistically based and has a reduced capability of detecting low contamination levels in individual product lots. However, with the smaller batch sizes of many short-lived products, this limitation will be reduced because the proportion of products tested relative to the batch size will increase.

This traditional sampling plan is not appropriate for a cell therapy product. For example, if 10 individual 60-mL IV bags of cells were prepared, then 4 bags would be sampled with 20 mL taken from each bag. With this sampling scheme 40% of the batch would be consumed for sterility testing representing not only a large economic loss, but also more importantly, a huge loss of therapeutic product that may prevent administration of a dose adequate to treat the patient.

In contrast, if 40 individual 1-mL vials were consumed for the sterility testing of a 40,000-vial batch of an injectable drug product, this would represent only 0.1% of the batch.

Reducing the sample size and the number of units tested will reduce the sensitivity of the sterility test.

Calculated from: p = (1 − p)n = qn

Alternative sampling plans have been proposed in other compendia. One recommended approach to sterility testing of cell therapy products for a batch size of less than 40 units is found in European Pharmacopoeia 9.0 chapter 2.6.27 Microbiological Examination of Cell-Based Preparations.

The contamination test sample size for a cell preparation with a volume between 10 and 1000 mL would be 1% of the total volume; for a cell preparation with a volume between 1 and 10 mL, it would be a sample size of 0.1 mL; for a cell preparation less than 1 mL, the preparation would not be tested. As suggested in 21 CFR 610.12 (Sterility), in-process testing using rapid methods may be used in lieu of final product release testing when fully justified.

In a similar fashion as cell-therapy preparations, the sample quantity and sampling plan for PET radiopharmaceuticals must also accommodate the limited number of vials (usually one) and the volume of product produced in a batch (usually less than 15 mL). If the batch is comprised of a single container, the sterility test sample size is at least 1% of the total batch volume.

Limit of Detection

Within the limitations of preparing inocula from a cell suspension with one or more colony-forming units, growth-based sterility tests can be shown to have at least a theoretical LOD of 1–3 cfu based on a Poisson distribution. Setting an LOD of a single viable cell with all technologies is an unrealistic barrier of entry for any sterility test, especially when the signal is not the colony-forming unit that is amplified by cultural enrichment.

Ability to Detect a Wide Range of Microorganisms

Although all the analytical platforms should have the ability to detect a wide range of bacteria, yeasts, and molds, it is of practical importance to demonstrate that technology chosen for an RMT is capable of detecting microorganisms implicated in sterility test failures, infection outbreaks, and product recalls associated with either CSPs, radiopharmaceuticals, cell therapies, or manufactured pharmaceuticals.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Sampling Dilemma

This passage reveals the fundamental contradiction in sterility testing of short-shelf-life products:

  • Conventional drugs: 40 vials taken from a 40,000-vial batch (0.1%) - the loss is negligible
  • Cell therapy: 4 bags (20 mL) taken from a 10-bag batch (40% consumed!) - patients may receive only half of their therapeutic dose as a result

This is why the European Pharmacopoeia (EP 2.6.27) takes a more pragmatic sampling approach for cell-based products: for 10–1000 mL products, only 1% of the total volume is sampled; for 1–10 mL products, only 0.1 mL; and products of <1 mL are not even tested.

Implication for CDMOs: when you accept an order to fill a cell therapy product, you must plan the sampling strategy at the process design stage to ensure that enough therapeutic dose remains.

Analogy: Quality Testing of Rare Jewels

Imagine you are a gemologist. To assess the purity of a batch of 10,000 industrial diamonds, you can pull 40 at random for destructive testing - a loss of less than 0.5%.

But what if the client sends 10 one-of-a-kind imperial emeralds? You cannot take 4 of them away and cut them open for inspection! You need a rapid, non-destructive test method - such as an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer - which may be less precise than destructive testing but at least will not ruin the client's gemstones.

The role of RMT for short-shelf-life products is like the role of non-destructive testing for rare gemstones: getting the best possible result from a limited sample volume.

Statistical Interpretation: What Tables 3 & 4 Mean

The probability of passing a sterility test is given by: P(pass) = qn

where q = 1 - p (p is the contamination rate) and n = the number of units sampled

Key takeaways:

  • With a batch contamination rate of 1%, sampling 20 vials still gives an 82% chance of passing the sterility test - showing that the statistical detection power of the traditional test is weak
  • When a CSP is sampled at only 6 vials, even a 10% contamination rate has a 53% chance of passing - close to a coin toss
  • When the sample number drops from 20 to 6, detection power falls sharply

Conclusion: a sterility test cannot assure sterility through statistical sampling - it must be combined with strict process control (GMP) and environmental monitoring (EM).

Key Point: An LOD above 1 cfu Does Not Mean Failure

USP states explicitly that requiring all RMT technologies to reach a detection limit of 1 cfu is an unrealistic barrier of entry. This is an important regulatory signal:

  • The 1–3 cfu LOD of the traditional ⟨71⟩ relies on multiplication of microorganisms in the culture medium (cultural enrichment)
  • Non-growth-based signals (ATP, DNA/RNA, fluorescent labeling) have no such natural amplification mechanism, so their LOD is inherently higher
  • The point is not whether the LOD equals 1 cfu, but whether, within the overall risk assessment, the RMT can provide meaningful safety information before administration

Rapid Microbial Test Methods for the Release of Sterile Short-Life Products

Original Text

Technologies recommended based on their match to the URS discussed above, suitable for an RMT are listed alphabetically as follows:

  • Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) bioluminescence
  • Flow cytometry
  • Isothermal microcalorimetry
  • Nucleic acid amplification
  • Respiration
  • Solid phase cytometry

Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) Bioluminescence

This is a well-established technology with luminometers and reagents available from multiple instrument manufacturers. The energy from living cells is stored as ATP and can be measured as light when exposed to luciferase from the American firefly. Each ATP molecule consumed by luciferase produces 1 photon of light. The result detected by a luminometer is typically expressed in relative light units (RLU) and is instrument, reagent, and organism dependent.

The ATP content of different microorganisms ranges from 2 to 4 × 10−18 mole/cfu for gram-negative bacteria, 5 to 8 × 10−18 mole/cfu for gram-positive bacteria, and 300 to 800 × 10−18 mole/cfu for fungi.

Given the high signal-to-noise ratio of the measurement and the background ATP in microbiological culture media, the microbiologically relevant instrument detection limit in broth is in the order of 5000 RLU equivalent to approximately 103 cfu.

This LOD will detect the presence of microorganisms at levels which are 3–4 logs less within an aliquot of the media than that is required for visual detection of growth in the media. For a rapid microbial test for the release of sterile short-life products, an enrichment culture either in liquid media to reach a threshold ATP level or on a membrane filter on solid media for the formation of microbial colonies could be used with an incubation time of 2–7 days.

Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry may be used to detect fluorescently labeled viable microbial cells after an enrichment culture step of 24–48 h. A labeling reagent consisting of either a fluorogenic substrate or vital stain is used to differentiate viable cells from dead cells and cellular debris. While bacteria are very small and may be hard to distinguish from cell debris, they can be differentiated by size, shape, and fluorescent intensity.

Cell viability is indicated by the ability of the intact cell membrane to retain a fluorochrome generated by nonspecific cellular esterase, or by labeling the cell with nucleic acid-specific vital stains. A laser illuminates each cell in the flow stream and the emitted light is detected by a dual photomultiplier array. The signal is digitized and interpreted by discrimination software. The LOD for this technology may be >1 cfu and an enrichment step may be necessary.

Isothermal Microcalorimetry

Isothermal microcalorimeters monitor enthalpy changes in closed vials (systems) related to microbial metabolic activity and growth. With current instruments, 104 active microbial cells can release enough heat to be detected and enrichment is needed for detection (2–7 days to result). Recently its application in pharmaceutical microbiology has been evaluated, although its specific application to release testing of sterile products has not been established yet.

Nucleic Acid Amplification

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has the potential to monitor the exponential phase of PCR through 36–48 cycles of amplification using universal primers and probes (termed “pan-bacterial” and “pan-fungal” methods) to estimate the initial quantity of the target DNA, which is in turn proportional to the number of microbial cells in the test sample.

Unlike DNA, cellular RNA is turned-over rapidly metabolically and would be a better indicator of viable microorganisms. For example, Escherichia coli contains 2 molecules of DNA and 2 × 104 molecules of 16S rRNA per cell. This process is achieved by the conversion of RNA into a complementary copy of DNA (cDNA) by the enzyme reverse transcriptase and the cDNA can be analyzed in real time.

Alternatively, for DNA-based PCRs, a sample pretreatment with ethidium monoazide or propidium monoazide may also provide the capability to differentiate live from dead microbial cells, or free microbial DNA may be removed from a test sample by a centrifugation/washing step and the bacterial pellet used for analysis.

Realistically, an LOD of a single viable cell is probably an insurmountable challenge, especially for a test relying on a DNA/RNA target and universal primers. Generally, the LOD ranges from 10 to 1000 viable cells/mL in a sample and, in some reported cases, from 10 to 100 viable cells/mL.

Recently it was shown that PCR may actually achieve detection of microorganisms with a limit of 102 to 103 cfu/mL in a sample containing a high concentration of up to 106 mammalian cells/mL without the need for pre-incubation in microbial growth media.

Adding a growth-based enrichment step for at least 24–48 h and comparing the PCR results before and after cultural enrichment may provide a practical solution for sterility testing.

Direct comparison of growth-based and nucleic acid amplification-based assays is complicated by the fact that nucleic acid amplification-based assays also detect non-viable organisms and are a measure of microbial genome copy number, not colony-forming units. For that reason, the LOD for a nucleic acid amplification-based assay should be defined in terms of genome equivalents per milliliter.

Non-growth based RMTs for the release of sterile short-life products like nucleic acid amplification may have additional advantages:

  • With close to real-time testing, the test will be completed before the short-life products are infused into a patient
  • Detection of culture-negative infectious agents
  • The test is minimally affected by antibiotics in the test sample
  • The test is less sensitive to background resulting from animal cell lysis (e.g., particles, ATP) as compared to other technologies since specific microbial genes are targeted

Respiration

This broad category ranges from classical respirometers, to gaseous headspace analyzers to automated blood culture systems. Aerobic and anaerobic broth formulations allow for the recovery of most microorganisms responsible for blood stream infections within a 5-day incubation. With some instruments, this includes incubation at both 20°–25° and 30°–35° as in ⟨71⟩.

This technology has been successfully extended to sterility testing of cell therapy products using a 7-day incubation as an alternative to the compendial sterility test for lot release.

Other instruments are available to detect and enumerate respiring microorganisms. For example, tunable diode laser absorption spectroscopy (TDLAS) can measure oxygen (O2) depletion or carbon dioxide (CO2) increase in closed units containing growing microorganisms in culture medium.

[Note—All the systems of the respiration platform require microbial growth and metabolic activity for detection, i.e., the usual time of 2–7 days to obtain results is required. However, when the results may be progressively monitored to detect a sterility test failure earlier in the incubation period, that is a huge advantage with short-life products.]

Solid Phase Cytometry

There are instrument systems based on solid phase cytometry combining fluorescent labeling and solid phase laser scanning to rapidly enumerate viable microorganisms in filterable liquids. Microorganisms are collected by filtration on 0.45-micron polyester membranes and treated with background and viability stains.

The filters are scanned in a cytometer by a high-speed, 488 nM argon laser. Multiple photomultiplier tubes, processed to differentiate between labeled microorganisms and background noise, based on size, shape, and fluorescence intensity, detect fluorescence. The scan is displayed as a map that identifies the position of the fluorescent events that are verified using an epifluorescence microscope with an automated motorized stage to locate the individual events.

The system is claimed to detect individual viable microorganisms in 2–3 h. It should be noted that most cell therapy products are non-filterable, so this technology may not be compatible with these types of products.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Six Detective Tools

Imagine you are a detective looking for traces left by a suspect at a crime scene. The six RMTs are like six different forensic tools:

  • ATP bioluminescence = luminol spray - it glows immediately when sprayed, so a reaction tells you "something is here", but not what it is
  • Flow cytometry = surveillance video playback - you can see who came and went, but you first have to pull up the footage for a specific time window
  • Isothermal microcalorimetry = infrared heat sensing - detects whether a living person hiding in the dark is giving off body heat
  • Nucleic acid amplification (PCR) = DNA identification - the most precise, but it takes time, and it also picks up the DNA of the dead
  • Respiration methods = measuring CO2 in a sealed room - the level rises if someone is breathing inside
  • Solid-phase cytometry = filtering and then scanning with ultraviolet light - fast, but suitable only for liquid samples

Key Point: The Four Unique Advantages of PCR

Among all RMTs, nucleic acid amplification (PCR) has four advantages that no other method can match:

  1. Near-real-time results (2–4 h) - can be completed before most short-shelf-life products are administered
  2. Detects culture-negative pathogens - some microorganisms cannot grow on conventional media at all (such as Tropheryma whipplei)
  3. Not affected by antibiotics - particularly important for compounding pharmacies producing injectable antibiotic solutions
  4. Not affected by animal cell lysates - because it targets microbe-specific genes, host cell debris in cell therapy products does not cause false positives

But there is also a critical limitation: PCR cannot distinguish live from dead organisms. DNA is amplified even if it comes from dead microorganisms. EMA/PMA pretreatment or the use of RNA (rRNA) as the target is needed.

Deeper Understanding: The Two Modes of ATP Bioluminescence

ATP bioluminescence is one of the most mature and widely used RMT technologies. It has two operating modes:

  • Direct detection mode: result in 30 minutes, but the LOD is only about 103 cfu - suitable for scenarios requiring immediate screening
  • Pre-incubation mode: the sample is first incubated in culture medium for 2–7 days until an ATP threshold is reached and then tested, giving an LOD of 1–10 cfu - sensitivity is greatly improved but speed is sacrificed

An interesting figure: the ATP content of fungi (300–800 × 10−18 mol/cfu) is 100–200 times that of Gram-negative bacteria (2–4 × 10−18 mol/cfu). This means ATP methods are inherently much more sensitive for detecting molds than bacteria.

Key Point: Limitations of Solid-Phase Cytometry

Solid-phase cytometry (SPC) is one of the fastest (2–3 h) and most sensitive (down to 1 cfu) technologies, and looks perfect. But USP points out one critical limitation: most cell therapy products cannot be filtered.

SPC relies on 0.45 μm membrane filtration to collect microorganisms. But the mammalian cells in cell therapy products (10–30 μm in diameter) clog the membrane, so filtration itself is not feasible. This is a good example of why technology selection must take product attributes into account.

Method Suitability Testing

Original Text

The method suitability requirements for a growth-based sterility test are given in ⟨71⟩. The suitability of the test must be demonstrated for each product to be tested.

The recovery of the USP challenge organisms at a level less than 100 cfu, in the presence of residual product, is demonstrated as clearly visible growth in the microbiological growth media for either the direct inoculation or membrane filtration method.

With signals other than the colony-forming unit derived from laboratory culture (e.g., nucleic acid, ATP, and fluorescent labeling of viable microbial cells), the results from testing actual samples may give results that are not equivalent to that using other technologies.

However, method suitability testing will confirm that product residues in the analyte will not inhibit the enzymatic steps associated with the signal generation by the rapid method.

"Method suitability testing will confirm that product residues in the analyte will not inhibit the enzymatic steps associated with the signal generation by the rapid method."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Method Suitability vs. Method Validation

Method suitability testing is the most critical validation step in implementing an RMT. Its core question is simple:

"Will your product residues interfere with signal generation in the rapid method?"

  • Traditional ⟨71⟩: inoculate <100 cfu of USP challenge organisms in the presence of product residues and check whether obvious growth appears in the medium
  • RMT methods: confirm that product residues do not inhibit the enzymatic reaction steps (such as luciferase ATP detection, PCR DNA amplification, and the staining reaction of fluorescent labeling)

Every product must be validated individually - you cannot assume that because product A passed, product B will be fine too.

Analogy: Pregnancy Test Strips and Product Interference

Method suitability testing is like checking whether a pregnancy test strip still works properly in different "environments".

A pregnancy test strip is designed to detect the hormone hCG. But if the urine contains a lot of protein, blood, or certain drug components, they may interfere with the chemical reaction on the strip, causing a false negative (showing negative although the woman is pregnant) or a false positive.

Method suitability testing adds a known quantity of the "target" (challenge organism) under simulated real conditions to confirm that your detection method can still detect it correctly in the presence of product residues. If the product contains bacteriostatic agents, antibiotics, or high levels of protein, you may need to add a dilution step or switch to membrane filtration.

Key Point: Method Suitability Strategy for CDMOs

In a CDMO environment you may manufacture dozens of products with different formulations at the same time. Suggested method suitability strategy:

  1. Grouping: place products with similar compositions in the same group and select the "hardest to detect" product as the representative for method suitability testing
  2. Worst-case testing: if a product contains known PCR inhibitors (such as heparin) or ATP interferents (such as fluorescent substances), validation must be performed under worst-case conditions
  3. Change control: method suitability must be reassessed after a formulation or process change - a newly added excipient may alter the interference profile

Section Summary

  • Traditional sampling strategies are not suitable for short-shelf-life products - sampling a cell therapy product per ⟨71⟩ could consume 40% of the batch, and EP 2.6.27 offers a more pragmatic 1% volume sampling rule
  • Inherent limits of statistical detection power - even at a 1% contamination rate, sampling 20 vials still gives an 82% chance of passing the sterility test; sterility assurance cannot rely on sampling alone
  • An LOD of 1 cfu is an unrealistic requirement - non-growth-based signals lack natural amplification through multiplication, and the emphasis is on the overall risk assessment
  • The six RMT technologies each have strengths and weaknesses - ATP is the most mature, PCR is the fastest and unaffected by antibiotics, respiration methods have a successful precedent in cell therapy, and SPC is the most sensitive but unsuitable for non-filterable products
  • Method suitability testing cannot be omitted - for every product you must verify that residues do not interfere with the signal generation mechanism of the RMT
  • RMT results are not equivalent to ⟨71⟩ results - results from different signal systems (cfu vs. genome equivalents vs. RLU) cannot be compared directly

User Requirement Specifications for Rapid Methods

Original Text

Technologies recommended based on their match to the URS discussed above, suitable for an RMT are listed alphabetically as follows:

  • Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) bioluminescence
  • Flow cytometry
  • Isothermal microcalorimetry
  • Nucleic acid amplification
  • Respiration
  • Solid phase cytometry

Each of these candidate advanced analytical platforms is briefly discussed separately and key references are provided. For an overview, see Moldenhauer (5).

Non-growth based RMTs for the release of sterile short-life products like nucleic acid amplification may have additional advantages due to the following:

  • With close to real-time testing, the test will be completed before the short-life products are infused into a patient
  • Detection of culture-negative infectious agents
  • The test is minimally affected by antibiotics in the test sample
  • The test is less sensitive to background resulting from animal cell lysis (e.g., particles, ATP) as compared to other technologies since specific microbial genes are targeted

In addition, RMTs or other rapid microbiological methods (RMMs) may be used as in-process controls prior to the final product release sterility test to provide faster information on the effectiveness of microbial controls and the early detection of gross contamination (enabling to investigate and restart production sooner) or probability that a product may fail sterility.

"RMTs or other rapid microbiological methods (RMMs) may be used as in-process controls prior to the final product release sterility test to provide faster information on the effectiveness of microbial controls."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: RMT Is Not Only for Release - The Value of In-Process Control

USP raises a point that is often overlooked: the value of RMT is not limited to final-product release testing. It can also be used as in-process control (IPC):

  • Early detection of gross contamination - if a problem is detected during filling, the line can be stopped and investigated immediately, avoiding rejection of the entire batch
  • Assessing the effectiveness of microbial control measures - verify in real time whether HEPA filtration and disinfection procedures are working properly
  • Predicting the final sterility test result - if the IPC results are normal, there is greater confidence that the final test will pass
  • Faster restart of production - after a problem is found, the investigation can be completed and production resumed more quickly

This "layered defense" thinking is fully consistent with the expectations of PDA TR 13 and EU GMP Annex 1 for sterile process assurance.

Analogy: Multiple Layers of Airport Security

Think of RMT as an airport security system. You would not do just one check at the boarding gate and call it done:

  • Perimeter patrol (environmental monitoring, EM) = patrolling the whole airport grounds
  • Baggage X-ray (RMT as IPC) = scanning at the check-in counter, so problems are found early
  • Security gate (RMT as release test) = the final confirmation before boarding
  • Interception in the air (traditional ⟨71⟩, result after 14 days) = the plane has already taken off before the problem is found, so all you can do is deal with it afterwards

The earlier a problem is found in the process, the lower the cost of handling it and the smaller the patient risk.

Key Point: Suggested RMT Implementation Strategy for CDMOs

For a CDMO, the most practical path to RMT implementation is not to replace ⟨71⟩ in one step but to proceed in phases:

  1. Phase 1: introduce RMT first as an IPC tool, running in parallel with the existing ⟨71⟩ and accumulating comparative data
  2. Phase 2: for specific short-shelf-life products (such as radiopharmaceuticals), apply to use RMT in place of ⟨71⟩ as the release test
  3. Phase 3: extend to more product lines and integrate RMT data into process capability indices (Cpk)

This gradual strategy reduces regulatory risk and also gives the QC team time to build operating proficiency.

Glossary

Original Text

Colony-forming unit (cfu): Viable microorganisms capable of growth on solid microbiological culture media forming discrete, visible colonies.

Limit of detection (LOD): The lowest signal representing a viable microorganism that can be routinely detected.

Patient safety: Reduction of morbidity and mortality of the recipients of a contaminated product that is promoted by the completion of a rapid microbial test prior to the administration.

Positron emission tomography (PET): A nuclear medicine functional imaging technique that is used to observe metabolic processes in the body as an aid to the diagnosis of disease. The system detects pairs of gamma rays emitted indirectly by a positron-emitting radionuclide (tracer), which is introduced into the body.

Risk-based rapid microbial test: A rapid microbial test selected by the stakeholder after a consideration of the LOD, sample size, specificity, and time to result that promotes patient safety by completing the test prior to the administration of a short-lived product to detect contamination, if any.

Specificity: Ability to detect a wide range of different bacteria, yeasts, and molds.

Time to result: Time to complete the microbial test and reach a conclusion about the lack of contamination of the sample test.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Translating Terms into Decisions

Think of these terms as attributes on a restaurant menu:

  • CFU = the unit of portion size (but different dishes use different units - cups, bowls, plates)
  • LOD = the sensitivity of your palate (how small a trace of chili you can taste)
  • Time to Result = serving time (a 5-minute stir-fry vs. a 3-hour slow braise)
  • Specificity = ingredient recognition (can you tell apart all the seasonings, or only salty from bland)
  • Patient Safety = food safety - making sure guests do not get sick from eating
  • Risk-based RMT = choosing the most suitable dish based on the guest's allergy history and dining time

The point is not which dish is the best, but which dish is best for this particular guest.

Key Point: The Special Definition of "Specificity" in ⟨1071⟩

Note that the definition of Specificity in ⟨1071⟩ differs from "specificity" in general analytical chemistry:

  • Specificity in analytical chemistry: the ability to distinguish a specific analyte from other substances (the narrower the better)
  • Specificity in ⟨1071⟩: the ability to detect a broad range of different bacteria, yeasts, and molds (the broader the better)

This is actually closer to the concept of "inclusivity" or "breadth of detection" in analytical chemistry. When writing an RMT validation protocol, make sure the team agrees on this definition, to avoid confusion with the terminology used in analytical method validation (such as ICH Q2).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: A Decision Tree for CDMO RMT Selection

Drawing on the whole chapter of ⟨1071⟩, a CDMO can use the following decision tree to select an RMT:

  1. Can the product be filtered?
    • Yes → solid-phase cytometry (SPC) is the fastest and most sensitive option
    • No (such as cell therapy) → exclude SPC
  2. How quickly is the result needed?
    • <4 hours (PET) → direct PCR or direct ATP detection
    • 24–48 hours (CSP) → respiration methods, flow cytometry, PCR + amplification
    • 2–7 days (acceptable) → ATP with amplification, isothermal microcalorimetry
  3. Does the product contain antibiotics?
    • Yes → prefer PCR (least affected by interference)
    • No → all methods can be considered
  4. Is there a regulatory precedent?
    • Respiration methods → Carticel precedent (approved by FDA)
    • Other methods → a more complete validation package is needed

Key Point: The Core Message of ⟨1071⟩

After reading the whole of USP ⟨1071⟩, the three most important takeaways are:

  1. An imperfect test that can be completed before administration beats a perfect test whose result arrives 14 days after administration - this is the core philosophy of the whole chapter
  2. No single RMT is best on every dimension - the choice must be based on a risk assessment of product characteristics
  3. Method suitability testing cannot be omitted - for every product you must verify the reliability of the RMT in its specific matrix
