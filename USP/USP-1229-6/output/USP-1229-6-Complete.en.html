Introduction

Original Text

Microorganisms are subject to destruction in a variety of ways. Aside from the classical methods of steam, dry heat, and radiation, destructive sterilization may also occur by immersion in a chemical solution. This is termed liquid-phase sterilization. A number of chemical agents, such as aldehydes, acids, bases, and strong oxidants in solution, under the appropriate conditions, are capable of destroying bacteria and fungi, including both vegetative cells and spores in a quantitative fashion. Objects to be sterilized are immersed in the solution of the chemical agent, after which the agent must be removed in a manner that preserves the sterilized object from recontamination.

Removal of the chemical sterilant from the exposed surfaces that have been sterilized must be accomplished in a manner that maintains the sterility of the item postprocessing. Recontamination falls outside the scope of usual consideration for sterilization processes. However, in liquid chemical sterilization it is customary to include the agent’s removal (whether this is accomplished by physical or chemical means) in the overall process, together with any needed additional steps to avoid recontamination.

A substantial number of liquids in aqueous solution are capable of sterilizing articles during immersion. Examples include the following:

  • Aldehydes — glutaraldehyde, formaldehyde
  • Acids — peracetic acid, nitric acid, sulfuric acid
  • Bases — sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide
  • Oxygenating compounds — hydrogen peroxide, ozone, chlorine dioxide
  • Halides — sodium hypochlorite, chlorine

As is the case for gas sterilization, the effectiveness of chemical sterilants varies with concentration and temperature. Other factors that affect antimicrobial activity include pH, extent of mixing (if present), and presence of cellular or other debris. Because of the limited number of variables, process control for sterilization by liquids is relatively simple.

Because there are no widely accepted biological indicators for sterilization by liquids, the use of a common mesophilic sporeformer such as Bacillus atrophaeus or B. subtilis is commonplace because these are the likely worst-case bioburden isolates.

The agents used for sterilization by liquids vary with respect to sterilant stability, effective pH range, concentration, temperature, contact time required, and potential interaction with the materials. When selecting the most appropriate sterilant, manufacturers must consider its effect on the materials, package components, and equipment, as is the case with all other sterilizing processes. The variety of agents, process diversity, and potential applications preclude a material-by-material review of these agents in this chapter. Manufacturers should note that these agents are highly toxic, and appropriate safety measures should be practiced at all times during cycle development, validation, and routine operation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Liquid-Phase Sterilization: a sterilization method in which items are immersed in a solution of a chemical sterilant, which chemically destroys microorganisms (including spores). Unlike physical methods such as steam or dry heat, the kill mechanism here is a chemical reaction.

How it differs from other sterilization methods:

  • Steam (<1229.1>) - high temperature and pressure; the most versatile
  • Dry Heat (<1229.8>) - depyrogenation
  • Radiation (<1229.10>) - sterilization of final packaging
  • Liquid-Phase (this chapter) - surface immersion sterilization, suited to items that cannot tolerate high temperatures

Key Note: Safety & Toxicity

The original text closes with a specific warning: these chemical agents are highly toxic. In a CDMO environment, handling reagents such as peracetic acid or sodium hypochlorite requires:

  • Appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE): goggles, gloves, face shield
  • Ventilation to avoid inhalation of vapors
  • A spill response plan
  • Environment, health and safety (EHS) training records

Key Notes

The five classes of chemical sterilants:

First choice for CDMOs: Peracetic Acid (PAA) - it breaks down into acetic acid and water, is environmentally friendly, and is highly compatible with single-use system materials. It is the chemical agent most commonly used for liquid-phase sterilization in the pharmaceutical industry.

Validation of Sterilization by Liquids

Original Text

Experimental evidence has shown that first-order kinetics is appropriate for microbial destruction, which makes validation a simple exercise. The validation of sterilization by liquids can be accomplished using either the half-cycle approach or the bracketing method.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: First-Order Kinetics

First-Order Kinetics: microbial death under a chemical sterilant follows first-order kinetics, meaning that on a logarithmic scale the number of surviving microorganisms is linear with exposure time. This is the same concept as the D-value in steam sterilization.

This keeps validation relatively simple: once the death rate under specific conditions is confirmed, the exposure time needed to reach the target SAL (Sterility Assurance Level, 10−6) can be calculated.

Half-Cycle Approach

Original Text

The half-cycle approach described below is a modification of the method described in Gaseous Sterilization 〈1229.7〉 (see Figure 1). It is a method that requires the destruction of a resistant microorganism at defined lethal conditions. In routine operation, the process dwell period is arbitrarily doubled and supports a theoretical reduction of the biological indicator (and thus the bioburden) to a probability of a nonsterile unit of at least 10−6 for a full cycle.

The half-cycle method originally was used for ethylene oxide sterilization when the relationship between the microorganisms and the delivered process parameters was less certain.

"In routine operation, the process dwell period is arbitrarily doubled and supports a theoretical reduction of the biological indicator to a probability of a nonsterile unit of at least 10−6."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Half-Cycle Method

How the half-cycle approach works:

  1. Validation phase: demonstrate complete kill of all biological indicators (total kill) within half of the exposure time (the half cycle)
  2. Routine operation: double the exposure time (the full cycle) to provide an additional safety margin
  3. Result: in theory, a probability of a non-sterile unit (PNSU) of at most 10−6

The advantage of this approach is that the D-value does not need to be determined precisely: once total kill is confirmed at the half cycle, the safety margin from doubling is sufficient to assure sterility.

Analogy

Swimming pool disinfection example: suppose testing shows that soaking in a given concentration of disinfectant for 30 minutes kills all test microorganisms. The half-cycle logic is to soak for 60 minutes (double) in routine operation, so that sterility assurance remains adequate even with variability.

Validation Logic

Half-cycle (validation):
  Expose BI at time T/2 → Total kill confirmed

Full cycle (routine):
  Expose at time T (= 2 × T/2)
  → Theoretical PNSU ≤ 10−6
Bracketing Approach

Original Text

In this method (see Figure 2), analysts evaluate conditions of concentration and temperature that bracket the defined process condition to support both over- and under-treatment of the materials and bioburden, respectively. Users can establish the death rate for the microbial population for each of the conditions that bracket the routine process.

Regardless of the approach used, in order to complete the cycle development and validation manufacturers must identify a rapid neutralization method that inactivates the chemical agent to allow microbial quantification after fractional kill exposure. The exposure periods may need to be brief because many of these agents have rapid kill rates.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Bracketing Method

How the Bracketing approach works:

  • High bracket (upper limit condition): concentration/temperature above routine, to confirm that the material is not damaged (over-treatment)
  • Low bracket (lower limit condition): concentration/temperature below routine, to confirm that microorganisms are still killed (under-treatment worst case)
  • Routine (normal conditions): set between the upper and lower limits, balancing sterilization efficacy and material compatibility

This approach is more precise than the half-cycle approach because it establishes complete kill kinetics data (D-values), but it also requires more experimental work.

Key Note: Neutralization Challenge

The key challenge in neutralization: the original text specifically stresses the need for a rapid neutralization method to stop the kill action of the chemical agent immediately after exposure. This is because:

  • Many chemical sterilants (such as PAA) kill very rapidly
  • If neutralization is too slow, fractional kill data will be distorted, because the actual exposure time is longer than designed
  • Common neutralizers: sodium thiosulfate for oxidizing sterilants; catalase (an enzyme) for H2O2
Equipment Qualification

Original Text

Equipment qualification is a predefined program that examines the equipment to confirm that it has been properly installed and operates as intended before the sterilization process. This activity for sterilization by liquid chemicals is simple, because the equipment used is rarely complex. Temperature control and agitation/recirculation rates are the essential considerations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: EQ for LPS

Equipment qualification is relatively simple for liquid-phase sterilization, because the equipment required is usually not complex. The core parameters to confirm are:

  • IQ (Installation Qualification): the immersion tank/vessel is installed in accordance with design specifications
  • OQ (Operational Qualification): the temperature control system can hold the set temperature; the agitation/circulation system operates correctly
  • PQ (Performance Qualification): the sterilant is distributed uniformly under loaded conditions

CDMO practice: for liquid-phase sterilization of SUS fluid paths, the "equipment" may be nothing more than a pump recirculation system with temperature sensors. Complete IQ/OQ/PQ documentation is still required.

Component & Load Definition

Original Text

Sterilization by liquids is a surface phenomenon, and all surfaces of the materials must be immersed in the sterilant. Treatment uniformity can be ensured by recirculation or mixing of the sterilant during the process. Penetration into needle lumens, closely fitted parts, and porous materials should be confirmed. The use of a maximum load per defined vessel or container represents the worst case because it provides the maximum surface area to be sterilized.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Surface Contact

Liquid-phase sterilization is a surface phenomenon, which is one of the most important principles of this chapter. The chemical sterilant must come into direct contact with every surface to be sterilized in order to be effective.

Key challenges:

  • Needle lumens: the inner diameter is very small, so the sterilant may not fully penetrate
  • Closely fitted parts: dead spaces may form between contact surfaces
  • Porous materials: the sterilant must penetrate the internal pores

Key Note: CDMO SUS Application

Load definition for SUS fluid path sterilization: in CDMO aseptic filling operations, when sterilizing SUS fluid paths with peracetic acid, the "maximum load" means the longest tubing configuration, the most connection points, and the most complex manifold. Validation must use this worst case.

The importance of circulation/agitation: for SUS fluid paths, pumped recirculation ensures that the sterilant reaches all internal surfaces, including T-connectors, valve dead spaces, and tubing bends. Static immersion is not sufficient in a complex fluid path.

Biological Indicators

Original Text

The common indicator organisms for chemical sterilization are B. atrophaeus ATCC 9372 or B. subtilis ATCC 6633. The spore challenge is inoculated directly onto the items. End users should determine the populations of inoculated items. Manufacturers should place indicators within loads at locations believed to be hardest for the agent to reach, on the basis of visual examination.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: BI Selection

Why choose B. atrophaeus / B. subtilis?

  • Both are mesophilic spore-forming organisms
  • They represent the worst-case bioburden most likely to be encountered in the actual production environment
  • Note: this differs from steam sterilization, which uses the heat-resistant Geobacillus stearothermophilus, because liquid-phase sterilization is usually performed at lower temperatures

Direct Inoculation: the spore challenge is inoculated directly onto the item to be sterilized, rather than using a commercial BI carrier. This better reflects the actual sterilization conditions.

Key Note: Placement Strategy

The original text states clearly that BIs should be placed where the chemical agent is hardest to reach. These locations typically include:

  • The furthest point of the tubing (furthest point from inlet)
  • Dead legs at T-connectors
  • Near valve sealing surfaces
  • Inside grooves or threads of components
Process Confirmation / Microbiological Challenge

Original Text

The core of the validation activity is confirmation of acceptable process parameters and inactivation of the microbial challenge. The end user should expect a linear death curve for the spore challenge and require total death of the challenge. The end user can consider adjustment in chemical sterilant concentration, process time, agitation, and other factors. Proof of cycle efficacy is provided in replicate studies in which the biological indicators are killed, and physical measurements are taken as documentation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Validation Core

Core elements of the validation activities:

  1. Linear death curve: on a semi-logarithmic plot, surviving count versus time should be a straight line, which confirms that the first-order kinetics assumption is correct
  2. Total kill: all BIs must be killed; partial survival is not accepted
  3. Replicate studies: industry practice is at least 3 consecutive successful runs
  4. Physical measurements: records of parameters such as temperature, concentration, and time serve as supporting documentation

Key Note: Adjustable Parameters

When sterilization efficacy is unsatisfactory, the process parameters that can be adjusted include:

  • Concentration - increase the concentration of the chemical agent
  • Process Time - extend the exposure time
  • Agitation - increase mixing to ensure contact
  • Temperature - raise the temperature to speed up the reaction

Note, however, that adjusting any parameter may affect material compatibility, so the impact on SUS materials or equipment must be evaluated at the same time.

Agent Neutralization / Removal

Original Text

After exposure, the sterilizing agent must be adequately removed from the items or neutralized before further processing. This segment of the process uses chemical neutralization or physical removal and must be executed in a manner that preserves the sterility of the items. Aseptic processing with appropriate capability demonstration should be provided. Process simulation beginning with the completion of sterilization through placement into a sealed sterile container is expected. See Sterility Assurance 〈1211〉 for additional information.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Post-Sterilization Handling

Post-sterilization handling is the most distinctive and most challenging part of liquid-phase sterilization. There are two ways to remove the agent:

  • Chemical neutralization: use a neutralizer (such as sodium thiosulfate) to inactivate the sterilant. Suitable for complex structures that are difficult to rinse
  • Physical removal: rinse with sterile water or WFI to remove sterilant residues. More commonly used for SUS fluid paths

Key Note: Sterility Maintenance

Key requirements for maintaining sterility:

  • The removal/neutralization process itself must be performed under sterile conditions, otherwise the freshly sterilized items will be recontaminated
  • A process simulation (media fill) is needed to validate the entire process from completion of sterilization to placement in a sealed container
  • The rinse water must be sterile water or WFI
  • All equipment and containers that contact the sterilized items must be pre-sterilized

CDMO practice tip: for the rinse after PAA sterilization of SUS fluid paths, sterile WFI is usually recirculated through the same pump system, and the completeness of residue removal is confirmed by TOC (Total Organic Carbon) or pH testing. The rinse effluent must be tested for residues to confirm compliance with limits.

Routine Process Control

Original Text

Liquid-phase sterilization must be subject to controls that maintain the validated state. The practices outlined in Sterilization of Compendial Articles 〈1229〉 address the general requirements for all sterilization systems. Sterilization is accomplished by means of a number of related practices that are essential for continued use of the process over an extended period of time, including calibration, chemical and physical measurements, ongoing process control, change control, preventive maintenance, periodic reassessment, and training.

Chapter Summary

  • Liquid-Phase Sterilization is a sterilization method in which items are immersed in a solution of a chemical sterilant, which chemically destroys microorganisms; it is primarily surface sterilization (a surface phenomenon)
  • The five classes of chemical sterilants: aldehydes, acids, bases, oxygenating compounds, halides; the one most used in CDMOs is peracetic acid (PAA)
  • Two validation strategies: Half-Cycle (simple, conservative, doubled safety margin) and Bracketing (precise, establishes complete kinetics data)
  • BI selection: B. atrophaeus (ATCC 9372) or B. subtilis (ATCC 6633), inoculated directly onto the item to be sterilized
  • Post-sterilization handling: neutralization or removal of the chemical agent is the most distinctive challenge of liquid-phase sterilization; it must be performed under sterile conditions and validated through process simulation
  • Routine control: shares the general requirements of USP <1229> with all sterilization methods: calibration, measurement, process control, change control, maintenance, periodic review, training
