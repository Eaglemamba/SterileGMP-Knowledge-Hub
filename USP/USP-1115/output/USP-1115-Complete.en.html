Introduction

Original Text

In terms of microbiological contamination risk control, there are two broad categories of drug products: (a) sterile products, in which the bioburden is essentially eliminated using validated methodologies, and (b) nonsterile products for which the final product bioburden is controlled to appropriate levels based on product attributes, route of administration, and target patient population.

Microbial content in nonsterile products is controlled to a level consistent with patient safety. Use of excessive controls that would add complexity or cost without a commensurate safety benefit is not advantageous in terms of added value to either the patient or the manufacturer. Therefore, a pragmatic scientific approach to management of the microbial bioburden in nonsterile products requires consideration of patient risk and contamination control objectives to achieve a practical and appropriate level of risk management.

A critical consideration in ensuring product quality is to prevent conditions within the manufacturing facility or manufacturing process that favor the proliferation or ingress of microorganisms. Microbial growth in excipients, components, and drug substances is a concern because it creates the possibility that viable microbial content could reach unacceptable levels. Bioburden levels in the ranges of those recommended in ⟨1111⟩ are recognized as safe and do not pose risk of infection or microbial toxins.

This chapter outlines a risk-based approach to the control of contamination in nonsterile product manufacturing.

"A pragmatic scientific approach to management of the microbial bioburden in nonsterile products requires consideration of patient risk and contamination control objectives to achieve a practical and appropriate level of risk management."

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts

Two broad categories of drug products: USP opens by dividing drug products into two categories:

  • Sterile products - bioburden must be "essentially eliminated" (e.g., injectables, ophthalmic preparations)
  • Nonsterile products - bioburden is controlled to an "appropriate level"; zero microorganisms is not required, but levels must be kept within a safe range

The three factors that determine the "appropriate level": product attributes (water activity, pH, etc.), route of administration (oral, topical, etc.), and the intended patient population (immunocompetent or immunocompromised).

Bioburden: the total amount of microorganisms present in a material, product, or manufacturing environment. For nonsterile products, a reasonable level of bioburden is acceptable.

Analogy

Tap water vs. sterile irrigation water in the operating room:

The tap water you drink at home is not sterile. A small number of microorganisms is tolerated as long as it stays within safety standards. The irrigation fluid used in an operating room, however, must be completely sterile because it comes into direct contact with open wounds.

Nonsterile drugs are like tap water: we do not aim for "zero microorganisms" but make sure the microbial content stays within a safe range. Sterile drugs are like operating-room water: no microorganisms can be tolerated. These two management strategies differ in nature. Confusing them wastes resources and may also cause you to overlook the real risks.

Key Notes

The pragmatic principle: USP explicitly states that "over-control" brings no benefit and only adds unnecessary complexity and cost. This is the core philosophy of microbial control for nonsterile products: scientifically sound, risk-based, neither excessive nor insufficient.

Proliferation vs. ingress: These two key terms represent two different modes of contamination. Proliferation is when microorganisms already in the system multiply under favorable conditions; ingress is when external microorganisms enter the system. The control strategy must address both routes.

  1. Your CDMO manufactures both sterile injectables and oral tablets. What are the fundamental differences between the microbial control strategies for the two? What problems would arise if sterile-process standards were applied to the oral tablet line?

Sterile vs Nonsterile Context

Original Text

The manufacture of nonsterile products and the management of their microbiological content are distinctly different from those required for sterile products. Sterile products are administered parenterally by means of injection or are applied topically to sensitive tissues where the risk of infection is comparatively high.

In contrast, nonsterile products are administered to regions of the human body that are rich in microbial flora and have physical or immunological barriers to infection. Examples include the oral cavity, skin, nasopharynx, vagina, and rectum, which harbor a high and diverse viable microbial population. A healthy adult has a bacterial population that averages approximately 1014 bacteria, a number that exceeds the individual's own cells by a factor of 10.

The proliferation of microbial contamination in a production facility, in products, or in product ingredients is an objectionable condition. Microbial proliferation within a facility creates conditions that favor the spread of contaminants, potentially in dangerous numbers, into ingredients, primary packaging materials, and even into product itself.

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts

The body's own microbial barrier: The sites where nonsterile drugs are administered (mouth, skin, nasopharynx, vagina, rectum) already host large microbial populations. In a healthy adult, bacterial cells outnumber the body's own cells by roughly 10 to 1 (1014).

Key logic: Since these sites already harbor huge numbers of microorganisms, a small number of microorganisms in the product (within a safe range) does not add risk. This is the scientific basis for tolerating a certain level of bioburden in nonsterile products.

But "proliferation" is the red line: Even though nonsterile products may contain some microorganisms, those organisms must not be allowed to proliferate, because proliferation can produce toxins and damage the chemical and pharmacological properties of the drug.

Analogy

Visitors vs. intruders:

Your skin is like a neighborhood with a good security system: residents (the normal flora) coming and going is perfectly normal. If a passerby (a few foreign microorganisms) walks past the gate, it usually causes no security problem.

But if someone pitches a tent in the neighborhood and settles there (proliferation), and then starts inviting more people to move in (spread of contaminants), it becomes a serious security problem. Microbial control for nonsterile products aims to prevent "settling and proliferation," not to forbid the brief presence of any microorganism.

Key Notes: CDMO Context

The CDMO's dual-track challenge: Your site may have both a sterile filling line and a nonsterile oral dosage line. The key is not to mix up the control logic of the two:

  • Aseptic area: all microorganisms must be eliminated - zero tolerance
  • Nonsterile area: microorganisms are controlled within a safe range - pragmatic management

But note this: if the raw materials of your nonsterile product are also used in the early stages of a sterile product process (for example, purified water from the same batch), then the bioburden control of the incoming material directly affects the safety of the downstream sterile process.

Practice Questions

  1. A client asks you to set microbial limits for an oral liquid intended for immunosuppressed patients. Although it is a nonsterile product, the target patient population is immunocompromised. How would you approach setting the limits? What additional risks need to be considered?
  2. What is the fundamental difference between "proliferation" and "presence" in microbial control? Why does USP specifically emphasize that proliferation is an objectionable condition?
Risk Hierarchy of Nonsterile Products

Original Text

The following list provides a hierarchy of broad categories of nonsterile pharmaceutical products with respect to potential risk of microbiological contamination (from high to low) (2):

  • metered-dose and dry powder inhalants
  • nasal sprays
  • otics
  • vaginal suppositories
  • topicals
  • rectal suppositories
  • oral liquids (aqueous)
  • liquid-filled capsules
  • oral tablets and powder-filled capsules

When formulators evaluate the susceptibility of nonsterile pharmaceutical products to microbial hazards, considerations include whether the active ingredient has inherent antimicrobial activity, the microbiological content of excipients, inclusion of antimicrobial preservatives in the formulation, and water activity. In addition, manufacturers should consider whether processing steps and hold periods could result in changes in the bioburden.

Trainer's Analysis

Analogy

An analogy for protection levels:

Imagine the rooms in your home each have a different "security level." The room with the safe (equivalent to the lungs) needs the highest-grade lock; the living room (equivalent to the skin) needs medium protection; and the front door (equivalent to the mouth/gut), where people come and go anyway, needs only basic security.

The microbial risk hierarchy of dosage forms follows the same logic: the more vulnerable the site of administration (the more it resembles the "safe room"), the stricter the microbial control must be.

Key Notes

The four formulation defense factors:

  • Water activity - products with low water activity (< 0.60) hardly support microbial growth. Oral tablets are a typical low-water-activity dosage form
  • Antimicrobial preservatives - added to multidose liquid preparations, such as benzalkonium chloride and methylparaben
  • Inherent antimicrobial activity - some active ingredients are themselves antibiotics or have bactericidal effects
  • Hold periods - waiting times during production can change the bioburden, especially for aqueous intermediates

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO is asked to manufacture a nasal spray with an aqueous base. Based on the risk hierarchy, what level of microbial control program would you need to establish for it? Compared with your existing oral tablet line, what additional control requirements apply?
  2. After compounding, an oral suspension must wait 8 hours before filling. What is the potential impact of this hold period on bioburden? What control measures would you recommend?
  3. If the active ingredient of a topical cream itself has antimicrobial activity, can microbial limit testing be waived? Why or why not?

Key Concepts: Risk Hierarchy

The logic of the risk ranking: Inhalants, at the top of the list, carry the highest risk because they contact the lungs, a relatively sterile and vulnerable tissue. Oral solid dosage forms, at the bottom, carry the lowest risk because the mouth and gut have strong immune barriers and abundant normal flora.

U.S. Regulatory Guidance Documents

Original Text

The U.S. Code of Federal Regulations includes the Food and Drug Administration's Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) requirements, which are found in Part 211 (3). Pertinent sections of 21 CFR 211 include:

  • 211.42 Design and construction — requires that buildings used for pharmaceutical manufacturing are of suitable size, construction, and location
  • 211.46 Ventilation, air filtration, air heating and cooling — requires adequate equipment to control microorganisms, dust, humidity, and temperature
  • 211.56 Sanitation — requires written procedures for cleaning and sanitizing agents, designed to prevent contamination
  • 211.82 Receipt and storage of untested components — requires acceptance, quarantine, testing, and release procedure
  • 211.110 Sampling and testing of in-process materials — requires testing of in-process manufacturing controls including bioburden determination
  • 211.84(d)(6) — requires microbiological testing of incoming containers and components with potential for objectionable contamination
  • 211.113(a) — requires written procedures to control objectionable microorganisms in drug products not required to be sterile
  • 211.165(b) — requires appropriate laboratory testing of each batch to be free of objectionable microorganisms

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts: Key Provisions of 21 CFR 211

21 CFR Part 211 is the FDA's core GMP regulation for finished pharmaceuticals. The provisions directly related to microbial control of nonsterile products fall into three groups:

  • Facility design (211.42, 211.46) - preventing contamination through the physical environment
  • Procedural controls (211.56, 211.82, 211.113(a)) - establishing cleaning, receipt, and control procedures through management systems
  • Testing and confirmation (211.84(d)(6), 211.110, 211.165(b)) - verifying the effectiveness of controls with data

Objectionable microorganisms: Note that FDA regulations refer to this concept repeatedly but never define a specific "list of objectionable organisms." This is deliberate, because which microorganisms are "objectionable" depends on the product, the route of administration, and the patient population, and manufacturers must assess this themselves.

Analogy

The regulatory framework is like a building code:

21 CFR 211 is like a building code. It specifies that your house (the plant) must have an adequate structure (211.42), ventilation (211.46), and a cleaning plan (211.56), and that the building materials you receive (raw materials) must be tested (211.82). But it does not tell you which brand of cement or what color of paint to use; those details are up to you, based on your specific needs.

Key Notes: CDMO Context

The regulatory basis for CDMO incoming material control: 211.84(d)(6) is the regulatory basis for a CDMO to perform microbial testing on incoming materials. This means:

  • Not every raw material needs microbial testing, only those with a potential risk of microbial contamination
  • For materials with low water activity, inherent antimicrobial properties, or a high degree of processing, testing frequency can be reduced based on a risk assessment
  • But for materials of natural origin, aqueous excipients, and high-water-activity materials, a rigorous incoming microbial testing program must be established

  1. An FDA investigator finds at your CDMO site that a lot of lactose raw material entered production without microbial testing. Microbial Control Considerations During Product Development

Original Text

A formal risk assessment program that identifies risk modalities and assigns critical control points for manufacture of nonsterile product is useful. Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point programs (HACCP) (4) are widely used to assess and mitigate microbial risk in food manufacturing and may also be useful for manufacturers of nonsterile drug products.

Points to be considered by pharmaceutical microbiologists when they assess the potential risk associated with nonsterile drug product manufacture are listed below:

  • synthesis, isolation, and final purification of the drug substance
  • microbiological attributes of the drug substance
  • microbiological attributes of excipients and intermediates
  • formulation and chemical and physical attributes of the drug product
  • antimicrobial properties of the material, e.g., water activity or others
  • manufacturing process
  • delivery system
  • packaging
  • storage conditions for intermediates and the finished dosage form
  • route of administration
  • expected treatment procedure and dosage regimen
  • population to which the product is delivered (e.g., neonates, immunocompromised patients, etc.)

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts: HACCP in Pharma

HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) originated as a methodology in the food industry, and USP recommends applying it to nonsterile drug manufacturing. The core logic is:

  1. Identify hazards - what microbial risks may exist?
  2. Determine critical control points (CCPs) - which steps in the process are critical for controlling microorganisms?
  3. Establish monitoring - how do you confirm that the CCPs are working properly?

The 12 risk assessment elements cover the complete chain from raw materials to the patient. The ones of most interest to a CDMO are: the microbial attributes of the drug substance, the microbial content of excipients, the storage conditions of process intermediates, and the target patient population.

Analogy

From food safety to drug safety:

A classic HACCP application in the food industry is fresh meat processing: which step is most likely to introduce bacteria (cutting)? Which step can kill bacteria (heating)? Which step, if the temperature goes out of control, lets bacteria multiply explosively (refrigeration)?

The same logic applies to nonsterile drugs: which raw material is most likely to bring in microorganisms (natural-origin excipients)? Which process step can reduce bioburden (drying, heating)? Which hold time, if too long, allows microorganisms to proliferate (aqueous intermediates)?

Key Notes

Microbial risk assessment for CDMO technology transfer: Whenever you take on a new nonsterile product project, the 12-element microbial risk assessment should be completed during the technology transfer stage. This assessment document becomes the foundation of your entire manufacturing microbial control strategy.

Considerations for special populations: The last item on the list, "intended patient population," is extremely important. Even for an oral tablet, if the target population is immunocompromised cancer patients, the microbial control standard may need to be stricter than for an oral tablet used by healthy adults.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO has just received a new nonsterile oral suspension project. Based on the 12 risk assessment elements in USP ⟨1115⟩, list what you consider the five most critical and explain why.
  2. HACCP originated as a food industry method. What adjustments do you think are needed, or what parts do you think do not apply, when using it in drug manufacturing?
Microbial Control During Manufacturing

Original Text

Although many factors (Figure 1) can result in the introduction of microorganisms, some of these are more likely to result in microbial contamination. These manufacturing factors are, in descending order (5):

  1. ingredient water
  2. pharmaceutical ingredients
  3. process equipment
  4. manufacturing personnel
  5. manufacturing environment

Water Systems and Use: Water used in active ingredient manufacturing, formulation, cleaning, and housekeeping is the single most important risk element contributing to the contamination of nonsterile products. Purified waters used in pharmaceutical manufacturing are deionized and thus do not contain chlorine to control microbial growth. Substantial populations of Gram-negative rod-shaped bacteria and some molds are able to grow in such purified dechlorinated water, particularly in holding tanks at or around ambient temperatures. Standing water should be drained or physically removed quickly and efficiently.

Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients, In-Process Materials, and Excipients: Ingredients and excipients used in nonsterile product manufacturing processes are important sources of microbiological contamination. Of particular concern are unprocessed materials of natural origin, those that have high water activity, synthetic processes with aqueous isolation steps, and biological processes with limited downstream purification.

Trainer's Analysis

Analogy

Water = a microbial buffet:

Purified water is like a restaurant that has been cleaned thoroughly (all chemicals and minerals removed). For Gram-negative bacteria, it is a dream growth environment: no chlorine (disinfectant), plenty of moisture, a suitable temperature, and only nutrients are missing. Even trace organics on the pipe walls are enough to feed a large biofilm community.

This is why the water system is the number one risk in nonsterile manufacturing. You must keep the water system continuously circulating, temperature-controlled, and regularly sanitized.

Key Notes: CDMO Water System

Key control points for a CDMO water system:

  • Continuous circulation - the purified water loop should circulate continuously (typically at a flow velocity of ≥ 1.5 m/s) to avoid dead legs and stagnation
  • Temperature control - ambient-temperature systems need more frequent sanitization; a hot-circulation system at 80°C greatly reduces microbial risk
  • Drainage - equipment and work surfaces must be drained of residual water promptly after use. "Standing water = microbial risk" is the core axiom
  • Incoming material microbial limits - confirm the microbial content of the APIs and excipients you receive. Natural-origin materials (such as gelatin and starch) carry higher risk

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO samples its purified water system once a month. The microbial trend shows that recoveries rise markedly in summer (June to August). What improvements would you recommend?
  2. A lot of natural-origin plant-based excipient arrives, and the supplier's CoA shows microbial counts within specification. But you know that natural-origin materials carry higher microbial risk. Besides reviewing the CoA, what additional risk controls would you apply?

Key Concepts: Five Contamination Sources

Ranking of the five contamination sources and what it means for a CDMO:

Why is water ranked first? Purified water has had its chlorine removed, which means the natural microbial inhibitor is gone. In addition, water itself is a prerequisite for microbial growth; given a suitable temperature and nutrients, microorganisms can grow explosively within hours.

Section Summary

  • Nonsterile vs. sterile: Microbial control of nonsterile products means "controlling to a safe range" rather than "eliminating completely." This calls for a pragmatic, scientific approach, not a blind pursuit of zero microorganisms
  • Risk hierarchy: inhalants > nasal sprays > otic preparations > vaginal suppositories > topicals > rectal suppositories > oral liquids > capsules > tablets - risk depends on the strength of the immune barrier at the site of administration
  • Regulatory basis: Several provisions of 21 CFR Part 211 require control of "objectionable microorganisms" but deliberately do not define a specific list - manufacturers must assess this themselves based on product characteristics
  • Risk assessment: The HACCP methodology can be applied to nonsterile drug manufacturing, and the 12 assessment elements cover the complete chain from raw materials to the patient
  • Five contamination sources: water > raw materials > equipment > personnel > environment - the water system is the number one microbial risk in nonsterile manufacturing
  • CDMO takeaways: Setting incoming material bioburden limits, monitoring the purified water system, and validating hold times are the three pillars of control in nonsterile product manufacturing

Microbial Control of Drug Substance Manufacturing

Original Text

The approaches to microbial control described in this chapter can be applied with minimal change to drug substances manufactured either by biological processes or organic synthesis. The processes in organic synthesis often use extremes of temperature, pressure, pH, and other conditions that inhibit or actively destroy microorganisms.

When the chemical process for manufacturing a drug substance is complex, the final steps often have the greatest potential effect on the bioburden. As is the case for drug product manufacturing, the water used in the process presents the greatest challenge to microbial control.

The types of water used in these processes vary. Potable water typically is used in early-stage processing and cleaning, and Purified Water and Water for Injection often are used for downstream processing and cleaning. Use of Water for Injection is typically restricted to the final steps of bulk chemical or biological drug substance manufacturing that will ultimately be used in the formulation of sterile products.

These processes are frequently performed almost entirely within closed vessels, which minimizes external facility considerations. Some process equipment is suitable for clean-in-place operations.

Trainer's Analysis

Analogy

The cleaning logic of cooking:

When you cook, the early steps (stir-frying at high heat, deep-frying) already kill microbes, so no extra disinfection is needed. The final steps (plating and garnishing before serving) are where hygiene matters most, because the food has cooled and now touches hands and tools with no heat to protect it.

API synthesis follows the same logic: the early steps that use organic solvents are not a microbial concern, but the final water wash, crystallization, and packaging steps are where microbial control is won or lost.

CDMO Context

Pre-sterilization bioburden at a CDMO: If your CDMO makes a drug substance that will ultimately be used in a sterile product, the water used in the final API step must be WFI. You also need to set a bioburden limit "before sterilization", which is the concept of pre-sterilization bioburden monitoring. The microbial quality of the API directly affects the safety of the downstream sterile process.

Advantage of closed systems: Most chemical synthesis takes place in closed vessels, which greatly reduces the risk of environmental contamination. However, the CIP (Clean-in-Place) system itself must also be validated to confirm that the cleaning agent effectively removes both chemical residues and microorganisms.

Practice Questions

  1. The last step of your CDMO's API synthesis is a crystallization wash with purified water, and the washed API will be used in a sterile injectable. Should this step use purified water or WFI? Why?
  2. During validation of a closed CIP system, the microbial count at one dead leg was found to be high. What is the potential impact on API quality, and what improvements would you recommend?

Key Concepts: API Manufacturing

The natural microbial barrier of chemical synthesis: The extreme conditions used in organic synthesis (high temperature, high pressure, strong acids/bases, organic solvents) are themselves powerful microbial kill mechanisms. This means the early and middle steps of synthesis generally need no additional microbial control.

But the final steps are critical: As API synthesis nears completion, particularly during final purification, crystallization, and drying, those extreme conditions typically no longer apply, and the microbial risk from the water system becomes the main concern.

Equipment Design and Use

Original Text

When possible, specifications for the selection of equipment that will be used in the manufacture of nonsterile products should include sanitary design. Equipment and utensils should be cleanable so contaminants and residual products can be reliably removed. Equipment should use sanitary fittings and should be designed for easy use of cleaning and sanitizing agents and complete rinse water drainage.

Residual water in tanks, piping, or on equipment surfaces introduces the risk of colonization by waterborne organisms. Manufacturing equipment that cannot be cleaned in place should be readily accessible for manual cleaning.

The preferred material of construction for equipment and utensils that will be in contact with product is austenitic stainless steel. Manufacturers should consider the surface finish on product-contact materials, for which a roughness average (RA) of 15–20 µm is a reasonable rule of thumb.

Process piping systems also should follow sanitary design principles and should be sloped to facilitate drainage, and the equipment should contain flush gaskets to prevent material build-up and to facilitate cleaning. The 3A Sanitary Standards provide generally useful guidance for process layout and design.

Equipment cleaning procedures should be detailed and should ensure that the equipment is completely dry after cleaning and is stored in a manner that prevents microbial proliferation. Cleaning and sanitization process validation should include the evaluation of microbial content both after sanitization and before use.

Manufacturers should evaluate whether products that are manufactured using a piece of processing equipment may, under some processing circumstances, promote the growth of microorganisms. This evaluation is necessary to properly establish processing hold times and to define equipment use conditions following cleaning.

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts: Sanitary Design

The four principles of sanitary design:

  • Cleanability - equipment surfaces must be able to reliably remove contaminants and residual product
  • Complete drainage - piping should be sloped and equipment should drain completely so that no residual water remains
  • Sanitary fittings - use flush gaskets to prevent material buildup
  • Material requirements - austenitic stainless steel is preferred for product-contact surfaces, with a surface roughness Ra of 15–20 µm

Ra (Roughness Average): A quantitative measure of surface roughness. The lower the Ra value, the smoother the surface and the harder it is for microorganisms to adhere. 15–20 µm is a "reasonable rule of thumb" for nonsterile products. Note that Ra requirements for sterile processes are usually stricter (≤ 0.8 µm).

Analogy

Kitchen drainage:

If you leave a puddle in the sink after washing dishes, by the next day the water has turned slimy and smelly, which is microbial growth. The same applies to industrial pharmaceutical equipment: any residual water is a breeding ground for microorganisms.

USP repeatedly stresses "dry after cleaning", just as your mother kept reminding you to dry the dishes after washing them. It sounds simple, but it is the most effective way to prevent microbial problems.

Hold Time Validation

Hold time validation, a core issue for CDMOs:

USP explicitly requires an evaluation of whether equipment under conditions of use could promote microbial growth. This relates directly to two kinds of hold time:

  • Clean hold time - the maximum permitted time between completion of equipment cleaning and its next use. Even dry, cleaned equipment left standing too long can be recontaminated by environmental microorganisms
  • Dirty hold time - the maximum permitted time between the end of production and the start of cleaning. If equipment containing product residue is not cleaned promptly, microorganisms may proliferate in the residue

Because CDMOs change over products frequently, hold time validation is especially important. You need to make sure microorganisms will not grow to an unacceptable level within the time the schedule allows.

The Manufacturing Environment

Original Text

The environmental risks and controls for nonsterile products are different from those for aseptically manufactured sterile products. Unlike aseptic processing for which facility requirements are generally uniform in specification, nonsterile product manufacturing environments typically involve diverse products and microbial contamination control requirements.

Common design elements to control microbial contamination may include the following:

  • Walls, ceilings, and floors are constructed of nonporous materials that are readily cleanable and resistant to cleaning agents and disinfectants
  • Floor drains are permitted provided they can be closed during processing or fitted with a suitable air break
  • Access should be limited to essential personnel
  • Material, equipment, and personnel flows should avoid contamination
  • Ventilation and air filtration should be adequate to maintain specified cleanliness
  • Good housekeeping and good general hygiene should be applied at all times
  • Product-contact or water-supply tubing, valves, and fittings should be cleaned and sanitized, stored dry, and labeled

Classified environments are not required for nonsterile product manufacturing, e.g., those specified in ISO 14644-1.

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts: Environment Design

The core principle of nonsterile environment design: "adequate but not excessive":

The most important statement: classified environments are not a requirement for nonsterile manufacturing. This means you do not need to build ISO 5/7/8 cleanrooms as you would for a sterile process. You still need:

  • Cleanable construction materials - nonporous and resistant to cleaning agents and disinfectants
  • Floor drain control - closable during processing, or fitted with a suitable air break
  • Separation of personnel and material flows - to avoid cross-contamination
  • Appropriate ventilation and air filtration - to maintain the specified level of cleanliness

Analogy

Food plant vs cleanroom:

A nonsterile manufacturing environment is like a well-run food processing plant: floors can be washed down with water, walls are smooth and cleanable, personnel wear appropriate work clothing, and there is a basic ventilation system. It is not a semiconductor cleanroom, and it does not need HEPA ceilings, airlocks, or particle counting.

The key is a "well-controlled general environment", not a "sterile environment".

Floor Drains

Floor drains, a frequently overlooked risk point:

USP allows floor drains in nonsterile manufacturing areas, with a condition: they must be closable during processing or fitted with an air break. The reason is that drains are a major habitat for microorganisms (especially Gram-negative bacteria and molds). If a drain is open and unprotected during production, the microorganisms inside can enter the production environment as aerosols.

Contrast with sterile manufacturing: In sterile manufacturing areas (Grade A/B), floor drains are generally prohibited outright.

  2. A floor drain in the production area is used during cleaning but has no air break installed, and the auditor has flagged it as a risk. How would you cite USP ⟨1115⟩ to justify your corrective approach?
Microbial Assessment of Nonsterile Manufacturing Environments

Original Text

Microbiological monitoring of the manufacturing environment can serve as an adjunct to control and generally is a qualitative assessment tool in a properly implemented risk-based microbiological control program. A monitoring program commensurate with the product bioburden risk can help confirm the effectiveness of microbiological controls.

Because the majority of organisms recovered in environmental monitoring are of human origin, the levels of transient contamination recovered depend largely on the level of human activity and gowning requirements.

During nonsterile product manufacturing, microbiological monitoring of the environment need not be as rigorous as that required during aseptic processing. Manufacturers should establish acceptable levels of microorganisms within each product and should sufficiently identify microorganisms to gain an appropriate understanding of bioburden patterns and seasonal variability.

Microbial Sampling: During nonsterile product manufacturing, the air sampling methods used for environmental monitoring are active or passive. Active devices sample appropriate air volumes and deposit viable organisms on solid media plates. Passive sampling in the form of settling plates can be used in lieu of active air samplers.

Personnel monitoring typically is not required in nonsterile product manufacturing except when near-aseptic gowning materials are employed.

The frequency of monitoring should reflect the potential risk associated with the dosage form. Products that are resistant to microbial colonization or have microbiocidal characteristics require little or no microbiological monitoring.

For most nonsterile product manufacturing environments, the establishment of alert and action levels may not be required. Environmental monitoring is considered an informational survey and should not be used in product-release decisions. Monitoring of unclassified environments is not required.

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts: Environmental Monitoring

The fundamental role of nonsterile environmental monitoring: an "informational survey", not a "basis for quality decisions":

This is the most important concept in this section. USP states explicitly that:

  • Environmental monitoring is an adjunct, not the primary means of contamination control
  • It is a qualitative assessment, not a quantitative judgment
  • It should not be used for product-release decisions
  • For most nonsterile environments, alert/action levels may not be needed
  • Unclassified environments need no monitoring

This is completely different from environmental monitoring in sterile processes, where monitoring data are one of the prerequisites for product release.

Analogy

Weather forecast vs air traffic control:

Nonsterile environmental monitoring is like a "weather forecast": it tells you current conditions and trends as a reference, but it will not stop you from going out because today is cloudy. Sterile environmental monitoring is like "air traffic control": the data directly decide whether the aircraft (the product) can take off (be released).

Understanding this difference is very important, because many CDMOs make the mistake of "over-monitoring", applying the monitoring logic of sterile environments to nonsterile lines and adding unnecessary cost and complexity.

Sampling Methods

Choosing a sampling method:

  • Active air sampling - uses an air sampler, with results expressed in CFU/m³
  • Passive air sampling - settling plates, which can replace active sampling
  • Personnel monitoring - generally not needed, unless gowning close to sterile grade is used

Alternative methods: USP allows nongrowth-based methods to replace traditional culture methods, and no method comparability study is required; only a small number of parallel samples is needed to establish a comparative baseline.

  2. A client asks you to provide environmental monitoring data for the nonsterile manufacturing area as one of the conditions for batch release. Based on USP ⟨1115⟩, is this request reasonable? How would you communicate with the client?
  3. USP says "personnel monitoring is generally not needed". But if your environmental monitoring trend shows elevated human-origin microorganisms, should you add personnel monitoring? Why?

Monitoring Frequency by Risk

The risk-based principle for monitoring frequency:

Products with antimicrobial properties (such as antibiotic tablets): USP states explicitly that they "require little or no microbiological monitoring", because the product itself inhibits microbial growth.

Active Measures for Microbial Control

Original Text

In addition to facility and process design considerations and equipment cleaning and storage controls, there are instances in which active means for addressing the contamination risk are required. The microbiological control of nonsterile products can be enhanced by the adoption of direct contamination control processes such as the following:

  • decontamination of product contact surfaces, materials, and containers, typically by means of heat treatment (in the most critical applications sterilization can be used)
  • chemical or physical (e.g., dry or moist heat) bioburden reduction treatments for raw materials and active ingredients
  • use of closed, cleaned or decontaminated systems for handling and transfer of materials
  • use of disposable components or utensils
  • improved gowning materials for operational personnel
  • use of classified environments in high-risk operations

Trainer's Analysis

Key Concepts: Active Control Measures

From passive design to active control: The sanitary equipment design, environmental controls, and cleaning procedures discussed so far are all "passive" defenses that minimize risk through design and process. At times, however, you need to take "active" measures:

  • Decontamination - heat treatment of product-contact surfaces, up to sterilization at the most stringent level
  • Raw material bioburden reduction - dry-heat or moist-heat pretreatment of high-risk raw materials
  • Closed systems - material transfers take place in closed piping
  • Disposable/single-use components - remove the need for cleaning validation
  • Classified environments - needed only for high-risk operations (such as inhalation product manufacturing)

Analogy

Preventive medicine vs emergency care:

Passive control is like preventive medicine: a balanced diet, regular exercise, and good hygiene habits. Active control is like emergency measures in a high-risk period: getting vaccinated (bioburden reduction), wearing a mask (improved gowning), and isolating at home (classified environments).

You do not need to take emergency measures all the time, but when risk rises, these tools must be available to deploy.

CDMO Single-Use

Single-use strategy for CDMOs: For a CDMO that changes products frequently, disposable components (such as single-use tubing, container bags, and filter housings) offer major advantages:

  • Removes the heavy workload of cleaning validation
  • Eliminates the risk of cross-contamination
  • Shortens changeover time between batches

Note, however, that evaluation of extractables/leachables for single-use components is still required.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO is considering converting some components of a nonsterile oral liquid line to single-use. Which components are best suited to conversion? What potential risks need to be considered?
  2. The raw materials for a nonsterile nasal spray include a naturally sourced polysaccharide excipient whose microbial count is often close to the specification limit. What "active control measures" would you recommend to reduce incoming bioburden?

Section Summary

  • API microbial control: The extreme conditions of chemical synthesis are a natural barrier, but the final steps (especially water washing and crystallization) are the key points of microbial risk; the final API step for sterile products should use WFI
  • Sanitary equipment design: four principles: cleanability, complete drainage, sanitary fittings, and appropriate materials (stainless steel, Ra 15–20 µm). "Dry after cleaning" is the simplest and most effective means of microbial control
  • Hold time validation: Both clean hold time and dirty hold time must be validated, which is especially critical for a CDMO's frequent product changeover schedule
  • Personnel gowning: Gowning requirements for nonsterile manufacturing are far lower than for sterile processes, and gloves and masks are needed only for direct product contact
  • Environmental monitoring: positioned as an "informational survey" and not used for product-release decisions; most nonsterile environments do not need alert/action levels; monitoring frequency should match the risk of the dosage form
  • Active control: heat decontamination, raw material bioburden reduction, closed systems, single-use components, and classified environments, "deployed as needed" based on risk assessment

Overall Management of a Microbiological Control Program

Original Text

The management of a successful microbiological control program includes the following: identification of suitable suppliers of pharmaceutical ingredients and excipients that have good microbiological quality; conducting a microbial risk assessment of the manufacturing process and packaging system; and the establishment of an appropriate monitoring and control system.

Although environmental contamination is by no means the most significant cause of nonsterile product recalls or contamination events, environmental monitoring may be a program adjunct to the microbiological control program. Microbial monitoring is an assessment and is not by itself a contamination control activity. There have been no scientifically controlled studies demonstrating what linkage, if any, exists between airborne or surface monitoring results and microbiological safety of the final product.

"Microbial monitoring is an assessment and is not by itself a contamination control activity."

The microbiological contamination control program should be developed for identifying and controlling product risk, based on a formal assessment of risk modalities. The risk analysis should result in the identification of critical control points and should facilitate proper equipment selection, process layout and design, and facility design requirements.

Instructor Analysis

Core Concepts: Program Architecture

The three pillars of a microbiological control program:

  1. Supplier Management — ensures the microbiological quality of raw materials and excipients. This is "source control": if incoming material quality is poor, even excellent downstream controls are closing the barn door after the horse has bolted
  2. Risk Assessment — a formal microbiological risk assessment of the process and packaging system to identify critical control points (CCPs)
  3. Monitoring & Control System — establishes appropriate monitoring and control mechanisms. Note that "monitoring" and "control" are two different activities

The most important sentence: "Microbial monitoring is an assessment and is not by itself a contamination control activity." Monitoring by itself does not control contamination. Testing more environmental sites will not make your product any cleaner.

Analogy

Thermometer vs. fever reducer:

Environmental monitoring is like taking a temperature: it tells you the current condition (whether you have a fever), but no matter how many times you measure, the fever will not go down. Bringing it down takes treatment (active control measures).

The same applies here: no amount of environmental microbial monitoring will lower your product's bioburden. What actually lowers bioburden is good water system management, qualified raw material suppliers, effective cleaning procedures, appropriate equipment design, and well-trained personnel.

USP goes further and notes that no scientific study has demonstrated a definite correlation between environmental monitoring results and the microbiological safety of the finished product. It is like saying "the temperature at your front door may have no direct relationship to how fresh the food in your refrigerator is."

  2. A quality manager argues that "environmental monitoring data can prove our nonsterile products are safe." According to USP ⟨1115⟩, what is wrong with this claim? How would you guide him to the correct understanding of the role of monitoring?

Key Points: Monitoring vs. Control

Monitoring ≠ control: common misconceptions at CDMOs:

CDMO practical advice: Do not over-invest in environmental monitoring. Focus on supplier quality, equipment cleaning validation, water system management, and hold time control. These are the real "fever reducers."

Critical Factors for Prevention

Original Text

Critical factors for the prevention of microbiological contamination during nonsterile product manufacturing are the control of the microbiological quality of ingredients and water, along with the development of proper cleaning and sanitization procedures. Microbiological monitoring does not mitigate risk, but it may serve as a sentinel.

"No monitoring program can provide the assurance of contamination control as effectively as sound, proactive and preventive measures."

Consistent control of contamination can be achieved mainly by an overall process evaluation assessing each of the control elements described above via risk assessment. Risk assessment may be coupled with active evaluation studies to ensure that appropriate measures are in place to prevent conditions conducive to contamination.

Instructor Analysis

Core Concepts: Prevention Hierarchy

USP's concluding statement: prevention is better than monitoring:

At the end of the chapter, USP sets out a clear order of priority:

Microbiological quality control of raw materials and water
↓
Development and validation of cleaning and sanitization procedures
↓
Environmental monitoring (as a sentinel only)

The sentinel concept: Monitoring acts like a sentry at the border. It cannot stop an attack (it cannot control contamination), but it can detect abnormalities early (early detection of trend changes).

Analogy

Smoke detectors vs. fire-safe design:

The fire safety of a building is not assured by installing many smoke detectors (monitoring). Real fire safety comes from non-combustible building materials (ingredient quality), proper fire compartments (process design), and sprinkler systems (cleaning/sanitization). A smoke detector only raises the alarm once a fire has already started. It is a "sentinel," not a "firefighter."

This is the point of USP's conclusion: "No monitoring program can provide the assurance of contamination control as effectively as sound, proactive and preventive measures."

Key Points: CDMO Control Program

Priorities for building a CDMO microbiological control program:

  1. Priority 1: Water systems — design, recirculation, temperature control, sanitization, bioburden monitoring
  2. Priority 2: Incoming material control — supplier evaluation, incoming bioburden limits, receipt testing, storage conditions
  3. Priority 3: Equipment cleaning — cleaning validation, hold time validation, dryness verification, CIP system maintenance
  4. Priority 4: Process control — identification of critical control points, in-process bioburden monitoring, hold time management
  5. Priority 5: Environmental control — personnel gowning, environmental cleaning, and environmental monitoring where necessary

Note that this order matches exactly the "five major sources of contamination" introduced in Section 0 (water > raw materials > equipment > personnel > environment).

Microbial Characterization & Objectionable Organisms

Original Text

It is not possible to provide a comprehensive product-by-product list of objectionable microorganisms. The degree to which any organism may be objectionable depends on the product attributes, route of administration, and patient population. Manufacturers are responsible for determining whether microorganisms recovered from drug products are objectionable.

In general, objectionable microorganisms are those that are known to be truly pathogenic, considering the product's route of administration. Risk may also arise where microorganisms are able to proliferate in sufficient numbers that could result in an unacceptable level of patient risk.

General information chapter ⟨1113⟩ Microbial Characterization, Identification, and Strain Typing contains general information about microbial characterization. In most nonsterile hygienic assessments, characterization of the microorganisms, cellular morphology, Gram reaction, and simple diagnostic testing are sufficient.

Manufacturers should sufficiently identify microorganisms to gain an appropriate understanding of bioburden patterns and seasonal variability.

Instructor Analysis

Core Concepts: Objectionable Organisms

The logic for judging "objectionable organisms": there is no one-size-fits-all list:

USP again emphasizes that there is no universal list of "objectionable organisms." Deciding whether an organism is "objectionable" requires considering three dimensions:

  • Product attributes — water activity, pH, preservative content, etc.
  • Route of administration — oral vs. topical vs. inhalation, etc.
  • Patient population — healthy adults vs. immunocompromised patients

Two sources of "objectionability":

  1. Known pathogens — such as Salmonella (oral products), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (topical products), Staphylococcus aureus (topical products)
  2. Excessive numbers — even if an organism is not a pathogen, if it can proliferate in the product to hazardous levels, this is also an "objectionable condition"

Analogy

Individual differences in food allergy:

Peanuts are a safe food for most people but can be fatal for someone with a peanut allergy. In the same way, certain microorganisms (such as common environmental flora) pose no threat at all in an oral tablet used by healthy adults, yet the same organism in an inhalation product for immunocompromised patients could be "objectionable."

This is why USP does not provide a universal list: "objectionable" is determined by the combination of product and patient, not by the organism itself.

Key Points: Identification Level

Depth of microbial identification: "sufficient" is enough:

USP states clearly that, for nonsterile hygiene assessment, the following are usually all that is needed:

  • Observation of cellular morphology
  • Gram reaction
  • Simple diagnostic tests

You do not need to identify to the species or even subspecies level as in sterile processing. But you do need enough identification to understand bioburden patterns and seasonal variation.

Seasonal variability: The microbial composition of nonsterile environments changes with the seasons. Summer typically brings more Gram-negative bacteria and molds (higher temperature and humidity), while winter may be dominated by Gram-positive bacteria. Understanding these patterns helps distinguish normal fluctuation from abnormal conditions.

  2. The CDMO's QC team proposes identifying all environmental monitoring isolates from nonsterile products to the species level. Is this necessary under USP ⟨1115⟩? What alternative would you suggest?

Integrated CDMO Perspective

Synthesis: Connecting the Dots

USP ⟨1115⟩ provides a comprehensive framework for nonsterile product bioburden control. For CDMOs that operate both sterile and nonsterile manufacturing lines, the key challenge is implementing proportionate controls — rigorous enough to ensure patient safety, but not so excessive as to waste resources or create unnecessary complexity.

The chapter emphasizes that nonsterile product manufacturing requires a fundamentally different mindset from sterile manufacturing:

  • Risk tolerance: Some bioburden is acceptable and expected
  • Monitoring philosophy: Qualitative assessment, not quantitative control
  • Prevention focus: Proactive design and process controls over reactive monitoring
  • Proportionality: Controls scaled to product risk, not applied uniformly

Instructor Analysis

Analogy

Airport domestic terminal vs. international terminal:

A single airport has a domestic terminal (nonsterile) and an international terminal (sterile). Security screening (monitoring) at the domestic terminal is simpler because the risk is lower; at the international terminal it is very strict because the risk is higher. But the two terminals share runways (water systems), baggage handling systems (raw material warehousing), and some staff.

Managing nonsterile and sterile lines at a CDMO is like managing these two terminals: different safety standards, but the shared infrastructure needs unified management.

Key Points: Action Items for CDMO

CDMO action list after reading USP ⟨1115⟩:

  • Review the existing microbiological control programs for nonsterile products: are standards from sterile processing being over-applied?
  • Confirm that incoming bioburden limits are set reasonably according to product risk level
  • Check that water system monitoring frequency and limits match the actual risk
  • Verify that equipment clean hold time and dirty hold time have been validated
  • Assess whether the environmental monitoring program is "sufficient but not excessive": are there unnecessary sampling sites or excessive frequencies?
  • Confirm that the objectionable organism assessment procedure is based on product characteristics, route of administration, and patient population
  • Check that the USP chapters cited in SOP documents are correct (take particular care not to confuse ⟨1116⟩ with ⟨1115⟩)

  • The core philosophy of USP ⟨1115⟩: a scientifically sound, risk-based, pragmatic approach to microbiological management of nonsterile products, neither excessive nor insufficient
