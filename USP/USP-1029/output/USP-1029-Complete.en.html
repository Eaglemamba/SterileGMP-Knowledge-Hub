Learning Objectives

  • Understand the core principles of Good Documentation Practices (GDocP), including accuracy, traceability, and timeliness
  • Master the components of the different types of GMP documents: batch records, SOPs, analytical procedures, CoAs, etc.
  • Identify the key considerations for electronic record systems (LIMS, ELN) in meeting 21 CFR Part 11 requirements
  • Be able to apply correct document correction and deviation recording procedures in a CDMO manufacturing environment
  • Understand the compliance requirements for training records and document retention policies
Section 1: Introduction — Purpose & Scope

Original Text

Purpose

Documentation can be viewed as the foundation of all quality systems because clear, complete, accurate records are essential to all operations and procedures. This general chapter provides guidelines on good documentation practices for the Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-regulated industries, to be used in the production and control of pharmaceutical products, active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), excipients, dietary supplements, food ingredients, and medical devices.

This chapter describes the underlying principles of proper documentation for GMP operations to assist the user while working with GMP activities. These guidelines should be helpful for building the basic foundation of a quality system that will ensure proper documentation as well as record integrity and control.

Scope

This chapter covers different levels and types of documentation, including paper and electronic records that consist of raw data, reports, protocols, and procedures related to manufacturing controls and analytical data. The chapter also includes recommendations on information that should be recorded for various types of GMP documents. Electronic systems should be developed to meet guidelines described in this chapter.

This chapter does not provide information about all applicable current legal requirements, nor does it affect any applicable current requirements under GMP regulations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

USP <1029> positions documentation as the foundation of the entire quality system, a position with far-reaching significance in the pharmaceutical industry. A quality system without sound documentation is like a building without a foundation: it may look intact, but it is fundamentally fragile. The chapter applies broadly to GMP-regulated industries, including pharmaceuticals (APIs and finished products), health supplements, and medical devices.

  • Paper records → require indelible ink and must not be alterable
  • Electronic records → must comply with 21 CFR Part 11
  • Both → traceability, accuracy, and completeness apply

Analogy

Think of GMP documents as the chain of custody for evidence in court. Every record must be able to answer: "Who did it? When? What was done?" If any link is broken, the whole chain of evidence is invalid. In pharmaceuticals, this chain runs from receipt of raw materials all the way to the patient taking the medicine, and the cost of a broken chain is patient safety.

Key Notes

This chapter does not replace current regulatory requirements. In a CDMO environment you must satisfy all of the following at once: the USP <1029> guidance + the client's specific GMP requirements + local regulatory requirements (such as FDA 21 CFR and EU GMP). Where they conflict, the most stringent applies.

Practice Questions

  1. In your facility, which documents are paper and which are electronic? What are the controls for each?
  2. If a page of a batch record is unclear, what is your procedure for handling it?
Section 2: Principles of Good Documentation

Original Text

All steps related to the manufacturing, testing, packing, or holding of pharmaceutical products, APIs, excipients, dietary supplements, food ingredients, and medical devices should be documented.

Good documentation principles for manual or electronic records include the following, as applicable:

  • Records should be clear, concise, accurate, and legible.
  • Data entries should be recorded promptly when actions are performed.
  • Backdating and postdating are not allowed.
  • All corrections to the original entries should be initialed and dated (or captured within an electronic audit trail), with an explanation included in cases where the reason for the change is not obvious.
  • Data entries should be traceable to the person who made the entry.
  • Uncommon abbreviations and acronyms should be defined.
  • Controls should be in place to protect the integrity of the records.
  • In the event that ink may have faded over time (e.g., thermal paper), a copy can be used with verification of its accuracy; the copy should be initialed and dated.
  • Notebooks, data sheets, and worksheets should be traceable.
  • An adequate documentation system is needed to ensure data integrity and availability of current and archived records.
  • Records should be retained per regulatory requirements and be readable during the retention period.
  • All pages should be paginated. Attachments should be paginated with a reference to the parent document.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — ALCOA+ Principles

These 12 principles correspond to the well-known ALCOA+ data integrity framework:

  • Attributable: traceable to a specific person
  • Legible: clearly readable and permanently recorded
  • Contemporaneous: recorded at the time of the activity, never filled in afterward
  • Original: the first-hand record, not a copy
  • Accurate: reflects what actually happened
  • +Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available

Analogy — The "Single-Line Strikethrough" Method of Correcting Records

Correcting a record is like amending a legal document: you cannot white out the original text. You draw a single line through it, write the correct information beside it, and sign and date it. It is like a judge amending a judgment: the original ruling remains visible, the amendment is clearly marked, and the person who made it is identified. The purpose is to ensure the original record is always traceable.

Key Notes — Special Handling of Thermal Paper

Thermal paper (such as cash register receipts) fades over time, and USP expressly allows a verified copy to be made as a substitute. In a CDMO, the printouts from some equipment fall into this category. Procedure: copy immediately after printing → annotate the copy "verified copy of original" → sign and date → file together with the original (or retain in place of the original once it has faded).

Practical Application

During aseptic filling, an operator writes a wrong number on the fill weight record. The correct way to fix it: draw a single line through the wrong number → write the correct number in the adjacent blank space → add your initials and the date/time → where appropriate, add an explanation of the correction (such as "transposition error"). Prohibited: using correction fluid, tearing out and rewriting the whole page, and unsigned changes.

Section 3: Data Collection and Recording

Original Text

Formats for data collection and recording include, but are not limited to, the following:

  • Paper forms, data sheets, and worksheets
  • Notebooks and logbooks
  • Instrument printouts
  • Electronic data obtained with a system such as an electronic data system, laboratory information management system (LIMS), or electronic laboratory notebook (ELN)

All data should be permanently recorded directly and legibly when the activity is performed. If it is paper record, then it should be recorded in indelible ink. All data entries should be traceable to who made the entry and when. Additionally, electronic records must meet the requirements of the Code of Federal Regulations Title 21 (21 CFR), Part 11.

Any change to an entry should be made in a way that does not obscure the original entry. For clarity, predefined correction codes may be used, for example, WD = wrong date.

Notebook pages and worksheets should be used consecutively, and information should be recorded chronologically. GMP records such as batch records, test methods, and specifications should be given unique identifiers and use version control for the documents.

All data entry fields should be completed. A single line and/or "N/A" should be drawn through portion(s) of a page that are not used.

All dates should be expressed in a format that clearly indicates the day, month, and year.

All GMP records for data collection should undergo appropriate review and signature by a second person to confirm the accuracy, compliance, and completeness of the entries. Additional signatures may be required on the basis of local SOPs for different levels of review (e.g., performed by, verified by, checked by, reviewed by, approved by) as accountability steps.

In the event that verified copies of raw data have been prepared, the verified copy may be substituted for the original source as raw data.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — 21 CFR Part 11

21 CFR Part 11 is the core US FDA regulation on electronic records and electronic signatures. If your facility uses any electronic system to record GMP data, it must meet the following:

  • Audit trail: automatically records all actions (who, when, what)
  • Electronic signatures: must uniquely identify an individual; shared accounts are not allowed
  • System validation: the LIMS/ELN itself must undergo computerized system validation (CSV)
  • Data security: prevents unauthorized deletion or modification

Analogy — Version Control

Document version control is like the "version history" function in Word, but more formal. Imagine every SOP has its own "ID number" (document number) plus a "date of birth" (version number and effective date). When a new version becomes effective, the old version is automatically "retired" but does not disappear, just like a company org chart: even when personnel change, the old charts are kept in the historical archive. This lets you reconstruct, at any point in time, which version of a procedure was in force then.

Formula — Standardizing Date Formats

Correct date format examples:
DD-MMM-YYYY  →  06-APR-2026
DD/MM/YYYY   →  06/04/2026
YYYY-MM-DD   →  2026-04-06

Avoid:
MM/DD/YY  →  04/06/26  (US style, easily confused with the European style)
4/6/26    →  (ambiguous: April 6 or June 4?)

Recommendation: the company should mandate a single format and apply it consistently across all SOPs and forms.

Key Notes — The Meaning of Second-Person Review

Second-person review is not a "rubber stamp". The reviewer must genuinely check: Are the calculations correct? Are the units consistent? Are the results within specification? Are missing fields explained? If the reviewer finds a problem, a deviation procedure should be initiated rather than filling in the gap on the author's behalf. Collusion (forging another person's signature) is a serious data integrity violation and can lead to an FDA warning letter.

Practice Questions

  1. How long is the audit trail in your facility's LIMS retained? Does it meet regulatory requirements?
  2. When a value on an instrument printout is found not to match the manual record, how should it be handled?
  3. Is there an SOP governing the use of "N/A"? Under what circumstances may N/A be entered?
Section 4: Different Types of GMP Documents

Original Text

Laboratory Records

Laboratory records should be organized to ensure that the records are concise, clear, legible, and accurate, and detail the following:

  • Description of materials, such as reagents (material name, manufacturer, lot number, titer/concentration, expiration date, grade)
  • Identification of equipment used (equipment name, unique control number, calibration expiration date)
  • Procedures used
  • Measurements
  • Formulae and calculations
  • Results and conclusions

Equipment-Related Documentation

All equipment used in manufacturing, testing, packing, or holding should be maintained and qualified for its intended use. Documentation includes:

  • Policies and procedures for operation and maintenance
  • Equipment use
  • Maintenance records
  • Calibration or qualification records
  • Instrument labeling

Deviations and Investigations

All aberrations, anomalies, and exceptions should be documented. Documentation of the investigation should include:

  • Description of the event
  • Root-cause investigation
  • Evaluation of data trend
  • Responsibilities of people involved
  • Impact assessment
  • Corrective Action and Preventive Action (CAPA) with timelines
  • Review and approval

Batch Records

A Master Batch Record (MBR) is created as a template for the manufacture of a specific product. An Executed Batch Record, based on the MBR, is used to document the steps and materials involved in the production of a specific batch. Typically includes:

  • Header information (product name, batch number, manufacturing site)
  • Unit of operation (blending, coating, filling)
  • Manufacturing process (target weights, conditions, deviations)
  • In-process sampling or testing
  • Sampling plan for release, stability, and retention
  • Review and approval

Certificate of Analysis

The purpose of the Certificate of Analysis (CoA) is to report analytical results for a specific batch. Typically includes:

  • Vendor/supplier/manufacturer information
  • Product information (name and strength)
  • Results for the specific batch (test name, acceptance criteria, result)
  • Conformance statement
  • Reference to procedure and specification document
  • Approval and date
  • Expiration date or retest information

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The GMP Document Hierarchy

GMP documents can be divided into three tiers, forming a pyramid structure:

  • Tier 1 (Policy): quality manual, quality policy; they explain "why"
  • Tier 2 (Procedures): SOPs, specifications, validation protocols; they explain "how"
  • Tier 3 (Records): batch records, laboratory records, deviation reports; they show "what was done"

The batch record is especially important: the MBR is the "recipe template", while the Executed Batch Record is the "cooking diary", recording everything that actually happened each time, including any deviations.

Analogy — The CoA Is the Product's "Report Card"

Think of the CoA as your high school report card: the student's name (product name), student ID (batch number), the grade for each subject (test results), the passing standard (acceptance criteria), and the principal's seal (authorized person's signature). Parents (clients) use the report card to judge whether the child (the product) meets the standard. If the report card has alterations or false data, it is not only a matter of integrity but could also cause patient harm.

Key Notes — Timeliness of CAPA

CAPAs from deviation investigations must include timelines. This is not formalism: a CAPA without a deadline is no commitment at all. During FDA/EMA inspections, inspectors pay particular attention to whether CAPAs were completed on time and whether they were effective. In a CDMO, clients usually require deviation reports to be completed and submitted within a set time (such as 30 or 60 days).

Practical Application — CDMO Scenario

In contract fill-finish operations, the MBR is provided by the client (or developed jointly with the CDMO). For each production run, the CDMO executes against the MBR and completes the Executed Batch Record. Key points: the client has the right to review the batch record → the CDMO must ensure the record is complete, legible, and free of unexplained blank fields. Any deviation must be raised in a deviation report; you cannot simply decide on your own that it is unimportant and let it go.

Section 5: SOPs, Protocols, Reports, Analytical Procedures

Original Text

Standard Operating Procedures

The purpose of an SOP is to provide directions to trained personnel regarding a given set of activities. SOPs should be clear and concise. Typical sections include:

  • Purpose and scope
  • Instructions and procedure
  • Responsibilities and roles
  • Materials or equipment, as appropriate
  • Definitions or references, as needed
  • Review and approval
  • Revision history

Protocols and Reports

Many tasks and activities are executed on the basis of a predefined, preapproved protocol. Examples of such activities:

  • Equipment qualification
  • Analytical method validation or verification
  • Manufacturing process validation
  • Analytical method or manufacturing technology transfer
  • Cleaning validation
  • Stability study or testing
  • Comparability study

Both the protocol and the report should typically include: Purpose; Plan or instructions; Predetermined acceptance criteria; Deviations or investigations (report only); Assessment or evaluation (report only); Data reference (report only); Review and approval; Revision history.

Analytical Procedures

Analytical procedures provide direction to an operator on how to perform a given analytical test. Typical sections include:

  • Purpose (test information, product information)
  • Safety information, if applicable
  • Materials and equipment
  • Procedure (system suitability; preparation of solutions, reagents, standards, and samples; instrument parameters; calculations and reporting)
  • Review and approval with approval dates
  • Revision history

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — Differences Between a Protocol and an SOP

SOPs and protocols are often confused, but they differ in essence:

  • SOP: applies to repetitive routine activities (such as cleaning procedures); remains in effect long term and is reviewed and updated periodically
  • Protocol: addresses a specific one-time or limited-occurrence activity (such as facility validation); has a defined start and end, and produces a Report once executed
  • Analytical Procedure: sits between the two; applies to a specific test method, is more detailed than an SOP, and includes system suitability requirements

Analogy — An SOP Is Like a "Recipe", a Protocol Like a "Tasting Trial Plan"

An SOP is a fixed recipe that any cook can use to make the same dish every day. A protocol is the "tasting trial plan" a Michelin chef writes to test a new ingredient: it has a clear test objective and predefined success criteria, and after the trial a "tasting report" (Report) is written to explain the results. Only then might the recipe become a formal SOP.

Key Notes — Pre-Defined Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria in a validation/qualification protocol must be defined before execution; you cannot decide "that counts as a pass" after seeing the results. This is the key safeguard against results-driven decisions. FDA inspectors pay particular attention to whether the criteria in the pre-execution version of the protocol are identical to those in the final report. If anything changed, a written Protocol Amendment explaining the reason is required.

Practice Questions

  1. How many years is the SOP review cycle in your facility? How do you ensure SOP content still reflects actual practice?
  2. If a system suitability test (SST) in an analytical procedure fails, what is your process?
  3. When should the acceptance criteria of a cleaning validation protocol be decided, and by whom?
Section 6: Training Documentation & Retention of Documents

Original Text

Training Documentation

Personnel should be trained to perform their assigned tasks. The training should be documented, and the training records should be retained and kept readily accessible. In general, training documentation should include:

  • Training description including name of training, version, and mode (self-training or instructor led)
  • Completion date
  • Information on the trainer, as applicable

Retention of Documents

An adequate policy for record retention and archiving should be established for the above records. The required length of time depends on the regulatory requirements or company procedures; however, it should be at least 1 year after the batch expiration date.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — Regulatory Requirements for Training Records

Training records are always examined in an inspection. Key elements:

  • The training title must include the version number, to ensure the version an employee was trained on matches the version currently in force
  • Training mode: self-training vs instructor-led have different documentation requirements
  • Completion date: training must be completed before the SOP effective date before the employee may perform the related operations
  • Trainer information: especially for OJT (on-the-job training), who did the training must be recorded

Analogy — Document Retention Is Like Medical Records

Pharmaceutical document retention is like a hospital's medical record retention system. USP requires retention for at least 1 year after the batch expiry date, but this is only the minimum. FDA 21 CFR Part 211 requires batch records to be retained for at least 1 year after expiry, and not less than 3 years. EU GMP requires at least 1 year (generally at least 2 years). In a CDMO, client contract requirements (usually longer) must also be considered. The recommendation is to follow the most stringent requirement and establish a unified Retention Matrix.

Key Notes — The Importance of Version Tracking

"Which version of the SOP was the employee trained on?" is a very important question during an inspection. If the SOP has been updated but the employee has not yet been trained on the new version, then legally the employee performs the operation under the old version. Solution: the training management system (LMS) should automatically track the document version each employee has been trained on, and automatically trigger a retraining requirement when a document is updated.

Practical Application

Establishing a Retention Matrix is best practice: list all document types × the retention requirements of each major regulator (FDA/EMA/Japan PMDA, etc.) → adopt the longest requirement among them as the company standard. For example: batch record = max(FDA 3 years, EU 5 years, client requirement) → the longest governs, ensuring global compliance.

Chapter Summary

  • Documentation is the foundation of the quality system: without documentation, there is no verifiable quality. GDocP ensures every step of an operation is traceable.
  • ALCOA+ principles: all records must be Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, and Accurate, plus Complete, Consistent, Enduring, and Available.
  • Correction rules: single-line strikethrough + signature + date + explanation where necessary; overwriting or obliterating is prohibited.
  • Electronic records: must comply with 21 CFR Part 11, including audit trails, electronic signatures, and system validation.
  • Six major GMP document types: laboratory records, equipment documentation, deviation investigations, batch records (MBR/Executed BR), CoA, and SOP/Protocol/analytical procedure.
  • Retention requirements: at least 1 year after batch expiry (requirements vary by region; apply the most stringent).
  • Training records: must record the training version number, completion date, and trainer information.
