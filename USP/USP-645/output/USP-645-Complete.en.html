1. Introduction and Instrument Specifications

Original Text

Electrical conductivity in water is a measure of the ion-facilitated electron flow through it. Water molecules dissociate into ions as a function of pH and temperature and result in a very predictable conductivity. Some gases, most notably carbon dioxide, readily dissolve in water and interact to form ions, which predictably affect conductivity also.

Water conductivity is also affected by the presence of extraneous ions. The extraneous ions used in modeling the conductivity specifications are the chloride and ammonia ions. The conductivity of the chloride ion (at 0.47 ppm) and the ammonium ion (at 0.3 ppm limit) represents a major portion of the allowed water ionic impurity level.

conductivity, κ (S/cm) = Θ (cm⁻¹) / R (Ω)

where Θ = cell constant, R = resistance

Cell Constant (Θ): Must be known within ±2%. Verified directly using a solution of known conductivity, or indirectly by comparing to a sensor of known cell constant.

Resistance Measurement: Use precision resistors traceable to NIST (accurate to ±0.1%). Measured conductivity must be within ±0.1 µS/cm of calculated value. Minimum resolution: 0.1 µS/cm on the lowest range.

Temperature Compensation: Values used in the Stage 1 test for Bulk Water are non–temperature-compensated measurements. Accuracy of temperature measurement must be ±2°.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Conductivity measures the total amount of ions in water. The conductivity of pharmaceutical water is determined mainly by the following factors:

  • Self-dissociation of water: H₂O → H⁺ + OH⁻, influenced by pH and temperature
  • Dissolved CO₂: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ (markedly increases conductivity)
  • Extraneous ions: Cl⁻ (chloride) and NH₄⁺ (ammonium) are the representative contaminants in the model

The three-stage test is designed so that most acceptable samples pass at Stage 1; only borderline samples need the more laborious follow-up tests.

Formula and Calculation

Conductivity calculation:
κ (S/cm) = Θ (cm⁻¹) / R (Ω)

Where:
  Θ = cell constant (d/A)
      d = distance between electrodes (cm)
      A = electrode area (cm²)
  R = resistance (Ω)

Calibration requirements:
  Cell constant Θ error ≤ ±2%
  Resistance measurement accuracy: ±0.1% (NIST traceable)
  Measurement error ≤ ±0.1 µS/cm
  Minimum resolution: 0.1 µS/cm

Temperature measurement accuracy: ±2°C

Analogy

A conductivity meter is like an "ion counter" for water: every ion dissolved in the water acts like a tiny battery, carrying a charge as it moves in the electric field. The more ions there are, the more easily current flows and the higher the conductivity. Because purified water contains very few ions, its conductivity is close to the theoretical minimum for pure water.

The cell constant is like the "calibration scale of the instrument": probes with different geometries need different correction factors, just as different rulers need to be checked to confirm that their graduations are accurate.

Key Notes

Why is temperature compensation disabled in Stage 1? Temperature compensation algorithms are built on models for specific ion types and may be inaccurate for the complex mix of ions in purified water. USP requires Stage 1 to use the non-temperature-compensated raw reading and compare it directly with the limit in the table (Table 1) for the corresponding temperature, avoiding false negatives or false positives caused by compensation error.

2. Bulk Water: Three-Stage Test

Original Text

This is a three-stage test method to accommodate online or offline testing. The procedure can be started at Stage 2 if offline testing is preferred.

STAGE 1 (online or offline):

  1. Determine temperature and non–temperature-compensated conductivity.
  2. Using Table 1, find the temperature value NMT the measured temperature. The corresponding conductivity is the limit. [Note—Do not interpolate.]
  3. If measured conductivity ≤ table value: passes. If higher: proceed to Stage 2.

Table 1 (selected values) — non-temperature-compensated measurements only.

STAGE 2 (offline, equilibrated):

  1. Transfer to suitable container. Maintain at 25 ± 1°C. Vigorously agitate while observing conductivity. When change < 0.1 µS/cm per 5 min, note conductivity. [May be temperature-compensated to 25°.]
  2. If conductivity ≤ 2.1 µS/cm: passes. If > 2.1 µS/cm: proceed to Stage 3.

STAGE 3 (pH equilibration):

  1. Within ~5 min of Stage 2. Add saturated KCl (0.3 mL per 100 mL). Determine pH to nearest 0.1 unit per USP 〈791〉.
  2. Refer to Table 2. If measured conductivity (from step 4) ≤ table value at measured pH: passes. If conductivity > table value OR pH outside 5.0–7.0: fails.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The design logic of the three-stage test reflects the possible causes of an out-of-limit conductivity result:

  • Stage 1 (rapid screening): compare directly against the temperature-dependent limit table; most acceptable samples pass here
  • Stage 2 (atmospheric CO₂ equilibration): allow atmospheric CO₂ to dissolve fully to reach equilibrium, ruling out false failures caused by insufficient CO₂; the 2.1 µS/cm limit is the maximum expected for pure water at 25°C after it has absorbed atmospheric CO₂
  • Stage 3 (pH confirmation): after adding KCl to the saturated solution, measure pH to confirm whether the out-of-limit conductivity is caused by ions that shift the pH (acid or base); this gives a precise limit within the pH range of 5.0–7.0

Three-Stage Test Flow and Key Values

STAGE 1 (online measurement, non-temperature-compensated):
  Look up Table 1 and take the value "not greater than the measured temperature"
  25°C → limit 1.3 µS/cm
  40°C → limit 1.7 µS/cm
  [No interpolation! Take the limit for the next lower temperature]

  Pass → done
  Fail → go to Stage 2

STAGE 2 (equilibrate at 25°C, temperature compensation permitted):
  Limit: 2.1 µS/cm (fixed value, all water types)
  Pass → done
  Fail → go to Stage 3

STAGE 3 (add KCl, measure pH):
  pH range: 5.0–7.0
  Look up the limit for the corresponding pH in Table 2
  pH 5.0 → 4.7 µS/cm
  pH 6.0 → 2.4 µS/cm (lowest point)
  pH 7.0 → 4.6 µS/cm
  pH outside 5.0–7.0 → fails outright

Analogy

The three-stage test is like a medical consultation: Stage 1 is a quick temperature check (rapid screening, most people pass); Stage 2 is a standard blood pressure measurement (a baseline test under standard conditions); Stage 3 is a blood draw and lab analysis (the most detailed confirmation, pinpointing the specific problem). The three stages go from fast to slow and from coarse to fine, so most normal samples never need the expensive, time-consuming follow-up tests.

Key Notes

Why "no interpolation" matters in Stage 1: If the water temperature is 27°C, Table 1 has no value for 27°C. Use the nearest temperature value "not greater than 27°C", that is, 1.3 µS/cm for 25°C, as the limit. Do not interpolate between the 25°C and 30°C values, because this would introduce a systematic error.

Stage 2 operating details: "Vigorously agitate" speeds up the dissolution of atmospheric CO₂; "change of < 0.1 µS/cm per 5 minutes" is the criterion for judging that equilibrium has been reached. If a closed container is used, CO₂ cannot dissolve sufficiently and the Stage 2 result will be invalid.

Practical Application

In the WFI system of a sterile manufacturing CDMO, an online conductivity meter is typically installed at key points of the distribution loop for continuous monitoring, with an alert limit (e.g., 1.0 µS/cm) and an action limit (e.g., 1.3 µS/cm). When the alert limit is triggered, the system records the event automatically and notifies QC, but production continues; when the action limit is triggered, the affected water batch is automatically quarantined and a deviation investigation is started. The Stage 1 table lookup is the regulatory basis for this kind of online monitoring.

3. Stage 3 Table and Sterile Water

Original Text

Table 2. Stage 3—pH and Conductivity Requirements (for atmosphere- and temperature-equilibrated samples only)

pH outside 5.0–7.0: water does not meet requirements.

STERILE WATER:

Applies to: Sterile Purified Water, Sterile Water for Injection, Sterile Water for Inhalation, Sterile Water for Irrigation.

Procedure:

  1. Obtain sample; vigorously agitate package before opening.
  2. Transfer to suitable container, stir. Maintain at 25 ± 1°C.
  3. Agitate while observing conductivity; when change < 0.1 µS/cm per 5 min, note conductivity.

Acceptance Criteria:

  • Containers ≤ 10 mL nominal volume: NMT 25 µS/cm
  • Containers > 10 mL nominal volume: NMT 5 µS/cm

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The "V-shaped curve" of Stage 3 Table 2 reveals an important chemical phenomenon:

  • pH < 6.0 (acidic): higher H⁺ concentration → higher conductivity → limit relaxed
  • pH 6.0–6.6 (purest range): close to neutral, fewest ions → lowest conductivity → strictest limit (2.1–2.4 µS/cm)
  • pH > 6.6 (alkaline): higher OH⁻ concentration → conductivity rises again → limit relaxed again

This "V shape" reflects a basic principle of ionic chemistry: pure water has its lowest intrinsic conductivity at about pH 6.0 (after CO₂ equilibration at 25°C).

Analogy

The pH-conductivity relationship in Stage 3 is like a batter's "sweet spot": pH 6.0–6.6 is the sweet spot, where pure water has the fewest ions; any shift toward acidic or alkaline means extra H⁺ or OH⁻ ions, and conductivity naturally rises. USP Table 2 is the precise mathematical expression of this chemical relationship.

Key Notes

Wider limits for Sterile Water: Sterile Water is allowed higher conductivity (≤5 µS/cm vs. ~1.3 µS/cm for Bulk Water at 25°C) for the following reasons:

  • Sterile Water in its container has already absorbed CO₂ fully, so its conductivity is inherently higher
  • The container material (glass/plastic) leaches ions, which increases conductivity
  • Small containers (≤10 mL) have a larger surface area-to-volume ratio and leach more → wider limit (25 µS/cm)

Why "shake vigorously before opening" is necessary: It homogenizes any leachables that may have settled at the bottom of the container, ensuring a representative sample and avoiding sampling bias.

Conductivity Limits: Sterile Water vs. Bulk Water

Bulk Water (25°C, Stage 1):
  Conductivity limit: 1.3 µS/cm (theoretical value for pure water)
  Applies to: online monitoring of WFI and purified water systems

Bulk Water (Stage 2, after atmospheric CO₂ equilibration):
  Conductivity limit: 2.1 µS/cm
  Applies to: offline confirmatory testing

Sterile Water (finished container test, 25°C, after CO₂ equilibration):
  Containers ≤ 10 mL: ≤ 25 µS/cm
  Containers > 10 mL: ≤ 5 µS/cm

Difference (fold):
  5 µS/cm / 1.3 µS/cm ≈ 3.8-fold
  25 µS/cm / 1.3 µS/cm ≈ 19-fold
  (reflects the additional contribution of container leachables)

Practical Application

In sterile manufacturing validation at a CDMO, the conductivity test is one of the indirect indicators of container-closure integrity. If the conductivity of the same batch of SWFI (sterile water for injection) differs significantly between containers (e.g., some containers at 3 µS/cm and others at 18 µS/cm), this may point to a closure problem, a batch mix-up, or abnormal glass quality in a particular lot. A deviation investigation must be started immediately and the affected samples quarantined.

Chapter Summary

  • Conductivity principle: conduction by ions; κ = Θ/R; cell constant Θ error must be ≤ ±2%
  • Main interfering ions: Cl⁻ (0.47 ppm model), NH₄⁺ (0.3 ppm limit); dissolved CO₂ is an intrinsic influence
  • Design logic of the three-stage Bulk Water test: Stage 1 (rapid, online) → Stage 2 (CO₂ equilibration, 25°C) → Stage 3 (pH confirmation)
  • Stage 1 requirements: non-temperature-compensated; table lookup without interpolation; limit at 25°C is 1.3 µS/cm
  • Stage 2 limit: 2.1 µS/cm (all Bulk Water after atmospheric CO₂ equilibration)
  • Stage 3 conditions: add KCl and measure pH; pH must be within 5.0–7.0; pH 6.0–6.6 is the lowest-conductivity region
  • Sterile Water procedure: shake before opening; equilibrate at 25°C until the change is < 0.1 µS/cm per 5 min
  • Sterile Water limits: 25 µS/cm for containers ≤10 mL; 5 µS/cm for containers >10 mL
  • Temperature accuracy: temperature measurement accuracy for all tests must be ±2°C
