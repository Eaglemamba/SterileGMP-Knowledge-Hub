Section 1: Introduction & Instrument Classification (Groups A/B/C)

Original Text

A large variety of analytical instruments, ranging from a simple apparatus to complex computerized systems, is used in the pharmaceutical industry to acquire data that will help ensure that products meet their specifications. In order to ensure "fitness for purpose", an integrated approach, based upon a risk assessment, is recommended.

The risk assessment for an AIQ enables the classification of the instrument to determine the extent of qualification and actions needed to demonstrate fitness for purpose. Generally, the more complex the instrument, or the higher the criticality of the measurement, the greater the amount of work that is required.

Instruments can generally be classified as belonging to Groups A, B, or C:

  • Group A: The least complex, standard instruments used without measurement capability or user requirement for calibration, such as a magnetic stirrer or vortex mixer. Proper function is ensured by observation, and no further qualification activities are needed.
  • Group B: Instruments that may provide a measurement or an experimental condition that can affect a measurement. Examples include a pH meter or an oven. May require only routine calibration, maintenance, or performance checks. Generally have firmware but not user-updated software.
  • Group C: Analytical instruments with a significant degree of computerization and complexity, such as high-pressure liquid chromatographs and mass spectrometers. All elements of qualification, including software validation, must be considered.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Practical Judgment in Instrument Classification

The core question in classification is: "Do this instrument's measurement results directly affect a release decision?"

  • Group A (observation is enough): stirrers, general-purpose centrifuges, ultrasonic cleaners. It is enough to confirm that the instrument is running.
  • Group B (calibration is enough): pH meters, balances, water baths, ovens, thermometers. These need periodic calibration and retained calibration certificates.
  • Group C (full AIQ): HPLC, GC, mass spectrometers, UV spectrophotometers, Karl Fischer titrators, particle size analyzers. These need the full DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ package.

Note: the same instrument model may fall into different groups depending on its use. For example, a balance used for general weighing = Group B; a balance used to weigh reference material for standard preparation may require Group C-level control.

Analogy: AIQ as a "Driver's License System"

Think of AIQ as a driver's license system. A motorcycle (Group A, simple) needs only a regular license; a car (Group B, moderate) needs a periodic roadworthiness inspection; a heavy trailer (Group C, complex) needs a special license, periodic inspection and a trip recorder. The more complex the instrument and the more critical its use, the more complete the "credentials" it requires.

Key Notes: Instruments of the Same Type Can Fall into Different Groups

USP states explicitly that "the same type of instrument may fall into different groups". A centrifuge used only to separate solids from liquids is Group A; if it is fitted with temperature control and temperature affects test results, it may need Group B control. When a new instrument is purchased or introduced, the AIQ team must classify it based on its actual intended use and document the rationale for the classification in writing.

Practice Questions

  1. What instruments does your laboratory have? Try classifying them as Group A/B/C and explain your reasoning.
  2. If a pH meter originally used as Group B starts to be used to prepare solutions for calibration curves, how should its classification be adjusted?
Section 2: Components of Data Quality — The Quality Triangle

Original Text

There are four critical components involved in the generation of reliable and consistent data (quality data). AIQ forms the base for generating quality data. The other components essential for generating quality data are analytical method validation, system suitability tests, and quality control check samples.

Analytical Instrument Qualification (AIQ): The collection of documented evidence that an instrument performs suitably for its intended purpose. Use of a qualified instrument in analyses contributes to confidence in the validity of generated data.

Analytical Method Validation: The collection of documented evidence that an analytical procedure is suitable for its intended use. Use of a validated procedure with qualified analytical instruments provides confidence that the procedure will generate test data of acceptable quality.

System Suitability Tests: Verify that the system will perform in accordance with the criteria set forth in the procedure. These tests are performed along with the sample analyses to ensure that the system's performance is acceptable at the time of the test.

Quality Control Check Samples: Provide an in-process or ongoing assurance of the test's suitable performance. System suitability tests and quality control checks help ensure the quality of analytical results immediately before or during sample analysis.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Layered Logic of the Quality Pyramid

The USP "quality triangle" runs from the bottom layer to the top:

  • Bottom layer (AIQ): the instrument itself must be qualified. "The tool is reliable."
  • Second layer (analytical method validation): the analytical method must be validated. "The method is reliable."
  • Third layer (system suitability): the system is verified before each analysis. "The system is reliable right now."
  • Top layer (QC samples): ongoing monitoring during the analysis. "The process is reliable."

If any layer fails, the final analytical result cannot be trusted. AIQ is the foundation of the whole quality structure; if the foundation is not stable, nothing built on top of it is worth anything.

Analogy: The Multiple Layers of Protection in Aviation Safety

These four layers are like an aircraft's multiple safety systems: AIQ = the airworthiness certification before the aircraft leaves the factory; method validation = the pilot's license; system suitability = the pre-flight check before takeoff; QC samples = instrument monitoring during the flight. Without any one layer the aircraft may still fly, but the systemic risk rises sharply.

Key Notes: The Sequence of AIQ and Method Validation

AIQ must be completed before method validation. The logic is that you cannot validate an analytical method on an instrument that has not been qualified, because you cannot tell whether the data reflect the performance of the method or a problem with the instrument. In CDMO technology transfer projects this sequence is especially important: instrument IQ/OQ/PQ is completed first, and only then can method validation or method transfer validation begin.

Practical Application

When a CDMO receives a technology transfer from a client, build an "instrument qualification status matrix": list all relevant instruments × qualification status (DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ completed or not) × date of last qualification × next PQ due date. This matrix is the basis for deciding which instruments can be used immediately for transfer method validation, and it is also important material to present during client audits.

Section 3: AIQ Process — Design Qualification (DQ) & Installation Qualification (IQ)

Original Text

Qualification Phases Overview

AIQ is not a single, continuous process but instead results from interconnected activities over the lifetime of the instrument. The first activity is the generation of a user requirements specification (URS), which defines the laboratory's particular needs and technical and operational requirements. The subsequent qualification activities may be grouped into four phases: design qualification (DQ), installation qualification (IQ), operational qualification (OQ), and performance qualification (PQ).

This framework may be extended for complex systems to include functional specifications (FS) and factory acceptance testing (FAT). Some activities may also be satisfied during instrument installation and start-up, referred to as site acceptance testing (SAT). All AIQ activities should be predefined and contemporaneously documented.

Design Qualification (DQ)

DQ is the documented collection of activities that define the functional and operational specifications and intended purpose of the instrument. DQ states what the laboratory wants the instrument to do and shows that the selected instrument is suitable. DQ may be performed by the instrument manufacturer or the user. For commercial, off-the-shelf instruments, DQ requirements will be minimal.

The supplier is generally responsible for robust design and maintaining documentation. The user should ensure instruments are suitable for their intended application and may evaluate whether the supplier has adopted a quality system. Users should also determine the supplier's capability to support installation, services, and training.

Installation Qualification (IQ)

IQ is the documented collection of activities necessary to establish that an instrument is delivered as designed and specified, is properly installed in the selected environment, and that this environment is suitable for the instrument. The activities and documentation typically associated with IQ are:

  • Instrument delivery: Ensure the instrument, software, manuals, supplies arrive as specified and are undamaged.
  • Description: Document supplier(s), model(s), serial number(s), software version(s), and location.
  • Utilities/Facility/Environment: Verify installation site meets supplier-specified environmental requirements.
  • Assembly and installation: Assemble and install the instrument; perform preliminary diagnostics. Document any abnormal events.
  • Software installation, network, and data storage: Connect to network for communications and data storage.
  • Installation verification: Perform initial diagnostics and testing after installation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The URS Is the Starting Point of AIQ

The user requirements specification (URS) is the "design blueprint" for the whole AIQ. Only when the URS is well written do all subsequent qualification tests have a basis. The URS should include:

  • Intended use (for example: an HPLC used for drug substance assay)
  • Technical requirements (wavelength range, flow rate accuracy, detection sensitivity, etc.)
  • Environmental requirements (temperature and humidity, power specifications, space)
  • Regulatory compliance requirements (21 CFR Part 11, GxP, etc.)
  • Supplier support requirements (technical service, spare parts availability)

Analogy: DQ/IQ Are Like Buying a House and Inspecting It

DQ = the needs assessment and specification before buying a house (number of rooms, layout requirements, location conditions); IQ = the inspection at handover (are the doors and windows installed correctly, are water and electricity connected, does the house match the specification). OQ/PQ are the usage tests after moving in (does the air conditioning work properly, is the noise within an acceptable range). Skipping DQ is like signing a contract without a requirements specification: the house you end up with may not meet your needs at all.

Checklist: Required IQ Documents

IQ Documentation Package:

1. Delivery Receipt
   - Instrument model / serial number ✓
   - Software version number ✓
   - Completeness of accessories ✓
   - No visible damage ✓

2. Installation Record
   - Installation location / environmental conditions (T, RH, voltage)
   - Installer (including qualifications)
   - Initial diagnostic results

3. Software/Network Setup
   - Software installation log
   - Network connection confirmation
   - Audit trail function confirmation

4. IQ execution report (with conclusion and approval signatures)

Key Notes: Responsibility for Reviewing Supplier Documents

The IQ documentation package provided by the supplier (such as the FAT report or factory test report) can "satisfy part of the IQ requirements", but the user remains responsible for reviewing whether these documents meet the user's own requirements. Simply filing the supplier documents does not complete the IQ. Review focus: do the test parameters cover the URS requirements? Does the test environment represent the actual use environment?

Section 4: Operational Qualification (OQ) & Performance Qualification (PQ)

Original Text

Operational Qualification (OQ)

OQ is the documented collection of activities necessary to demonstrate that an instrument will function according to its operational specification testing in the selected environment. OQ demonstrates fitness for the selected use, and should reflect URS. Testing activities in the OQ phase may consist of:

  • Fixed parameters: Non-changing parameters such as length, height, weight, voltage inputs, acceptable pressures, and loads. Fixed parameters do not change over the life of the instrument, and therefore never need to be retested.
  • Software functions: Critical elements of the configured application software—data capture, analysis, reporting, security, access control, and audit trail.
  • Secure data storage, backup, and archiving: Test secure data handling at the user's site.
  • Instrument function tests: Verify that the instrument operates as intended by the manufacturer.

OQ tests can be modular or holistic. Modular testing of individual components may facilitate interchanging components without requalification but requires a risk assessment. Holistic tests, which involve the entire system, demonstrate that the whole system complies with URS.

Performance Qualification (PQ)

PQ is the documented collection of activities necessary to demonstrate that an instrument consistently performs according to the specifications defined by the user, and is appropriate for the intended use. After IQ and OQ have been performed, the instrument's continued suitability for its intended use is demonstrated through continued PQ.

PQ may include:

  • Performance checks: Usually based on the instrument's typical on-site applications, may consist of analyzing known components or standards. Testing frequency depends on the ruggedness of the instrument and the criticality of the analytic method.
  • Preventive maintenance and repairs: Periodic preventive maintenance activities. When an instrument fails to meet PQ criteria or malfunctions, the cause must be investigated and documented. The relevant OQ or PQ test(s) should be repeated after maintenance or repair.
  • Practices for PQ, change control, and periodic review: Each PQ, maintenance, and calibration activity should be documented. Change control for instrument configuration, including firmware and software. Critical instruments should have a periodic review.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Essential Difference Between OQ and PQ

This is one of the most commonly confused concepts:

  • OQ: tests what the instrument "can do". It demonstrates conformance to the manufacturer's specifications, usually with reference standards or simulated conditions, and is essentially a one-time activity (repeated after major changes).
  • PQ: tests whether the instrument "continues to perform well for our intended use". It demonstrates conformance to the user's requirements, using actual samples or near-actual conditions, and is ongoing.
  • Analogy: OQ = the performance test of a new car at the factory (how many seconds from 0 to 100 km/h); PQ = the routine check you do before each drive (fuel gauge, tire pressure, engine oil).

Analogy: PQ Is Like a Regular Health Checkup

Instrument PQ is like a person's regular health checkup: it is not done because the instrument is "sick", but to confirm periodically that it is "still healthy". The frequency is determined by risk: an older instrument, heavy use (high workload), or measurement of critical parameters (high-risk use) means a higher checkup frequency. A record is created for every PQ, and the accumulated PQ history becomes the instrument's "health record", which can be used for trend analysis and predictive maintenance.

Key Notes: The Need for Periodic Review

USP specifically notes that "critical instruments should have periodic review". The review covers: is the qualification/validation status still valid? Are the user procedures current? Are the change control records complete? Are the system-generated records correct and complete? Do electronic record backup and restore work properly? This review is usually done annually and is part of the quality system, not just a task for the instrument department.

Practical Application

In the QC laboratory of a CDMO handling sterile products, an HPLC is used for drug substance assay (a critical release test). Suggested PQ frequency: a performance check once per quarter (using reference standards to test system precision and repeatability), plus a system suitability test (SST) before each use as the routine verification. This tiered qualification strategy (PQ + SST) both satisfies regulatory requirements and effectively controls the qualification workload.

Section 5: Roles, Responsibilities, Software Validation & Change Control

Original Text

Roles and Responsibilities

Users: Ultimately responsible for specifying their needs and ensuring that a selected instrument meets them, and that data quality and integrity are maintained. Users should be adequately trained and their training records maintained. Users should be responsible for qualifying their instruments—their training and expertise make them the best-qualified group to design instrument tests and specifications.

Quality Unit: Responsible for ensuring that the AIQ process meets compliance requirements, that processes are being followed, and that the intended use of the instrument is supported by complete, valid, and documented data.

Manufacturers, Suppliers, Service Agents, and Consultants: Manufacturers are responsible for designing and manufacturing the instrument. There should be a quality or technical agreement between the user organization and manufacturers/suppliers/service agents/consultants who supply calibration, maintenance, qualification, or validation services.

Software Validation

Software used for analytical instruments can be classified into four groups: firmware, instrument control software, data acquisition software, and processing software.

Firmware: Considered a component of the instrument itself. When the hardware is qualified, the firmware is also essentially qualified. The firmware version should be recorded as part of the IQ activities.

Instrument Control, Data Acquisition, and Processing Software: Three types: 1) non-configurable software; 2) configurable software; and 3) configurable software with custom additions. Integrated qualification of instrument plus software validation involves the entire system.

Change Control

A change control process should be in place to guide the assessment, execution, documentation, and approval of any changes to instrumentation. Users should assess the effects of changes to determine what, if any, requalification activities are required. After implementation, perform any required testing. Document all details of the change, including description, rationale, and identification of new components.

Documentation

Documents obtained during qualification activities should be retained in an accessible manner. During change control, additional documents may supplement those obtained during the qualification process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The User Is Responsible for AIQ

USP states explicitly that responsibility for instrument qualification lies with the user, not the manufacturer or supplier. This differs from what many people assume: it is not the case that "the equipment vendor has done the IQ/OQ, so we are done". Even when an external service provider is engaged to perform the qualification, the user must still: review whether the qualification plan meets the URS, then participate in or oversee execution, then review and approve the qualification report, and take responsibility for the qualification conclusions. The role of the quality unit is to ensure compliance, not to replace the user in performing the qualification.

Analogy: The "Three Kinds of Cooks" in Software Validation

Software categories are like three restaurant cooking models: Non-configurable = buying a boxed lunch (fixed menu, the vendor is responsible for quality); Configurable = a semi-prepared meal kit (fixed framework, but you can adjust the seasoning); Custom-coded = cooking from scratch (full control, but you are fully responsible for quality). The higher the degree of customization, the greater the validation effort and the more responsibility you carry for quality.

Key Notes: Triggers for Change Control

Instrument change control must be initiated in the following situations: replacement of parts (especially critical components such as the HPLC column oven or detector); software/firmware version upgrades; relocation of the instrument to a new location; after major repairs; and a change of intended use (for example, adding a new type of test method). Some work that looks like "routine maintenance" may also require change control if the replacement part has a different model, and a change cannot be bypassed on the grounds that it is minor.

Chapter Summary

  • Instrument classification (Groups A/B/C): classification is based on complexity and the criticality of the measurement, and determines the depth of qualification. The same instrument may fall into different groups depending on its use.
  • Four components of data quality: AIQ (foundation) → method validation → system suitability testing → QC samples. None can be omitted.
  • Four AIQ phases: URS → DQ → IQ → OQ → PQ. The sequence cannot be reversed, and each phase has clear documentation requirements.
  • OQ vs. PQ: OQ is the functional confirmation of what the instrument "can do" (mainly one-time); PQ is the performance confirmation that it "continues to perform well" (ongoing).
  • Responsibility: the user has ultimate responsibility for AIQ results; the quality unit ensures compliance; the supplier provides support but does not replace the user's responsibility.
  • Software validation: firmware is qualified together with the hardware; the validation depth for control/acquisition/processing software depends on the degree of customization.
  • Change control: any change to a qualified instrument must be assessed to decide whether requalification is needed and what its scope should be.
