Section 1 — Purpose, Key Terms & Background

Original Text

PURPOSE

This general chapter presents a framework for the design, justification, and implementation of assessments for drug product leachables derived from pharmaceutical packaging and delivery systems. A scientifically sound leachables assessment is important to manufacturers and their various suppliers primarily as a means of establishing the suitability for use of pharmaceutical packaging/delivery systems, as leachables can potentially affect drug product efficacy, safety, and quality.

Designing an individual leachables assessment is a process that strikes a balance between sound science, prudent resource allocation, and effective risk management with an emphasis on patient safety and product quality.

KEY TERMS (Selected)

Leachables: Foreign organic and inorganic chemical entities that are present in a packaged drug product because they have leached into the packaged drug product from a packaging/delivery system, packaging component, or packaging material of construction under normal conditions of storage and use or during accelerated drug product stability studies. Leachables are typically a subset of extractables or are derived from extractables.

Threshold of Toxicological Concern (TTC): A level of exposure for all chemicals, whether or not there is specific toxicity data, below which there would be no appreciable risk to human health. The TTC approach is a form of risk characterization in which uncertainties arising from the use of data on other compounds are balanced against the low level of exposure.

Safety Concern Threshold (SCT): The threshold below which a leachable would have a dose so low as to present negligible safety concerns from carcinogenic and noncarcinogenic toxic effects. For OINDP: SCT = 0.15 µg/day TDI.

Qualification Threshold (QT): The threshold below which a given noncarcinogenic leachable is not considered for safety qualification (toxicological assessments) unless the leachable presents structure-activity-relationship (SAR) concerns. For OINDP: QT = 5 µg/day TDI.

Analytical Evaluation Threshold (AET): The threshold at or above which a leachable should be characterized and reported for toxicological assessment. The AET can be mathematically derived from the SCT based on dosing parameters of the drug product.

Leachables Studies: Laboratory investigations into the qualitative and quantitative nature of a particular leachables profile(s) over the proposed shelf-life of a particular drug product.

BACKGROUND

Management of leachables is important to pharmaceutical and biotechnology/biologic product manufacturers and regulatory authorities because certain leachables above specific concentrations can present safety concerns for patients and/or compatibility issues for drug product formulations. During the 1980s, the U.S. FDA began to formally address leachables in drug products after findings of patient sensitivity induced by leachables and other potential safety concerns. Since then, management of both extractables and leachables for packaging systems and final drug products has become an important part of pharmaceutical development and regulatory submissions.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Positioning USP 1664 vs 1663

USP 1663 and 1664 are sister chapters that together form the complete framework for E&L assessment:

  • USP 1663: how to study the packaging material itself (under laboratory extraction conditions) → extractables assessment
  • USP 1664: how to study leachables in the actual drug product (under normal storage/use conditions) → leachables assessment

Key link: a leachables study under 1664 normally uses the results of the 1663 extractables assessment as prior knowledge (what substances might leach).

USP 1664 (Official as of 01-Dec-2024) is more recent than 1663 and reflects the industry's growing scientific understanding of leachables management.

Core Concept — The Three-Tier Safety Threshold System

USP 1664 defines three related but distinct safety thresholds (using OINDP as the example):

  • TTC (Threshold of Toxicological Concern): from the EMA, used to assess genotoxic impurities, 1.5 µg/day (risk factor 10⁻⁵). The TTC is the theoretical basis of the whole threshold system.
  • SCT (Safety Concern Threshold) = 0.15 µg/day: proposed by PQRI for OINDP, stricter than the TTC (risk factor 10⁻⁶). Below the SCT, no safety assessment is needed.
  • QT (Qualification Threshold) = 5 µg/day: a substance below the QT with no structural alert for carcinogenicity does not need a compound-specific safety assessment.

Note: the SCT and QT are "evaluation thresholds", not "control limits" or "safety limits". Exceeding them means an assessment is required, not that the product is unsafe.

Analogy — An Intuitive Reading of the Risk Matrix

The risk matrix in Table 1 classifies dosage forms along two dimensions:

  • Vertical axis: concern for the route of administration (inhalation > injection > topical): the more directly a substance enters the body, the higher the risk
  • Horizontal axis: likelihood of interaction between drug product and packaging: liquids extract substances from packaging more readily than solids

The highest-risk position is the upper left corner (inhalation aerosols): the drug goes directly into the lungs, and the formulation contains organic solvents and is used long term. The lowest risk is in the lower right corner (oral solids).

A CDMO should use this matrix to confirm the risk level of the client's drug product and then decide how rigorous the leachables assessment needs to be.

Key Point — "Low Risk" Does Not Mean Zero Risk

USP 1664 specifically points out that even low-risk dosage forms (oral tablets, topical powders) can still have leachables problems.

Real example: chemical additives in label adhesive can migrate through plastic primary packaging and appear in the impurity profile of an oral solid drug product, causing OOS results.

Conclusion: although low-risk dosage forms do not require a routine leachables study as part of the marketing application, a CDMO should still consider this possibility in its risk assessment, especially when investigating OOS results.

Section 2 — Concepts: Safety Thresholds & Information Sharing

Original Text

CONCEPTS — General Concepts for Leachables Assessment

Leachables studies can be used within the context of an overall leachables assessment to:

  • Facilitate the timely development of safe and effective dosage form packaging/delivery systems by assisting in the selection of components and materials of construction
  • Facilitate the establishment of qualitative and quantitative leachables–extractables correlations in drug products
  • Establish the worst-case drug product leachables profile in a manner that facilitates the development of drug product leachables specifications and acceptance criteria
  • Identify trends in drug product leachables accumulation levels over the shelf-life of a particular drug product
  • Facilitate change-control processes for drug product packaging/delivery systems
  • Facilitate investigations into the origin(s) of identified leachables whose presence causes out-of-specification (OOS) results for a marketed drug product

Safety Thresholds

Safety thresholds allow for a science- and risk-based determination of acceptable levels of leachables. Because safety thresholds are derived from exposure data, they are considered in terms of units of exposure, such as Total Daily Intake (TDI). Any safety threshold must be converted into units of concentration (e.g., µg/mL) so that it can be applied as an analytical threshold in the laboratory.

Examples of safety thresholds:

  • TTC (EMA): 1.5 µg/day TDI for genotoxic impurities, using an excess cancer risk factor of 10⁻⁵
  • PQRI SCT for OINDP: 0.15 µg/day TDI (risk factor 10⁻⁶). Below the SCT, identification and safety evaluation of leachables generally would not be required
  • PQRI QT for OINDP: 5 µg/day TDI. Below the QT, leachables without structure alerts for carcinogenicity or irritation would not require compound-specific safety risk assessment

Note that neither the SCT nor the QT is a control threshold or safety-driven limit. Rather, they are leachables evaluation thresholds.

Special case compounds for OINDP requiring lower thresholds based on specific analytical capabilities: polyaromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs/PNAs), N-nitrosamines, and 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (see USP 〈1664.1〉).

Information Sharing

To successfully manage leachables throughout the drug product lifecycle, it is critical to establish close and regular communication among stakeholders: chemists, toxicologists, packaging engineers, manufacturing operations, procurement, etc. With respect to leachables, communication between the analytical chemist and toxicologist is critical.

Information sharing between packaging component manufacturers/suppliers and drug product developers/manufacturers is also important in order to guide packaging component and materials of construction selection, provide knowledge of potential extractables and leachables, and facilitate leachables–extractables correlations via knowledge of packaging component chemical compositions.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Logic Behind the SCT

Why did PQRI choose 0.15 µg/day for OINDP? The logic behind the number:

  1. The EMA's TTC approach: 1.5 µg/day, using a cancer risk factor of 10⁻⁵ (an increase of 1 case per 100,000 people)
  2. PQRI considered that OINDP needs a stricter standard because: (a) the drug goes directly into sensitive organs such as the lungs, (b) it may be used for life, and (c) leachables are usually industrial chemicals unrelated to the active ingredient
  3. A risk factor of 10⁻⁶ was therefore adopted (an increase of 1 case per 1,000,000 people), giving SCT = 1.5 × (10⁻⁶/10⁻⁵) = 0.15 µg/day

The same logic applies to other high-risk dosage forms: the SCT for injectables may fall in a similar range, but it must be calculated from the exposure pattern of the specific dosage form.

Analogy — The Three Thresholds Are Like a Traffic Light

The traffic light analogy shows the decision made for a leachable at each of the three thresholds:

  • Below the SCT (0.15 µg/day) = green light: the substance is present but in a very small amount; no identification and no safety assessment needed. Proceed.
  • Between the SCT and QT (0.15–5 µg/day) = yellow light: chemical identification is needed (confirm what the substance is), but if there are no structural alerts (SAR concerns), no individual toxicological assessment is needed. Proceed with caution.
  • Above the QT (>5 µg/day) = red light: full chemical identification plus a compound-specific toxicological assessment are needed. Stop and assess.

Remember: these "lights" are assessment decisions, not judgments of "safe/unsafe". Even above the QT, a leachable remains acceptable if the toxicological assessment shows it is safe.

Key Point — Special Case Compounds (OINDP)

The following three classes of substances are "special case" compounds in OINDP; because of their particularly high toxicity, they require thresholds lower than the SCT:

  • PAHs/PNAs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons): carcinogenic and widely present in vulcanized rubber products. The threshold is set based on the detection capability of GC/MS.
  • N-nitrosamines: potent carcinogens that can form during rubber curing, and a class of substances related to the recent NDMA/NDEA problems.
  • 2-Mercaptobenzothiazole (2-MBT): a degradation product of rubber accelerators, and toxic.

When a CDMO evaluates packaging systems containing rubber components (rubber stoppers, silicone tubing), it must develop methods specifically for these three classes and set a lower AET.

  1. In a leachables study, a substance is found at a concentration corresponding to a patient exposure of 0.8 µg/day. Based on the OINDP SCT and QT thresholds, what assessment steps are needed?
  2. Why is information sharing (the supplier providing the material formulation) so important to the efficiency of leachables assessment? If a supplier refuses to provide formulation information, what alternatives are there?

Practical Application — E&L Elements in CDMO Supplier Audits

When auditing a packaging material supplier, the information-sharing requirements of USP 1664 can be turned into specific audit items:

  • Can the supplier provide the complete material formulation (including types and proportions of additives)?
  • Has the supplier performed extractables studies and provided the reports?
  • Could the supplier's manufacturing processes (curing, sterilization, molding) introduce special case compounds?
  • Is the Change Notification procedure robust? Any change that affects the leachables profile must be notified to the customer.

A supplier that cannot provide this information should be classified as a "high-risk supplier", and the CDMO must take on responsibility for a more complete extractables assessment itself.

Section 3 — Leachables Study Design

Original Text

LEACHABLES STUDY DESIGN

Although leachables studies may be accomplished at any time during the drug product development/manufacturing lifecycle, leachables studies are especially relevant during late stage product development or during formal product stability assessment. Ideally, leachables assessment is conducted as follows:

  • The assessment is performed on the actual drug product and not simulations thereof
  • The assessment is performed with the actual packaging and delivery system in the form it will be commercialized, not with a prototype or on system components
  • The related extractables assessments are accomplished on the same lots of packaging components used to manufacture the drug product lots on which the leachables assessments are performed
  • The assessment is performed on a product manufactured under conditions that reflect the actual commercial processes; leachables studies may include accelerated storage conditions, but they cannot be limited to accelerated conditions and must include real-time assessment

Development Stage Leachables Studies

Leachables studies can also be performed early in the drug product development process (e.g., preclinical stage) in order to facilitate the selection of packaging components. Such leachables studies are particularly useful for certain "high-risk" dosage forms where selecting appropriate packaging components and materials of construction is critical.

For high-risk dosage forms, leachables characterization is recommended for any drug product batches that are used as test articles in any definitive toxicology or clinical studies.

For low-risk dosage forms (e.g., solid orals, topical powders) leachables studies conducted throughout development might be appropriate in order to assess, and thereby avoid, problems with packaging systems that might appear either in later stage development or marketed product.

Design Considerations

The design of a particular leachables study requires:

  1. Information on identities and maximum possible accumulation levels of all potential leachables from the packaging component manufacturers
  2. Consideration of chemical diversity and physical/chemical properties of potential leachables (polarity, volatility, solubility)
  3. Assessment of nature of formulation contact: direct or indirect; transient or continuous
  4. A rigorous leachables assessment considers leachables from sources other than primary packaging, including necessary secondary packaging and, in certain situations, tertiary packaging
  5. Various characteristics of the drug product formulation must also be considered (physical state, changes in state during production or use)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Four Ideal Conditions for Study Design

The four ideal conditions listed in USP 1664 define what a "high-quality" leachables study is:

  1. The actual drug product (not a placebo): some leachables form only when the active ingredient is present (e.g., chemical reaction products)
  2. The final commercial packaging configuration: a prototype package may have a different leachables profile from the commercial package
  3. The same lot of packaging material: this ensures that the extractables study and the leachables study use the same source, which is a prerequisite for establishing an L-E correlation
  4. Must include real-time evaluation: accelerated stability (e.g., 40°C/75%RH) can help with early prediction but cannot replace long-term stability studies at 25°C/60%RH

In real CDMO operations, these four conditions are often difficult to satisfy simultaneously (cost and time constraints), so a science-based risk-benefit discussion with the client is needed.

Analogy — A Leachables Study Is Like Observing Married Life

Continuing the "chemical marriage" analogy from USP 1663:

Extractables study (USP 1663): the prenuptial agreement: under controlled laboratory conditions, you force an understanding of each side's "chemical personality".

Leachables study (USP 1664): real married life: you observe what chemical reactions actually occur after the two "chemical personalities" live together in reality for 2–3 years, and which substances really leach into the drug product.

However thorough the premarital investigation, it cannot guarantee there will be no surprises after the wedding. The extractables assessment provides a prediction; the leachables study provides confirmation.

Key Point — Why Packaging Lot Consistency Matters

The requirement for the "same lot of packaging material" is easy to overlook, but it is the foundation for establishing a valid L-E correlation:

Example: if the extractables study used Lot A rubber stoppers but the leachables study used Lot B rubber stoppers, and the Lot B formulation differs slightly, then:

  • The extractables results may be unable to explain a substance that appears in the leachables study
  • The L-E correlation cannot be established
  • Regulators may require the studies to be repeated

Practical recommendation: when filling production lots, retain some packaging material samples at the same time for later confirmatory extractables studies.

Practice Questions

  1. An injectable drug product under an NDA is in Phase III clinical trials. Describe what USP 1664 recommends for leachables studies at this stage and how the CDMO should help the client meet this requirement.
  2. Why can a leachables study under accelerated stability conditions (40°C/75%RH) not fully replace a real-time study (25°C/60%RH)? Explain from the standpoint of chemical kinetics.

Practical Application — Leachables Assessment in CDMO Change Control

USP 1664 specifically emphasizes that any change that causes patients to be exposed to a different leachables profile requires a leachables study as part of change control.

In day-to-day CDMO operations, the following changes should trigger a leachables assessment:

  • A change of rubber stopper or vial supplier (even if the specifications are the same)
  • An adjustment of pH or solvent ratio in the drug product formulation
  • A change in terminal sterilization conditions (e.g., from dry heat to moist heat, or an adjustment of the F0 value)
  • Replacement of filling line equipment (the drug-contact materials may differ)

Establish a "Leachables Impact Assessment" SOP as a standard step in the CDMO's change assessment procedure.

Section 4 — Leachables Characterization & AET Calculation

Original Text

LEACHABLES CHARACTERIZATION

The primary goal of any leachables study is leachables characterization; i.e., the discovery, identification, and quantitation of leachables present in a particular drug product. Analytical methods for leachables characterization are developed based on:

  • The nature of the drug product matrix
  • The identities and possible accumulation levels of potential leachables
  • The required sensitivity based on an adopted leachables evaluation threshold and the capabilities of the analytical methods employed

Unlike a typical drug product impurity method where target analytes are related to the drug substance, leachables have a wide chemical diversity and can come from various sources in the packaging/delivery system. This presents a significant challenge for trace analysis, especially in the case of organic leachables identification.

Analytical Thresholds

The starting action in leachables method development is to establish the level at which the method must perform — the analytical threshold. An example of a safety-based threshold is the SCT as established for OINDP (0.15 µg/day). In order to define the SCT in terms that facilitate laboratory analysis, it must be converted from units of exposure (µg/day) to units of concentration (e.g., µg/mL). The resulting analytically useful threshold is termed the Analytical Evaluation Threshold (AET).

General formula for converting SCT to AET:

AET (µg/container) = (0.15 µg/day ÷ doses/day) × (labeled doses/container)

For liquid dosage forms:

AET (µg/mL) = AET (µg/container) ÷ mL/container

For solid dosage forms:

AET (µg/g) = AET (µg/container) ÷ g/container

Analytical Method Requirements

Analytical method requirements are based on the determined AET and information on potential leachables obtained from extractables assessments. Since leachables are typically a subset of extractables, analytical methods for leachables characterization can often be based on those used for extractables characterization.

Any analytical method for leachables used for:

  • Drug product stability studies in support of product registration
  • Establishing leachables–extractables correlations for high-risk dosage forms
  • Development of leachables specifications and acceptance criteria

must be subject to complete validation using industry-accepted validation practices.

Preparing the Drug Product for Analysis — Sample Preparation

Sample preparation approaches by dosage form:

  • Aqueous dosage forms (inhalation solutions, parenterals, ophthalmics): liquid-liquid extraction with water-immiscible organic solvents (dichloromethane, hexane, petroleum ether); pH manipulation to enhance extraction of weakly acidic or basic leachables
  • Solid dosage forms (solid orals, inhalation powders, lyophilized powders): dissolve in aqueous solution, apply liquid-liquid extraction; or headspace GC for volatiles
  • Oral liquid dosage forms: dilute in aqueous solution, apply liquid-liquid extraction
  • Cream and ointment dosage forms: dissolve in aqueous solution, filter, apply liquid-liquid extraction
  • Nonaqueous vehicles (MDI with organic propellants): see USP 〈1664.1〉

Analytical Techniques

The most useful analytical techniques for discovery, identification, and quantitation of organic leachables: GC/MS and HPLC (LC)/MS. Headspace sampling can also be interfaced with GC/MS to address volatile compounds.

Analytical methods for leachables should be capable of characterizing both target and new (or unspecified) leachables. Structural analysis of leachables should be accomplished with a systematic process identical to that described in USP 〈1663〉 for extractables.

Tutorial Commentary

Formula and Calculation — A Complete AET Calculation Example

Scenario: evaluating a rubber stopper for an inhalation solution (OINDP)
• Daily dose: 4 inhalations/day
• Labeled doses per container: 200 inhalations
• Volume per container: 25 mL
• SCT (OINDP): 0.15 µg/day

Step 1: Calculate the AET per container
AET (µg/container) = (0.15 µg/day ÷ 4 doses/day) × (200 doses/container)
             = 0.0375 × 200
             = 7.5 µg/container

Step 2: Convert to a concentration (liquid dosage form)
AET (µg/mL) = 7.5 µg/container ÷ 25 mL/container
            = 0.3 µg/mL

Conclusion: the analytical method must be able to detect and quantify
      any unknown leachable at a concentration ≥ 0.3 µg/mL

This AET value directly determines the minimum limit of quantitation (LOQ) required of the analytical method and is the starting point for method development.

Core Concept — Why Is Leachables Analysis Harder Than Drug Impurity Analysis?

The logic of conventional drug impurity analysis (ICH Q3B): impurities arise from drug synthesis, their chemical structures are related to the active ingredient, and they can be predicted and reference standards prepared.

The difficulties of leachables analysis:

  • Extremely high chemical diversity: from volatile organics to metallic elements, covering almost every chemical class
  • Drug product matrix interference: the concentrations of active ingredients and excipients are far higher than those of the leachables, causing analytical interference
  • Not all targets can be determined in advance: non-targeted (screening) analytical methods are needed
  • Reference standards may be unavailable: some structurally complex extractables may have no commercial reference standard

These challenges explain why a "complete" leachables study is technically difficult and why the AET is a pragmatic compromise.

Analogy — Method Validation Is Like Calibrating a Scale

Why must a leachables analytical method be fully validated?

Imagine you are weighing gold (the leachables concentration). If your scale (the analytical method) has not been calibrated (validated), the number you report is meaningless: you do not know whether a reading of 0.3 µg/mL is truly 0.3 or a wrong number caused by an inaccurate method.

USP 1664 requires that methods used for regulatory submissions be fully validated, which means:

  • Accuracy: does your scale read correctly?
  • Precision: are repeated measurements consistent?
  • Specificity: is what you are weighing really gold and not some other metal?
  • Detection limit/quantitation limit (LOD/LOQ): how small an amount can you weigh? It must be ≤ the AET.

Key Point — The Design Logic of Sample Preparation

Design principle of sample preparation: create a sample form in which the analytical technique can "see" the leachables while removing as much of the drug product matrix interference as possible.

Practical tips:

  • Accelerated stability samples (40°C/75%RH, 3 months) can be used for method development because they contain higher concentrations of leachables, making it easier to establish the method and evaluate recovery
  • For drug products containing organic solvents (e.g., MDI), sample preparation must preserve the volatile components; otherwise volatile leachables will be underestimated
  • Use spike recovery to evaluate the sample preparation method and ensure the leachables are not lost during processing

Practice Questions

  1. Calculation: an injectable drug product (aqueous, pH 6.5) is injected at most 2 times per day; each vial has a volume of 10 mL and is labeled for 10 injections. Using SCT = 0.15 µg/day, calculate the AET (µg/mL) for this drug product.
  2. When validating a leachables quantitation method, what properties must the blank matrix you choose have? Why can ultrapure water not be used directly as the blank matrix?

Practical Application — Leachables Method Development Strategy for CDMOs

When undertaking a leachables study commissioned by a client, the CDMO laboratory should follow this strategy:

  1. Determine the AET: calculate it from the client's drug product dosage form, daily dose, and container volume
  2. Obtain extractables data: request existing extractables reports from the client or packaging material supplier as the basis for the target compound list
  3. Select the analytical platforms: GC/MS (volatiles) + LC/MS (non-volatiles) + ICP/MS (elements) is the standard combination
  4. Develop the sample preparation method: evaluate it with spike recovery, ensuring recovery is within 70%–130%
  5. Perform full validation: accuracy, precision, LOQ (≤ AET), specificity, robustness
  6. Perform method system suitability: confirm the system is working properly before each use of the analytical method
Section 5 — L-E Correlation, Specifications & Simulation Studies

Original Text

ESTABLISHING A LEACHABLES–EXTRACTABLES CORRELATION

A leachables–extractables correlation is established when actual drug product leachables can be linked either qualitatively or quantitatively with extractables from corresponding extractables assessments. Leachables–extractables correlations are important for:

  • Justifying the use of routine extractables release tests of packaging components as an alternative to leachables testing during stability studies for high-risk drug products
  • Establishing the source of a leachable producing an OOS result for a low-risk drug product
  • Change control
  • Ongoing quality control

Qualitative correlation: A leachable is linked either directly or indirectly to an extractable. Example: hexadecanoic acid in leachables profile directly linked to hexadecanoic acid in extractables profile; the ethyl ester of hexadecanoic acid can be indirectly linked to hexadecanoic acid in extractables profile (when ethanol is a formulation constituent and esterification is possible during storage).

Quantitative correlation: The quantity of any individual leachable over the shelf-life must be mathematically related to the quantity of the corresponding extractable in its source. The quantity of the leachable should be less than or equal to the quantity of the corresponding extractable.

Example of quantitative correlation:

  • BHT in drug product formulation: 5 µg/mL
  • BHT extracted from primary packaging component: 300 µg/component
  • Drug product volume: 50 mL per component
  • BHT extracted: 300 µg; BHT leached: 250 µg (5 µg/mL × 50 mL)
  • Conclusion: quantitative correlation established; 50 µg unaccounted for (likely not leached from component)

CONSIDERATIONS IN DEVELOPING LEACHABLES SPECIFICATIONS

One means of developing specifications: testing a minimum of three drug product batches to determine leachables levels. After thorough chemical and safety evaluation, acceptance criteria can be established consistent with:

  1. Qualitative and/or quantitative results of leachables studies
  2. Capabilities of the drug product's manufacturing process
  3. Potential safety, compatibility, and/or drug product quality impact of the leachables

Leachables specifications should be applicable to a product during all stages of its shelf-life, including release and at end of shelf-life. This is the case since leachables accumulate over the entire shelf-life of a drug product.

A typical leachables specification could include:

  • Quantitative end of shelf-life limits for target leachables (apply over the entire shelf-life)
  • A quantitative limit for "unspecified" (previously unidentified and uncorrelated) leachables

SIMULATION STUDIES

Occasions may arise in which it is not analytically feasible to successfully discover and identify all actual leachables in a drug product leachables study (e.g., due to challenging thresholds). In such cases, the activities of discovery and identification of probable leachables are accomplished in an extraction study using a simulating solvent.

Key design differences between a simulation study and a drug product leachables study:

  1. The drug product formulation has been replaced with a simulating solvent that mimics the formulation
  2. The conditions of contact have been accelerated to increase concentrations of probable leachables
  3. The test article can be either the complete packaging system or separate components

Key rule: For a correlation to be valid, each leachable concentration in the drug product must be less than or equal to the corresponding extractable concentration in the simulated extract (accounting for uncertainty and justifiable "exaggeration factors").

INORGANIC (ELEMENTAL) LEACHABLES

Testing of plastic packaging systems and their materials of construction will establish extractable elemental impurities relevant to a particular packaging system. The results should be used to establish those elemental impurities that should be monitored as targeted elemental leachables in the drug product. Atomic spectroscopy does not establish the compound form of the detected element — the form (e.g., sulfur as elemental sulfur vs. sulfate vs. 2-mercaptobenzothiazole) may have a marked effect on safety impact.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Practical Value of an L-E Correlation

The most important practical value of establishing an L-E correlation: using packaging material release testing (extractables testing) in place of costly drug product stability leachables testing.

The logic: if we can show that "whenever a packaging material lot's extractables are at a controlled level, the leachables in the drug product are necessarily at a safe level", then we do not need to run a full leachables stability study on every drug product lot; we only need to test extractables at packaging material release.

This has a major cost and time impact on the QC strategy for high-risk CDMO clients (e.g., inhalation products, injectables). Without an L-E correlation, every drug product lot needs stability follow-up of up to 2 years.

Formula and Calculation — Verification Logic for a Quantitative L-E Correlation

L-E correlation verification for BHT (USP 1664 example):

Known conditions:
• BHT concentration in the drug product (latest stability time point): 5 µg/mL
• Drug product volume per container: 50 mL
• BHT in the extractables study of the same packaging lot: 300 µg/component
• Components used per container: 1

Calculation:
• Total BHT as a leachable: 5 × 50 = 250 µg
• Total BHT as an extractable: 300 µg

Verification: 250 µg (leached) ≤ 300 µg (extracted) ✓
→ The quantitative correlation holds!

Meaning: more BHT was extracted from the packaging material than actually appeared in the drug product
(the 50 µg difference may be BHT that did not leach or that was lost through degradation)
→ Extractables are a conservative estimate of leachables ✓

Analogy — A Simulation Study Is Like Food Simulant Testing

The concept of a simulation study has long existed in food packaging regulations (e.g., 21 CFR 177):

Food regulations allow "food simulants" (e.g., water, acetic acid, ethanol, olive oil) to be used in place of real food for packaging compatibility testing, because testing with real food (such as a barrel of pasta sauce) directly is both expensive and complicated.

The logic of a USP 1664 simulation study is the same: a simulating solvent whose chemical characteristics are similar to the drug product formulation replaces the actual drug product in an extractables study, and the results are then used to predict leachables.

Key requirement: the "extraction capability" of the simulating solvent must be ≥ that of the actual drug product, ensuring that the simulation study is a conservative estimate (overestimate) rather than an underestimate.

Key Point — The Special Nature of Leachables Specifications

Leachables specifications differ from ordinary drug product specifications (e.g., assay, impurities) in one important way:

Leachables accumulate throughout the shelf life, so the specification must apply at all time points of the shelf life, including release and end of shelf-life.

This means:

  • Leachables concentrations at release are usually lower than at end of shelf-life, so the specification must be set based on the highest expected concentration at end of shelf-life
  • When setting acceptance criteria, the stability trend must be considered (does it accumulate linearly? does it level off at some time point?)

Practice Questions

  1. If, in the drug product stability study at 18 months, the concentration of a leachable exceeds the maximum possible accumulation estimated from the extractables study, what does this mean? What are the possible causes? How should it be handled?
  2. A drug product simulation study shows a concentration of 2 µg/mL for a substance in the simulated extract, while the AET is 0.5 µg/mL. Can the simulation study result be used directly to claim that "leachables meet the requirements"? Why or why not?

Practical Application — Building an L-E Correlation Database at a CDMO

For a CDMO that handles many similar drug products (e.g., injectable fill-finish work using packaging from the same supplier), building a systematic L-E correlation database has great long-term value:

  • Upfront investment: build a complete extractables database for standard packaging material combinations (e.g., a specific supplier's rubber stopper + glass vial)
  • Long-term return: when new clients use the same packaging materials, historical data can be reused, greatly shortening the time and cost of extractables assessment
  • Applying the L-E correlation: once an L-E correlation is established for a specific packaging material in a specific type of formulation, a packaging material extractables release specification can replace costly drug product stability leachables testing
  • Regulatory leverage: robust L-E correlation data can substantially increase reviewer confidence in the packaging section during NDA/ANDA review

Chapter Summary

  • Positioning of USP 1664: the sister chapter of 1663, moving from extractables (packaging material studies) to leachables (drug product studies); the second step of the E&L assessment framework.
  • Three-tier safety thresholds: TTC (1.5 µg/day) → SCT (0.15 µg/day, OINDP) → QT (5 µg/day, OINDP); all are evaluation thresholds, not control limits.
  • AET calculation: SCT ÷ doses/day × labeled doses/container, then divided by the container volume to give µg/mL or µg/g. The AET determines the minimum LOQ required of the analytical method.
  • Four principles of study design: actual drug product, commercial packaging, same lot of packaging material, and real-time evaluation included.
  • Value of an L-E correlation: once established, packaging material extractables release testing can replace drug product leachables stability testing, an important tool for CDMOs to reduce QC cost.
  • Simulation study: when direct leachables analysis is technically infeasible, an extractables study with a simulating solvent is used instead, provided the extraction capability of the simulating solvent is ≥ that of the actual drug product.
  • Specification setting: based on data from at least three lots, balancing science (L-E correlation), manufacturing capability, and safety; the specification must apply over the entire shelf life (including end of shelf-life).
  • Special case compounds (OINDP): PAHs, N-nitrosamines, and 2-MBT require lower thresholds, and CDMOs must pay particular attention to them when evaluating rubber components.
