Introduction

Original Text

Sterilization can be accomplished using sporicidal agents suspended in air (i.e., vapor). Sterilizing agents that operate in this fashion include hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), peracetic acid (CH3CO3H), formaldehyde (CH2O), and glutaraldehyde [CH2(CH2CHO)2] in aqueous solution. At room temperature these are liquids or solids that can be vaporized for introduction into a vessel or chamber.

They differ from sterilizing gases and liquids in that there are multiple phases within the vessel during sterilization. Vapor sterilization systems are well suited for heat-sensitive materials and surface sterilization. Items exposed to the process should have their surfaces exposed to the greatest extent possible.

Vapor sterilization processes require appropriate sterilant concentration, temperature, and relative humidity, all of which may be variable during the exposure period. Because the agent is ordinarily supplied as an aqueous solution, moisture is introduced with the agent. The consequences of variation in these parameters may be localized differences in relative humidity, agent concentration, and condensation rates on the surfaces to be treated, resulting in variations in process lethality.

The parameters to be established include sterilant amount (usually derived from injection quantities), relative humidity, and temperature. There is no demonstrated correlation between gas phase conditions, surface conditions, and microbial kill. For this reason, online monitoring of vapor phase concentration is not widely utilized as a control parameter.

Efforts to develop a standardized biological indicator for vapor systems have been hampered by the multiphasic nature of these sterilants. Selection of the appropriate biological indicator (BI) and resistance should be based on experimentation within the user’s system. Only under well-defined, specific conditions (e.g., agent concentration, humidity level, temperature, substrate, and phase) can a reliable D-value be established (for a definition of D-value, see Sterilization of Compendial Articles ⟨1229⟩).

"There is no demonstrated correlation between gas phase conditions, surface conditions, and microbial kill. For this reason, online monitoring of vapor phase concentration is not widely utilized as a control parameter."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

No standardized D-value: the most distinctive feature of vapor phase sterilization:

Steam sterilization (121°C) and dry heat sterilization (250°C) both have well-defined D-values because the process conditions are uniform and controllable. Vapor phase sterilization, however, is multiphase:

  • The microbial kill rates of the gas phase and the liquid phase differ (the liquid phase is usually more lethal than the gas phase)
  • Condensation, humidity, and concentration differ from one surface to another
  • There is no demonstrated correlation between online monitoring of gas-phase concentration and the actual kill on surfaces

Therefore, validation of vapor phase sterilization must use an empirical approach: the process effect is demonstrated through a comprehensive BI challenge rather than by theoretical calculation.

Analogy

Spray cleaner vs. steam cleaning:

Imagine using a spray bottle to disinfect a room. Some of the droplets float in the air (gas phase), some land on the desk and condense into beads of water (liquid phase), and some corners may never get sprayed (dead spots). You cannot judge whether every surface has been adequately disinfected just by measuring the disinfectant concentration in the air; you have to go to every corner and place a test strip to confirm. This is why vapor phase sterilization needs extensive BI distribution testing rather than reliance on online gas concentration monitoring.

Key Concepts

Vapor phase sterilization: uses the vapor state of a sporicidal agent to sterilize surfaces inside a container or chamber. Key features:

  • Multiphase system — during sterilization, both gas phase and liquid phase (condensed droplets) are present in the chamber. This is the most fundamental difference from gas sterilization (e.g., EtO)
  • Surface sterilization — vapor phase sterilization acts mainly on the surfaces of items and is not suited to penetrating sterilization. All surfaces must be fully exposed
  • Suitable for heat-sensitive materials — it operates at or near room temperature, and is the preferred method for bio-decontamination inside isolators and RABS

The four main vapor phase sterilants:

Hydrogen Peroxide

Original Text

The efficacy of hydrogen peroxide as a liquid sterilant has been long established.1 There are several effective approaches to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) injection, including continuous, intermittent, or all at once.

Some of the systems utilize an evacuation or drying step prior to introduction of the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to allow for increased concentration without excess condensation. Alternatively, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) can be introduced as a liquid, followed by target heating.

Following the exposure period, the chamber or target is aerated to an acceptable level for further processing of materials and/or personnel exposure (whichever is lowest) prior to opening and removing the sterilized article.

Tutorial Commentary

CDMO Practical Insights

H2O2 injection strategies:

  • Continuous — H2O2 is supplied continuously to maintain a stable gas-phase concentration. Advantage: good uniformity. Disadvantage: possible over-condensation
  • Intermittent / Pulsed — injected in multiple pulses with drying intervals in between. Many modern isolator systems use this approach
  • All at once / Flash — all of the H2O2 is injected in a single shot. Commonly seen in smaller systems

Aeration optimization is the key to CDMO efficiency: in an isolator decontamination cycle, aeration is often the most time-consuming phase. A typical decontamination cycle may take 2-4 hours, of which aeration can account for 1.5-3 hours. By optimizing inlet airflow, HEPA filter configuration, catalytic decomposition, and similar measures, aeration time can be shortened significantly, directly improving line utilization.

Analogy

Preheating and cooling an oven:

A VPHP cycle is like using an oven: you first preheat it (conditioning), then hold the baking temperature (decontamination), and after baking you must wait for the oven to cool before you can safely open the door and take the food out (aeration). Just as you would not open the oven door while it is still at 200°C, you cannot open the isolator while the H2O2 residue is still high. Moreover, cooling down (aeration) usually takes longer than the baking itself, which is exactly the pain point CDMOs want to optimize.

Peracetic Acid

Original Text

Peracetic acid (CH3CO3H), alone or mixed with hydrogen peroxide, is a sporicidal agent that has been proven effective.1 Peracetic acid is introduced as a liquid through an atomizer, resulting in the presence of liquid and vapor phases within the chamber. After the process dwell period, it is removed by evaporation.

Tutorial Commentary

PAA vs. VPHP

Characteristics of peracetic acid (PAA):

  • Method of introduction — introduced as a liquid through an atomizer, rather than vaporized in an evaporator as H2O2 is. As a result, the liquid-phase component is larger
  • Can be used alone or mixed with H2O2 — mixed formulations can provide a synergistic microbicidal effect
  • Method of removal — removed by evaporation; no dedicated aeration step like that for H2O2 is needed, but the residue (acetic acid) may require additional consideration

CDMO Context

Considerations when choosing between VPHP and PAA:

In a CDMO aseptic filling environment, VPHP is almost the only choice for isolator decontamination. PAA is more often used for large-area decontamination in non-isolator environments.

Validation of Vapor Sterilization

Original Text

Standard sterilizing conditions have not been defined due to the varying phase and multiphase nature of the sterilant during sterilization processes. Therefore, no standardized BIs with D-values that may be used for conventional predictive analysis of kill rates exist.

In the absence of BI D-values, and due to the variation in vapor sterilization cycle parameters, an empirical approach must be used. The kill rates in the gas and liquid phases that constitute the vapor may be substantially different (liquid kill rates are considered greater than gaseous kill rates). Destruction of BIs distributed across the system or load demonstrates lethality regardless of which phase effects the kill.

Sterilization process parameters (usually time) that do not kill the BIs may be adjusted until a complete kill is achieved. This establishes the minimum conditions necessary for a complete kill. Vapor sterilization may be validated using a half-cycle, bracketing, or other suitable approach as defined in ⟨1229⟩ and Gaseous Sterilization ⟨1229.7⟩.

The half-cycle validation method requires the destruction of a suitable concentration of a resistant microorganism under defined, minimum conditions for a complete kill. Then, in routine operation, the minimum lethal time period is arbitrarily doubled, which supports a doubling of the spore log reduction of the BI, and is more than sufficient to inactivate the bioburden.

A bracketing approach, which better supports process operating ranges for the critical parameters than does the half-cycle method, also can be employed. In the bracketing method, one evaluates conditions that bracket the defined process condition in order to establish parameters for the minimum and maximum effects on the materials and bioburden. The minimum lethal process dwell time establishes the worst case for microbial kill. Incremental increases in process dwell time beyond the minimum lethal time period are used to establish the maximum exposure periods, which impart the greatest effect on materials. In addition, adjustments to the quantity of agent introduced, operating temperature, and relative humidity are utilized to further enhance the bracketing approach. By this method, the routine process conditions may be established between the minimum and maximum process conditions to ensure complete microbial kill while maintaining the integrity of the materials.

"Destruction of BIs distributed across the system or load demonstrates lethality regardless of which phase effects the kill."

Tutorial Commentary

Empirical Approach

The core logic of the empirical approach:

  1. Start: distribute BIs (containing a known number of resistant spores) throughout the system
  2. Find the minimum lethal time: start with a short cycle and gradually increase the exposure time until all BIs are killed. This is the minimum lethal time
  3. Build a safety margin: the half-cycle approach simply doubles this time; the bracketing approach additionally tests longer times to assess the upper limit of material effects
  4. Routine use: daily operation is carried out with parameters within the safety margin

This approach is necessary because, unlike steam sterilization, you cannot use theoretical calculations based on D-values and F0 values. You must "prove" that your cycle is effective through actual experiments.

Analogy

Heating food in a microwave:

The half-cycle approach is like reheating a lunch box in a microwave: you try 2 minutes and find the center still cold, 3 minutes is still not enough, and at 4 minutes it is finally hot all the way through (minimum lethal time = 4 minutes). To be safe, you always set 8 minutes (2 times). With the bracketing approach, you would additionally try 10 and 12 minutes to see whether the lunch box overheats and dries out (the upper limit of material damage), and finally decide on a daily setting of 8-10 minutes.

Validation Activities

Original Text

The following activities are defined for a batch process, and therefore appropriate adaptation is necessary when they are applied to intermittent or continuous sterilization processes.

Equipment qualification — The equipment qualification for vapor sterilization mimics that of other sterilization processes in order to confirm that the equipment has been properly installed and operates as intended.

Empty chamber parameter distribution — Although multipoint measurement is possible, it lacks correlation to surface microbial kill. Humidity and temperature measurements, along with chemical indicators, can provide a limited indication of sterilant distribution. BIs are not required in the evaluation of the empty chamber.

Component mapping — For surface sterilization, internal mapping of load items is not required. Although vapors are primarily used as surface sterilants, when they are used for packaged articles with internal surfaces and volumes that need to be sterilized, internal mapping should be performed, with BIs placed in difficult-to-penetrate locations to confirm process lethality.

Load mapping — Humidity and temperature measurements, along with chemical indicators, can provide a limited indication of sterilant distribution on component surfaces. BIs are not required. Effects of load size and patterns should be assessed.

Biological indicators — The use of multiple BIs at each test location is recommended to more adequately support the process lethality.

Process confirmation and microbiological challenge — The core of the validation activity is the confirmation of acceptable process parameters and inactivation of the microbial challenge. Proof of cycle efficacy is provided in replicate studies in which the BIs are killed and chemical or physical measurements are utilized.

Tutorial Commentary

CDMO Isolator Validation

Practical points for isolator VPHP decontamination validation:

  • BI selection: Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores are commonly used, carried on stainless steel or glass carriers. The population is usually 106 CFU/carrier (note: the D-value cannot be standardized and should be verified in your own system)
  • BI placement: cover at least the hardest-to-reach (worst-case) locations, such as near door seals, corners, pipe connections, and glove ports
  • Multiple-BI principle: USP recommends placing multiple BIs at each location. This increases statistical confidence and prevents a false positive or false negative from a single BI from affecting the conclusion
  • Repeatability: process qualification requires replicate studies, usually at least three consecutive successful runs with all BIs killed

Material Compatibility

Material compatibility testing: not explicitly stated in USP, but a CDMO must consider it:

  • Glove material: Hypalon® (CSM) is the most common isolator glove material and tolerates H2O2 well, but it still needs regular replacement (usually every 200-500 cycles, or as specified in the SOP)
  • Gaskets: EPDM rubber is generally compatible; natural rubber and some silicones may age faster
  • Stainless steel 316L: compatible with H2O2, but long-term exposure to PAA may cause corrosion
  • Plastic components: PC (polycarbonate) viewing windows may become hazy or crack, so the number of cycles should be tracked

Routine Process Control

Original Text

Vapor sterilization is subject to controls that maintain a validated state over time. The practices outlined in ⟨1229⟩ describe the general requirements appropriate for all sterilization systems. The essential practices required to maintain validated status include calibration, physical measurements, use of BIs, physical or chemical integrators and indicators, ongoing process control, change control, preventive maintenance, periodic reassessment, and training.

"The essential practices required to maintain validated status include calibration, physical measurements, use of BIs, physical or chemical integrators and indicators, ongoing process control, change control, preventive maintenance, periodic reassessment, and training."

Tutorial Commentary

Ongoing Control

Common CDMO audit questions:

  • BI use frequency — the FDA and EU Annex 1 have different expectations for routine BI use in isolator decontamination. The conservative practice is to use BIs for every production batch; some companies reduce the frequency based on a risk assessment, but this requires scientific justification and supporting data
  • Trend analysis — monitor long-term trends in cycle parameters (injection amount, aeration time, residual H2O2 level) to detect equipment performance degradation early
  • Change control triggers revalidation — replacing gloves, replacing HEPA filters, or changing the H2O2 supplier or concentration must all be assessed for whether they trigger revalidation

Chapter Summary

  • Vapor phase sterilization uses sporicidal agent vapor to sterilize surfaces and is the standard method for isolator and RABS decontamination
  • The multiphase nature is the central challenge of vapor phase sterilization: gas and liquid phases coexist, so a standardized D-value cannot be established and an empirical approach must be used for validation
  • H2O2 (VPHP) is the preferred sterilant for pharmaceutical isolator decontamination; peracetic acid (PAA) is better suited to large-area or room-level decontamination
  • The half-cycle approach is the most widely used validation strategy: find the minimum time to kill all BIs, then double it. It is simple and effective
  • The bracketing approach validates a wider operating range while also assessing the upper limit of material compatibility
  • Validation activities include equipment qualification, cavity distribution, load mapping, BI challenge, and process qualification; multiple BIs per location are recommended
  • Routine maintenance must cover the nine elements (calibration, BI, CI, change control, preventive maintenance, etc.) to ensure the validated state is maintained
  • CDMO efficiency focus: optimizing aeration time is the biggest opportunity to improve isolator line utilization
