0.0 Chapter Scope & Precautions Against Contamination

Original Text

Portions of this general chapter have been harmonized with the corresponding texts of the European Pharmacopeia and/or the Japanese Pharmacopeia. Those portions that are not harmonized are marked with symbols to specify this fact.

These Pharmacopeial procedures are not by themselves designed to ensure that a batch of product is sterile or has been sterilized. This is accomplished primarily by validation of the sterilization process or of the aseptic processing procedures.

The test is applied to substances, preparations, or articles which, according to the Pharmacopeia, are required to be sterile. However, a satisfactory result only indicates that no contaminating microorganism has been found in the sample examined under the conditions of the test.

"These Pharmacopeial procedures are not by themselves designed to ensure that a batch of product is sterile or has been sterilized."

Precautions Against Microbial Contamination

The test for sterility is carried out under aseptic conditions. In order to achieve such conditions, the test environment has to be adapted to the way in which the sterility test is performed. The precautions taken to avoid contamination are such that they do not affect any microorganisms that are to be revealed in the test. The working conditions in which the tests are performed are monitored regularly by appropriate sampling of the working area and by carrying out appropriate controls.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Fundamental Limitation of the Sterility Test

This is the most important sentence at the opening of USP ⟨71⟩: the sterility test itself cannot guarantee that a batch is sterile. This is not a design flaw in the test; it is an inherent statistical limitation.

Take a typical injectable batch of 10,000 vials. Even if 20 vials are tested and all pass, statistically the test can only detect a contamination rate of roughly 14% or higher. For a sterility assurance level (SAL) of 10-6, the sterility test can provide almost no meaningful statistical confidence.

The real sources of sterility assurance are:

  • Sterilization process validation
  • Aseptic process validation (Aseptic Process Simulation / Media Fill)
  • Environmental monitoring
  • Personnel training and qualification

Analogy

Access control and security system: the sterility test is like a building's badge-swipe log. It can tell you whether any suspicious person was detected entering today, but the log itself cannot guarantee that the building is safe. Real safety comes from the design of the security system (sterilization process validation), the training of the security staff (personnel qualification), and full camera coverage (environmental monitoring).

The access log is only the last layer of confirmation. If the security system itself has gaps, checking the log alone will not catch the problem.

Key Point: Harmonization of the Three Major Pharmacopoeias

USP ⟨71⟩ is harmonized with EP 2.6.1 and JP 4.06. This means the sterility test requirements of the three major pharmacopoeias are largely aligned, although some content remains non-harmonized (marked with special symbols).

CDMO practical significance: when you run sterility tests for clients in different markets, following the harmonized requirements generally allows "one test, accepted in multiple markets". However, you need to watch for non-harmonized differences, for example the minimum sample quantities for certain dosage forms.

Contamination Precautions in CDMO Practice

The sterility test must be performed under aseptic conditions, yet those conditions must not affect the detection of any microorganisms that may be present. This is a delicate balance:

  • Environmental requirements: typically performed in an ISO 5 / Grade A isolator or laminar air flow (LAF) workstation
  • Monitoring requirements: regular environmental sampling (settle plates, air sampling, surface microbial monitoring) and negative controls
  • False positive risk: if the test environment itself is contaminated, false positives result, leading to unnecessary batch rejection, which means enormous cost losses for a CDMO
0.1 Culture Media & Incubation Temperatures

Original Text

Media for the test may be prepared as described below or equivalent commercial media may be used provided that they comply with the requirements of the Growth Promotion Test of Aerobes, Anaerobes, and Fungi.

Fluid Thioglycollate Medium (FTM)

pH after sterilization: 7.1 ± 0.2

Incubation temperature: 30°–35°

If more than the upper one-third of the medium has acquired a pink color, the medium may be restored once by heating the containers in a water-bath or in free-flowing steam until the pink color disappears and by cooling quickly, taking care to prevent the introduction of nonsterile air into the container.

For products containing a mercurial preservative that cannot be tested by membrane filtration, FTM incubated at 20°–25° may be used instead of SCDM provided it has been validated.

Soybean–Casein Digest Medium (SCDM / TSB)

pH after sterilization: 7.3 ± 0.2

Incubation temperature: 22.5 ± 2.5° (i.e., 20°–25°)

Media for Penicillins or Cephalosporins

For the Direct Inoculation method, transfer aseptically a quantity of β-lactamase sufficient to inactivate the amount of antibiotic in the specimen under test. Confirm that an appropriate amount of β-lactamase is incorporated using less than 100 cfu of Staphylococcus aureus as the challenge.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Two "Traps" Catching Different Prey

Imagine you want to confirm whether any animals have invaded a forest. FTM is like a trap set beside a burrow (detecting anaerobes underground), while SCDM is like a camera mounted in a tree (detecting aerobes and fungi on the ground and in the trees).

With only one type of trap, you could miss an entire class of intruders. This is why USP requires that both media must be used.

Resazurin Indicator: the Quality Alarm System of FTM

The resazurin in FTM is a redox indicator:

  • Colorless / pale = reduced state = anaerobic environment well maintained
  • Pink = oxidized state = oxygen has penetrated

Rule: if no more than half of the upper portion is pink, the medium can be used. If more than the upper 1/3 is pink, it may be restored by heating once. The "restore only once" limit is important, because repeated heating degrades the quality of the medium.

CDMO Practice: Why β-Lactamase Matters

If your CDMO takes on filling of penicillin or cephalosporin antibiotics, you must add β-lactamase to the medium to neutralize residual antibiotic activity. Otherwise, even if the product really is contaminated, the antibiotic will kill the microorganisms in the test, causing a false negative. This is more dangerous than a false positive, because you would wrongly judge the product as acceptable and release it.

Questions to Consider

  1. Why is the incubation temperature of FTM (30°–35°) higher than that of SCDM (20°–25°)? How does this relate to the types of microorganisms each medium is meant to detect?
  2. If your CDMO QC laboratory receives a batch of FTM and you find that more than half of it has turned pink, how would you handle it?

The Complementary Roles of the Two Media

The two media specified in USP ⟨71⟩ each have their own specialty:

Used together, they cover aerobic bacteria, anaerobic bacteria and fungi, the three major groups of microorganisms most likely to contaminate sterile products.

0.2 Test Organisms & Growth Promotion Test

Original Text

Table 1. Strains of the Test Microorganisms

Alternative: Kocuria rhizophila (Micrococcus luteus) ATCC 9341 may substitute for P. aeruginosa. Bacteroides vulgatus ATCC 8482 may substitute for C. sporogenes when a non-spore-forming organism is desired.

Growth Promotion Test

Test each lot of ready-prepared medium and each batch of medium prepared from dehydrated medium or from ingredients.

  • Inoculate FTM with not more than 100 cfu each of: C. sporogenes, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus (separate portions)
  • Inoculate SCDM with not more than 100 cfu each of: A. brasiliensis, B. subtilis, and C. albicans (separate portions)
  • Incubate: not more than 3 days for bacteria, not more than 5 days for fungi
  • Seed lot culture maintenance: viable microorganisms used are not more than 5 passages removed from the original master seed-lot

Acceptance criteria: The media are suitable if a clearly visible growth of the microorganisms occurs.

Sterility of Media

Incubate portions of the media for 14 days. No growth of microorganisms occurs.

Tutorial Commentary

Six Challenge Strains: Why These Six?

The six microorganisms selected by USP cover the most common sources of contamination in sterile products:

  • S. aureus: a common human skin organism, representing the risk of contamination from personnel
  • B. subtilis: an environmental spore-former, representing spore resistance
  • P. aeruginosa: a common water system organism, representing equipment/water contamination
  • C. sporogenes: an anaerobic spore-former, representing strictly anaerobic contamination
  • C. albicans: a yeast, representing fungal (yeast) contamination
  • A. brasiliensis: a mold, representing filamentous fungal contamination

They are not just "randomly chosen microorganisms": each one represents a real contamination threat scenario in the pharmaceutical environment.

Analogy: The Medical Checkup Report for Culture Media

The Growth Promotion Test is the "fitness test" of the culture medium. Just as a new recruit must pass a physical fitness test to prove capability before enlisting, every lot of medium must pass the growth promotion test before being used in a formal sterility test, to prove that it is capable of supporting microbial growth.

If the medium itself has a problem (pH drift, insufficient nutrients, accidental antibiotic contamination), microorganisms will not grow and you will get a false negative: you think the product passed, when in fact the medium has failed.

The Quality Logic of "Not More Than 100 cfu" and "Not More Than 5 Passages"

Inoculum of ≤ 100 cfu: a low inoculum is used to simulate the ability to detect a "low contamination level". If too many organisms were used (for example 106), even a poor-quality medium would show growth, defeating the purpose of the screening.

Limit of ≤ 5 passages: after multiple passages, microorganisms may undergo genetic variation and lose their original characteristics (for example slower growth or altered morphology). Limiting to 5 passages ensures the strains used in the test remain representative.

CDMO Practice: Media QC Timeline

Under the batch release time pressure of a CDMO, the pace of media quality control is critical:

  • Media sterility confirmation: requires 14 days of incubation, so it must be prepared in advance
  • Growth promotion test: bacteria ≤ 3 days, fungi ≤ 5 days
  • Can be run in parallel with the product sterility test (parallel testing), but if the GPT fails, the entire sterility test result is invalid

Time management tip: start the GPT and sterility confirmation immediately after the medium is received, rather than waiting until the product test.

Questions to Consider

  1. In a Growth Promotion Test, if C. albicans shows no obvious growth within 5 days but the other five strains all pass, can this lot of medium be used? What action would you take?
  2. Why does USP allow the use of "equivalent commercial media" instead of requiring in-house preparation? What practical benefits does this give a CDMO?
0.3 Diluting & Rinsing Fluids

Original Text

Fluid A

Dissolve 1 g of peptic digest of animal tissue in water to make 1 L, filter or centrifuge to clarify, if necessary, and adjust to a pH of 7.1 ± 0.2. Dispense into containers, and sterilize using a validated process.

For penicillins or cephalosporins: Aseptically add a quantity of sterile β-lactamase sufficient to inactivate any residual antibiotic activity on the membranes.

Fluid D

To each L of Fluid A add 1 mL of polysorbate 80, adjust to a pH of 7.1 ± 0.2, dispense into containers, and sterilize. Use this fluid for articles containing lecithin or oil, or for devices labeled as “sterile pathway.”

Fluid K

Dissolve 5.0 g of peptic digest of animal tissue, 3.0 g of beef extract, and 10.0 g of polysorbate 80 in water to make 1 L. Adjust the pH to obtain, after sterilization, a pH of 6.9 ± 0.2. Dispense into containers, and sterilize.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: The Logic of Washing Dishes

It is just like washing dishes: a water glass needs only clean water (Fluid A), a greasy pan needs a little dish soap (Fluid D), and a heavily greasy pan needs a lot of dish soap (Fluid K). The goal is always the same: to rinse away completely whatever is "hiding in the grease" (the microorganisms).

CDMO Product Selection Guide

Based on common CDMO product types:

  • Aqueous injectables (such as protein drug solutions) → Fluid A
  • Liposomal formulations, emulsions → Fluid D or Fluid K
  • Ophthalmic preparations containing preservatives → Fluid A (an additional neutralizing agent may be needed)
  • Prefilled syringes → select according to the contents; the needle portion requires additional handling

Selection Logic for the Three Rinsing Fluids

Core principle: the choice of rinsing fluid depends on the physicochemical properties of the product. The oilier the product, the higher the concentration of surfactant (Polysorbate 80) needed to ensure microorganisms are effectively rinsed from the filter membrane into the culture medium.

0.4 Method Suitability Test

Original Text

Carry out a test as described below under Test for Sterility of the Product to be Examined using exactly the same methods, except for the following modifications.

Membrane Filtration

After transferring the content of the container(s) to the membrane, add an inoculum of a small number of viable microorganisms (not more than 100 cfu) to the final portion of sterile diluent used to rinse the filter.

Direct Inoculation

After transferring the contents of the container(s) to the culture medium, add an inoculum of a small number of viable microorganisms (not more than 100 cfu) to the medium.

Use the same microorganisms as those described under Growth Promotion Test. Perform a growth promotion test as a positive control. Incubate all containers containing medium for not more than 5 days.

If clearly visible growth is obtained, visually comparable to the control vessel without product, the product possesses no antimicrobial activity under the conditions of the test, or such activity has been satisfactorily eliminated. The test for sterility may then be carried out without further modification.

If clearly visible growth is not obtained in the presence of the product, the product possesses antimicrobial activity that has not been satisfactorily eliminated. Modify the conditions and repeat the Method Suitability Test.

This method suitability is performed (a) when the test for sterility has to be carried out on a new product; and (b) whenever there is a change in the experimental conditions of the test. The method suitability may be performed simultaneously with the Test for Sterility.

Tutorial Commentary

Method Suitability Test = "Will the product kill the bacteria in the test?"

The Method Suitability Test (MST) answers one core question: will your product itself inhibit microbial growth and cause a false negative?

Many drug products have inherent antimicrobial activity (containing preservatives or antibiotics, extreme pH, high osmolarity, etc.). If you do not first confirm that the product's antimicrobial activity can be eliminated, your sterility test results are unreliable.

Decision logic:

  • Microbial growth in the presence of the product is comparable to the control → pass → the sterility test can proceed directly
  • No microbial growth in the presence of the product → fail → the conditions must be modified (more rinses, larger dilution volume, addition of a neutralizing agent) and the test repeated

Analogy: Pre-testing a Pregnancy Test Strip

The Method Suitability Test is like testing a pregnancy test strip with a known positive sample before using it, to confirm that the strip itself works. If it cannot detect even the known positive sample, its "negative" results are meaningless.

In a sterility test, if the product itself kills bacteria, then even if the product really is contaminated, nothing will grow in the medium. You think it passed, but the test was actually interfered with.

MST Timing: Not Performed for Every Batch

USP explicitly states that the MST needs to be performed only in the following situations:

  • (a) New product: when the sterility test method is established for the first time
  • (b) Change in conditions: when the test conditions (such as rinse volume, neutralizing agent, medium brand, etc.) change

This matters for a CDMO: every new product from a new client requires an MST. But routine batch release of the same product does not require repeating it each time.

Common Strategies for Eliminating Antimicrobial Activity

When the MST fails, the following modifications can be tried:

  • Increase the number of rinses (membrane filtration): up to 5 × 100 mL
  • Increase the dilution volume (direct inoculation): so that the product makes up ≤ 10% of the medium volume
  • Add a neutralizing agent, such as β-lactamase, lecithin, or polysorbate
  • Use concentrated medium to compensate for the nutrient reduction caused by dilution

Note, however: even if the MST shows that rinsing 5 × 100 mL still cannot completely eliminate the antimicrobial activity, USP specifies that this rinse volume must not be exceeded.

  1. Your CDMO receives a preservative-containing (benzalkonium chloride) ophthalmic preparation from a new client. What preparatory work do you need to do before starting routine sterility testing?
  2. The MST can be run in parallel with the formal sterility test. What is the risk of this? If the MST result shows failure on day 5, but the sterility test has already been incubated for 5 days, how would you handle it?

Section Summary

  • Fundamental statement of USP ⟨71⟩: the sterility test cannot guarantee that a batch is sterile; sterility assurance comes from process validation (sterilization validation or aseptic process simulation)
  • Two media: FTM (30°–35°, mainly for anaerobes) + SCDM (20°–25°, mainly for fungi and aerobes); both must be used together
  • Six challenge strains: covering aerobic bacteria (3 strains), anaerobic bacteria (1 strain) and fungi (2 strains), each representing a different contamination scenario
  • Growth Promotion Test: must be passed for every lot of medium before use; inoculate ≤ 100 cfu, with clear growth required within 3 days for bacteria / 5 days for fungi
  • Method Suitability Test: for new products or changes in conditions; confirms the product does not interfere with microbial detection, and eliminating antimicrobial activity is the key
  • Three rinsing fluids: Fluid A (general), Fluid D (containing fats/lecithin), Fluid K (for rinsing oily products); selection depends on product characteristics

Tutorial Commentary

The logic of Table 2: how much do you test?

Table 2 is designed to balance statistical meaning against practical feasibility:

  • Small volume (<1 mL): use the entire contents, because the volume is so small that testing only part of it makes no sense
  • Medium volume (1–40 mL): use half the contents, which balances representativeness against the need to keep retention samples
  • Large volume (>40 mL): use a fixed 20 mL or 10%, because more than that does not significantly increase the detection rate

Remember that every medium needs this quantity. If the contents of one container are not enough for both media, you must double the number of containers sampled.

Analogy: the statistics of a lottery drum

Imagine a drum holding 10,000 balls, some of which may be bad. You do not need to check every ball, but you cannot draw a conclusion from just one either.

The USP sample sizes work like a carefully calculated "minimum spot-check plan": a small drum (small batch) needs a larger proportion checked, because even a single bad ball is a high percentage of the total. For a large drum (large batch), 20 balls are enough, because if the problem is systemic, 20 are sufficient to detect it.

Practical considerations for CDMO batch release

For a CDMO, sterility test sampling directly affects the number of saleable units:

  • 1,000-vial batch: 20 vials (2%) × 2 media (FTM + SCDM) = 20 vials (if one vial is enough for both media) or 40 vials (if not)
  • Adding retention samples and other QC tests, destructive sampling can account for 5–10% of the batch size
  • This is why batch planning must take the QC sample quantity into account

1.1 Membrane Filtration Method

Original Text

Use membrane filters having a nominal pore size not greater than 0.45 μm, in which the effectiveness to retain microorganisms has been established. Cellulose nitrate filters are used for aqueous, oily, and weakly alcoholic solutions; and cellulose acetate filters are used for strongly alcoholic solutions.

The technique described assumes membranes about 50 mm in diameter. If filters of a different diameter are used, the volumes of the dilutions and washings should be adjusted accordingly.

The filtration apparatus and membrane are sterilized by appropriate means. The apparatus is designed so that the solution can be introduced and filtered under aseptic conditions: it permits the aseptic removal of the membrane for transfer to the medium, or it is suitable for carrying out incubation after adding the medium to the apparatus itself.

Aqueous Solutions

Transfer a small quantity of a suitable, sterile diluent such as Fluid A onto the membrane and filter. Transfer the contents of the container(s) to the membrane. Filter immediately. If the product has antimicrobial properties, wash the membrane not less than three times by filtering through it each time the volume of the chosen sterile diluent used in the Method Suitability Test. Do not exceed a washing cycle of five times 100 mL per filter. Transfer the whole membrane to the culture medium or cut it aseptically into two equal parts, transferring one half to each of two suitable media. Incubate the media for not less than 14 days.

Soluble Solids

Dissolve in a suitable solvent such as Sterile Water for Injection, sterile saline, or Fluid A, and proceed as directed for Aqueous Solutions.

Oils and Oily Solutions

Oils of sufficiently low viscosity may be filtered without dilution through a dry membrane. Viscous oils may be diluted with a suitable sterile diluent such as isopropyl myristate. Allow the oil to penetrate the membrane by its own weight, then filter, applying pressure or suction gradually. Wash at least three times with about 100 mL of Fluid A containing a suitable emulsifying agent (e.g., polysorbate 80 at 10 g per L — Fluid K).

Ointments and Creams

Dilute to 1% in isopropyl myristate, by heating if necessary to not more than 40°. In exceptional cases, heating to not more than 44° may be necessary. Filter as rapidly as possible.

Prefilled Syringes

For prefilled syringes without attached sterile needles, expel the contents into membrane filter funnels or pooling vessels. If a separate sterile needle is attached, directly expel contents. Test the sterility of the needle using Direct Inoculation.

Antibiotic Solids for Injection

  • Pharmacy Bulk Packages, <5 g: From each of 20 containers, transfer about 300 mg of solids into a sterile 500-mL conical flask, dissolve in about 200 mL of Fluid A
  • Pharmacy Bulk Packages, ≥5 g: From each of 6 containers, transfer about 1 g of solids into a sterile 500-mL conical flask, dissolve in about 200 mL of Fluid A

Sterile Aerosol Products

Freeze the containers in an alcohol-dry ice mixture at least at −20° for about 1 hour. If feasible, allow the propellant to escape before aseptically opening. Add 100 mL of Fluid D to the pooling vessel and mix gently.

Devices with Pathways Labeled Sterile

Aseptically pass not less than 10 pathway volumes of Fluid D through each device tested. Collect the fluids and proceed as directed for Aqueous Solutions.

Tutorial Commentary

Membrane filtration: the preferred method for sterility testing

USP states explicitly that membrane filtration should be used whenever the nature of the product permits. The reasons are:

  • Good separation: microorganisms are retained on the filter while the product's antimicrobial components are rinsed away, greatly reducing the risk of false negatives
  • Wide applicability: aqueous solutions, alcoholic solutions, oils, and solids soluble in aqueous or oily solvents
  • High sensitivity: large sample volumes can be processed, which increases the probability of detecting microorganisms

Filter specification: nominal porosity ≤ 0.45 μm. Note that this is not the 0.2 μm used for sterilizing filtration; the purpose is to retain the microorganisms that can be detected in the culture media.

Analogy: panning for gold

Membrane filtration is like panning for gold. You pour sandy water (the product) containing gold (microorganisms) through a sieve (the filter): the gold stays on the sieve and the water runs away. You then rinse the sieve repeatedly with clean water (rinsing fluid) to wash the sand off while the gold remains. Finally, you put the sieve into the detection equipment (culture medium) to see whether there is any gold.

The rinsing step is critical. If the sandy water contains a "gold killer" (antimicrobial substance), you must rinse it out completely; otherwise the gold is destroyed too, and you will wrongly conclude that there was none.

Rinse limit: the hard rule of 5 × 100 mL

USP specifies rinsing with no more than 5 × 100 mL, even if method suitability testing (MST) shows that this rinse volume still cannot completely eliminate the antimicrobial activity.

The reason is that excessive rinsing may:

  • wash microorganisms off the filter (reducing detection sensitivity)
  • damage the integrity of the filter
  • increase the risk of environmental contamination as handling time increases

If 5 × 100 mL still cannot eliminate the antimicrobial activity, you need to consider switching to another strategy (such as adding a neutralizing agent or using direct inoculation).

Operating guide by dosage form for a CDMO

Based on the typical product lines of a CDMO:

  • Small-volume injectables (1–10 mL vials/syringes): membrane filtration with Fluid A rinse
  • Large-volume injectables (100–1000 mL bags/bottles): membrane filtration, using 20 mL or 10%
  • Lyophilized products: reconstitute first with WFI or Fluid A, then use membrane filtration
  • Prefilled syringes: the contents are tested by membrane filtration and the needle separately by direct inoculation
  • Liposomes/emulsions: rinse with Fluid D or Fluid K

The 14-day incubation period: the bottleneck of batch release

USP requires incubation for not less than 14 days. This is the biggest bottleneck in the batch release timeline:

  • Day 0: filling is complete and the sterility test is started
  • Days 1–14: incubation and observation period (with regular visual inspection)
  • Day 14: final reading; if the result is acceptable, the batch can be released

These 14 days are a hard regulatory requirement and cannot be shortened. A CDMO's production schedule, warehouse planning and customer delivery commitments must all take these 14 days into account.

Link to media fill: the aseptic process simulation (media fill) also uses a 14-day incubation, so this time frame is found everywhere in aseptic pharmaceutical manufacturing.

1.2 Direct Inoculation of the Culture Medium

Original Text

Transfer the quantity of the preparation prescribed in Tables 2 and 3 directly into the culture medium so that the volume of the product is not more than 10% of the volume of the medium, unless otherwise prescribed.

If the product has antimicrobial activity, carry out the test after neutralizing this with a suitable neutralizing substance or by dilution in a sufficient quantity of culture medium. When it is necessary to use a large volume of the product, it may be preferable to use a concentrated culture medium.

Oily Liquids

Use media to which have been added a suitable emulsifying agent at a concentration shown to be appropriate in the Method Suitability Test (e.g., polysorbate 80 at 10 g per L).

Ointments and Creams

Prepare by diluting to about 1 in 10 by emulsifying with the chosen emulsifying agent in a suitable sterile diluent such as Fluid A. Transfer the diluted product to a medium not containing an emulsifying agent.

Solids

Transfer a quantity corresponding to not less than the quantity indicated in Tables 2 and 3. Transfer the material to 200 mL of Fluid Thioglycollate Medium, and mix. Similarly, transfer the same quantity to 200 mL of Soybean–Casein Digest Medium, and mix.

Purified Cotton, Gauze, Surgical Dressings

From each package, aseptically remove two or more portions of 100- to 500-mg each from the innermost part of the sample. From individually packaged, single-use materials, remove the entire article. Immerse in each medium.

Sterile Devices

Articles can be immersed intact or disassembled. Immerse in a volume of medium sufficient to immerse the device completely. For catheters where the inside lumen and outside are required to be sterile, either cut them into pieces such that the medium is in contact with the entire lumen or fill the lumen with medium, and then immerse the intact unit.

Incubation: Incubate the inoculated media for not less than 14 days. Observe the cultures several times during the incubation period. Shake cultures containing oily products gently each day. However, when FTM is used for anaerobic detection, keep shaking or mixing to a minimum to maintain anaerobic conditions.

Tutorial Commentary

Direct inoculation: when to use it?

Direct inoculation is the alternative method, used when membrane filtration is not feasible:

  • The product cannot be filtered (high viscosity, solids, devices)
  • The product quantity is very small or the product is of a special nature
  • Medical devices that require whole-item immersion testing

Limitation of direct inoculation: the product comes into direct contact with the medium, so if the product has antimicrobial activity it will directly inhibit microbial growth. The 10% limit on the product volume relative to the medium is therefore key, as it minimizes the product's interference with the medium.

Analogy: two ways of solving a case

Membrane filtration is like CSI forensics: the evidence (microorganisms) is first separated from the crime scene (product) and then sent to the laboratory (culture medium) for analysis, so interfering substances at the scene (antimicrobial components) do not affect the analysis.

Direct inoculation is like doing the forensic work right at the crime scene. It is cruder, but for some "large crime scenes" that cannot be moved (medical devices), it is the only feasible way. The drawback is that chemicals at the scene may interfere with the results.

The shaking paradox of anaerobic culture in FTM

This is a classic operational contradiction:

  • Oily products need to be gently shaken every day, to ensure adequate contact between the product and the medium
  • Anaerobic incubation in FTM requires avoiding agitation, to maintain the anaerobic environment at the bottom

Solution: if an oily product must be incubated in FTM, the shaking should be extremely gentle, just enough to disperse the oil without disrupting the redox gradient of the medium. This depends on the experience and judgment of the operator.

When to use concentrated media

When the product volume is large and a high dilution is needed (for example, 20 mL taken from a large-volume injectable), the medium must be at least 200 mL to keep the product at ≤ 10% of the medium volume. If still more dilution is needed to eliminate antimicrobial activity, a standard-strength medium would have its nutrients diluted too far.

In this case, use a concentrated medium (for example, 2× or 3× strength), which maintains an adequate nutrient concentration even after a large dilution and ensures that microorganisms can grow normally.

  • Table 2 (minimum quantity): increases with container size: <1 mL use all, 1–40 mL use half, >40 mL use 20 mL, >100 mL use 10%. Each medium requires this quantity separately
  • Table 3 (minimum number of items): injectables fall into three tiers by batch size: ≤100 items, 10% or 4 containers, whichever is greater; 101–500 items, 10 containers; >500 items, 2% or 20 containers, whichever is less

2.0 Observation & Interpretation of Results

Original Text

At intervals during the incubation period and at its conclusion, examine the media for macroscopic evidence of microbial growth. If the material being tested renders the medium turbid so that the presence or absence of microbial growth cannot be readily determined by visual examination, 14 days after the beginning of incubation transfer portions (each not less than 1 mL) of the medium to fresh vessels of the same medium, and then incubate the original and transfer vessels for not less than 4 days.

If no evidence of microbial growth is found, the product to be examined complies with the test for sterility.

If evidence of microbial growth is found, the product to be examined does not comply with the test for sterility, unless it can be clearly demonstrated that the test was invalid for causes unrelated to the product to be examined.

"If evidence of microbial growth is found, the product to be examined does not comply with the test for sterility, unless it can be clearly demonstrated that the test was invalid for causes unrelated to the product to be examined."

Tutorial Commentary

Special Handling When the Product Causes Turbidity

Some products (for example suspensions, emulsions and protein formulations) make the medium turbid once added, so that growth cannot be judged by eye. The USP solution is:

  1. On day 14, transfer at least 1 mL from each vessel to a fresh vessel of the same medium
  2. Continue incubating both the original and the new vessels for at least 4 days
  3. Observe the new vessels (free of product interference) for microbial growth

This means that for turbid products the total incubation time can reach 18 days (14 + 4), further extending batch release time.

Analogy: The Goldfish in Murky Water

Imagine looking for a goldfish in a tank of clear water: it is obvious at a glance. But if the water is murky (because of the product), you cannot see. What do you do?

The USP approach is to scoop some of the murky water into a new tank of clear water. If there really is a fish (microorganism) in the murky water, it will keep swimming in the clear water and will soon be seen. If there is still no fish in the clear water after 4 days, you can be confident that the original murky water has none either.

CDMO Observation Schedule

USP requires observation "at intervals during the incubation period". The CDMO best practice is to establish a clear observation schedule:

  • Day 3-5: First observation. Fast-growing bacteria may already be visible
  • Day 7: Mid-point observation. Most bacteria should be visible
  • Day 10-11: Late observation. Slow-growing bacteria and fungi
  • Day 14: Final reading. Formal result recorded

Every observation must be recorded in the laboratory records, including the observer signature, the observation result and a description of the medium appearance.

Interpretation Logic: Simple but High-Impact

The interpretation of results in USP ⟨71⟩ looks simple, but every word bears on a batch release or rejection decision worth millions:

The key phrase is "macroscopic evidence", meaning evidence visible to the naked eye. This includes medium turbidity, pellicle formation, color change of the medium, sediment and so on.

2.1 Invalid Test & Retest Procedures

Original Text

The test may be considered invalid only if one or more of the following conditions are fulfilled:

  1. The data of the microbiological monitoring of the sterility testing facility show a fault.
  2. A review of the testing procedure used during the test in question reveals a fault.
  3. Microbial growth is found in the negative controls.
  4. After determination of the identity of the microorganisms isolated from the test, the growth of this species (or these species) may be ascribed unequivocally to faults with respect to the material and/or the technique used in conducting the sterility test procedure.

If the test is declared to be invalid, it is repeated with the same number of units as in the original test.

If no evidence of microbial growth is found in the repeat test, the product examined complies with the test for sterility.

If microbial growth is found in the repeat test, the product examined does not comply with the test for sterility.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: A "Mistrial" in Court

Think of the sterility test as a trial. Once a "guilty" (fail) verdict has been reached, a "mistrial" can be declared and the case retried only if you can show that the trial procedure itself was flawed (not that something was wrong with the defendant).

You cannot ask for a retrial just because you dislike the verdict. You need specific evidence of a procedural flaw. Likewise, you cannot simply repeat a failed sterility test; you need clear evidence of one of the four conditions above.

Condition 4: The Most Contested and Most Frequently Invoked

Condition 4 ("unequivocally ascribed to faults") is the condition most often used in practice, and also the one regulatory inspectors most readily challenge.

The key word is "unequivocally":

  • You must identify the species of the contaminating microorganism (species identification)
  • You must demonstrate that the organism clearly originated from the test operation or environment and not from the product
  • You need to cross-check against historical environmental monitoring data
  • If the isolate is a human skin organism (such as Staphylococcus epidermidis) and your process is a personnel-intensive aseptic filling operation, it is very hard to rule out product contamination "unequivocally"

Retest Rules: Only One Chance

The USP retest rules are extremely strict:

  • Retest sample size = original test sample size (it cannot be reduced)
  • The test may be repeated only once, with no third chance
  • Retest passes: the product complies
  • Retest fails: the product does not comply (final decision)

This means that for a CDMO, a positive result in the initial test creates enormous pressure: you must make a well-founded decision between declaring the test invalid and accepting the failure, and you have only one chance at a retest.

CDMO Standard Process for a False Positive Investigation

When a sterility test gives a positive result, the standard CDMO investigation process is:

  1. Quarantine the batch immediately: suspend release and place it under investigation
  2. Microbial identification: identify the microorganism from the positive medium to species level (16S rRNA sequencing or an automated identification system)
  3. Environmental monitoring data review: pull the environmental monitoring data for the test day and several days before and after (settle plates, active air, surface monitoring)
  4. Operating record review: check the analyst operating records, medium lot numbers and equipment calibration records
  5. Negative control confirmation: confirm that the negative controls of the same test run were acceptable
  6. Trend analysis: determine whether the historical positive rate for the product or the laboratory is abnormal
  7. Overall determination: based on the above evidence, decide whether to declare the test invalid and retest OR to declare a failure
  8. CAPA: raise corrective and preventive actions regardless of the outcome

Tutorial Commentary

Importance of the "Same Sample" Principle

USP explicitly requires that the sterility tests for bacteria and fungi be performed on the same sample. This means:

  • The contents of the same container (or the same set of containers) must be divided between FTM and SCDM
  • Different containers may not be used to test for bacteria and fungi separately
  • If the volume in one container is not enough for both media, the contents of several containers may be pooled

Why? If different containers were used, one might be contaminated while the other is not. Using the same sample ensures that both tests face exactly the same microbiological status.

Membrane Filtration: "Whenever Possible, the Whole Contents"

USP uses a subtle but important wording for membrane filtration: "whenever possible, the whole contents". This means:

  • If technically feasible, the entire contents of the container should be filtered through the membrane
  • The quantities in Table 2 are the minimum requirement, not a target
  • Using the entire contents maximizes the probability of detecting microorganisms

In CDMO practice, for small-volume injectables (1-5 mL) the whole volume can usually be used. For large-volume products (100+ mL), taking the minimum quantity per Table 2 is sufficient.

2.3 Sterility Test Decision Flowchart

Original Text

Based on the complete decision logic described in USP ⟨71⟩, the following flowchart summarizes the entire sterility test pathway from sample collection to final batch disposition.

Step 1: Select samples per Table 3 (based on batch size)

Step 2: Determine quantity per Table 2 (based on container volume)

Step 3: Choose method — Membrane Filtration (preferred) or Direct Inoculation

Step 4: Inoculate FTM (30°–35°C) + SCDM (20°–25°C)

Step 5: Incubate ≥ 14 days with periodic observation

Step 6: Final reading at Day 14

  → No growth → PASS → Batch release

  → Growth detected → Investigate

Step 7: Evaluate 4 invalidity criteria

  → Criteria met → Declare invalid → Retest (same sample size)

  → Criteria NOT met → FAIL → Batch reject

Step 8: Retest result

  → No growth → PASS → Batch release

  → Growth detected → FAIL → Batch reject (final)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Branch Points in the Decision Flow

The whole logic of USP ⟨71⟩ can be summarized in three key branch points:

  1. Day 14 reading: growth vs no growth
  2. Invalidity assessment: whether at least one of the four conditions is met
  3. Retest result: the second (and final) chance

The decision at each branch point must be supported by written documentation and requires formal approval by Quality Assurance (QA).

Key Focus Areas for Regulatory Inspectors

When inspecting sterility testing, FDA and other regulators pay particular attention to:

  • Positive rate trend: a consistently high positive rate in your laboratory (for example > 1%) indicates a systemic problem
  • Frequency of invalidation: frequent invalidation = possible "testing into compliance" (repeating tests until they pass)
  • Quality of the investigation: whether the use of condition 4 is backed by sufficient microbiological evidence
  • Retest success rate: if retests always pass, inspectors will question whether the problem in the initial test was really resolved
  • CAPA effectiveness: whether similar problems recur and whether corrective actions are in place

Section Summary

  • Interpretation of results: no growth at 14 days = PASS; growth = FAIL, unless the test can be shown to be invalid
  • Handling of turbid products: on day 14 transfer ≥ 1 mL to fresh medium and incubate for a further ≥ 4 days; total incubation can reach 18 days
  • Four invalidity conditions (the only ones): (1) environmental monitoring abnormality (2) lapse in operating procedure (3) growth in the negative control (4) contaminating organism unequivocally ascribed to a testing fault
  • Retest rules: only one retest, with the same sample size as the original test; if the retest passes the product complies, if it fails the batch is finally rejected
  • Same sample principle: the bacterial (FTM) and fungal (SCDM) tests must use contents from the same sample
  • CDMO time impact: release at 14 days in the normal flow; positive result + investigation + retest may delay release to week 5 or later
  • Core of a false positive investigation: microbial identification + cross-check against environmental monitoring data + review of operating records; the evidence must be "unequivocal"
  • Regulatory focus: positive rate trend, frequency of invalidation, quality of investigation and CAPA effectiveness; avoid any suspicion of "testing into compliance"
