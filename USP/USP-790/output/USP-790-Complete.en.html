Chapter Learning Objectives

  • Understand the mandatory status of USP <790>: every injectable product must pass visual inspection for visible particulates before batch release
  • Master the legal definition of "essentially free" of visible particulates and its quantitative expression in AQL sampling
  • Know the standard operating conditions for visual inspection: illumination (2000–3750 lux), black and white backgrounds, 5-second inspection time
  • Recognize how the ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 (ISO 2859-1) sampling plan is applied to batch release, and what an AQL of 0.65% means
  • Master the hierarchy between <790>, <788> (subvisible particles) and <1790> (informational chapter)
  • Understand the practical requirements for 100% visual inspection on a CDMO filling line: automated vs manual, inspector qualification, defect classification
Introduction

Original Text

All products intended for parenteral administration must be visually inspected for the presence of particulate matter as specified in Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉. Dry solids, from which constituted solutions are prepared for injection, meet the requirements for Completeness and clarity of solutions in Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉 when they are prepared just before use.

Where used in this chapter, the term essentially free means that when injectable drug products are inspected as described herein, no more than the specified number of units may be observed to contain visible particulates.

Particulate matter is defined in Particulate Matter in Injections 〈788〉 as extraneous mobile undissolved particles, other than gas bubbles, unintentionally present in solutions. See Subvisible Particulate Matter in Therapeutic Protein Injections 〈787〉 for additional information on inherent, intrinsic, and extrinsic particulates.

Examples of such particulate matter include, but are not limited to, fibers, glass, metal, elastomeric materials, and precipitates. However, some products may contain inherent particles or agglomerates; in such cases, requirements for these particular visible particulates are specified in the individual monograph or in the approved regulatory application but the control and acceptance criteria for extraneous particles described in this chapter apply.

Where the nature of the contents or the container–closure system permits only limited capability for inspection of the total contents, the 100% inspection of a batch shall be supplemented with the inspection of constituted (e.g., dried) or withdrawn (e.g., dark amber container, suspensions, highly colored liquids) contents of a sample of containers from the batch. The destructive nature of these tests requires the use of a sample smaller than those traditionally used for non-destructive acceptance sampling after 100% inspection.

Although the tests described in this chapter may be useful during studies to examine product stability, this chapter is not intended to establish any new testing requirements for stability studies.

"All products intended for parenteral administration must be visually inspected for the presence of particulate matter."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Mandatory status of USP <790>: This is a mandatory general chapter. USP general chapters numbered below 1000 are legally enforceable when referenced by individual monographs. Unlike <1790> (numbered above 1000, an informational chapter), the requirements of <790> must be met in drug registration and in GMP inspections.

  • Essentially Free: This does not mean "zero particles". It means that statistical sampling confirms that "no more than a specified number of units contain visible particulates". The definition is tied directly to the AQL sampling plan
  • Particulate Matter: Defined in <788> as extraneous, mobile, undissolved particles, excluding gas bubbles. Note the word "extraneous": particles inherent to the product (such as protein aggregates) are controlled by different specifications
  • Inherent vs Extraneous: If the product itself forms visible particles (for example, aggregates of certain protein drugs), the acceptance criteria are set by the individual monograph or the approved marketing application, but extraneous particles must still comply with <790>

Analogy

Quality standard for bottled water:

Imagine you manage a bottled-water production line. "Essentially free" works like a rule that, out of every 1000 bottles, no more than a specified number may show floating matter under the light. It does not say "not a single one" (unrealistic in industrial production); instead, statistical methods ensure the quality level of the whole batch.

Also, if the water naturally contains mineral sediment (inherent particles), a separate standard governs that. But a plastic fragment that fell in from the bottle cap (an extraneous particle) is unacceptable, regardless of whether the water has natural sediment.

CDMO Practical Application

Compliance points on the filling line:

  • 100% in-process visual inspection is the baseline requirement: every vial must be visually inspected (manually or automatically)
  • After 100% inspection, statistical sampling inspection per ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 is still required before batch release
  • For lyophilized products (dry powder), the solution state usually cannot be seen after filling and closing, so sampling inspection is performed after reconstitution
  • A client may have internal standards stricter than USP (for example, AQL 0.10% rather than 0.65%); the CDMO must confirm the applicable acceptance criteria with the client

Key Notes

Hierarchy of <790> vs <788> vs <1790>:

Cases of limited inspection capability: For products that are difficult to inspect directly, such as dark containers, suspensions and strongly colored liquids, samples must be drawn from the batch and the contents removed for inspection. This is destructive inspection, so the sample size is smaller.

Inspection Procedure

Original Text

Used along with 100% inspection during the manufacturing process, this procedure is sufficient to demonstrate that the batch is essentially free of visible particulates. A complete program for the control and monitoring of particulate matter remains an essential prerequisite.

Inspected units must be free of visible particulates when examined without magnification (except for optical correction as may be required to establish normal vision) against a black background and against a white background. Illumination at the inspection point is maintained at a minimum intensity between 2000 and 3750 lux. This can be achieved through the use of two 13-W or 15-W fluorescent lamps (e.g., F13/T5 or F15/T8). The use of a high-frequency ballast to reduce flicker from the fluorescent lamps is recommended. Alternative light sources (e.g., incandescent, LED) that provide illumination at the point of inspection within the specified minimum intensity range are acceptable. Higher illumination intensity is recommended for examination of colored solutions or product in containers other than clear glass.

Before performing the inspection, remove any adherent labels from the container, and wash and dry the outside. The unit under inspection should be gently swirled and/or inverted, ensuring that no air bubbles are produced, and inspected for approximately 5 s against each of the backgrounds. The presence of any particles should be recorded.

"Inspected units must be free of visible particulates when examined without magnification against a black background and against a white background."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three key elements of inspection conditions:

  • Illumination of 2000–3750 lux: This is the scientifically validated optimal range. Below 2000 lux, light-colored or transparent particles are missed; above 3750 lux, glare may occur and actually reduce detection sensitivity. Note: for dark solutions or opaque containers, higher illumination is recommended
  • Black and white backgrounds: White particles are easiest to see against a black background; dark particles (such as metal or rubber fragments) are most obvious against a white background. Both backgrounds are indispensable
  • Unaided eye: No magnifying lens is used, which is a key limitation. Only corrective eyeglasses or contact lenses are allowed. This means <790> targets particles visible to the naked eye (typically ≥ 100–150 μm)

Technique: Gently swirl and/or invert the container so that particles settled at the bottom or clinging to the wall are set in motion, but without generating bubbles (bubbles interfere with particle assessment). Each background is inspected for about 5 seconds, for a total of about 10 seconds per vial.

Analogy

Standardization in gem grading:

This is like a gemologist grading the clarity of a diamond. You cannot do it in a dimly lit bar (insufficient illumination), nor with a magnifying glass (which would reveal flaws of a different grade). The industry standard specifies particular lighting conditions and unaided-eye observation, so that every grader worldwide reaches comparable conclusions under the same conditions.

The logic of black and white backgrounds is also intuitive: just as black dots are easy to see on white paper and white dots on black paper, you need to look against both so that nothing is missed.

Key Notes

100% inspection + sampling inspection = double assurance:

<790> states explicitly that this procedure is used in conjunction with 100% inspection during manufacture. This means:

  1. First line of defense: 100% visual inspection on the filling line (in-process control, IPC), which rejects units with obvious particulates
  2. Second line of defense: statistical sampling before batch release (the procedure described in this chapter), which confirms the quality of the whole batch

Choice of light source: The original text mentions fluorescent tubes (F13/T5 or F15/T8), but alternative sources (such as incandescent lamps or LEDs) are also permitted as long as the illumination is within the specified range. Modern inspection stations mostly use high-quality LED sources, which have a more stable spectrum and no flicker.

Key Notes: Automated vs Manual Visual Inspection

The practical choice on a CDMO filling line:

Whether manual or automated inspection is used, statistical sampling per <790> is still required before batch release. Units rejected by an automated machine do not mean the batch fails; what counts is the sampling result.

Sampling at Batch Release (After 100% Manufacturing Inspection)

Original Text

Sample and inspect the batch using ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 (or ISO 2859-1). General Inspection Level II, single sampling plans for normal inspection with an AQL of 0.65%. Alternative sampling plans with equivalent or better protection are acceptable. NMT the specified number of units contains visible particulates.

"Sample and inspect the batch using ANSI/ASQ Z1.4... General Inspection Level II, single sampling plans for normal inspection with an AQL of 0.65%."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Key parameters of the AQL sampling plan:

  • ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 (equivalent to ISO 2859-1): This is the internationally accepted attribute sampling inspection standard used to decide whether a batch is acceptable. It is widely used for visual inspection in the pharmaceutical industry
  • General Inspection Level II: The standard inspection level, providing a moderate degree of discrimination. Level I has a smaller sample size (less stringent) and Level III a larger sample size (more stringent)
  • AQL 0.65%: Acceptable Quality Limit. It means that when the batch defective rate is 0.65%, the probability of accepting the batch is about 95%. Note: the AQL is not an upper limit on the permitted defective rate; it is a statistical probability reference point
  • NMT (Not More Than): The number of units in the sample containing visible particulates must not exceed the acceptance number (Ac) given in the Z1.4 table

Sampling Example

Lot size 35,001–150,000 vials (a common CDMO batch size):

General Inspection Level II → Sample Size Code Letter = N
Sample Size Code N → n = 500 units

AQL = 0.65%
Ac (Accept number) = 7
Re (Reject number) = 8

Decision rule:
  Draw 500 vials → count vials with visible particulates found
  ≤ 7 vials → batch accepted (Pass)
  ≥ 8 vials → batch rejected (Fail)

Important: Different lot sizes correspond to different code letters and sample sizes. Always consult the Z1.4 tables to determine the correct n, Ac and Re values.

Key Notes

Alternative plans offering "equivalent or better protection": The original text permits alternative sampling plans, but they must provide equivalent or better quality protection. Common alternatives include:

  • Use Tightened Inspection as the routine standard for batch release
  • Use AQL 0.10% (stricter) instead of 0.65%, which some clients or regulatory systems may require
  • For high-risk products (such as ophthalmic injectables), the client may require zero-defect sampling plans (c = 0 plans)

CDMO note: Your quality assurance department must establish switching rules defining when to move from normal inspection to tightened or reduced inspection. These rules must be consistent with the quality standards in the client agreement.

CDMO Practical Application: Inspector Qualification

Qualification management of visual inspectors:

  • Visual acuity: Near-vision acuity of 20/20 (corrected is acceptable), tested periodically (usually annually)
  • Initial training: Training with standard sets known to contain particles (Knapp set / defect standard set)
  • Qualification: Pass the standard set test; the detection rate for known defective units must meet the target (usually ≥ 70%)
  • Periodic requalification: Re-confirm qualification every 6–12 months
  • Fatigue management: Continuous inspection time should not exceed 30–45 minutes, with breaks scheduled (an EU GMP Annex 1 expectation)

These requirements are not specified in detail in <790> itself, but are covered in depth in EU GMP Annex 1 (2023) and <1790>.

Product in Distribution

Original Text

If it becomes necessary to evaluate product that has been shipped to customers (e.g., because of a complaint or regulatory concern), sample and inspect 20 units. If no particles are observed in the sample, the batch is considered essentially free of visible particulates. If available, additional units may be inspected to gain further information on the risk of particulates in the batch.

Note: Testing outlined in Product in Distribution is permissible only if Sampling at Batch Release (after 100% Manufacturing Inspection) has been successfully completed.

"Testing outlined in Product in Distribution is permissible only if Sampling at Batch Release (after 100% Manufacturing Inspection) has been successfully completed."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Conditions for inspecting product in distribution:

  • Prerequisite: This inspection applies only to product that has already passed batch release sampling. If a batch never completed the <790> release sampling, this simplified procedure cannot be used
  • Triggering situations: a customer complaint (receipt of product containing particles), a regulatory authority request for investigation, a problem found in a stability study, and so on
  • Sample size of 20 units: Much smaller than the sample size for batch release. This reflects that the product has already passed the earlier full inspection, and this is only additional confirmation
  • Acceptance criterion: 0 of 20 units containing visible particulates = acceptable. Any unit containing particles requires further investigation

Key Notes

Link to the CDMO complaint-handling process:

When a particulate complaint is received from a customer, the typical investigation flow is:

  1. Confirm the batch release records: Retrieve the <790> AQL sampling results for the batch. Did it pass? What were the results?
  2. Retention sample inspection: Take 20 units from the retention sample and inspect them per <790>
  3. Particle identification: If particles are found, perform particle identification analysis (FTIR, SEM-EDX, etc.) to determine the source (container, stopper, process environment, product degradation)
  4. Risk assessment: Assess the impact of the particles on patient safety and decide whether a recall is needed
  5. CAPA: Initiate corrective and preventive actions based on root cause analysis

Analogy

Factory-outgoing inspection vs recall inspection for cars:

AQL sampling at batch release is like quality sampling before cars leave the factory: a certain proportion of every batch of cars gets a full inspection. "Inspection of product in distribution" is like the investigation after an owner complaint: you do not need to recall and inspect every car sold, only a few (20 units) to confirm whether the problem is widespread. But this presupposes that the batch passed the factory quality check (batch release) in the first place; otherwise this simplified procedure does not apply.

CDMO Context: Defect Classification

Extended Context

Although USP〈790〉does not explicitly define a defect classification system, CDMO operations must integrate visible particulate inspection with a comprehensive defect classification framework for 100% inspection on the filling line. This framework typically categorizes defects by severity and directly impacts AQL settings and rejection decisions.

Key point: Visible particulates per〈790〉are classified as Critical defects in most CDMO quality systems. This means the AQL for particulates during 100% in-process inspection is effectively 0% — any unit with visible particulates is rejected. The 0.65% AQL in〈790〉applies to the batch release sampling step that follows.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Two-Tier Logic of Defect Classification

Different standards for 100% inspection vs AQL sampling:

Here is a concept that is often confused:

  • During 100% in-line inspection: Visible particles are a "critical defect"; once found, the unit is rejected, and there is no such thing as an "acceptable" particulate vial
  • During AQL sampling (<790>): AQL 0.65% is the statistical tool used at batch release. It acknowledges the reality that, even after 100% inspection, a few units containing particles may still be missed

In other words, the goal of 100% inspection is "zero defects passing", but AQL sampling acknowledges that inspection is not perfect, and uses statistical methods to confirm that the miss rate is within an acceptable range.

Key Notes: EU GMP Annex 1 (2023) Requirements for Visual Inspection

The latest EU Annex 1 has more detailed provisions on visual inspection (sections 8.22–8.25):

  • 100% visual inspection is mandatory (not merely recommended)
  • Automated inspection systems must be validated to demonstrate that they are equivalent or superior to manual inspection
  • Inspectors must be periodically requalified on their detection ability
  • The reject rate trend must be recorded and investigated
  • Units rejected by an automated system must not be reintroduced into the production flow without manual confirmation

As a CDMO, if your product is exported to the EU market, it must comply with both USP <790> and EU GMP Annex 1.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Complete Architecture of the USP Particulate Control System

USP builds a tiered particulate control system, with each chapter responsible for a different particle size range:

<1> Injections: parent chapter (overall product quality requirements)
↓
<790> Visible particulates (≥ ~100 μm, visual inspection)
↓
<788> / <787> Subvisible particulates (≥10 μm / ≥25 μm, instrumental testing)

The role of <1790>: As an informational chapter, <1790> provides detailed guidance on visual inspection best practices, inspector training methods, validation strategies for automated inspection and more. Although it is not enforceable, it is the most important reference when establishing a visual inspection SOP.

CDMO Practical Application: A Complete Particulate Control Strategy

A complete particulate control plan compliant with USP and EU GMP should include:

  1. Process controls: environmental monitoring, component cleaning validation, filter integrity testing
  2. 100% visual inspection (<790> + Annex 1): manual or automated inspection on the filling line
  3. AQL statistical sampling (<790>): formal sampling-based disposition before batch release
  4. Subvisible particle testing (<788> / <787>): light obscuration or microscopic methods to measure particles ≥10 μm and ≥25 μm
  5. Trend analysis and CAPA: track reject rate trends and particle identification results for continual improvement

Key Notes: Special Level Sampling Plans

Practical significance of Footnote 1:

For products that cannot undergo non-destructive inspection (such as lyophilized products and dark containers), <790> suggests using the Z1.4 Special Inspection Levels (S-1 to S-4). The sample sizes at these levels are much smaller than General Level II, which suits the economics of destructive inspection.

Chapter Summary

  • USP <790> is a mandatory general chapter: every injectable product must pass visual inspection for visible particulates before batch release, with no exemption
  • Quantitative definition of "Essentially Free": determined by statistical sampling under ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 (ISO 2859-1) General Inspection Level II at AQL 0.65%
  • Standardized inspection conditions: illumination of 2000–3750 lux, black and white backgrounds, unaided eye (corrective eyewear permitted), about 5 seconds per background, gentle swirling/inversion without generating bubbles
  • Double line of defense: in-process 100% visual inspection (IPC) + AQL statistical sampling at batch release (<790>)
  • Inspection of product in distribution: applies only to product that has passed batch release; draw 20 units; pass with zero defects
  • CDMO practical integration: defect classification system, inspector qualification management, automated inspection validation, EU GMP Annex 1 compliance, and integration with <788>, <787> and <1790>
