1. Introduction

Original Text

1. INTRODUCTION

Depyrogenation by filtration involves the physical removal of endotoxins from pharmaceutical solutions by adsorption and size exclusion.

Endotoxins are responsible for making up the majority of pyrogens, which must be removed from pharmaceutical products including injectable biologics. There are many factors to be considered when designing a depyrogenation filtration process for solutions containing proteins and peptides: type of target protein and its concentration, electrolyte concentration, pH and buffer system, protein molecular weight and isoelectric point (pI), filtration parameters (e.g., flow rate), and interactions with other components causing aggregation. In general, a combination of these factors determines the most effective depyrogenation method.

Depyrogenation of liquids may be accomplished by means of filtration through various types of filter media including microporous membranes, reverse osmosis (RO) membranes, ultrafilters, charge-modified depth filters, activated carbon, and membrane absorbers.

"Depyrogenation filtration processes are not intended to remove microorganisms from a process stream; however, by their nature, filters selected for use in depyrogenation processes may also be capable of retaining many types of microorganisms."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Depyrogenation by Filtration: The physical removal of endotoxin from parenteral drug solutions, relying mainly on two mechanisms:

  • Adsorption: Physicochemical interaction between endotoxin and the filter media surface (electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic interaction)
  • Size Exclusion: Endotoxin aggregates are too large to pass through the membrane pores

Endotoxin: Fragments of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria; the most important and quantifiable pyrogen in injectable products.

Isoelectric point (pI): The pH at which a protein has zero net charge. When designing depyrogenation with membrane adsorbers, the operating pH relative to the pI determines the charge state of the protein and of the endotoxin, and is the core basis for choosing a strategy.

Analogy

Imagine cleaning a bucket that contains a mixture of sand grains (endotoxin aggregates) and salt (protein).

  • Size exclusion is like using a fine mesh strainer: the sand grains are too large to pass, while the small salt molecules go straight through.
  • Adsorption is like coating the surface of the strainer with glue: even if some sand grains squeeze through the mesh, they stick and stay behind.

But what if your target molecule (the protein) also sticks? This is the greatest challenge in designing a depyrogenation process for protein drugs.

Key Notes

Key point: Depyrogenation by filtration is not the same as sterilizing filtration. Although both goals are sometimes achieved by the same filter, their design purposes are fundamentally different.

USP states explicitly that a depyrogenation filtration process is not intended to remove microorganisms, although the selected filter often also has the ability to retain microorganisms. In CDMO process development, these two functions must be clearly distinguished and addressed separately in the validation documents.

Complexity of design decisions: Designing depyrogenation for protein-containing solutions requires considering six or more variables at once (protein type, concentration, pH, electrolyte concentration, molecular weight, pI). There is no "universal formula"; each product must be developed and validated individually.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can endotoxin fragments (typically <0.025 µm) pass through microporous membranes used to retain bacteria? What does this imply for the depyrogenation performance of microporous membranes?
  2. A monoclonal antibody (mAb) has a molecular weight of about 150 kDa. Can you use an ultrafiltration membrane (MWCO 30–100 kDa) to remove endotoxin? Why or why not? What strategy does this problem illustrate?
  3. Which filtration depyrogenation technology is best suited to an injectable small-molecule drug (MW <500 Da) and to a biologic protein drug (MW ~120 kDa), respectively? Explain your reasoning.
2. Technologies Used for Depyrogenation by Filtration

Original Text

2.1 Microporous Membrane Filtration

Microporous membranes (typically with pore size or retention ratings between 1.0 and 0.1 µm) can be very effective in removing intact bacteria via size exclusion and adsorption within flow pathways. The use of microporous membranes on a freshly prepared solution to be filtered can effectively prevent bacterial proliferation in the solution, along with any potential subsequent endotoxin formation.

Endotoxin, however, is composed of fragments of bacterial cell wall, often <0.025 µm that may easily penetrate most bacteria-retentive membrane filters. These negatively charged particles with endotoxin activity can be removed via adsorption by positively charged membranes. Adsorption of endotoxin has also been shown by hydrophobic membranes, where it is thought that a hydrophobic interaction occurs between the Lipid A core and hydrophobic sites on the membrane flow path surfaces. Reduction or removal of endotoxin activity by adsorption to microporous membranes can be dependent on flow rate, pH, concentration, and fluid and membrane surface properties. Once the effective binding capacity of the membrane approaches saturation under applied conditions, remaining endotoxin will pass through the membrane.

2.2 Reverse Osmosis

RO membranes are the tightest membranes in size separation. They can separate dissolved salts and sugars from water. Pyrogens, and essentially everything else, are removed from water via size exclusion. RO systems are operated most efficiently at high pressure (200–1000 psi) to overcome osmotic pressure. RO membrane rating or tightness is measured and expressed with retention or rejection of marker salts such as sodium chloride or magnesium sulfate.

RO membranes may be composites (thin film coated on top of ultrafiltration membranes) or cast as a single layer (cellulose acetate type). Configuration of RO membrane modules can be flat sheet, tubular, or hollow fiber. All commercially available RO membranes are polymeric, and most are of a spiral-wound, flat-sheet format.

RO systems are not intended to remove all bacteria, and because they are run at ambient temperatures, microbiological contamination is a concern. Ultraviolet (UV) light may be used in the system downstream from the RO units to control microbiological contamination.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Microporous Membrane Filtration

Microporous Membrane: Pore size 0.1–1.0 µm; retains bacteria mainly by size exclusion, but endotoxin removal relies on adsorption rather than size exclusion.

Two adsorption mechanisms:

  • Electrostatic adsorption: A positively charged membrane surface attracts negatively charged LPS particles (endotoxin is naturally negatively charged)
  • Hydrophobic adsorption: The lipid A core structure of LPS is highly hydrophobic and interacts with hydrophobic membrane surfaces

Binding Capacity: The adsorption capacity of the membrane is finite; once it is saturated, subsequent endotoxin passes straight through. This is the fundamental limitation of microporous membranes for depyrogenation.

Key Notes: RO and Microbial Risk

RO is the membrane system with the strongest size exclusion capability and can remove almost all solutes, including pyrogens. However, it carries a frequently overlooked risk:

Because RO operates at ambient temperature and is not designed to remove bacteria completely, microorganisms tend to accumulate downstream of the system. USP recommends using UV lamps downstream of RO to control microbial contamination.

Practical implication for CDMOs: RO in a WFI (water for injection) system is only one step in producing purified water. It must be followed by distillation (or ultrafiltration) and by maintaining a circulation temperature of ≥70°C to ensure that WFI quality meets USP standards.

Analogy: Saturation of Binding Capacity

Think of the adsorption capacity of a microporous membrane as a parking lot: the parking spaces (binding sites) are limited.

When the lot first opens (a new membrane), almost every car (endotoxin molecule) finds a space (is adsorbed). But as the spaces fill up, later cars simply drive past (breakthrough).

This is why, during process development for depyrogenation by filtration, the maximum throughput of the membrane must be determined; beyond this amount, depyrogenation performance cannot be assured.

2.3–2.4 Ultrafiltration & Charge-Modified Depth Filters

Original Text

2.3 Ultrafiltration

Ultrafiltration (UF) is a process whereby a fluid is passed through membranes with pore sizes nominally between about 1 and 100 nm under pressure. The filters are usually not rated by the pore size but by the molecular weight cut-off (MWCO). The methods to determine the MWCO vary by the manufacturer and usually involve measuring passage of molecules of a certain size, such as a solution of mixed dextrans, polyethylene glycol, or proteins to assign a numerical rating.

UF membranes are usually polymeric porous structures, manufactured from a range of materials, most commonly regenerated cellulose or polyether sulfone, but also ceramics. UF membranes may be produced as flat sheet, hollow fibers, or ceramic tubes.

UF is generally operated in tangential/cross flow mode, which separates the starting (feed) solution into two components: permeate (the portion of solution going through the membrane) and retentate (the concentrated solution that is passed over the membrane).

It is generally assumed that the basic subunit of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is about 10–20 kDa. Membranes of 6–10 kDa cut-off are often used for depyrogenation by size exclusion. However, monomeric LPSs are rarely found in solution because of their poor solubility in water. LPS is usually present in aggregated forms, such as vesicles ranging in molecular weight from 300 to 1000 kDa. Thus, endotoxin can be successfully removed by higher flux membranes, with MWCOs of 30–100 kDa.

Adsorption, in addition to size exclusion, also can be a mechanism of endotoxin removal by UF. UF has been used successfully to depyrogenate small molecule drugs, buffers, electrolytes, antibiotics, and antifungal agents. UF is generally not recommended for endotoxin removal from solutions containing larger molecules such as proteins.

2.4 Charge-Modified Depth Filters

Depth filters exhibit two primary clarification mechanisms: size exclusion (through sieving or entrapment) and adsorption (through electrokinetic or hydrophobic interactions).

Electrokinetic adsorption is attributed to the resin binders and filter aids that impart a net-positive charge, positive zeta potential, to the filter medium. This positive zeta potential can remove negatively charged particles smaller than the nominal rating of the depth filter medium.

Most cellulose-based depth filters contain a filter aid to enhance particle retention and flow characteristics. Common filter aids include diatomaceous earth, perlite (volcanic origin), carbon (natural sources), and silica- and/or metallic-based materials.

Benefits of charge-modified depth filters include removal of bacterial endotoxin [4–5 log reduction value (LRV)], DNA fragments, host cell protein, reduction of viruses, and economical throughput with low extractable levels.

Cellulosic depth filters commonly contain extractable Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL)-reactive materials that are often determined to be β-1,3-glucans. β-1,3-Glucans activate an alternative LAL pathway, Factor G, causing a non-endotoxin-positive LAL result. To reduce the risk of β-1,3-glucan extractables, it is important to follow the recommended rinse conditions of the specific depth filter.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Physical Chemistry of UF and LPS

MWCO (Molecular Weight Cut-Off): A specification of ultrafiltration membranes, indicating the molecular size (expressed in Da or kDa) at which 90% of molecules are retained.

Key insight: The molecular weight of an LPS monomer is approximately 10–20 kDa, but LPS almost never exists as monomers in aqueous solution; it usually aggregates into vesicles with molecular weights as high as 300–1000 kDa. This means that even UF membranes with a relatively high MWCO (30–100 kDa) can remove pyrogens effectively, because the target is the aggregate, not the monomer.

Tangential Flow Filtration (TFF): Also called cross-flow filtration; the solution flows parallel to the membrane surface, dividing the feed into permeate and retentate. Compared with dead-end filtration, TFF greatly reduces concentration polarization at the membrane surface and is suitable for long batch operations.

Analogy: The Dual Mechanism of Depth Filters

A charge-modified depth filter is like an airport with a multi-layer security system:

  • First layer: size screening (the X-ray machine): oversized baggage (large particles) is stopped directly.
  • Second layer: electrostatic adsorption (the magnetic security gate): small negatively charged particles (LPS, DNA) are attracted to and retained by the positively charged filter media; even if they get through the pores, they cannot escape.

This dual mechanism gives depth filters far better depyrogenation performance (4–5 LRV) than filtration based on size alone.

Key Notes: β-1,3-Glucan Interference with the LAL Test

This is a pitfall that is very easy to overlook in CDMO practice:

Cellulose-based depth filters (such as many common commercial filters) release β-1,3-glucan into the solution. β-1,3-glucan can activate the Factor G pathway in LAL reagent and cause a "false positive" LAL result: the test indicates pyrogens, but the actual cause is interference from filter extractables.

Mitigation strategies:

  • Pre-flush the filter strictly according to the manufacturer's recommended flushing conditions
  • Use an LAL reagent that is insensitive to β-glucan (Factor G-blocked LAL)
  • Add a β-glucan blocking buffer to the LAL sample

If this problem is not identified during the validation stage, it may cause QC release failure for commercial batches.

Molecular Weight Levels of LPS

LPS monomer:              10–20 kDa
→ requires a 6–10 kDa MWCO membrane for retention

LPS aggregate vesicles:   300–1000 kDa
→ can be retained with a 30–100 kDa MWCO membrane
  (more practical, since this is the form actually present in solution)

Practical operating strategy:
Use a 30–100 kDa UF membrane → retains LPS aggregates
Protein MW > MWCO → protein is also retained (not applicable)
Small-molecule drug MW << MWCO → passes through the membrane, pyrogens removed ✓

Practical Application

A CDMO client wants to establish a depyrogenation step for its peptide drug (MW 3.5 kDa); the buffer currently contains a small amount of residual LPS.

Analysis: The peptide has an MW of 3.5 kDa and the LPS aggregates are 300–1000 kDa. If a UF membrane with a 10 kDa MWCO is used, the LPS aggregates (300–1000 kDa) are retained in the retentate, while the peptide (3.5 kDa) passes through with the permeate, achieving both goals: depyrogenation and retention of the target molecule. This is the classic strategy for using UF to depyrogenate small molecules.

2.5–2.6 Activated Carbon & Membrane Adsorbers

Original Text

2.5 Activated Carbon Depth Filters

Depth filtration, using activated carbon as a filter aid adsorbent, removes color, odor, and bacterial endotoxins and nucleic acids. Activated carbon is derived from organic materials, e.g., peat, wood, coconut, bone, lignite coal. The microstructure of the carbon contains millions of pores that create a highly adsorptive material with a vast internal effective surface area as compared to polymeric microporous structures.

These carbon filter aids are typically activated by steam or chemical treatment such as acid. Although highly effective in reducing endotoxin (4–5 log reduction) and other undesirable contaminants, active carbon may, because of this highly adsorptive characteristic, remove other process components and target molecules due to this nonspecific adsorption property. The high loading capacity and strong adsorptive attributes make activated carbon depth filtration an attractive alternative to conventional filtration methods or addition of bulk carbon, where care must be taken to remove fine carbon particulates in the effluent.

2.6 Membrane Adsorbers

When the target protein in the solution is in the same molecular weight range as that of the endotoxins (10–20 kDa for endotoxin monomers), the target proteins cannot be separated by UF. Ion exchange chromatography is the most common depyrogenation method for proteins; however, it has some drawbacks, which limit its usefulness as a depyrogenation step.

Charge-modified membrane adsorbers with ion exchange ligands functionalized on the membrane surface can provide the required performance needed for depyrogenation from the laboratory up to process scale. Generally, two strategies can be used:

  • Using the strong basic anion exchanger of quaternary amine (Q) type in a buffer with pH lower than the pI of the protein — endotoxin will bind to the charged membrane substrate, and protein will pass through the membrane (negative chromatography).
  • Alternatively, a strong acidic ion exchanger type S can be used with a buffer pH lower than the pI of the protein. In this case, the endotoxin will pass through, and the protein will be bound to the charged membrane substrate, which can be subsequently eluted using appropriate buffers.

Such membrane adsorbers have been used as validated endotoxin clearance steps in downstream processing of monoclonal antibody (mAb) or recombinant protein manufacturing. Typical log reduction values (LRVs) reported are >4 based on lab scale testing.

Another solution is to use mixed-mode membrane adsorbers exhibiting both anionic and hydrophobic chemistries. Endotoxins (hydrophobic and negatively charged) tightly bind onto the membrane surfaces. By adjusting the concentration of salt or pH appropriately, proteins flow through the mixed-mode membrane adsorber by charge repulsion, while endotoxins remain bound. Typical LRVs reported are in the 3–4 range based on lab scale testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Activated Carbon as a Double-Edged Sword

Activated carbon is highly effective for depyrogenation (4–5 LRV), but its greatest weakness is nonspecific adsorption: it indiscriminately adsorbs any hydrophobic molecule, including your target drug substance.

Risk scenario: If the drug itself contains hydrophobic structures (as many small molecules and some peptides do), using activated carbon may cause significant product loss, affecting batch yield and cost.

Best practice: Before using activated carbon, perform compatibility testing to confirm that recovery of the target molecule meets the acceptance criteria.

Key Concepts: Two Strategies for Membrane Adsorbers

Core strategy: Use the different charge behavior of endotoxin (negatively charged, hydrophobic) and protein at a specific pH and salt concentration to selectively retain one of them.

Q-type anion exchange membrane (negative chromatography):

  • Operating pH < protein pI → protein is positively charged → does not bind to the Q membrane → passes through (flow-through)
  • LPS is negatively charged → binds to the Q membrane → retained
  • Collected flow-through = pyrogen-free protein solution

Mixed-mode membrane: Combines anion exchange and hydrophobic interaction, giving stronger retention of LPS (because LPS is both negatively charged and hydrophobic); suitable for high-salt buffers (100–500 mM).

Analogy: Selectivity of Membrane Adsorbers

Imagine looking for people in red clothing (LPS) in a crowd where everyone else (the protein) wears blue.

The Q-type membrane (negative chromatography) is like a "magnetic doorframe" that attracts only red (negatively charged LPS). The blue proteins walk straight through, while the red ones are stopped.

The key trick is adjusting the pH: at pH < pI, a protein that would otherwise be negatively charged becomes positively charged, is not attracted to the Q membrane, and passes through safely.

Section Summary

  • The two mechanisms of depyrogenation by filtration, adsorption and size exclusion, are emphasized differently in different technologies; no technology relies entirely on a single mechanism
  • In solution, LPS exists as high-molecular-weight aggregates (300–1000 kDa), which makes depyrogenation with ultrafiltration membranes (30–100 kDa MWCO) possible, especially for small-molecule drugs
  • Depyrogenation of protein-containing solutions requires particular attention to the molecular weight overlap between the target molecule and endotoxin; Q-type or mixed-mode membrane adsorbers are the preferred strategy in this situation
  • Charge-modified depth filters combine two mechanisms (size exclusion + electrostatic adsorption) and can achieve 4–5 LRV, but note that cellulose-based filters may release β-1,3-glucan, which interferes with LAL test results
  • Activated carbon is strongly effective for depyrogenation but has nonspecific adsorption; its impact on the target molecule must be evaluated before use

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Structure of the USP <1228> Chapter System

The validation section of <1228.3> is only one sentence, pointing to the parent chapter <1228>. This is not laziness but the design logic of the USP architecture: each subchapter (.1/.2/.3/.4/.5) focuses on technology description, while the validation methodology is consolidated in <1228>.

Structure of the USP <1228> series:

  • <1228>: General principles of depyrogenation: validation framework, use of endotoxin reference standards, LRV calculation
  • <1228.1>: Dry Heat Depyrogenation
  • <1228.2>: Depyrogenation by Radiation
  • <1228.3>: Depyrogenation by Filtration (this chapter): principles and selection of six technologies
  • <1228.4>: Depyrogenation by Rinsing
  • <1228.5>: Endotoxin Indicators for Depyrogenation

In a CDMO's validation documents, cite both <1228> (general validation principles) and <1228.3> (technical basis) to establish a complete regulatory compliance foundation.

Key Notes: Core Validation Elements (per <1228>)

Although <1228.3> itself does not elaborate on validation details, under the <1228> framework the validation of depyrogenation by filtration must cover:

  • Endotoxin Challenge: Challenge the filtration system with a known concentration of CSE or RSE
  • Log Reduction Value (LRV): Record the endotoxin concentrations at the inlet and outlet and calculate the log reduction
  • Process parameter boundaries: Confirm the ranges of flow rate, pH, temperature, operating time, and other parameters, and their effect on LRV
  • Worst-case design: Validation should be performed under the most unfavorable conditions to ensure a safety margin in routine operation
  • Binding Capacity confirmation: Determine the maximum throughput before the membrane saturates

Analogy: The Chapter System Is Like a Legal Framework

Think of the USP <1228> series as a body of law:

  • <1228> is the constitution: it sets the basic principles and validation framework that all subchapters must follow
  • <1228.3> is a specific statute: it regulates depyrogenation filtration technology in detail, but when it comes to "how to validate," it refers directly to the constitution rather than legislating again

In a CDMO's SOPs and Validation Master Plan (VMP), this citation relationship must be clearly presented so that FDA or TFDA inspectors can quickly find the regulatory basis.

  • Each of the six depyrogenation filtration technologies has clear use cases and limitations; there is no universal "best choice", and the design must be tailored to the properties of the target molecule (MW, pI, hydrophobicity)
  • The β-1,3-glucan interference problem of charge-modified depth filters is a common source of false-positive LAL results and must be identified during validation, with mitigation measures established
  • Membrane adsorbers (Q-type, mixed-mode) are strategic tools for downstream depyrogenation of biologics; especially when the protein MW overlaps with that of LPS, they are the preferred alternative to UF
