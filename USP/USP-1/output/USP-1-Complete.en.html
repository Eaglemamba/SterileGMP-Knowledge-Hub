Introduction

Original Text

Parenteral drug products include both injections and implanted drug products that are injected through the skin or other external boundary tissue, or implanted within the body to allow the direct administration of the active drug substance(s) into blood vessels, organs, tissues, or lesions. Injections may exist as either immediate- or extended-release dosage forms. Implanted parenteral drug products are long-acting dosage forms that provide continuous release of the active drug substance(s), often for periods of months to years.

Routes of administration for parenteral drug products include intravenous, intraventricular, intra-arterial, intra-articular, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intrathecal, intracisternal, and intraocular.

Parenteral dosage forms include solutions, suspensions, emulsions, sterile powders for solutions and suspensions (including liposomes), implants (including microparticles), and products that consist of both a drug and a device such as drug-eluting stents. The definitions and descriptions of these dosage forms, and brief information about their composition and manufacturing processes, are found in Pharmaceutical Dosage Forms ⟨1151⟩.

[Note — All references to chapters above 1000 are for informational purposes only, for use as a helpful resource. These chapters are not mandatory unless explicitly called out for application.]

This chapter is divided into three main sections: (1) universal product quality tests that are applicable to parenteral dosage forms; (2) specific product quality tests, which are tests that should be considered in addition to Universal Tests; and (3) product quality tests for specific dosage forms, which list applicable tests (universal and specific) for the specific dosage form.

This chapter applies, in whole or in part, when referenced in a drug product monograph (see General Notices, 3.10 Applicability of Standards).

The pharmacopeial definitions for sterile preparations for parenteral use may not apply to some biologics because of their special nature and licensing requirements (see Biologics ⟨1041⟩). However, some biological finished drug products containing “Injection” in the monograph title must meet the requirements of ⟨1⟩ or indicated chapter subparts, where it is specified in the monograph.

"This chapter applies, in whole or in part, when referenced in a drug product monograph."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept Analysis

The "trigger" role of USP ⟨1⟩: This chapter is the "master index" of the USP quality testing system for sterile products. When an individual USP monograph references ⟨1⟩, it activates a whole series of quality testing requirements.

  • Chapters <1000 = Mandatory: must be complied with
  • Chapters ≥1000 = Informational: for reference only unless explicitly cited by a monograph

Three-tier structure:

  • Universal Tests: basic tests that all injections must undergo
  • Specific Tests: additional tests to be considered depending on the dosage form
  • Dosage Form-Specific Tests: the complete test list for each dosage form (a combination of Universal and Specific tests)

Analogy

The tiered system of a driving test:

Think of USP ⟨1⟩ as the general rules of a driving test set by the transport authority. Whether you are testing for a car, a motorcycle or a bus license, there are "common subjects" (the written exam and the basic road-test items); these are the Universal Tests. But if you are testing for a bus license, there are additional special subjects (reversing into a bay, S-curves); these are the Specific Tests. And the complete exam list for each vehicle type is the Dosage Form-Specific Tests.

Key Point: CDMO Practice

Practical impact of mandatory vs. informational: In a CDMO setting, this distinction is critical. When a client says "my product needs to comply with USP ⟨1⟩", your QC team must perform all Universal Tests and decide on the Specific Tests according to the dosage form. This is not a recommendation; it is a regulatory requirement.

The special nature of biologics: The original text notes that biologics may not fully fit the definitions in ⟨1⟩, but if the monograph title includes "Injection", compliance is still required. When a CDMO takes on a biologics client, it needs to confirm carefully the specific scope of the monograph's references.

Universal Tests: Tests Applicable to All Products

Original Text

Universal tests are listed below and are applicable to parenteral dosage forms.

Identification

Identification tests are discussed in General Notices, 5.40. Identification should establish the identity of the drug or drugs present in the article and should discriminate between compounds of closely related structure that are likely to be present.

Assay

A specific and stability-indicating test should be used to determine the strength (content) of the drug product. In cases where the use of a nonspecific assay is justified, other supporting analytical procedures should be used to achieve overall specificity. A specific procedure should be used when there is evidence of excipient interference with the nonspecific assay.

Impurities

Tests for Impurities are discussed in General Notices, 5.60 Impurities and Foreign Substances. All articles should be tested to ensure that they meet the requirements.

Foreign and Particulate Matter

Articles intended for parenteral administration should be prepared in a manner designed to exclude particulate matter as defined in Subvisible Particulate Matter in Therapeutic Protein Injections ⟨787⟩, Particulate Matter in Injections ⟨788⟩, or Subvisible Particulate Matter in Intraocular Solutions ⟨789⟩, as well as excluding other foreign matter as appropriate for the dosage form.

Each final container of all parenteral preparations should be inspected to the extent possible for the presence of observable foreign and particulate matter (hereafter termed visible particulates) in its contents. The inspection process should be designed and qualified to ensure that every lot of all parenteral preparations is essentially free from visible particulates, as defined in Visible Particulates in Injections ⟨790⟩.

Every container in which the contents show evidence of visible particulates must be rejected.

Large-volume injections for single-dose infusion, small-volume injections, and pharmacy bulk packages (PBPs) are subject to the light obscuration or microscopic procedures and limits for subvisible particulate matter set forth in ⟨788⟩, unless otherwise specified.

Sterility

The sterility of all drug products intended for parenteral administration should be confirmed by the use of methods described in Sterility Tests ⟨71⟩ or by an approved alternative method.

Bacterial Endotoxins

All articles intended for parenteral administration should be prepared in a manner designed to limit bacterial endotoxins as defined in Bacterial Endotoxins Test ⟨85⟩ or Pyrogen Test ⟨151⟩.

Container Content

Container contents should be determined when appropriate (see Container Content for Injections ⟨697⟩).

Packaging Systems

The packaging system should not interact physically or chemically with the preparation to alter its strength, quality, or purity beyond the official or established requirements. The packaging system should meet the requirements in Elastomeric Components ⟨381⟩, Packaging and Storage Requirements ⟨659⟩, Containers — Glass ⟨660⟩, Plastic Packaging Systems ⟨661⟩, Plastic Materials of Construction ⟨661.1⟩, and Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use ⟨661.2⟩.

Container–Closure Integrity

The packaging system should be closed or sealed in such a manner as to prevent contamination or loss of contents. Validation of container integrity must demonstrate no penetration of microbial contamination or gain or loss of any chemical or physical parameter deemed necessary to protect the product (see Package Integrity Evaluation ⟨1207⟩, ⟨1207.1⟩, ⟨1207.2⟩, and ⟨1207.3⟩).

Labeling

All articles intended for parenteral administration should meet the labeling requirements defined in Labeling ⟨7⟩.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The pre-flight safety checklist:

Universal Tests are like the "standard checklist" before an aircraft takes off. Whether it is a Boeing 737 or an Airbus A380, certain basic items must be completed: the engines work (Assay), the airframe is correctly identified (Identification), there are no contaminants in the fuel (Impurities), the cabin is sealed (CCI), and the passengers are safe (Sterility). Skip any one item and the plane cannot take off; likewise, skip any one Universal Test and the batch cannot be released.

Key Point: The Four Lines of Defense Against Particulate Matter

⟨1⟩ is particularly strict about particulate matter and establishes a four-layer testing system:

  • ⟨787⟩: subvisible particulate matter in protein injections (specific to therapeutic proteins)
  • ⟨788⟩: subvisible particulate matter in injections in general (light obscuration / microscopy)
  • ⟨789⟩: subvisible particulate matter in ophthalmic solutions
  • ⟨790⟩: visual inspection standard for visible particulates

CDMO practice: Your 100% visual inspection process must be qualified, and "any container with visible particulates must be rejected". This is a zero-tolerance standard.

Key Point: Container-Closure Integrity (CCI)

The importance of the ⟨1207⟩ series: CCI has been a focus of regulators in recent years. The original text requires that validation demonstrate "no microbial ingress" and "no increase or decrease in chemical or physical parameters".

This means you cannot rely on microbial challenge testing alone. You also need to show that the container does not allow gas leakage (which affects the vacuum in lyophilized products) or moisture ingress (which affects the stability of the lyophilized cake).

Specific Tests: Dosage Form-Specific Tests

Original Text

In addition to the Universal Tests listed above, the following specific tests may be necessary depending on the dosage form.

Uniformity of Dosage Units

This test is applicable for parenteral drug products and dosage forms packaged in single-unit containers. It includes both the mass of the dosage form and the content of the active substance in the dosage form (see Uniformity of Dosage Units ⟨905⟩).

Vehicles and Added Substances

Aqueous vehicles: Aqueous vehicles must meet the requirements of ⟨151⟩ or ⟨85⟩, whichever is specified in the monograph. Water for Injection is generally used as the vehicle. Sodium chloride or dextrose may be added to render the resulting solution isotonic.

Nonaqueous vehicles: Fixed oils are classified under nonaqueous vehicles. Fixed oils used as vehicles are of vegetable origin and are odorless. They must meet specific requirements:

  • Saponification Value: Between 185 and 200
  • Iodine Value: Between 79 and 141
  • Unsaponifiable Matter: NMT 1.5%
  • Acid Value: NMT 0.2
  • Peroxide Value: NMT 5.0
  • Water content (Method Ic): NMT 0.1%
  • Limits for Copper, Iron, Lead, and Nickel per ⟨232⟩/⟨233⟩

Added substances: Suitable substances may be added to increase stability or usefulness unless proscribed in the monograph. No coloring agent may be added solely for the purpose of coloring the finished preparation. Special care should be observed for preparations exceeding 5 mL:

  • Mercury and cationic surface-active agents: NMT 0.01%
  • Chlorobutanol, cresol, phenol: NMT 0.5%
  • Sulfur dioxide or equivalent sulfite/bisulfite: NMT 0.2%

Antimicrobial Preservatives

Antimicrobial agents must be added to preparations intended for injection that are packaged in multiple-dose containers unless: (1) the monograph directs otherwise; (2) the substance contains a radionuclide with a physical half-life of less than 24 h; or (3) the active ingredients are themselves antimicrobial. Substances must meet the requirements of ⟨51⟩ and ⟨341⟩.

Water Content

The water content of freeze-dried (lyophilized) products should be determined when appropriate (see ⟨921⟩).

Aluminum Content

See Labeling ⟨7⟩ for information related to specific labeling requirements associated with aluminum content in LVIs, SVIs, and PBPs used in Parenteral Nutrition (PN) Therapy.

Completeness and Clarity of Solutions

The following tests are performed to demonstrate suitability of constituted solutions prepared before administration:

  • The solid dissolves completely, leaving no undissolved matter.
  • The constituted solution is not significantly less clear than an equal volume of the diluent or of purified water.
  • The constituted solution is free from particulate matter that can be observed on visual inspection (see ⟨790⟩).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The "On-Demand Trigger" Logic of Specific Tests

Specific Tests are not "always applicable" the way Universal Tests are. They are additional tests that are "activated on demand" according to product characteristics. The key triggers are:

  • Single-dose container → Uniformity of Dosage Units ⟨905⟩ is required
  • Multiple-dose container → a preservative must be added and the product must pass the ⟨51⟩ test
  • Lyophilized product → Water Content ⟨921⟩ must be tested
  • Nonaqueous vehicle → fixed oils must meet 7 quality criteria
  • Constituted product → Completeness and Clarity must be tested

Analogy

The "5 mL watershed" for added substances:

The original text sets stricter limits on added substances for injections larger than 5 mL. Why 5 mL? Because the larger the injected volume, the greater the total amount of the added substance the patient is exposed to. A 0.5% preservative is only 5 mg in a 1 mL injection, but in a 100 mL large-volume infusion it is 500 mg, a completely different toxicity risk.

It is like the concept of drug dose: a little alcohol is a flavoring, a lot is a poison.

Key Point: Preservative Requirements for Multiple-Dose Containers

"Must be added", not "may be added": The original text uses "must be added": multiple-dose injections must contain an antimicrobial preservative. Each withdrawal introduces a potential for microbial contamination, and the preservative is the last line of defense against in-use contamination.

Three exceptions: where the monograph provides otherwise, radiopharmaceuticals (half-life <24 h), and active ingredients that are themselves antimicrobial.

CDMO impact: If a client asks you to manufacture a multiple-dose injection, your formulation development team must perform the ⟨51⟩ Antimicrobial Effectiveness Test to confirm that the preservative is effective.

Key Point: The "No Added Colorants" Rule

USP ⟨1⟩ explicitly prohibits adding colorants to injections "solely for the purpose of coloring". This rule occasionally causes disputes in a CDMO setting, as some clients may want to distinguish products of different strengths by color. But USP's position is clear: injections neither need nor should rely on color for identification.

  2. Are nonaqueous injections (such as intramuscular injections using fixed oils) becoming more common in CDMO settings? What additional challenges do the special quality requirements of these products pose for the QC laboratory?
Container & Labeling Specifications

Original Text

Packaging Systems

The packaging system should not interact physically or chemically with the preparation to alter its strength, quality, or purity. The packaging system should meet the requirements in:

  • Elastomeric Components ⟨381⟩ — Rubber closures and plungers
  • Packaging and Storage Requirements ⟨659⟩
  • Containers — Glass ⟨660⟩
  • Plastic Packaging Systems ⟨661⟩
  • Plastic Materials of Construction ⟨661.1⟩
  • Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use ⟨661.2⟩

Further information regarding packaging systems testing may be found in Assessment of Extractables ⟨1663⟩ and Assessment of Drug Product Leachables ⟨1664⟩.

Container–Closure Integrity

Validation of container integrity must demonstrate no penetration of microbial contamination or gain or loss of any chemical or physical parameter deemed necessary to protect the product. See Package Integrity Evaluation ⟨1207⟩, ⟨1207.1⟩, ⟨1207.2⟩, and ⟨1207.3⟩.

Labeling

All articles intended for parenteral administration should meet the labeling requirements defined in Labeling ⟨7⟩.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: A USP Chapter Map for Packaging Compatibility

USP ⟨1⟩ triggers a whole chain of packaging test chapters. This is a complex area that CDMOs often face:

  • ⟨381⟩: elastomeric components (stoppers, syringe plungers)
  • ⟨660⟩: glass containers (Type I borosilicate, etc.)
  • ⟨661⟩/⟨661.1⟩/⟨661.2⟩: plastic packaging systems and materials
  • ⟨1663⟩/⟨1664⟩: assessment of extractables and leachables

Key principle: Packaging must not alter the strength, quality or purity of the drug product. This means your container-closure system needs a complete E&L (Extractables & Leachables) study.

Key Point: CCI Is an Inspection Focus in Recent Years

New expectations from the FDA and EU Annex 1: CCI testing technology has changed significantly over the past 10 years. The traditional blue dye ingress test is being replaced by deterministic methods:

  • Vacuum decay
  • High voltage leak detection (HVLD)
  • Helium leak detection
  • Laser-based headspace analysis

The ⟨1207⟩ series provides a framework for selecting and validating these methods. When choosing a CCI technology, a CDMO needs to consider the product form (liquid/lyophilized) and the container type (vial/prefilled syringe/ampoule).

Exercises for Reflection

  1. Which CCI test method does your CDMO use? Have you moved from probabilistic methods (such as dye ingress) to deterministic methods (such as vacuum decay)?
  2. If a client brings a prefilled syringe product, which packaging components does ⟨1⟩ require you to assess for compatibility?

Solutions

Original Text

A solution is a clear, homogeneous liquid dosage form that contains one or more chemical substances (e.g., drug substances or excipients) dissolved in a solvent (aqueous or nonaqueous) or a mixture of mutually miscible solvents.

Solutions intended for parenteral administration (e.g., by injection or for irrigation) must be sterile and biocompatible with the intended administration site. This includes consideration of factors such as tonicity, pH, pyrogenicity, extraneous particulate matter, and physicochemical compatibility, among others.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests are required for solutions for injection:

  • Universal Tests (all 9 categories)
  • Specific Tests: Antimicrobial Preservatives (if multi-dose)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Solutions Are the "Most Basic Injectable Dosage Form"

Solutions are the most common injectable dosage form in a CDMO. The definition emphasizes two key characteristics:

  • Clear: free of insoluble particles, optically transparent
  • Homogeneous: the drug is completely dissolved, with no phase separation

The quality considerations listed in the original text (tonicity, pH, pyrogenicity, particulate matter, compatibility) are items that your formulation development and process validation need to evaluate one by one.

Key Point: CDMO Practice

The test list for solutions is the most "streamlined": Basically only the Universal Tests are needed, with the preservative effectiveness test required in addition only for multiple-dose presentations. This makes solutions the dosage form with the lightest quality testing burden, but it also means that no Universal Test can be omitted.

Sterile Powders for Solutions (Constituted to a Solution)

Original Text

Sterile powders for solutions (also referred to as sterile powders for injection) consist of drug substances and other components as dry-formulation ingredients to ensure the chemical and physical stability of the presentation within a final-use container. Companion sterile diluent or diluent compartments may be provided to facilitate constitution to the desired final volume.

The sterile article for injection may be presented in several forms: lyophilized powder intended for final solution, powdered solids intended for final solution, or dry solids that form viscous liquids upon constitution.

The dosage form is a homogeneous solid that is readily constituted to the final form with the specified diluent, and dispersion is completed with gentle agitation.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests apply to sterile powders for injection:

  • Universal Tests
  • For constituted solutions:
    • ⟨905⟩: Uniformity of Dosage Units — assay on well-mixed constituted material, express results as delivered dose
  • For dry cake:
    • Loss on Drying ⟨731⟩: Amount of volatile matter driven off
    • Water Determination ⟨921⟩: Water or solvent content control
    • Appearance: Color of Dry Cake, Texture and Homogeneity, Presence of Foreign Material — all units with visible foreign material must be rejected

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The "Dual Identity" Testing of Lyophilized Powders

What makes a lyophilized product unique is that it has two states that need to be tested:

  • Dry state (dry cake): test appearance, water content and volatiles
  • After constitution (constituted solution): test uniformity of dosage units and clarity

This means your QC laboratory needs two different testing workflows: one performed immediately after lyophilization is complete (appearance, water content), and the other performed after constitution (content, particulate matter).

Analogy

Quality control of instant coffee:

Lyophilized powder is like high-quality instant coffee. You need quality control at two stages: (1) the coffee powder itself: is the color uniform? Is there any caking? Is the moisture content acceptable? (2) after brewing: has it dissolved completely? Is the color correct? Are there any undissolved particles floating?

If the coffee powder looks normal but there is sediment after brewing, that is a quality problem. Likewise, if the lyophilized cake looks perfect but there are insoluble particles after constitution, the batch fails.

Key Point: "Zero Tolerance" for Lyophilized Cake Appearance

"All units with visible foreign material must be rejected": any container of lyophilized product containing visible foreign matter must be rejected. This is a clear pass/fail criterion with no gray area.

CDMO impact: Your 100% visual inspection process must cover the appearance assessment of the lyophilized cake: color, texture, uniformity and foreign matter. This requires specially trained inspectors and qualified lighting/magnification conditions.

Exercises for Reflection

  1. In your CDMO, what range is the moisture specification of lyophilized products usually set at? Why is moisture content so critical to the stability of lyophilized products?
  2. What is the difference between ⟨731⟩ Loss on Drying and ⟨921⟩ Water Determination? Under what circumstances would you choose one over the other?
Suspensions

Original Text

Parenteral suspensions are liquid dosage forms that contain solid particles in a state of uniform dispersion. Suspensions for parenteral administration must be sterile and compatible with the administration site.

Consideration should be given to pH and pyrogenicity, and appropriate limits should be identified. Physical stability evaluations should include evaluations to confirm that the particle size range of suspended matter does not change with time and to confirm that the solids in the preparation can be readily resuspended to yield a uniform preparation.

The following tests are required for suspensions for injection unless otherwise justified:

  • Universal Tests
  • Specific Tests: Uniformity of Dosage Units, Antimicrobial Preservatives

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Two Additional Challenges of Suspensions

Compared with solutions, suspensions add two key quality control dimensions:

  • Particle size stability: particle size must not change over time (growth = aggregation; shrinkage = dissolution/degradation)
  • Resuspendability: after settling, the product must be easily shaken back to a uniformly dispersed state

These two requirements directly affect stability study design: your stability protocol must include time-point testing of particle size distribution and resuspendability.

Key Point: CDMO Setting

Uniform filling of suspensions is a manufacturing challenge: On the filling line, a suspension needs continuous agitation to maintain uniform dispersion. If agitation is insufficient, containers filled first may contain fewer solid particles and those filled later more, which directly causes failure of ⟨905⟩ Uniformity of Dosage Units.

This is why suspension filling requires special equipment design (such as a holding tank with agitation) and validated filling parameters.

Liposomes

Original Text

Liposomes are unique drug products with unique properties that can be either solutions or suspensions. Liposomes are aqueous dispersions of amphiphilic lipids and have low water solubility. They are organized as a bilayer sheet that encloses an internal aqueous compartment and are known as lipid bilayer vesicles.

Liposomes can have a single lipid bilayer (unilamellar vesicle) or can have an onion-like multilayered structure (multilamellar vesicle). The physical properties of the liposome and its bilayer can vary widely and depend on lipid composition, aqueous composition, and temperature relative to the acyl components’ phase transition points.

A liposome drug product consists of the drug substance, liposome components, and other inactive but critical ingredients such as an aqueous dispersion unless the contents are a lyophilized product.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests are required for liposomes:

  • Universal Tests
  • Specific Tests: Globule Size Distribution in Lipid Injectable Emulsions ⟨729⟩

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Unique Position of Liposomes

Liposomes are a pioneering technology among drug delivery systems. The original text specifically points out that they "can be solutions or suspensions"; this ambiguous classification reflects the complexity of liposomes.

  • Unilamellar: smaller, usually used for intravenous injection
  • Multilamellar: larger, with a multilayered structure like an onion

Special applicability of ⟨729⟩: Liposomes require ⟨729⟩ for particle size distribution. This chapter was originally designed for lipid emulsions, but USP extends it to liposomes because both involve lipid particles dispersed in an aqueous phase.

Key Point: CDMO Market Trends

The lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) used in COVID-19 mRNA vaccines (such as BNT162b2) have a structure similar to traditional liposomes. As mRNA therapies expand, CDMO demand for liposome/LNP manufacturing capability is growing rapidly. Understanding the test requirements ⟨1⟩ sets for liposomes is the foundation for entering this market.

Sterile Powders for Suspensions (Constituted to a Suspension)

Original Text

Sterile powders for suspensions consist of drug substances and other components as dry-formulation ingredients. Companion sterile diluent or diluent compartments may be provided to facilitate constitution to the desired final volume.

The sterile article for injection may be presented in several forms: lyophilized powder intended for final suspension, powdered solids intended for final suspension, and microparticles that retain their integrity and are delivered as a sterile suspension.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests apply to sterile powders for injection:

  • Universal Tests

Microparticles: Some microparticles are provided as a sterile powder to be reconstituted as a suspension before injection. For quality test requirements, please refer to Implants.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Difference from "Constituted to a Solution"

Although the names are similar, powders "constituted to a suspension" and powders "constituted to a solution" differ in one key quality requirement:

  • Constituted to a solution → the drug must dissolve completely; test "Completeness and Clarity"
  • Constituted to a suspension → the drug is uniformly dispersed as solid particles; test particle size and resuspendability

Special classification of microparticles: The original text points the test requirements for microparticles to the "Implants" section, because long-acting microparticle injectables (such as Lupron Depot) are closer to implants than to traditional injections in their quality testing logic.

Key Point

Long-acting injectables (LAIs) are a high-growth area for CDMOs in recent years. These products are far more complex to manufacture than ordinary solutions, and their QC test lists are also longer. Understanding the classification logic of ⟨1⟩ helps you quickly determine quality testing needs at the order evaluation stage.

Emulsions

Original Text

Emulsions for parenteral dosage forms are liquid preparations of drug substances dissolved or dispersed in a suitable emulsion medium. Oil-in-water or water-in-oil emulsions typically entrap the drug substance.

Emulsions typically are white, turbid, homogeneous liquid dosage forms. Emulsions intended for intravenous administration must be sterile and must be compatible with the intended administration site.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests are required for emulsions for injection:

  • Universal Tests
  • Specific Tests: ⟨729⟩ Globule Size Distribution

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Safety-Critical Parameters of Emulsions

The most critical quality test for intravenous emulsions (such as propofol and lipid nutrition emulsions) is ⟨729⟩ Globule Size Distribution. The reasons:

  • Oversized droplets (>5 µm) → may block pulmonary capillaries → fatal fat embolism
  • PFAT5 (the percentage of droplets larger than 5 µm) is the key limit indicator in USP ⟨729⟩

This is a test that bears directly on patient life safety, not a theoretical quality requirement.

Key Point

Water-in-oil vs. oil-in-water: Intravenous emulsions must be oil-in-water (O/W), because the external (continuous) phase must be an aqueous phase compatible with blood. Water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions cannot be used for intravenous injection and can only be used for intramuscular or subcutaneous injection.

Implants

Original Text

Implants for extended release consist of a matrix of drug substance and polymeric excipient that may or may not have an outer rate-controlling membrane. The polymeric excipient must be biocompatible but may or may not be bioresorbable.

Some implants are made from medical-grade metal with an osmotic pump inside that effects the extended release of the drug substance. Implants must be sterile and usually are formed in the shape of a cylinder.

Solvents used to dissolve the formulation can lead to sterilization, and thus the internal sterility test method should demonstrate that the sample preparation does not lead to sterilization of the test sample.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests are required for implants:

  • Universal Tests
  • Specific Tests: Uniformity of Dosage Units

In situ Gels

Sterile in situ gels are liquid preparations that are intended for injection into specific therapeutic targets. Typically they consist of polymers in organic solvents, and upon injection the solvents migrate away from the site, leaving a gelled mass.

Required tests: Universal Tests + Specific Tests (Antimicrobial Preservatives, Microparticles).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Sterility Testing Trap for Implants

The most subtle sentence in the original text is the warning about sterility testing: "the internal sterility test method should demonstrate that the sample preparation does not lead to sterilization of the test sample".

What does this mean? The organic solvents used in implants (such as NMP and DMSO) may themselves have a bactericidal effect. If your sterility test sample preparation step does not remove these solvents thoroughly, the test result will be a "false negative": the product looks sterile, but in fact the solvent has simply killed the microorganisms in the test medium.

Analogy

Testing for bacteria in disinfectant:

Imagine you want to test whether pool water contains harmful bacteria. But the pool water itself contains chlorine (a bactericide). If you add the water sample directly to the culture medium, the chlorine will kill all the bacteria in the medium, and you will get a "sterile" result, but that does not mean the water source is sterile. You must first neutralize the effect of the chlorine before you can perform a valid bacterial test. Sterility testing of implants faces exactly the same challenge.

Key Point: The Emerging Trend of In Situ Gels

In situ gels are an innovative dosage form that is liquid at the time of injection and turns into a gel in the body after injection. A CDMO taking on such products needs special sterile manufacturing capability, because the handling of organic solvents poses additional challenges to the cleanroom environment and to personnel safety.

  1. When your QC laboratory performs sterility testing of implants, do you have a dedicated sample preparation step to remove solvents that may be bactericidal? How do you validate the effectiveness of that preparation?
  2. What is the difference between biocompatible and bioresorbable? Why does ⟨1⟩ distinguish between these two concepts specifically?
Microparticles

Original Text

Injectable, resorbable microparticles for extended release generally range from 20 to 100 µm in diameter. They consist of drug substances embedded within a biocompatible, bioresorbable polymeric excipient, e.g., polyester excipients.

Microparticles are provided as a sterile powder in a vial or syringe. Just before intramuscular or subcutaneous administration, the microparticle powder should be suspended in an aqueous injection vehicle (diluent). The injection vehicle usually consists of water for injection, surfactant, and a viscosity enhancer, and the vehicle may contain a compound that adjusts osmolality and a compound that controls pH. The injection vehicle must be sterile and must be tested according to requirements for solutions intended for parenteral administration.

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests are required for microparticles for injection:

  • Universal Tests
  • Specific Tests: Uniformity of Dosage Units, Water Content

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The "Two-Component" Testing Logic of Microparticles

A microparticle injectable is a "two-component" system:

  • Component A: the microparticle powder (drug + polymer matrix): test Universal Tests + Water Content
  • Component B: the injection vehicle for constitution (WFI + surfactant + viscosity enhancer): must be tested independently as a solution

This means your QC release process needs to run complete quality testing on the two components separately before they can be released as a combination.

Key Point: The Significance of the 20-100 µm Size

The size range of microparticles (20-100 µm) is not chosen arbitrarily:

  • <20 µm → may be engulfed by macrophages, shortening the duration of efficacy
  • >100 µm → blocks the needle during injection, or causes severe local reactions at the injection site
  • 20-100 µm → the optimal range, which both avoids clearance and allows smooth injection

Particle size control is one of the most critical process parameters in microparticle manufacturing.

Drug-Eluting Stents

Original Text

Drug-eluting stents are tiny metal or polymer scaffolds used to keep arteries open following a medical intervention; the drug substance is incorporated into or onto the stent platform. Drug-eluting stents typically have two components of testing:

  • (1) Functional tests — generally ASTM International methods that fall outside the scope of this chapter
  • (2) Analytical tests — covered by this chapter

Unless otherwise justified, the following tests are required for drug-eluting stents:

  • Universal Tests
  • Specific Tests:
    • Uniformity of Dosage Units — content of active substance applicable for single-unit containers. With appropriate justification, the number of stents tested may be fewer than recommended in ⟨905⟩.
    • Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vivo ⟨88⟩

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Dual Regulation of Drug-Device Combination Products

The drug-eluting stent is a typical drug-device combination product. It is subject to both:

  • Drug regulations: drug content, uniformity of dosage units, sterility (under USP ⟨1⟩)
  • Medical device regulations: mechanical performance, biocompatibility (ASTM + ⟨88⟩)

Unique applicability of ⟨88⟩: Drug-eluting stents are the only dosage form in ⟨1⟩ that explicitly requires the ⟨88⟩ Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vivo. This is because stents are implanted in the body long term, so it must be shown that the materials do not cause adverse biological reactions.

Key Point: The Flexibility in ⟨905⟩

The original text specifically notes that "with appropriate justification, the number of stents needed for this test may be fewer than the recommended number". This is a rare flexibility in USP on sampling quantities for ⟨905⟩. The reason is that each stent is a high-value, precision-manufactured product, and the number of samples used in destructive testing must balance quality assurance against economics.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: How to Use This Matrix

This matrix is the "quick reference sheet" of a CDMO QC manager. When a new product enters your facility, you need to:

  1. Confirm the product's dosage form (find the correct column)
  2. Scan vertically for all "Y" items: this is your complete release test list
  3. Confirm the special conditions (the notes for * / ** / †)
  4. Look up the corresponding USP chapter methods

Analogy

Ordering from a set menu vs. add-ons:

Universal Tests are like the restaurant's "set menu": every guest (every dosage form) receives it. Specific Tests are like "add-on items", added according to the guest's particular needs (the dosage form's characteristics). Some guests need only the set menu (Solutions), while others need several additional dishes (Lyophilized Powders).

Key Point: The CDMO Quality Agreement

In a CDMO setting, this matrix should become the basis for the Quality Agreement you sign with the client. The list of "release test items" in the Quality Agreement should include at least all the "Y" items for that dosage form in this matrix. A client may ask for additional tests, but may not remove the required items that ⟨1⟩ calls for.
