Section 1 — Purpose, Key Terms & Scope

Original Text

PURPOSE

This general information chapter presents a framework for the design, justification, and execution of an extractables assessment for pharmaceutical packaging and delivery systems. The chapter establishes critical dimensions of an extractables assessment and discusses practical and technical aspects of each dimension.

Although intended to be helpful and generally applicable, the chapter is for informational purposes and does not establish specific extraction conditions, analytical procedures, or mandatory extractables specifications and acceptance criteria for particular packaging and delivery systems or dosage forms.

Designing an individual extractables assessment is a process that balances sound science, prudent resource allocation, and effective risk management. Achieving this balance is the responsibility and obligation of the drug product manufacturer.

KEY TERMS (Selected)

Packaging System (also referred to as a container–closure system): The sum of packaging components and materials that together contain and protect the product.

Extractables: Organic and inorganic chemical entities that are released from a pharmaceutical packaging/delivery system, packaging component, or packaging material of construction and into an extraction solvent under laboratory conditions. Extractables themselves, or substances derived from extractables, have the potential to leach into a drug product under normal conditions of storage and use and thus become leachables.

Leachables: Foreign organic and inorganic chemical entities that are present in a packaged drug product because they have leached into the packaged drug product from a packaging/delivery system under normal conditions of storage and use or during accelerated drug product stability studies. Leachables are typically a subset of extractables or are derived from extractables.

Migrants: Also foreign chemical entities in the packaged drug product, but differentiated from leachables in that migrants accumulate in the drug product after crossing a physical barrier (e.g., primary and secondary packaging). Thus migrants are derived from secondary and tertiary packaging and ancillary components.

Safety Concern Threshold (SCT): The threshold below which a leachable has a dose so low that it presents negligible safety concerns from carcinogenic and noncarcinogenic toxic effects.

Analytical Evaluation Threshold (AET): The threshold at or above which a leachable should be characterized and reported for toxicological assessment. The AET can be mathematically derived from the SCT based on dosing parameters of the drug product.

SCOPE

The scientific principles and best-practices are intended to apply to any extractables assessment or extraction study of pharmaceutical packaging/delivery systems, packaging components, or materials of construction. Applications include:

  • Characterization, selection, and qualification of materials of construction, packaging components, or packaging/delivery systems
  • Establishing leachables–extractables correlations
  • Simulating worst-case drug product leachables profiles
  • Materials of construction and functional components of manufacturing equipment (e.g., filters, tubing, tanks)
  • Materials of construction for the medical device component of a combination product

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The E&L Triangle

USP 1663 establishes the foundational framework for extractables assessment. It is essential to understand how its three core concepts relate to one another:

  • Extractables: chemical substances that are "forced out" of packaging materials under laboratory-enhanced conditions.
  • Leachables: substances that actually migrate into the drug product under real storage and use conditions. Leachables are a subset of extractables.
  • Migrants: substances that cross a physical barrier (such as the outer packaging) into the drug product; their source is the secondary or tertiary packaging.

Key logic: extractables are potential leachables; extractables are studied to predict the worst case for leachables.

Analogy — Squeezing the Sponge

Think of a packaging material as a sponge soaked with chemicals.

An extractables study is like "wringing the sponge dry": in the laboratory, enhanced conditions such as solvents and high temperature are used to force out everything that could possibly be released, giving a complete list.

A leachables study instead observes how much the sponge naturally releases during normal use (stored in the drug product for 2 years).

The logic: first learn the worst case (wringing it dry), then assess whether the normal case (natural leaching) falls within safe limits.

Key Point — Nature and Regulatory Status of the Chapter

USP 1663 is an informational chapter, not a mandatory chapter (such as USP 661, 661.1 and 661.2). It provides a scientific framework and best practices, but does not specify extraction conditions or acceptance criteria.

This means that designing the individual assessment program is the responsibility of the drug product manufacturer, who must balance science, regulatory requirements and risk management.

The Relationship Between SCT and AET

The SCT (Safety Concern Threshold) is a toxicological concept, defined on the basis of patient exposure (µg/day). Below this value, no carcinogenic or non-carcinogenic toxicity assessment is needed.

The AET (Analytical Evaluation Threshold) is a laboratory working concept, derived from the SCT (taking dosing parameters into account) and expressed in measurable concentration units (µg/mL). The AET tells the analyst which chemicals must be identified and reported to the toxicologist.

In short: the SCT is the end goal, and the AET is the starting point for laboratory work.

Practical Application — CDMO Material Qualification

In CDMO fill-finish operations, when a client brings a new container closure system (such as the rubber plunger of a prefilled syringe), the USP 1663 framework provides a systematic assessment approach:

  1. Ask the supplier for the material formulation (composition data) as prior knowledge for the extractables study
  2. Design the simulating extraction media based on the pH and solvent characteristics of the drug product
  3. Link to USP 1664 to confirm that the extractables assessment results can be used to predict the leachables in the actual drug product

This process directly supports the CDMO change control procedure and packaging material supplier audits.

Section 2 — Background Information & Uses of Extractables Assessment

Original Text

BACKGROUND INFORMATION

During the course of manufacturing, packaging, storage, distribution, and administration; dosage forms and their constituents can contact components and materials of construction of manufacturing and packaging equipment, and primary and secondary packaging components and systems. Leachables, which can include both organic and inorganic chemical entities with wide chemical diversity, are of concern due to their potential safety risk to patients and potential compatibility risks for the drug product (e.g., drug substance interaction/degradation, pH change, appearance change, particle formation, protein aggregation/structure change, etc.).

In order to assess these risks and manage the potential issues posed by leachables, it is necessary to know the identities and the levels to which leachables will accumulate in the finished drug product over its shelf-life. These two pieces of information can be used to establish the magnitude of patient exposure (dose) and therefore the safety risk posed by an individual leachable, as well as the likelihood of any compatibility issues involving the drug product.

Uses of Extractables Assessments

Extractables assessments can be used to:

  • Characterize packaging/delivery systems, packaging components, combination product medical device components, manufacturing components, and their various materials of construction
  • Facilitate the timely development of safe and effective dosage form packaging/delivery systems, manufacturing systems and processes by assisting in the selection of components and materials of construction
  • Understand the effects of various manufacturing processes (e.g., sterilization) on packaging components and their potential leachables
  • Establish the worst-case potential leachables profile in a manner which facilitates leachables studies, the development of leachables specifications and acceptance criteria (should these be required), and the safety evaluation/qualification of potential and actual leachables
  • Establish the worst-case potential leachables profile in a manner which facilitates the safety evaluation/qualification of probable leachables when it is not scientifically possible to determine actual leachables
  • Facilitate the assessment of patient exposure to chemical entities resulting from direct contact between a patient's body tissue(s) and a packaging or combination product medical device component
  • Facilitate the establishment of qualitative and quantitative leachables–extractables correlations
  • Facilitate the development of extractables specifications and acceptance criteria (if required) for packaging components and materials of construction
  • Facilitate investigations into the origin(s) of identified leachables whose presence causes quality and/or safety issues (such as out-of-specification results) for a marketed product

In these ways, extractables assessments can support Quality by Design (QbD) principles for the development and manufacture of pharmaceutical packaging/delivery systems and drug products.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Why Is an Extractables Assessment Needed?

Contact between a drug product and its packaging is an unavoidable physical reality. From manufacturing, filling and sterilization through to storage, the drug product is in contact with packaging materials throughout. The question is not "will substances migrate?" but "how much has migrated, and is it safe?"

The fundamental purpose of an extractables assessment: to establish a list of chemical substances that could leach into the drug product and to assess their safety. This list must be completed before the drug product is actually marketed, rather than investigated only after a problem appears.

Analogy — Drug Product Compatibility Is Like a Chemical Marriage

The relationship between a drug product and its packaging is like a "chemical marriage" lasting 2–3 years. During that time, the chemical components of both sides influence each other:

  • Plasticizers in the packaging material may leach into the drug solution
  • The pH of the drug solution may accelerate the aging of rubber
  • The sterilization process may change the chemical properties of the material and produce new degradation products

An extractables assessment is the prenuptial agreement: understand the "chemical personalities" of both parties in advance and predict what problems may arise.

Key Point — The Three Most Important of the Nine Uses

From a CDMO practice perspective, the following three uses are the most common:

  1. Material selection and qualification: assess the packaging materials of a new supplier and confirm compatibility during the client's process technology transfer (tech transfer)
  2. Establishing an L-E correlation (leachables-extractables correlation): link the extractables study with the leachables data from drug product stability studies
  3. OOS investigation: when an unknown impurity peak appears in a stability study, use the extractables assessment results to trace its source

Practice Questions

  1. An injection vial of your client shows an unknown impurity peak in an 18-month stability study. Describe how you would use the results of the extractables assessment to conduct the OOS investigation.
  2. Why is "sterilization" specifically listed as a process whose effect on packaging materials needs to be evaluated? Give a specific example of a chemical change.

Practical Application — QbD Integration in a CDMO

Integrating the extractables assessment into the QbD process:

  • Design space definition: the extractables profile of the packaging material is part of the product design space and should be clearly defined in the QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile)
  • Risk assessment: use FMEA to evaluate the leachables risk level of each packaging component
  • Control strategy: establish packaging material release specifications based on extractables data, as an alternative to leachables monitoring

This integration can greatly reduce packaging-related OOS events after marketing.

Section 3 — Generating the Extract

Original Text

GENERATING THE EXTRACT — General Concepts

Extractables are derived from a variety of sources and exhibit extensive chemical diversity. Primary sources of extractables include:

  • Chemical additives in individual elastomeric/polymeric packaging components and raw materials, including impurities in these additives
  • Chemical entities and additives that are present in packaging components composed of glass and metals
  • Entities related to the dissolution of the packaging component itself (e.g., iron extracted from a stainless steel material, silicon extracted from glass)
  • Monomers and higher molecular weight oligomers derived from incomplete polymerization
  • Migrants from secondary and tertiary packaging components, such as inks, label adhesives, and volatiles from cardboard shipping containers, plastic storage bags, and other shipping aids such as wooden pallets
  • Surface residues, such as heavy oils and degreasing agents on metal canisters and containers
  • Chemical substances on the surfaces of component fabrication machinery (mold release agents, antistatic and antislip agents)

The means by which an extraction process is accomplished are reflected in the juxtaposition of several experimental parameters:

  • The chemical nature of the extracting media
  • The time duration of the extraction process
  • The temperature and pressure at which the extraction is performed
  • The stoichiometry of the extraction process (extracted surface area per unit volume of extracting solution)
  • The mechanism or process by which the extraction is accomplished

Chemical Nature of the Extracting Medium

Of all the parameters involved in generating the extract, the extracting medium is the most critical because it is the extracting medium that accomplishes the extraction, and all other parameters merely facilitate the extraction.

Strategically, if the purpose of a particular extraction study is to simulate a worst-case leachables profile, then the ideal situation is for the extracting solvent to have a similar or greater propensity to extract substances as the formulation. Therefore, the most logical tactic for this simulation study is to use the formulation itself as the extracting medium.

If the use of the drug product as the extracting solvent is not feasible, then the drug product vehicle, or placebo, could be used as an effective extracting medium.

Extraction Time and Temperature

Extraction time and temperature are critical factors. The combination of extraction time and temperature establishes the magnitude of the driving force and the degree to which equilibrium is actually achieved. In a simulating extraction study, the purpose of elevated temperature is to increase the extraction rate, so that a short experimental time may simulate longer leaching times.

Because extraction is a diffusion process, the relationship between the diffusion rate and temperature can be expressed empirically by the Arrhenius equation. Extractables profiles should be monitored for equilibrium or the attainment of asymptotic levels of extractables.

Extraction Stoichiometry

Extraction stoichiometry considers the physical mass and/or surface area of the test article relative to the volume of the extracting medium. Stoichiometry can be manipulated to facilitate production of a more concentrated extract.

In summary, extraction stoichiometry can be based on:

  • The known chemical ingredients in a component or material
  • Safety-based thresholds for drug product leachables
  • The known or determined sensitivities of analytical instrumentation

Mechanism of Extraction — Extraction Technique

Common laboratory extraction techniques include:

  • Maceration (solvent soaking): test article allowed to soak in organic or aqueous extracting solvent at temperatures below the solvent's boiling point
  • Reflux: test article immersed in boiling solvent
  • Soxhlet: test article in the "thimble" of a Soxhlet apparatus; extraction solvent is redistilled repeatedly
  • Sealed vessel: test article and extracting solvent sealed inside a container capable of withstanding elevated temperatures and pressures, placed into a laboratory autoclave
  • Instrument-based solvent extraction: pressurized fluid extraction, microwave-assisted extraction, supercritical fluid extraction
  • Sonication: test article and extracting solvent in a glass container partly immersed in ultrasonic bath

For volatile organic compounds, extraction techniques include headspace analysis (HD/GC), direct thermal desorption (TD/GC), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA/GC).

Core Concept — Priorities in Extraction Design

USP 1663 clearly states that the extracting medium is the most critical parameter, and that the other parameters (time, temperature and stoichiometric ratio) are "secondary".

The order of priority in selecting the extracting medium:

  1. First choice: use the actual drug product formulation itself as the extracting medium
  2. Second choice: use the placebo/vehicle as the extracting medium
  3. Third choice: use simulating solvents, which require scientific justification

Analogy — Arrhenius Accelerated Aging

Raising the extraction temperature is like "fast-forwarding a movie": a 2-year leaching process is completed in a few days.

The Arrhenius equation tells us that for every 10°C increase in temperature, the reaction rate roughly doubles (the Q10 effect). Therefore, extracting for 3 days at 40°C is equivalent to extracting for about 12 days at 25°C.

There is one important caution, however: the temperature must not be too high, otherwise the extraction conditions themselves change the chemical nature of the extractables (for example, thermal decomposition of some organic compounds) and produce false-positive results.

Formula and Concept — The Logic of the Stoichiometry Calculation

Target: the AET concentration in the extract should be ≥ 5 ng/µL (a typical GC/MS analytical requirement)

Example: designing an extraction study for the rubber stopper of a 5 mL intravenous vial
• Drug product volume: 5 mL/vial
• AET: 0.1 µg/mL (calculated from the SCT)
• Maximum possible leachable amount per vial: 0.1 µg/mL × 5 mL = 0.5 µg/vial

To ensure the extract concentration is ≥ 0.5 µg/vial (that is, ≥ 5 ng/µL in 100 mL of extract):
→ Extract 20 rubber stoppers in 100 mL of solvent
→ Concentration factor = 20-fold (more stringent than the actual drug product contact conditions)

This logic ensures that the extraction study is sensitive enough to detect any chemical above the AET.

Key Point — Applications of Non-Solvent Extraction

Non-solvent extraction methods (headspace/TD/TGA) must be considered in the following situations:

  • Dry powder inhaler (DPI) capsules: volatile substances may migrate from the capsule material into the powder
  • Oral solid drug products: volatile organic compounds from the label adhesive may penetrate the plastic bottle
  • Inhalation solutions in low-density polyethylene containers: volatile substances from wooden pallets may migrate through the plastic container

When evaluating a client's packaging system, a CDMO should not consider liquid extraction alone, but should also assess the migration risk of volatile substances.

Practical Application — Extraction Study Design Decisions in a CDMO

The decision process for designing an extraction study in actual CDMO client service:

  1. Understand the drug product characteristics: pH, organic solvent content, injection or inhalation product? (determines solvent selection)
  2. Confirm the purpose of the assessment: material characterization or simulation of the worst case for leachables? (determines extraction intensity)
  3. Set the extraction conditions: temperature (considering Arrhenius acceleration), time (monitor whether equilibrium is reached), stoichiometric ratio (ensure sufficient sensitivity)
  4. Select the extraction technique: soaking/reflux for solid or elastomeric materials; headspace for assessing volatile substances
Section 4 — Characterizing the Extract

Original Text

CHARACTERIZING THE EXTRACT — Objectives and Challenges

Once an extract has been generated, the next objective is to perform a thorough chemical characterization of the extract. The extract characterization phase of the extraction study must enable the realization of the overall goals of the extractables assessment.

The practical objective of extract characterization must therefore be an exercise of due diligence in the discovery, identification, and quantitation to a reasonable degree of scientific certainty of all individual extractable chemical entities present in an extract above a specified level or threshold.

1. SCOUTING

The most useful analytical techniques in a scouting exercise provide information regarding bulk chemical properties of organic and/or inorganic chemical entities present in an extract, which can be used to guide extractables discovery, identification, and quantitation. Scouting analysis is not capable in and of itself of realizing the practical objective of extract characterization.

Examples of scouting utility:

  • Significant levels of nonvolatile residue (NVR by gravimetric analysis) could suggest significant inorganic chemical entities
  • Significant UV absorbance suggests organic entities with UV chromophores (e.g., phenolic antioxidants)
  • Characteristic features in an FTIR spectrum provide more detailed insights into chemical classes of organic extractables
  • For aqueous extracts, total organic carbon (TOC) provides a measure of total organic extractables

2. DISCOVERY

The process of discovery involves detecting instrumental responses from individual organic and inorganic extractables proportional to their levels within the extract. Trace organic analysis typically involves:

  • GC (Gas Chromatography): enormous separating capability for volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds
  • HPLC (High-Performance Liquid Chromatography): most applicable to semi-volatile and relatively nonvolatile organic compounds

GC and HPLC are complementary and orthogonal for the significant chemical diversity of extractables. Inorganic extractables (trace elements and metals) are typically discovered, identified, and quantitated by atomic emission spectroscopy.

3. IDENTIFICATION

Identification of an extractable can be accomplished either by structural analysis or qualitative analysis. The analytical techniques most applicable involve the combination of chromatography with mass spectrometry: GC/MS and LC/MS.

Individual extractables identifications are designated in the categories of Confirmed, Confident, or Tentative:

  • Tentative: data consistent with a class of molecule only (e.g., fragmentation behavior only)
  • Confident: tentative identification bolstered by additional confirmatory information (e.g., molecular weight, elemental composition)
  • Confirmed: only via mass spectral and retention time match with an authentic reference compound

For inorganic extractables: elemental analysis provides element-specific identifications and quantitations, but not the chemical speciation of the extractable. For example, the safety impact of sulfur as elemental sulfur may be different than that of sulfur as sulfate.

4. QUANTITATION

Quantitation is typically based on the instrumental response of an individual extractable relative to an authentic reference compound. Calibration is accomplished by analysis of authentic reference compounds (external standards). One or more internal standards can also be included to increase accuracy and precision.

Preparation of Extracts for Analysis

A good "rule of thumb" is that in order to accomplish a complete structural analysis of an unknown extractable, a GC/MS requires approximately 5 ng injected into the instrument, suggesting a concentration of 5 ng/µL or 5 µg/mL.

If the analyte concentration is insufficient, the following parameters can be optimized:

  • Extraction stoichiometry (extract more material or use more extracting solvent)
  • Extraction conditions (higher temperatures, longer times, more aggressive techniques)
  • Extract processing (concentration of the extract)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Four-Step Characterization Process

The complete characterization of an extract is divided into four steps, each with different analytical tools and purposes:

  1. Scouting: use non-specific analyses (UV, FTIR, NVR, TOC) to obtain the overall chemical character and guide the direction of subsequent analysis. Optional but helpful.
  2. Discovery: use GC and HPLC to separate and detect the presence of individual chemical substances (instrument response signals).
  3. Identification: use GC/MS or LC/MS to confirm chemical structures. There are three confidence levels: Tentative, Confident and Confirmed.
  4. Quantitation: measure the concentration of each chemical substance and compare it with the AET.

Important: completing discovery alone does not mean the objective of the assessment has been met. The final objective must include qualitative identification (at least at the Confident level) and quantitative data for each chemical substance.

Analogy — Identification Confidence Levels Are Like Standards of Proof in Court

The confidence of the three identification levels is analogous to legal standards:

  • Tentative: only indirect evidence, such as "the mass spectral fragmentation pattern is consistent with a certain class of compounds". Like "circumstantial evidence" in court: it might be this person, but it cannot be certain.
  • Confident: several lines of supporting evidence, such as molecular weight confirmation and elemental composition, but no comparison with a reference standard. Like having "multiple eyewitnesses" but no DNA evidence.
  • Confirmed: the mass spectrum and retention time match completely with an authentic reference compound. Like a DNA match: conclusive beyond doubt.

Regulators generally expect substances above the AET to be identified to at least the Confident level; Confirmed is the highest standard and applies to substances that require a safety assessment.

Key Point — The Complementarity of GC and LC

GC and HPLC are complementary in extractables analysis, and must be used together to obtain a complete extractables profile:

  • GC strengths: volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds (antioxidants, plasticizers, residual monomers); coupled with MS, it allows searching of mass spectral libraries
  • HPLC strengths: non-volatile organic compounds (large-molecule additives, polymer oligomers); suitable for direct analysis of aqueous extracts

Using GC/MS alone will miss high-boiling/non-volatile substances; using HPLC alone will miss volatile substances. A high-quality extractables assessment must use both.

The Special Challenge of Inorganic Elemental Extractables

The analysis of inorganic extractables (metallic elements) has a unique challenge: atomic spectroscopy (AAS, ICP-AES, ICP/MS) can only tell you "how much sulfur is present", but cannot tell you the form in which the sulfur exists:

  • Elemental sulfur (S₈) — relatively low toxicity
  • Sulfate (SO₄²⁻) — generally acceptable
  • 2-Mercaptobenzothiazole (2-MBT) — has toxicity concerns and is a special case compound for OINDP

This means that for important inorganic extractables, additional speciation analysis may be needed to complete the safety assessment.

Practice Questions

  1. If, in a GC/MS analysis, you find a chromatographic peak and the mass spectral library search gives a 70% match to BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene), which identification level does this fall under? What additional steps do you need to raise it to the Confirmed level?
  2. An extract shows a strong absorption peak in a UV scan, yet neither GC/MS nor HPLC/MS detects any compound. What are the possible reasons, and how should you investigate?

Practical Application — Building CDMO Analytical Capability

Based on the USP 1663 framework, the E&L analytical platform of a CDMO should have the following core capabilities:

  • GC/MS (essential): for the discovery and identification of volatile and semi-volatile organic extractables
  • LC/MS or LC/UV (essential): for non-volatile organic extractables
  • ICP/MS or ICP-AES (essential): for inorganic elemental extractables
  • Headspace GC/MS (strongly recommended): for volatile organic compounds, especially in the assessment of inhalation or dry powder products
  • NMR (additional capability): provides structural confirmation and supplements MS data

A CDMO without these analytical capabilities should establish a partnership with an accredited third-party laboratory and clearly define the division of analytical responsibilities in the contract.

Section 5 — Summary: Assessing Completeness

Original Text

SUMMARY — Assessing the Completeness of an Extractables Assessment

The completeness of an extractables assessment can only be judged against the overall goals of the assessment. For example, an extractables assessment accomplished solely for materials characterization might include one extracting solvent, one extraction technique, and one set of extraction conditions; along with a materials-based threshold (e.g., 10 ppm w/w). Such an extractables assessment might be considered complete if all extractables above the defined threshold were identified to the confident level (defined above) and quantitated.

For an extractables assessment designed to establish a rigorous leachables–extractables correlation for a high risk drug product, where a challenging safety threshold might apply (e.g., 0.15 µg/day), good scientific practice and due diligence requires the following:

Generation of extracts should be accomplished with:

  • Multiple solvents or extracting media with varying extracting power based on the known extracting power of the drug product vehicle
  • Multiple and complementary extraction techniques, including those with the capability for volatiles analysis
  • Extraction conditions that allow equilibrium to be achieved

Characterization of extracts should use:

  • Multiple and complementary analytical techniques
  • Careful sample preparation, keeping the analytical technique(s) in mind
  • A systematic process for identification and quantitation of extractables

In this case, the extractables assessment might be considered complete if all extractables above the defined threshold were identified to at least the confident level, quantitated, and correlated both qualitatively and quantitatively with drug product leachables data (if available) and the known ingredients in the packaging system, packaging component(s), or material(s) of construction.

Example Extractables Profiles — Materials Characterization

Extractables profiles (GC/MS and HPLC/UV) from a hexane Soxhlet extract of a cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) material of construction. COC materials are used in the fabrication of pre-filled syringes, vials for small-volume parenterals, and bags for large-volume parenterals.

The extracts were generated by subjecting approximately 5 g of suitably sized material to 16 hours of Soxhlet extraction with 125 mL of hexane, adjusted relative to an extractables identification threshold of 10 ppm (µg/g).

The major peak in the HPLC/UV extractables profile above the materials-based threshold was the known additive (antioxidant) Irganox 1010 (Peak 2).

Key lesson: GC/MS and HPLC/UV are complementary and orthogonal, illustrating the concept that multiple analytical methods are required to typically elucidate the complete extractable profile.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Completeness Is Relative and Depends on the Purpose

An important philosophy of USP 1663: there is no absolute standard for the "completeness" of an assessment, only a standard relative to its purpose.

A comparison of two extreme scenarios:

  • Material characterization (low-risk purpose): 1 solvent + 1 technique + a material baseline value (10 ppm) → complete once the Confident identification level is reached
  • Establishing an L-E correlation for a high-risk drug product (high-risk purpose): multiple solvents + multiple complementary techniques + volatiles analysis + equilibrium conditions reached + SCT/AET baseline values → complete once all substances above the threshold are identified to the Confident level and correlated with the leachables data

Before accepting a client's E&L assessment, a CDMO must first confirm: what is the purpose of this assessment? The purpose determines the scope and depth of the assessment.

Analogy — Key Lessons from the COC Material Case

USP 1663 uses an actual case of Cyclic Olefin Copolymer (COC) material to illustrate a key fact:

GC/MS (which detects volatile substances) and HPLC/UV (which detects non-volatile substances) each found different compounds in the same extract. If only GC/MS had been run, the major antioxidant Irganox 1010 might have been missed; if only HPLC/UV had been run, volatile small-molecule additives might have been overlooked.

This case is like a jigsaw puzzle: each analytical technique sees only one part of the puzzle, and they must be combined to see the whole picture. This is why USP 1663 calls for the use of "multiple and complementary analytical techniques".

Key Point — The Structure of the USP Chapter System

The position of USP 1663 within the USP E&L chapter system:

  • USP 1663: extractables assessment framework (this chapter) — informational, guidance
  • USP 1664: leachables assessment framework — informational, guidance
  • USP 661/661.1/661.2: specific test methods for plastic packaging systems — mandatory
  • USP 381: testing of elastomeric components (rubber stoppers, etc.) — mandatory
  • USP 87/88: biological reactivity tests (in vitro/in vivo) — mandatory

Chapters 1663 and 1664 provide the scientific framework, while the 661 series and 381 provide the specific execution standards. The two are complementary, not substitutes for one another.

Practical Application — E&L Assessment Scope Confirmation Checklist

Before starting any extractables assessment, the CDMO should confirm the following key decision points with the client:

  1. Purpose of the assessment: material screening? Supplier qualification? Establishing an L-E correlation? OOS investigation?
  2. Drug product risk level: confirm the depth of the assessment according to the FDA table (injection and inhalation = highest risk)
  3. Applicable threshold: SCT = 0.15 µg/day (OINDP), or the corresponding threshold for other dosage forms?
  4. Packaging component information: can the supplier provide the material formulation (composition data)? This greatly improves the efficiency of the assessment
  5. Analytical resources: in-house analytical platform or outsourcing? Are all the necessary analytical techniques (GC/MS + LC/MS + ICP/MS) available?
  6. Completion criteria for the assessment: the identification confidence level the client expects (Confident or Confirmed)?

Chapter Summary

  • E&L definitions: extractables (laboratory-enhanced extraction) → potential leachables (leaching under actual storage/use conditions). Migrants come from substances migrating across a physical barrier.
  • Assessment framework: USP 1663 provides an informational framework; its core question is "if an assessment is needed, how should it be performed?" rather than prescribing when one is required.
  • The extracting medium is the most critical: use the actual formulation or placebo first; high-risk dosage forms must use multiple solvents (covering different polarities).
  • Four characterization steps: scouting → discovery → identification (Tentative/Confident/Confirmed) → quantitation.
  • Safety thresholds: the SCT (a toxicological concept, µg/day) is converted into the AET (the laboratory analytical threshold, µg/mL), which is the criterion for deciding whether further identification and reporting are needed.
  • Completeness depends on purpose: material characterization vs. establishing an L-E correlation require completely different degrees of rigor.
  • CDMO application: the USP 1663 framework directly supports packaging material qualification, supplier audits, client technology transfer and OOS investigation processes.
