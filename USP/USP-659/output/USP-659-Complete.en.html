Section 1: Introduction & General Packaging Requirements

Original Text

INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this chapter is to provide packaging definitions, auxiliary packaging information, and storage condition definitions relevant to the storage and distribution of active ingredients, excipients, and medical products, such as pharmaceuticals, combination products, and, when labeled as being USP compliant, dietary supplements.

PACKAGING

Packaging materials must not interact physically or chemically with a packaged article in a manner that causes its safety, identity, strength, quality, or purity to fail to conform to established requirements. Any plastic material used to construct a Packaging system must meet the applicable requirements of Plastic Materials of Construction 〈661.1〉. (Official 1-Dec-2025)

All Packaging systems must meet the applicable requirements specified in Containers—Glass 〈660〉, Plastic Packaging Systems and Their Materials of Construction 〈661〉, Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use 〈661.2〉, (Official 1-Dec-2025) and Auxiliary Packaging Components 〈670〉. All elastomeric closures must meet the applicable requirements in Elastomeric Components in Injectable Pharmaceutical Product Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈381〉.

Every monograph in USP–NF must have packaging and storage requirements. For active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), the choice would be a tight or well-closed container (as specified in the monograph), and, where needed, a light-resistant container. For excipients, a well-closed container is an appropriate default requirement.

Articles must be protected from moisture, freezing, and excessive heat (see General Definitions) when no specific directions or limitations are provided.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

USP <659> is the foundation chapter of the entire USP-NF packaging requirements framework. It establishes two core principles:

  • Compatibility principle: packaging materials must not interact physically or chemically with the drug product in a way that alters its safety, identity, strength, quality or purity (the five SIQOP elements)
  • Compliance linkage principle: <659> is itself an index chapter and must be applied together with its related chapters, such as <660>, <661>, <661.1>, <661.2>, <670> and <381>

Analogy

Think of packaging as the drug product's "protective armor": <659> states that the armor must not harm its wearer (the drug product), while the individual related chapters (<660> glass, <661> plastics, <381> elastomeric components) set the specific standards for each material. If any layer is missing, the armor is incomplete.

Key Notes

December 2025 update: <661.1> (Plastic Materials of Construction) and <661.2> (Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use) became official on December 1, 2025, with early adoption permitted. By default, an API uses a tight container, and an excipient, because it is packaged in bulk, uses a well-closed container.

Section 2: Poison Prevention Packaging Act (PPPA)

Original Text

POISON PREVENTION PACKAGING ACT

This act, which is administered by the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), requires special packaging for most human oral prescription drugs, oral controlled drugs, certain non-oral prescription drugs, certain dietary supplements, and many over-the-counter (OTC) drug preparations, to protect the public from personal injury or illness from misuse of these preparations [16 CFR §1700].

The primary packaging of substances regulated under the Poison Prevention Packaging Act (PPPA) must comply with the special packaging standards. These apply to all packaging types, including reclosable, non-reclosable, and unit-dose types.

Special packaging is not required for drugs dispensed within a hospital setting for inpatient administration. Also, special packaging does not need to be used by manufacturers and packagers of bulk-packaged prescription drugs that will be repackaged by the pharmacist. PPPA-regulated prescription drugs may be dispensed in non-Child-resistant packaging upon the request of the purchaser or when directed in a legitimate prescription.

Manufacturers or packagers of PPPA-regulated OTC preparations are allowed to package one size in non-Child-resistant packaging as long as popular-size, special packages are also supplied. The non-Child-resistant packaging requires special labeling.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The Poison Prevention Packaging Act (PPPA) is enforced by the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and requires child-resistant packaging. It applies to:

  • Oral prescription drugs and controlled substances
  • Some non-oral prescription drugs
  • Some dietary supplements
  • Many OTC drug products

Exemptions: drugs for hospitalized patients, bulk packages from which pharmacists repackage, and cases where the patient or prescriber requests otherwise.

Analogy

Child-resistant packaging is like a car's airbag: it does not get in the way in normal use and provides protection in an emergency. Adult patients (especially the elderly) who find it hard to open can request "non-child-resistant packaging" as permitted by law, which reflects a design balance between safety and convenience.

Key Notes

An OTC manufacturer may supply one size in non-child-resistant packaging, provided that the popular size is also supplied in child-resistant packaging. Non-child-resistant packaging must carry special labeling. Hospital inpatient drugs need no child-resistant packaging because they are administered by medical staff.

Section 3: General Definitions: Packaging

Original Text

GENERAL DEFINITIONS — Packaging Definitions

Packaging system (also referred to as a Container–closure system): The sum of Packaging components and materials that together contain and protect the article. This includes Primary packaging components as well as Secondary packaging components when such components are required to provide additional protection.

Container: A receptacle that holds an intermediate compound, API, excipient, or dosage form, and is in direct contact with the article (e.g., ampules, vials, bottles, syringes, and pen injectors).

Closure: A material that seals an otherwise open space of a Container and provides protection for the contents. It also provides access to the contents of the Container (e.g., screw caps and stoppers).

Packaging component: Any single part of the Package or Container–closure system, including: the Container; Closures; ferrules and overseals; Closure liners; Container liners; inner seals; administration ports; overwraps; administration accessories; labels; cardboard boxes; and shrink wrap.

Primary packaging component: A Packaging component that is in direct contact with or may come into direct contact with the article.

Secondary packaging component: A Packaging component that is in direct contact with a Primary packaging component and may provide additional protection for the article.

Tertiary packaging component: A Packaging component that is in direct contact with a Secondary packaging component and may provide additional protection for the article during transportation and/or storage.

Ancillary component: A component or entity that may come into contact with a Tertiary packaging component during the distribution, storage, and/or transportation of the packaged article (e.g., pallets, skids, and shrink wrap).

Associated component: A Packaging component that is typically intended to deliver the drug article to the patient but is not stored in contact with the article for its entire shelf life (e.g., spoons, Dosing cups, and dosing syringes).

Materials of construction: The materials (e.g., glass, plastic, elastomers, and metal) of which a Packaging component consists.

Small-volume injection (also referred to as Small-volume parenteral): An injectable dosage form that is packaged in Containers labeled as containing 100 mL or less.

Large-volume injection (also referred to as Large-volume parenteral): An injectable dosage form that is packaged in Containers labeled as containing more than 100 mL.

Hermetic container: A Container–closure system that is impervious to air or any other gas under the ordinary or customary conditions of handling, shipment, storage, and distribution.

Tight container: A Container–closure system that protects the contents from contamination by extraneous liquids, solids, or vapors; from loss of the article; and from efflorescence, deliquescence, or evaporation under the ordinary or customary conditions of handling, shipment, storage, and distribution, and is capable of tight reclosure. Where a tight container is specified, it may be replaced by a hermetic container for a single dose of an article.

Well-closed container: A Container–closure system that protects the contents from contamination by extraneous solids and from loss of the article under the ordinary or customary conditions of handling, shipment, storage, and distribution. See 〈671〉.

Light-resistant container: A Container–closure system that protects the contents from the effects of light by virtue of the specific properties of the material of which it is composed, including any coating applied to it. A clear and colorless or a translucent container may be made light-resistant by means of an opaque covering or by use of secondary packaging.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: The Packaging Hierarchy

USP <659> establishes a five-level packaging structure:

  • Primary: in direct contact with the drug product, such as glass ampuls, injection vials and rubber stoppers
  • Secondary: in contact with the primary packaging, such as outer cartons and blister foil
  • Tertiary: in contact with the secondary packaging, such as shipping cartons and trays
  • Ancillary: in contact with the tertiary packaging, such as pallets and shrink wrap
  • Associated: used for administering the drug but not for storage throughout its life, such as dosing cups and oral syringes

Analogy: Container Protection Levels

Imagine the four container types as buildings with different levels of weatherproofing:

  • Hermetic: a fully sealed submarine, impervious even to air
  • Tight: a mountaineering tent that keeps out water, dust and vapor, protecting against liquids, solids and vapors
  • Well-closed: an ordinary house, which protects against extraneous solids and loss of contents but need not keep out vapor
  • Light-resistant: a room designed to block light, whose main function is protection from light

Level of protection: hermetic > tight > well-closed. Light-resistant is an independent light-protection attribute that can be combined with any of the types.

Key Notes

Small vs. Large Volume Parenteral: SVP (≤100 mL) vs. LVP (>100 mL). This distinction matters greatly in product development, regulatory filing and manufacturing classification. All injection packaging must be confirmed for container-closure integrity; see the <1207> series of chapters.

Special provision for tight containers: where a monograph or specification calls for a tight container, a hermetic container may be used instead for a single-dose product.

Practice Questions

  1. Is the packaging for a 250 mL intravenous infusion bottle an SVP or an LVP? What is the practical significance of this classification?
  2. An API needs protection from moisture. Which type of container should be chosen? What additional requirement applies if it also needs protection from light?

Practical Application

In aseptic fill-finish, the choice of primary packaging (glass vials, rubber stoppers) directly affects product stability. For example, Type I borosilicate glass (meeting <660>) may be selected as the injection container, and the rubber stopper must pass the <381> elastomeric component compatibility tests. All of these requirements derive from the basic framework of <659>.

Section 4: Injection and Noninjection Packaging Systems

Original Text

Table 1. Packaging Systems Definitions: Injection versus Noninjection

Injection Packaging Systems

Multiple-dose container (also referred to as Multi-dose): A Container–closure system that holds a sterile medication for parenteral administration that has met antimicrobial effectiveness testing requirements. A Multiple-dose container is intended to contain more than one dose of a drug product. Multiple-dose containers are generally expected to contain 30 mL or less of medications. The beyond-use date for an opened or entered (e.g., needle-punctured) Multiple-dose container is 28 days unless otherwise specified by the manufacturer on the label.

Single-dose container: A Container–closure system that holds a sterile medication for parenteral administration that is not required to meet the antimicrobial effectiveness testing requirements. A Single-dose container is designed for use with a single patient as a single injection/infusion. Examples of Single-dose containers are vials, ampules, and prefilled syringes.

Pharmacy bulk package: A Container–closure system of a sterile preparation for parenteral use that contains many single doses. The contents are intended for use in a pharmacy admixture program and are restricted to the preparation of admixtures for infusion or, through a sterile transfer device, for the filling of empty sterile syringes. The Closure must be penetrated only one time after constitution. The Pharmacy bulk package is to be used only in a suitable work area such as a laminar flow hood.

Noninjection Packaging Systems

Multiple-unit container: A Container–closure system that permits withdrawal of successive portions of a noninjection article without changing the safety, strength, quality, or purity of the remaining portion (e.g., bottle of capsules, tablets, oral or topical liquids, and semisolids).

Single-unit container: A Container–closure system that holds a quantity of a noninjection article intended for administration as a single dose or a single finished device intended for use promptly after the Packaging system is opened.

Unit-dose container: A single-unit Container–closure system for an article intended for administration by other than the parenteral route as a single dose.

Unit-of-use container: A Container–closure system that contains a specific quantity of an article that is intended to be dispensed as such without further modification except for the addition of appropriate labeling. It is not permitted to repackage Unit-of-use containers for sale.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Classification of Injection Packaging

The core differences in injection packaging lie in the antimicrobial requirement and repeated use:

  • Multiple-dose: must pass Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing, has a 28-day period of use after opening (unless the label states otherwise), and usually has a volume of ≤30 mL
  • Single-dose: Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing is not required; it is used only once, any remainder after opening must be discarded, and the volume limit is 1 L (with specific exemptions)
  • Pharmacy bulk package: contains multiple single doses but is restricted to pharmacy compounding use; the closure is penetrated only once; exempt from the 30 mL limit

Analogy: Classification of Noninjection Packaging

Compare the noninjection container types to food packaging:

  • Multiple-unit: a large jar of cookies, which can be taken from repeatedly without affecting the quality of the rest
  • Single-unit: an individually wrapped candy, which is opened and used up entirely
  • Unit-dose: a single-use pack prepared by the pharmacy, a single dose for a route of administration other than parenteral
  • Unit-of-use: a bottle with a pre-counted quantity, dispensed by the pharmacist directly to the patient without repackaging (repackaging for sale is prohibited)

Key Notes

The 28-day rule for multiple-dose containers: this is the USP default, and the manufacturer may specify a different period on the label (shorter or longer, supported by data). If a multiple-dose container is not used up within 28 days of opening, it must be discarded even if it looks normal.

No repackaging of unit-of-use containers: this requirement protects the integrity of the original packaging and ensures that the patient receives the correct dose. Pharmacists may not open a unit-of-use container and repackage its contents for sale.

Practice Questions

  1. A sterile heparin sodium injection is used in an ICU after opening, and some remains on day 25. How should it be handled under USP?
  2. What is the biggest difference between a pharmacy bulk package and a multiple-dose container? In which situations is each type used?

Practical Application

In aseptic filling, the choice between a single-dose and a multiple-dose packaging system has a major impact on process design: a multiple-dose product needs an added antimicrobial preservative (such as benzyl alcohol or thimerosal) and must complete <51> Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing. This decision must be made at the very beginning of new product development.

Section 5: Injection Packaging Requirements & Associated Components

Original Text

INJECTION PACKAGING

Packaging for sterile products intended for injection must be validated as meeting the containment, protection, and compatibility requirements that are essential for maintaining the article's strength, quality, and purity. Refer to Package Integrity Evaluation—Sterile Products 〈1207〉, Package Integrity Testing in the Product Life Cycle—Test Method Selection and Validation 〈1207.1〉, Package Integrity Leak Test Technologies 〈1207.2〉, and Package Seal Quality Test Technologies 〈1207.3〉 for further information regarding sterile product container–closure integrity testing and validation.

The volume of injection in a Single-dose container provides the amount specified for one-time parenteral administration, and in no case is more than sufficient to permit the withdrawal and administration of 1 L. Preparations intended for intraspinal, intracisternal, or peridural administration are packaged in Single-dose containers only.

Unless otherwise specified in the individual monograph, a Multiple-dose container contains a volume of injection sufficient to permit the withdrawal of NMT 30 mL.

The following injections are exempt from the 1-L restriction:

  • Injections packaged for extravascular use as irrigation solutions or peritoneal dialysis solutions
  • Injections packaged for intravascular use as parenteral nutrition or as replacement or substitution fluid to be administered continuously during hemofiltration

ASSOCIATED COMPONENTS

Many Associated Components are graduated for measurement and dose administration. The markings on associated components must be in metric units only and limited to a single measurement scale that corresponds with the dosing instructions on the OTC or prescription container label. Under expected conditions of use, the volume error incurred in measuring an individual dose by means of such graduated components should be NMT 10% of the indicated amount.

Dosing cup: A measuring device consisting of a small cup that may be packaged with oral liquid articles.

Dosing spoon: A measuring device consisting of a bowl and handle that may be packaged with oral liquid articles. The handle may be a graduated tube.

Medicine dropper: A measuring device consisting of a transparent or translucent barrel or tube that is generally fitted with a collapsible bulb.

Oral syringe: A measuring device consisting of a plunger and barrel made of transparent or translucent plastic material and a seal on the end. It may be packaged with oral liquid articles.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Volume Limits for Injections

The volume limits for injection packaging reflect a "patient safety" design:

  • Single-dose maximum volume of 1 L: prevents a large-volume preparation from being given in full in one administration by mistake
  • Multiple-dose maximum volume of 30 mL: limits the number of withdrawals from a multiple-dose container and reduces the risk of contamination
  • Intraspinal/epidural administration: must use single-dose containers, the highest level of safety
  • Exemptions: irrigation solutions, peritoneal dialysis solutions and hemofiltration replacement solutions may exceed 1 L for therapeutic reasons

Analogy: Accuracy of Associated Dosing Devices

A dosing cup is like a measuring spoon in cooking: it must be accurate to ensure efficacy. USP requires a measurement error of NMT 10%, which means that for a 10 mL dose the error may not exceed ±1 mL. Only one scale is permitted, to prevent patients from misreading units (for example, mistaking mL for tsp).

Key Notes

CCI testing obligation: all injection packaging must be verified for container closure integrity (CCI), and appropriate test methods (leak testing, seal quality testing, etc.) are selected in accordance with the <1207> series of chapters. CCI is a shared requirement of ICH Q6A, FDA's aseptic processing guidance and PIC/S Annex 1.

Labeling requirements for associated components: the graduations must be in metric units (mL), and two units (for example, both mL and tsp) are not permitted. This is a pediatric safety measure that FDA has enforced since 2020.

Practice Questions

  1. A manufacturer wants to produce a 2 L intravenous dextrose injection (for TPN parenteral nutrition). Does this comply with the USP volume limits?
  2. A pediatric oral suspension is supplied with a dosing cup marked with both mL and tablespoon (tsp) graduations. Does this comply with USP <659>?
Section 6: Temperature and Storage Definitions

Original Text

TEMPERATURE AND STORAGE DEFINITIONS

Freezer: A place in which the temperature is controlled between −25° and −10° (−13° and 14° F). It is noted that, in some instances, articles may have a recommended storage condition below −20° (−4° F). In such cases, the temperature of the storage location should be controlled to ±10° of the recommended storage condition.

Refrigerator: A cold place in which the temperature is controlled between 2° and 8° (36° and 46° F).

Cold: Any temperature not exceeding 8° (46° F).

Cool: Any temperature between 8° and 15° (46° and 59° F). [Note—An article for which storage in a cool place is directed may, alternatively, be stored and shipped as refrigerated, unless otherwise specified by the individual monograph.]

Room temperature (also referred to as Ambient temperature): The temperature prevailing in a working environment.

Controlled cold temperature: The temperature maintained thermostatically between 2° and 8° (36° and 46° F), which allows for temperature excursions between 2° and 15° (36° and 59° F) that may be experienced during storage, shipping, and distribution, but not to exceed 24 h, such that the allowable calculated mean kinetic temperature (MKT) is NMT 8° (46° F) with no excursions below 2° (36° F) or above 15° (59° F). Additionally, controlled cold excursions may only occur one time during the possession of the product within the supply chain.

Controlled room temperature: The temperature maintained thermostatically that encompasses the usual and customary working environment of 20°–25° (68°–77° F). MKT may be used during an excursion provided: 1) MKT does not exceed 25° (77° F); 2) excursion between 15° and 30° (59° and 86° F); 3) transient excursions are NMT 40° (104° F); and 4) excursion time is NMT 24 h.

Warm: Any temperature between 30° and 40° (86° and 104° F).

Excessive heat: Any temperature above 40° (104° F).

Dry place: A place that does not exceed 40% average relative humidity at 20° (68° F) or the equivalent water vapor pressure at other temperatures. The determination may be made by direct measurement at the place. Determination is based on NLT 12 equally spaced measurements that encompass either a season, a year, or, where recorded data demonstrate, the storage period of the article. There may be values of up to 45% relative humidity provided that the average value does not exceed 40% relative humidity.

Do not refrigerate: The Container label will bear an appropriate instruction to protect the article from refrigeration in cases where refrigeration exposes the article to loss of strength or potency or destructive alteration of its characteristics (e.g., precipitation, cloudiness).

Protect from freezing: The Container label will bear an appropriate instruction to protect the article from freezing in cases where freezing exposes an article to loss of strength or potency or to destructive alteration of its characteristics.

Protect from light: Where light subjects an article to loss of strength or potency or to destructive alteration of its characteristics, the Container label bears an appropriate instruction to protect the article from light. The article must be packaged in a light-resistant Container.

Tutorial Commentary

Formulas and Calculations: MKT (Mean Kinetic Temperature)

Controlled room temperature MKT excursion conditions:
1. MKT ≤ 25°C (77°F)
2. Excursion temperature: 15°C–30°C (59°F–86°F)
3. Transient maximum temperature: NMT 40°C (104°F)
4. Excursion duration: NMT 24 hours

Controlled cold temperature:
• Main condition: 2°C–8°C
• Permitted excursion: 2°C–15°C (<24 hours, one time only)
• Calculated MKT ≤ 8°C (46°F)
• Must not fall below 2°C or exceed 15°C

Dry place:
• Average relative humidity ≤ 40% RH at 20°C
• Maximum permitted value 45% RH (provided the average is ≤40%)
• At least 12 equally spaced measurements are required (over one season, one year or the storage period)

Analogy: What MKT Means

MKT (mean kinetic temperature) is like calculating the "equivalent thermal effect" of a journey. It is not a simple average but is weighted according to the Arrhenius equation. If a product is stored at 25°C for 23 hours and reaches 35°C for 1 hour along the way, it meets the controlled room temperature requirement if the calculated MKT is ≤25°C. This reflects the fact that drug degradation is a chemical reaction, whose response to temperature is exponential rather than linear.

Key Notes

Why "Do not refrigerate" matters: some drug products (such as certain injectable suspensions and ethanol-containing preparations) may precipitate or phase-separate at refrigerated temperatures. The label must state "Do not refrigerate" explicitly to prevent storage or dispensing errors.

The "Protect from freezing" scenario: if an aqueous injection freezes, the glass container may break, or a protein biologic may be denatured and inactivated by ice crystal formation. Cold chain transport needs particular care to prevent freezing, a common quality problem in international winter logistics.

The rigor of the dry place definition: at least 12 equally spaced measurements covering a season or a full year are required. Occasional high humidity (up to 45% RH) is permitted; what matters is that the average does not exceed 40% RH.

Practice Questions

  1. A vaccine has a recommended storage condition of 2°C–8°C. During air transport, one record showed the temperature reached 12°C for 18 hours, and the calculated MKT was 7.5°C. Does this batch meet the "controlled cold temperature" requirement?
  2. An oral solution is labeled "store at room temperature", and the warehouse reaches a maximum of 38°C in summer. Does this comply? What would be different if the label said "controlled room temperature"?
  3. A manufacturer wants to assess whether its warehouse meets the "dry place" definition. It monitored relative humidity 8 times over one year, with an average of 38% RH. Does this monitoring plan meet USP requirements?

Practical Application

In pharmaceutical warehouse management, the USP temperature definitions are the basis for setting temperature monitoring alarm limits. For example, the upper alarm limit for a cold storage room (2°C–8°C) is usually set at 10°C (leaving a buffer) so that there is enough time to act before 15°C is exceeded, in order to comply with the excursion provisions for controlled cold temperature. These definitions also directly affect stability study design (the accelerated, intermediate and long-term conditions of ICH Q1A).

Chapter Summary

  • Basic requirements for packaging materials: must not impair the drug product's safety, identity, strength, quality or purity (SIQOP), and must meet related chapters such as <660>, <661>, <670> and <381>
  • Packaging hierarchy: Primary (direct contact) → Secondary (contacts primary) → Tertiary (contacts secondary) → Ancillary (logistics support) → Associated (administration accessories)
  • Container protection levels: Hermetic (fully airtight) > Tight (protects against liquids/solids/vapors) > Well-closed (protects against solids); Light-resistant is an independent attribute
  • Injection classification: Single-dose (single use, no antimicrobial testing required) vs. Multiple-dose (repeated use, antimicrobial testing required, ≤30 mL, 28 days after opening) vs. Pharmacy bulk package (multiple doses, single penetration, for pharmacy compounding only)
  • Noninjection classification: Multiple-unit (repeated withdrawal), Single-unit (single use), Unit-dose (nonparenteral single dose), Unit-of-use (pre-counted quantity, no repackaging)
  • Temperature definitions: Freezer (−25° to −10°), Refrigerator (2° to 8°), Cool (8° to 15°), Controlled RT (20° to 25°), Warm (30° to 40°), Excessive heat (>40°)
  • MKT application: controlled room temperature excursion conditions (NMT 25°C, 15°–30°C, NMT 24 h, not above 40°C); controlled cold temperature excursion (2°–15°C, NMT 24 h, one time only)
  • Dry place: average RH ≤40% (up to 45% RH permitted), with ≥12 equally spaced measurements
