Introduction

Original Text

〈771〉 OPHTHALMIC PRODUCTS—QUALITY TESTS

INTRODUCTION

Ophthalmic products are sterile products that are intended for application to any ocular structure, including any space adjacent to an ocular structure and its immediate surrounding spaces.

The routes of administration of ophthalmic products fall into three general categories: topical, intraocular injections, and extraocular injections. Topical drug products are intended to be administered to an ocular surface component, such as the eyelid, conjunctiva, or cornea, and can produce local or systemic effects. Intraocular and extraocular injections are administered through external boundary tissue. Due to the potential sensitivity of some ocular tissues, injections have additional requirements for subvisible particulate matter content (see Particulate and Foreign Matter, Subvisible Particulate Matter in Intraocular Solutions 〈789〉, and Visible Particulates in Injections 〈790〉). The ophthalmic routes of administration include, but are not limited to: topical, subconjunctival, sub-Tenons, subretinal, subchoroidal, intracorneal, intrascleral, suprachoroidal, intravitreal, intracameral, juxtascleral, and retrobulbar routes.

Ophthalmic products are administered to the eye in a wide variety of dosage forms, including but not restricted to: solutions, suspensions, ointments, gels, emulsions, strips, injections, inserts, and implants.

"This chapter provides lists of consolidated common product quality test requirements in a concise and coherent fashion. This chapter applies, in part or in its entirety, when referenced in a drug product monograph."

This chapter includes the quality tests for the specific route of administration. The quality tests listed can be used, as appropriate, by manufacturers toward the development of new drug product monographs for submission to the USP–NF.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Ophthalmic products: all sterile preparations intended for use on the structures of the eye, including the surface of the eyeball and the spaces around it. Sterility is the most basic and least negotiable requirement for ophthalmic products.

Three classes of route of administration:

  • Topical: applied to the eyelid, conjunctiva or cornea surface; the most common route
  • Intraocular: penetrates boundary tissues such as the cornea or sclera; the highest risk
  • Extraocular: injected into the spaces around the eyeball, such as retrobulbar injection

The stringency of the particulate requirements differs by route of administration. Intraocular injections must additionally meet the subvisible particulate standards of <789>, because the vitreous cavity and the anterior chamber have almost no ability to clear particles themselves.

Analogy

Imagine the eye as a sealed fish tank. Instilling eye drops is like pouring the medicine onto the lid of the tank, and it seeps in slowly; an intraocular injection pushes the syringe straight into the tank. The two call for completely different levels of "water quality" (particulate cleanliness): once impurities enter the water in the tank, the fish (the ocular tissue) are harmed immediately, and there is no way to put it right by changing the water (flushing with tears).

Key Notes

The scope of USP <771> is applied passively: it applies in whole or in part only when a drug product monograph explicitly refers to this chapter. This is the principle in General Notices 3.10. When writing the quality specifications for a new drug application, a manufacturer may use the quality tests provided in this chapter as a basis for a draft monograph for the new drug.

Also remember that performance testing (dissolution/drug release) is outside the scope of this chapter; see <1771> Ophthalmic Products—Performance Tests.

  2. Is USP <771> a mandatory chapter or an optional (guideline) chapter? When does it become a mandatory requirement?
Ophthalmic Dosage Forms

Original Text

OPHTHALMIC DOSAGE FORMS

This chapter discusses the specific characteristics of dosage forms that are administered to any structure in the eye. See Pharmaceutical Dosage Forms 〈1151〉 for additional information pertaining to the description and manufacturing of pharmaceutical dosage forms.

Ophthalmic products have the same or similar requirements as injectables and implants. For more information on the requirements for these dosage forms, see Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉.

Degradation and/or morphological changes of the drug product can occur during the sterilization step of the manufacturing process depending on the formulation, container-closure system, and sterilization process. These changes should be minimized. Temperatures required for autoclaving can cause irreversible damage to certain dosage forms such as suspensions, semisolids, emulsions, and others, whereas filtration is applicable only to formulations containing particulates with particle sizes <0.2 µm. An alternative approach is to manufacture the drug product from sterile ingredients maintained within an aseptic environment.

Multi-dose products shall contain a suitable antimicrobial preservative, and the effectiveness of the preservative over the shelf life of the product must be verified. Alternatively, preservative-free multi-dose products require a packaging system that has been demonstrated to maintain sterility over the shelf-life of the product.

Solutions

[Note—Included in this section are solutions and those solid products that, when reconstituted according to the instructions in the labeling, result in a solution.]

The corneal contact time of topical ophthalmic solutions increases with the viscosity of the formulations. Several synthetic polymers, including but not limited to polyvinyl alcohol, polyvinylpyrrolidone, polyethylene glycol, poly(acrylic acid), and cellulose derivatives such as hypromellose and hydroxyethyl cellulose, are commonly used as viscosity enhancers because of their physiologic compatibility and desirable physicochemical properties.

Another approach to increase the corneal contact time involves the use of polymers that provide the liquid formulation with semisolid consistency only when the liquid formulation is placed in the conjunctival or corneal area. "Hydrogel", or "water-based gels", are three-dimensional networks of polymer chains containing water within the network. The in-situ gelling phenomenon is caused upon delivery to the eye by a change in the conformation of the polymer(s) that can be triggered by external stimuli such as temperature, pH, ionic content, and lacrimal fluid.

Solutions can be injected not only by the intravitreal route but also by other routes, such as subconjunctival, sub-Tenons, retrobulbar, suprachoroidal, and subretinal routes.

Suspensions

Use of aqueous or oily suspensions may be considered for a number of reasons: 1) a drug is poorly soluble in water, 2) a drug has poor stability in water, and 3) in cases where there is a need to increase contact time with the eye and to increase wetting of the eye. A drug particle size of <10 µm is desirable to reduce irritation.

After topical instillation, particles are expected to be retained in the cul-de-sac, and the drug slowly dissolves or is released from the polymeric structures by diffusion, dissolution, polymer degradation, or ion-exchange.

Ointments

Eye ointments are semisolid products usually intended for application to the conjunctiva, cornea, or eyelid. They have longer contact time when compared to many solutions. A suitable ophthalmic ointment base is nonirritating to the eye and permits diffusion of the drug substance throughout the secretions bathing the eye.

The majority of water-free, oleaginous eye ointment bases are composed of white petrolatum and liquid petrolatum (mineral oil). They are designed to melt at body temperature.

Gels

Semisolid gel products are an alternative to traditional ointments and are based on the effect of increasing the viscosity to prolong the retention of the drug in the eye. Several types of gelling agents can be used, such as poly(acrylic acid) derivatives (e.g., carbomer) or hypromellose.

Emulsions

Topical ophthalmic emulsions are generally prepared by dissolving or dispersing the drug substance into an oil phase, adding suitable emulsifying and suspending agents, and vigorously mixing these components with water to form a uniform oil-in-water emulsion. Each phase is typically sterilized before or during charging into the mixing vessel. High-shear homogenization may be used to reduce oil droplet size to submicron size.

Strips

Ophthalmic strips can have different ophthalmic uses including, but not limited to, use as a diagnostic tool to visualize defects or aberrations in the corneal epithelium or to measure the amount of tear production. They are made of filter paper, and they can contain compounds such as fluorescein sodium. They are individually packed to preserve sterility until the moment of use.

Inserts

Ophthalmic inserts and ocular systems are solid dosage forms of appropriate size and shape that are placed in the conjunctival fornix, in the lacrimal punctum, or on the cornea. Inserts can usually be removed, if adverse effects develop, and can provide extended release of the drug over a certain period of time. Inserts can be classified as bioabsorbable (soluble) or non-bioabsorbable (insoluble).

Soluble inserts may be placed in the lower cul-de-sac and generally dissolve within 12–24 h. Bioabsorbable polymeric products undergo gradual dissolution while releasing the drug. Insoluble inserts need to be removed after a certain period of time.

Implants

Implants are injected or implanted into the anterior segment (intracameral, subconjunctival, sub-Tenon, or other parts of the anterior segment) or intravitreal cavities. Implants can be made of non-bioabsorbable or bioabsorbable polymers.

Drug–Device Combination Products

An ophthalmic drug–device combination product is constituted, in most cases, of two components. One is a pharmaceutical dosage form containing the drug substance(s), and the other is a device that will activate or facilitate the penetration of the drug substance(s) from the dosage form into a particular region of the eye. Some examples of the devices are those that generate waveforms (heat or light). Light sources can activate photoresponsive drugs.

This chapter is applicable only to the pharmaceutical dosage form component of the ophthalmic drug–device combination product. The appropriate FDA regulations regarding medical devices should be used for the device component.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The tiered logic of choosing a sterilization strategy: each of the three sterilization routes for ophthalmic products has its limits:

  • Autoclave (steam sterilization): the temperature may damage heat-labile dosage forms such as suspensions, semi-solids and emulsions
  • Filtration: applicable only to preparations whose particles are <0.2 µm; it cannot handle suspensions or semi-solids
  • Aseptic manufacturing: production from sterile raw materials in a controlled environment; the alternative when the first two are not feasible

These three routes directly affect a manufacturing site's facility investment and validation strategy, and are the first technical node a CDMO evaluates when taking on a project.

Analogy

The drug residence time of ophthalmic dosage forms can be compared to different ways of applying something to the eye:

  • Solution = a water mist sprayed on glass, which runs off quickly
  • Viscosity-enhanced solution = honey spread on the glass, which flows much more slowly
  • In-situ gel = molten jelly poured on, which solidifies on contact with the eye and greatly extends contact time
  • Ointment = petroleum jelly, which adheres best but blurs vision
  • Insert = a sustained-release capsule placed in the conjunctival sac, which dissolves slowly over 12–24 hours

Each dosage form has a different degree of "stickiness", and the sterility challenges in manufacturing differ as well. Semi-solid preparations are the hardest to sterilize by filtration and can only rely on aseptic manufacturing or pre-sterilized raw materials.

Key Notes

Preservative requirements for multi-dose products: once a product is opened and used repeatedly, it must have either an effective antimicrobial preservative or a packaging system that maintains sterility (such as a special closure design for preservative-free products). Preservative effectiveness must be demonstrated according to <51> Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing and maintained throughout the shelf life.

Upper limit of particle size in suspensions: a drug particle size of <10 µm is the key threshold for reducing ocular irritation. Oversized particles cause a gritty sensation and directly affect patient tolerability. This figure is a hard design target during suspension formulation development.

Key Numbers

Condition for sterilizing filtration: particle size < 0.2 µm
Drug particle size target for suspensions: < 10 µm (to reduce irritation)
Dissolution time of an ophthalmic insert: 12–24 hours (soluble type)
Ointment: designed melting point = body temperature (about 37°C)

Practical Application

As a manufacturing scientist at a CDMO, when a client brings a poorly water-soluble ophthalmic drug, your option matrix is:

  • If it can be made into a nanosuspension (<10 µm) → consider an ophthalmic suspension; redispersibility must be validated
  • If the drug is light-sensitive → a drug-device combination (light-activated system) may be considered, but the device is regulated separately under FDA medical device regulations
  • If long-acting local effect is needed → an ocular insert or implant, but process complexity increases substantially
  • Every route requires an aseptic manufacturing assessment: which sterilization route is feasible?

Practice Questions

  1. A drug is heat-labile and its molecular size is too large for filtration. Which sterilization strategy should be used, and what additional validation activities are needed?
  2. For a drug-device combination ophthalmic product, which regulatory requirements apply to the "dosage form" and to the "device" respectively? Where does the jurisdiction of USP <771> stop?

Section Summary

  • Ophthalmic products cover a broad range of dosage forms (solutions through implants); their common requirement is sterility, and all are governed by USP <771> (when referenced by a drug product monograph)
  • The three classes of route of administration (topical, intraocular, extraocular) set a stepped scale of particulate control stringency: intraocular injections are the strictest (they must meet <789>)
  • The choice of sterilization strategy is constrained by dosage form: heat-labile preparations must move to aseptic manufacturing, and filtration suits only solutions (<0.2 µm)
  • Multi-dose preparations must have an effective preservative system; the device part of a drug-device combination product is outside the scope of <771>

Drug Product Quality — Universal Tests Overview

Original Text

DRUG PRODUCT QUALITY

Procedures and acceptance criteria for testing ophthalmic products are divided into two categories:

  1. Those that assess general quality attributes, e.g., identification, potency, purity (and impurities), sterility, and particulate matter
  2. Those that assess in vitro product performance, i.e., dissolution or drug release of the drug substance from the drug product

Quality tests assess the integrity of the dosage form, whereas the performance tests assess drug release and other attributes that relate to in vivo drug performance. Taken together, quality and performance tests ensure the identity, strength, quality, purity, and efficacy of the drug product. This chapter addresses the quality tests for ophthalmic products. The performance tests (dissolution/drug release) are addressed by Ophthalmic Products—Performance Tests 〈1771〉.

Universal Tests

Universal tests in this chapter mean the tests that are applicable to all ophthalmic products, regardless of the dosage form type.

description

A qualitative description of the drug product should be provided. The acceptance criteria should contain the final acceptable appearance, including clarity and color, of the dosage form and packaging. If the color changes during storage, a quantitative procedure may be appropriate. This is not a compendial test but is part of the manufacturer's specification of the drug product.

identification

Identification tests are discussed in General Notices, 5.40 Identification. Identification tests should establish the identity of the drug or drugs present in the article and should discriminate between compounds of closely related structures that are likely to be present. Identity tests should be specific for the drug substance(s). If the test is nonspecific, at least two orthogonal nonspecific tests should be used. Identification by use of a single chromatographic retention time does not constitute a specific test.

assay

A specific and stability-indicating test should be used to determine the strength (content) of the drug product. In cases where the use of a nonspecific assay test is justified, other supporting analytical procedures should be used to achieve overall specificity. A specific procedure should be used when there is evidence of excipient interference with the nonspecific assay test. Additional information on specific assays may be found in Antibiotics—Microbial Assays 〈81〉, Chromatography 〈621〉, and Ion Chromatography 〈1065〉.

impurities

Process impurities, synthetic byproducts, and other inorganic and organic impurities may be present in the drug substance and excipients. Organic impurities arising from the degradation of the drug substance in the drug product and those impurities arising during the manufacturing process should be monitored. All articles meet the requirements in Elemental Impurities—Limits 〈232〉 and Residual Solvents 〈467〉.

pH

See pH 〈791〉. Normal tears have a pH of about 7.4. The eye can tolerate products over a range of pH values from about 3.0 to about 8.6, depending on the buffering capacity of the formulation. The pH value of the formulation should be the one where the drug product is the most stable. Formulations that target the extremes of the acceptable pH range will have better patient acceptability if the formulations have a low buffering capacity.

osmolarity

See Osmolality and Osmolarity 〈785〉. Ophthalmic products may be tolerated over a wide range of tonicity (0.5%–5% sodium chloride equivalent, about 171–1711 mOsm/kg). Hypotonic solutions are better tolerated than hypertonic solutions. Precautions should be taken to ensure that the product maintains its osmolarity during shelf life. Any possible contributions or interferences from the packaging system should be considered.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The fundamental distinction between quality tests and performance tests:

  • Quality tests: confirm the "integrity of what is there" in the dosage form: sterility, purity, content, particulates, pH. Governed by <771>.
  • Performance tests: confirm whether the drug "can be released from the dosage form into the ocular tissue": dissolution, drug release. Governed by <1771>.

This division of labor is similar to "food safety inspection" versus "nutrition labeling": the former ensures that the food has no problems, the latter that the food has its intended value. Only together do they ensure that the drug product is both effective and safe.

Stability-indicating assay: the assay method must be able to distinguish the drug substance from its degradation products, so that the presence of degradants does not cause the active content to be over- or underestimated. This is one of the core requirements of ICH Q2 validation.

Key Numbers

Normal tear pH: 7.4
Tolerable pH range for the eye: 3.0 ~ 8.6
(depending on buffer capacity)

Tolerable osmolarity range for the eye:
  171 ~ 1711 mOsm/kg
  equivalent to 0.5% ~ 5% NaCl equivalent

Isotonic standard (target):
  ~308 mOsm/kg (equivalent to 0.9% NaCl)

Hypotonic < isotonic < hypertonic
Tolerability: hypotonic > hypertonic

Analogy

Balancing pH and buffer capacity: imagine pouring lemon juice (pH ≈ 2) into the eye. Even if it is diluted to pH 4.0, if the buffer capacity is strong (as if a lot of phosphate buffer had been added), the tears cannot neutralize it quickly and the irritation lasts a long time. If the buffer capacity is low, however, the tears quickly pull the pH back to 7.4 and the patient hardly feels any irritation.

So when developing an ophthalmic formulation, an extreme pH (such as pH 3.0) needs to be paired with low buffer capacity to be clinically acceptable to patients.

Key Notes

The specificity rule for identification tests: USP expressly states that "a single chromatographic retention time does not constitute specific identification". This means that mass spectrometry, UV spectra or other orthogonal methods must be combined with it to meet the identification requirement. When applying for marketing authorization, one of the most common review comments on the analytical methods section is insufficient identification specificity.

Effect of packaging materials on osmolarity: during long-term storage of eye drops, the packaging material (especially LDPE bottles) may absorb water or release soluble substances, affecting osmolarity. This parameter should be monitored closely in shelf-life stability studies.

  1. An eye drop assay method is HPLC, measuring only retention time, with no spectral confirmation. Does this method meet the identification requirement of USP <771>? Why?
  2. A formulation developer wants to set the pH of an eye drop at 4.5 (on the low side but within the tolerable range) to improve drug stability. What measures should be taken to improve patient tolerability?
Particulate and Foreign Matter

Original Text

particulate and foreign matter

As described in Visual Inspection of Injections 〈1790〉, 4.1 Extrinsic, Intrinsic, or Inherent Particles, unwanted particulate matter is categorized as:

  • Extrinsic (foreign matter)
  • Intrinsic (formulation-, process-, or container-related matter or particulate matter due to product instability or product package interactions)
  • Inherent: a physical state that is an expected attribute of the product, such as a suspension or emulsion

It is expected that the manufacturer has established an acceptable control level for any inherent attribute, such as solution color, haze, or drug/excipient-related particle content.

The term 100% unit inspection is defined in 〈1790〉, 3.1 100% Inspection as the complete nondestructive inspection of the container–closure system and its contents. Therefore, evaluation of package and fill acceptability is shown by using the methodology in Visible Particulates in Injections 〈790〉, and this methodology is applied to all ophthalmic products.

Note: Chapter 〈787〉 may be used instead of 〈788〉 for protein biotherapeutic formulas.

For nonsolution dosage forms (e.g., ointments, gels, suspensions, and emulsions) and dosage forms packaged in opaque containers (e.g., opaque bottles, metal tubes), the approach must be modified because these products are not directly amenable to the tests listed in Table 1. It is recommended to use 100% unit inspection per 〈790〉 for package acceptability (including but not limited to integrity, form, dimensions, cleanliness, label); whereas, an alternate testing strategy may be used for foreign particles where the dosage form hinders or precludes the use of these tests. For these products, a statistically relevant sample, examined by a destructive or other alternative method, will determine batch acceptability for visible particles.

All ophthalmic products will benefit from evaluation of unwanted particulate matter content through the control strategy discussed in 〈1790〉 and in the FDA's current good manufacturing practices guidance. The final product benefits from a control strategy including comprehensive evaluation of excipient and active ingredient quality, processing effects, package quality, and fill control.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three-category particulate classification (extrinsic / intrinsic / inherent) is the core framework of regulatory review:

  • Extrinsic: comes from external contamination, such as fibers, glass fragments or items dropped by operators; a sign that process control has failed, and the category regulators accept least
  • Intrinsic: comes from the formulation, the process, or the container closure system itself, such as protein aggregates, silicone oil droplets and degradation products; requires a systematic control strategy
  • Inherent: a physical state that is expected to be present in the product itself, such as the drug particles of a suspension; an acceptable range needs to be established

100% unit inspection: every container must undergo visual or automated non-destructive inspection to confirm package integrity and the acceptability of the contents. This is a basic requirement for ophthalmic products, unlike the sampling inspection that may be used for ordinary drug products.

Analogy

The three categories of particulates are like foreign objects found in restaurant food:

  • Extrinsic: a hair or pencil shavings in the soup → a kitchen hygiene failure; the customer sends it back immediately and the restaurant faces a complaint
  • Intrinsic: cooking oil solidifying into white granules at low temperature → caused by the process (temperature management) or the ingredients (type of fat), and can be solved by adjusting the formulation
  • Inherent: the pulp in orange juice → it is supposed to be there; consumers need to be told, and an acceptable range for "pulp content" needs to be established

Key Notes

The key escalation points in particulate requirements: the core pattern of Table 1 is:

  • All ophthalmic products (including topical) must meet <790> visible particulates
  • Extraocular (non-topical) injections additionally require <788> subvisible particulates (light obscuration method)
  • Intraocular injections (those that penetrate boundary tissues) additionally require <789> subvisible particulates (a stricter standard for intraocular solutions)

Protein biologic formulations may use <787> in place of <788>, because the traditional light obscuration method is not suitable for protein aggregates.

Non-transparent dosage forms such as ointments and suspensions cannot be directly inspected for visible particulates; alternative methods (such as microscopic examination or light obscuration) must be used, with statistical sampling to establish batch acceptability.

Practical Application

Suppose a CDMO takes on a protein ophthalmic drug for intravitreal injection (an intravitreal biologic). According to Table 1, the quality testing strategy must include:

  • <790> visible particulates: 100% automated visual inspection (AVI) + manual visual inspection (MVI)
  • <789> subvisible particulates (specific to intraocular solutions, stricter than <788>)
  • <787> (in place of <788>, because it is a protein biologic)
  • Endotoxins, sterility, pH and osmolarity must all be included

These test items need to be listed one by one in the Quality Agreement and the specification sheet.

  2. A batch of intravitreal injection passes visual inspection but exceeds the <789> limit. Can this batch be released? Why?
Sterility

Original Text

sterility

Ophthalmic dosage forms must meet the requirements of Sterility Tests 〈71〉. If the specific ingredients used in the formulation do not lend themselves to routine sterilization techniques, ingredients that meet the sterility requirements described in 〈71〉, along with aseptic manufacture, may be used. The immediate container for ophthalmic products shall be sterile at the time of filling and closing. See also Universal Tests, Container–Closure Integrity.

antimicrobial preservatives

Antimicrobial agents must be added to products that are packaged in containers that allow for the withdrawal or administration of multiple doses, unless one of the following conditions prevails:

  1. There are different directions in the individual monograph;
  2. The radiopharmaceutical drug product contains a radionuclide with a physical half-life of <24 h;
  3. The drug product, without additional agents, is sufficiently microbicidal to meet the requirements of Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing 〈51〉;
  4. The container–closure system is capable of maintaining sterility of the product throughout its shelf life until opened or accessed. Once opened or accessed, it must maintain antimicrobial effectiveness during the intended use period.

Antimicrobial agents must meet the requirements of 〈51〉 and Antimicrobial Agents—Content 〈341〉. Acceptance criteria for antimicrobial preservative content in multi-dose products should be established.

bacterial endotoxins

All injected ophthalmic drug products shall be prepared in a manner designed to minimize bacterial endotoxins as defined in Bacterial Endotoxins Test 〈85〉. The limits are:

  • Not more than 0.5 EU/mL for ophthalmic irrigation products
  • Not more than 2.0 EU/dose/eye for injected or implanted drug products

Typically, this test is not required for topically applied ophthalmic products. This chapter does not address the endotoxin limits for devices that are injected or implanted.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The place of sterility in ophthalmic products: according to all the requirements of this chapter, sterility is the most basic prerequisite. The eye has no natural flora protection mechanism like the intestinal tract, and any microbial contamination may cause a serious eye infection (such as endophthalmitis) and even blindness. Sterility assurance is therefore a non-negotiable priority for ophthalmic products.

Conditions for exemption from antimicrobial preservatives (four exceptions): multi-dose preparations must in principle contain a preservative, but four situations are exempt. The most important is condition 4: the container-closure system itself maintains sterility and maintains antimicrobial effectiveness during use. This is the current direction of technical innovation for preservative-free multi-dose eye drops.

Endotoxin Limits

Ophthalmic irrigation preparations: ≤ 0.5 EU/mL
Injectable/implantable ophthalmic preparations: ≤ 2.0 EU/dose/eye

For comparison: general intravenous injection limit = 5 EU/kg/hr
      intrathecal (lumbar puncture) injection limit = 0.2 EU/mL (the strictest)

Ophthalmic injections fall between the two.
Topical eye drops: endotoxin testing is usually not required.

Analogy

The multi-dose preservative requirement for ophthalmic products is like the problem of a restaurant's self-service sauce bottle: after opening, people scoop from it with a spoon every time, and with no antimicrobial mechanism at all, bacteria will grow in the bottle within a few days. There are two solutions:

  • Add a preservative (such as benzalkonium chloride) = add a preserving ingredient to the sauce, but some people are allergic to preservatives (patients who use eye drops long term, such as glaucoma patients)
  • Special packaging system (sterile self-sealing valve) = a bottle-mouth design that keeps outside bacteria out and reseals automatically after each use; this is the design thinking behind modern preservative-free multi-dose eye drops

Key Notes

The container (primary container) must already be sterile at filling: this is a direct requirement of the manufacturing process. Container washing, depyrogenation and sterilization before production (such as a DET tunnel oven) are all critical process steps, and any deviation may lead to batch rejection.

The boundary of applicability of endotoxin testing: usually not required for topical eye drops, but once ocular tissue is penetrated (injection, implantation), it must be strictly controlled. Once the blood-ocular barrier of the eye is broken by the act of injection, endotoxin may enter the systemic circulation and trigger a severe inflammatory reaction.

Practice Questions

  1. A preservative-free multi-dose eye drop uses the "COMOD system" (a special nitrogen-pressurized bottle) and can be used for 6 months after opening. Under which of the four exceptions is this product exempt from preservatives, and what validation is needed as proof?
  2. The bacterial endotoxins limit for intravitreal injections is 2.0 EU/dose/eye. If one dose has an injection volume of 0.05 mL, what is the permissible upper limit per milliliter in EU/mL?

Section Summary

  • Quality tests for ophthalmic products fall into two broad groups: universal quality tests (identification, assay, impurities, pH, osmolarity, particulates) and performance tests (dissolution/drug release, governed separately by <1771>)
  • The three-category particulate framework (extrinsic / intrinsic / inherent) determines the tier of control strategy; Table 1 specifies the particulate chapter requirements for each route of administration, with intraocular injection the strictest (<790> + <789>)
  • Sterility is the most basic requirement for ophthalmic products; multi-dose preparations must have an antimicrobial preservative system (or meet one of the four exemptions)
  • Bacterial endotoxins limits: irrigation solutions ≤0.5 EU/mL, injectable/implantable ≤2.0 EU/dose/eye; topical preparations are usually exempt from testing

Uniformity, Container Contents, Leachables & Extractables

Original Text

uniformity of dosage units

This test is applicable to dosage forms packaged in single-unit containers. It includes both the mass of the dosage form and the content of the drug substance(s) in the dosage form. The test can be performed by either content uniformity or weight variation (see Uniformity of Dosage Units 〈905〉).

container contents

Container contents of ophthalmic products should be determined (see Minimum Fill 〈755〉).

leachables and extractables

The packaging system should not interact physically or chemically with the product in any manner to alter the strength, quality, or purity of the drug product. The packaging system should meet the requirements in Elastomeric Components in Injectable Pharmaceutical Product Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈381〉, Containers—Glass 〈660〉, and, depending on which standards the manufacturer has implemented, Plastic Packaging Systems and Their Materials of Construction 〈661〉 or Plastic Materials of Construction 〈661.1〉 and Plastic Packaging Systems for Pharmaceutical Use 〈661.2〉. Further information regarding packaging systems testing can be found in Assessment of Extractables Associated with Pharmaceutical Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈1663〉 and Assessment of Drug Product Leachables Associated with Pharmaceutical Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈1664〉.

The assessment of possible leachables and/or extractables and the establishment of any acceptance criteria for these compounds should consider the risk assessment of the product, its indication, and its packaging system. This assessment is done in a case-by-case approach during product development and re-evaluated when any changes are made to the product including, but not restricted to, changes in manufacturing process, formulation, and packaging material.

Furthermore, it should be noted that a risk assessment of extractable/leachable impact on the topical or intraocular route of administration is a challenging undertaking. Toxicological or safety assessments of primary or secondary packaging component extractables and leachables are not typically available for ophthalmic routes of administration. A risk assessment may include evaluation of toxicology and safety from other routes of administration and an assessment of the total daily intake of the extractable/leachable being evaluated. The preponderance of such assessments leads to an estimate of extractable/leachable risk via ocular delivery to the patient.

container–closure integrity

The packaging system should be closed or sealed in such a manner as to prevent contamination or loss of contents and should incorporate a tamper-evident design. Validation of container integrity must demonstrate no penetration of microbial contamination or of chemical or physical impurities (see Package Integrity Evaluation—Sterile Products 〈1207〉).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The key difference between leachables and extractables:

  • Extractables: chemicals forcibly extracted from packaging materials under harsh conditions (such as strong solvents and high temperature). A laboratory study result representing the worst-case "potential" list.
  • Leachables: chemicals that actually migrate from the packaging materials into the drug product under normal storage and use conditions. This is the risk the patient is really exposed to.

Evaluation flow: first conduct an extractables study to establish the list of compounds, then a leachables study to confirm the actual amounts migrating, and finally a toxicological safety assessment (TTC analysis or species-specific toxicology).

Container-closure integrity: it is not just "the cap is on tight"; it must be demonstrated through validation testing (such as CCIT, container closure integrity testing) that microorganisms cannot penetrate the sealing system, throughout the entire shelf life.

Analogy

An E&L assessment is like choosing the material of a coffee cup:

  • Extractables study: soak the plastic cup in organic solvent for 24 hours to find every chemical that could possibly "come out", the worst-case list of possibilities
  • Leachables study: actually fill this cup with hot coffee and keep it for three years, testing regularly to see which migrants actually appear in the coffee; this is the risk the patient really faces
  • Toxicological assessment: ask "if I drink this coffee every day, does the amount of these substances I take in affect my health?"

The challenge for ophthalmic products is that toxicology data for the ocular route are very scarce; usually one can only borrow intravenous or oral toxicology data to extrapolate, so the uncertainty is high.

Key Notes

The E&L assessment must be re-evaluated when changes are made: any change in process, formulation or packaging material may affect leachables. When a client requests process optimization, a CDMO must assess whether it triggers a repeat of the E&L study.

Special challenges of E&L assessment for the eye: the eye (especially for intraocular injection) is in direct contact with neural tissue, so toxicity thresholds (TTC) cannot be applied directly from oral standards. In addition, ophthalmic TK/PK data are scarce, and regulators' (FDA/EMA) guidance is relatively conservative, requiring case-by-case assessment.

Tamper-evident design: ophthalmic packaging must be designed with tamper-evident features so that users can confirm the product has not been opened or contaminated. This is both a patient safety design and a regulatory compliance requirement.

  2. What does Minimum Fill <755> aim to ensure? If an eye drop bottle is underfilled, what is the practical effect on the patient?
Specific Tests

Original Text

Specific Tests

Viscosity

An increase in viscosity increases the residence time of ocular formulations. However, drug diffusion out of these formulations into the ocular tissue may be modified due to high product viscosity. Ophthalmic ointments are designed to be of very high viscosity to prolong the residence time of the formulation in the eye. The inclusion of viscosity evaluation in the specification of the product should be based on the type of dosage form and whether changes in product viscosity will affect its performance. This is not a compendial test but is part of the manufacturer's specification of the drug product. See Viscosity—Capillary Methods 〈911〉, Viscosity—Rotational Methods 〈912〉, and Viscosity—Rolling Ball Method 〈913〉.

Antioxidant content

If antioxidants are present in the drug product, tests of their content should be established, unless oxidative degradation can be detected by another test method such as impurity testing. Acceptance criteria for antioxidant content should be established. They should be based on the levels of antioxidant necessary to maintain the product's stability at all stages throughout its proposed usage and shelf life.

Resuspendability/redispersibility

Consideration must be given to establishing good physical stability of a suspension. If the particles settle and eventually produce a cake at the bottom of the container, they must redisperse readily at the time of use to achieve dosage uniformity. Quantitative demonstration of the active ingredient's resuspendability/redispersibility during product development is also recommended.

Particle size and particle size distribution

The potential for any changes in the particle size of ophthalmic suspensions and emulsions needs to be evaluated through stability testing (see Light Diffraction Measurement of Particle Size 〈429〉).

Drop size

For ophthalmic drug products dispensed as drops, drop sizes may typically range from 20–70 µL. Drop size can be controlled by weight or by volume, and it is typically evaluated during product development. Drop size can be determined by any appropriate validated procedure.

Added substances

Suitable substances may be added to ophthalmic products to increase stability, unless proscribed in the individual monograph, provided they are harmless in the amounts administered and do not interfere with therapeutic efficacy or with responses to the specified assays and tests. Typically, these substances should be evaluated during product development.

"The use of ingredients solely to impart an odor or flavor is prohibited. The use of ingredients solely to impart a color is prohibited unless coloring is needed to visualize the administration location of the product."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The double-edged effect of viscosity: increasing viscosity can prolong contact time with the eye, but it may also hinder diffusion of the drug from the dosage form into the ocular tissue. Viscosity is therefore not "the higher the better"; a balance must be found between residence time and drug release rate.

The clinical significance of resuspendability: if the patient does not shake an ophthalmic suspension sufficiently before use, or the suspension forms a cake that cannot be redispersed, the dose delivered will not be uniform from one application to the next. In severe cases the first drop contains very little drug and the last few drops contain too much, or the product cannot be redispersed at all, resulting in dosing failure.

The clinical reality of drop size: the capacity of the conjunctival sac is only about 7–10 µL, normal tear volume is about 7 µL, and the maximum tolerable volume is about 30 µL. If more than 30 µL is instilled, the excess liquid overflows from the corner of the eye and enters the nasolacrimal duct, causing systemic absorption and unnecessary side effects. The 20–70 µL drop size range is therefore designed so that one drop can deliver the drug effectively without much waste.

Key Numbers

Conjunctival sac capacity: ~7–10 µL
Normal tear volume: ~7 µL
Maximum tolerable volume: ~30 µL
Standard drop size range: 20–70 µL (per USP)

Ideal drop size design target:
  maximize corneal contact → minimize loss from overflow
  → usually 25–35 µL is recommended

Viscosity and contact time (qualitative relationship):
  low viscosity (<5 mPa·s) → runs off quickly
  medium viscosity (5–50 mPa·s) → moderately prolonged
  high viscosity (>50 mPa·s) → stays a long time but may blur vision

Analogy

Resuspendability is like mixing a salad dressing (vinaigrette): after standing, the oil and water separate, and it must be shaken thoroughly to mix evenly before use, otherwise the oil-to-water ratio poured out is different every time. Ophthalmic suspensions are the same: "shake well before use" on the instructions is not a suggestion but a necessary step to ensure dose uniformity.

"Cake formation" is like a salad dressing left standing too long, where the settled solids stick together and cannot be shaken loose however hard you try. This is a failure of formulation stability design and must be avoided through formulation optimization at the development stage.

The color restriction on added substances is like food regulations banning purely decorative food colorings: adding color to a drug product must serve a functional purpose (such as visualizing the injection site); a dye that may cause ocular irritation must not be added just to make the product look nice.

Key Notes

The difference between specific tests and universal tests: the specific tests in this section (viscosity, particle size, drop size, etc.) are not needed for every ophthalmic product; they are chosen according to the characteristics of the dosage form. For example:

  • Suspensions → particle size and resuspendability tests are required
  • Solution eye drops → a drop size test is required
  • High-viscosity gels → a viscosity specification is required
  • Preparations containing antioxidants → an antioxidant content test is required

The choice of viscosity test method depends on the viscosity range: the <911> capillary method suits low-viscosity liquids; the <912> rotational method suits medium to high viscosity; the <913> rolling ball method is used for specific applications. The specification must state the method and the instrument settings.

The stability challenge of particle size: the particle size of suspensions and emulsions may grow during storage through Ostwald ripening or aggregation, which directly affects drug absorption and ocular irritation, and must be monitored in accelerated and long-term stability studies.

Practical Application

What specific tests should be included in the quality specification sheet for an ophthalmic suspension (containing a corticosteroid, topical eye drop)?

  • Particle size and distribution (<429>): confirm drug particles are <10 µm to avoid ocular irritation
  • Resuspendability: uniformity meets requirements after N shakes (the shaking method needs to be defined)
  • Drop size (within the 20–70 µL range): the nozzle design needs to be verified
  • Viscosity (if a viscosity enhancer is added): confirm batch-to-batch consistency
  • Antimicrobial preservative content (such as benzalkonium chloride): meets <51> requirements over the entire shelf life
  • Universal tests: pH, osmolarity, sterility, particulates (<790>), assay, impurities

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The overall architectural logic of the USP <771> quality test framework:

The quality tests of the whole of <771> can be understood as a decision tree:

  1. Basic tests that all ophthalmic products must undergo (universal tests): identification, assay, impurities, pH, osmolarity, visible particulates, sterility, minimum fill, packaging material E&L assessment, container-closure integrity
  2. Additional tests determined by route of administration: intraocular injections add endotoxins and <789>; extraocular non-topical injections add <788>
  3. Specific tests chosen according to dosage form characteristics: suspensions add particle size + resuspendability; multi-dose products add preservatives; products containing antioxidants add antioxidant content; eye drops add drop size

This framework ensures that every link is covered, from the most basic "what drug is it" (identification), to "how safe is it" (sterility, particulates, endotoxins), to "can it be used normally" (resuspendability, drop size, viscosity).

Key Notes

Reconfirming the sterility-first principle in the quality system: looking back over the whole of <771>, sterility is always the first requirement. All other test items, whether pH, particulates, preservatives or resuspendability, are further quality assurance built on the foundation of "sterile". If sterility fails, it is no use if everything else passes.

The division of labor between USP <771> and <1771>: <771> = product quality, ensuring the drug product has "no problems"; <1771> = performance tests, ensuring the drug product is "useful". Both documents must be covered in a marketing application.

Analogy

The complete quality test framework of USP <771> is like the acceptance checklist for a building:

  • Basic structure (universal tests): foundation, walls, roof; every building needs them, and they are indispensable basic elements
  • Use-related requirements (route-of-administration tests): an office building needs a stricter fire protection system than an ordinary warehouse, and intraocular injections have more requirements than topical eye drops
  • Functional equipment (specific tests): a building with a swimming pool needs a waterproofing test; a suspension preparation needs a resuspendability test

No single test list is "one size fits all" for every ophthalmic product; it must be customized to the characteristics of the preparation, but <771> provides a systematic decision framework.

Practical Application

As a CDMO quality head, a client brings a new long-acting sustained-release polymer suspension for intravitreal injection. Determine which USP quality tests are needed:

  • All universal tests apply (identification, assay, impurities, pH, osmolarity)
  • Particulates: <790> + <789> (intraocular injection)
  • Sterility: <71> (mandatory)
  • Bacterial endotoxins: <85>, limit 2.0 EU/dose/eye
  • Dose uniformity: <905> (single-dose syringe)
  • Minimum fill: <755>
  • Particle size and distribution: <429> (suspension)
  • Resuspendability (suspension)
  • E&L assessment: syringe components (rubber plunger, glass barrel, needle cover film)
  • Container-closure integrity: <1207>
  • Viscosity (if the polymer affects viscosity)

This list represents a complete quality specification framework for an ophthalmic product, and each item needs to be justified in the CMC section.

Section Summary

  • Dose uniformity applies to single-dose containers (content uniformity or weight variation method, <905>); minimum fill applies to all ophthalmic products (<755>)
  • E&L assessment uses a case-by-case risk assessment approach, which must consider the dosage form, route of administration, duration of use and any packaging material/process changes; the scarcity of toxicology data for the ocular route is a special challenge
  • Specific tests (viscosity, particle size, resuspendability, drop size, etc.) are chosen as applicable to the dosage form characteristics and are not mandatory universal tests
  • The overall USP <771> framework: universal quality tests (basis) → additional tests by route of administration (particulates/endotoxins) → dosage form-specific tests; the three-tier structure ensures comprehensive quality coverage
