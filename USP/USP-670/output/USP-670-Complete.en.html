〈670〉 AUXILIARY PACKAGING COMPONENTS

Auxiliary packaging components are articles that are used to support or enhance container–closure systems. These articles include, but are not limited to, pharmaceutical coil, desiccants, and odor adsorbents for containers. The components covered in this chapter must meet the applicable requirements provided and the additional applicable requirements provided in other specified chapters.

"The components covered in this chapter must meet the applicable requirements provided and the additional applicable requirements provided in other specified chapters."

Components covered in USP 〈670〉:

  • Pharmaceutical Coil (Cotton, Rayon, Polyester)
  • Desiccants (Bentonite, Calcium Chloride, Calcium Oxide, Carbon Activated, Molecular Sieves, Silica Gel)
  • Odor Adsorbents (Carbon, Activated — with VOC testing)
  • Oxygen Scavengers (referenced by other USP chapters)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Auxiliary Packaging Components: Supporting materials that do not contact the active ingredient directly but protect the product by improving the packaging environment. For example, desiccants prevent moisture ingress, and coil prevents tablets from breaking during transport.

Container-Closure System: The core USP framework for pharmaceutical packaging integrity. Auxiliary packaging components are extra elements that "add value" within this system; they are not the primary structure.

Cross-reference principle: USP <670> is not a stand-alone document. It expressly requires components to also meet the requirements of other specified chapters, such as <731> Loss on Drying, <281> Residue on Ignition, and <791> pH.

Analogy

Imagine a high-end jewelry gift box: the star is the jewelry (the drug product), the box is the container-closure system, and the velvet lining and anti-moisture packet inside are the auxiliary packaging components. They are not the star, but without them the star might be damaged in transit.

In pharmaceutical packaging, the desiccant is like that anti-moisture packet and the coil is like that velvet, both there so the medicine reaches the patient safely.

Key Notes

Regulatory status (2026 perspective): This chapter became official in March 2024 and was confirmed as the current official version in February 2025. Any FDA review may cite this version. When selecting auxiliary packaging components, a CDMO must ensure that suppliers comply with the latest USP version.

Meaning of "other specified chapters": This cross-reference makes the requirements of USP <670> broader. Meeting this chapter alone is not enough; the additional requirements of related container chapters such as <661> and <671> must also be met.

Pharmaceutical Coil

Original Text

PHARMACEUTICAL COIL

Pharmaceutical coil is used as a filling material in multiple-unit containers for solid oral dosage forms to prevent breakage of tablets or capsules during shipment. The filling material should be discarded once the bottle is opened.

Solutions (Reagents for Testing)

  • Iodinated zinc chloride solution: Dissolve 20 g of zinc chloride and 6.5 g of potassium iodide in 10.5 mL of Purified Water. Add 0.5 g of iodine, and shake for 15 min. Filter if necessary. Protect from light.
  • Zinc chloride–formic acid solution: Dissolve 20 g of zinc chloride in 80 g of an 850-g/L solution of anhydrous formic acid.
  • 1% DuPont Fiber Identification Stain No. 4 solution: Dissolve 3.8 g of powdered stain in 378.5 mL of deionized water.

Cotton Pharmaceutical Coil

Purified cotton is the hair of the seed of cultivated varieties of Gossypium hirsutum Linné, or of other species of Gossypium (Fam. Malvaceae). It is deprived of fatty matter and bleached and does not contain more than traces of leaf residue, pericarp, seed coat, or other impurities. Cotton pharmaceutical coil is used in bottles of solid oral dosage forms to prevent breakage.

Identification

  • A. When examined under a microscope, each fiber is seen to consist of a single cell, up to about 4 cm long and 40 µm wide, in the form of a flattened tube with thick and rounded walls that are often twisted.
  • B. When treated with Iodinated zinc chloride solution, the fibers become violet.
  • C. To 0.1 g of fibers add 10 mL of Zinc chloride–formic acid solution, heat to 40°, and allow to stand for 2 h, shaking occasionally: the fibers do not dissolve.
  • D. Fibers become green when treated with 1% DuPont Fiber Identification Stain No. 4 solution.

Additional Tests (Cotton Coil):

  • Acidity or alkalinity: Neither phenolphthalein nor methyl orange portion appears pink against a white background after 2-h maceration in 100 mL Purified Water.
  • Fluorescence: Only slight brownish-violet fluorescence under UV at 365 nm; no intense blue fluorescence.
  • Residual hydrogen peroxide concentration: NMT 50 ppm by reflectometry strip method or permanganate titration.
  • Loss on Drying 〈731〉: NMT 8.0% (5 g at 105° to constant weight)
  • Residue on Ignition 〈281〉: NMT 0.20%
  • Water-soluble substances: Residue NMT 0.35%
  • Fatty matter: Residue NMT 0.7% (Soxhlet extraction with ethyl ether)
  • Dyes: Percolate may show yellowish color but not blue or green tint
  • Other foreign matter: No oil stains or metallic particles by visual inspection

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Pharmaceutical Coil: Filler material used in multiple-unit containers (such as bottles) to prevent solid oral dosage forms from breaking due to vibration during transport. There are three main materials: cotton, rayon, and polyester.

Important warning: The coil must be discarded after the bottle is opened. This is a safe-use instruction; if patients mistakenly believe the coil has a therapeutic effect and keep it, it may become a source of contamination.

Reagent systems: Iodinated zinc chloride solution (iodine reagent), zinc chloride-formic acid solution (solubility reagent), and DuPont No. 4 stain (fiber identification). The three reagents have clearly separate roles, each testing a different physical or chemical property of the fiber.

Analogy

Pharmaceutical coil is like the foam dividers in an egg carton: its job is to absorb shock and keep the star (the tablets) from breaking, not to be something the consumer swallows. USP stresses "discard after opening" precisely because patients may mistake the coil for part of the medicine.

The three identification tests for cotton (color reaction, solubility, staining) are like a three-gate identity check: only by passing all three can the material be confirmed as true pharmaceutical-grade cotton rather than industrial cotton.

Key Notes

Importance of residual hydrogen peroxide: A bleaching process (H₂O₂) may be used in cotton manufacturing, and if the residual level is too high (>50 ppm), it may cause oxidative degradation of the drug product. This is why USP includes this test, and it is also CoA data that a CDMO must require from the supplier when purchasing coil.

Significance of the fluorescence test: Excessive blue fluorescence suggests that the cotton may contain optical brighteners, chemicals that are considered potential contaminants in pharmaceutical packaging.

Formula

Residual hydrogen peroxide calculation (cotton coil):

% H₂O₂ = [(mL of Titrant × 0.0017) / 20] × 100

Conversion to ppm:
ppm H₂O₂ = % H₂O₂ (w/w) × 10,000

Acceptance criterion: NMT 50 ppm (applies to both methods)

Practical Application

As a CDMO QA professional reviewing a new coil supplier, which CoA (Certificate of Analysis) items should you require from the supplier?

At a minimum: Loss on Drying (≤8.0%), Residue on Ignition (≤0.20%), residual H₂O₂ (≤50 ppm), water-soluble substances (≤0.35%), fatty matter (≤0.7%), and fiber identification (four tests: A, B, C, and D).

  1. Why does USP design four different identification tests (A, B, C, D) for cotton rather than relying on a single one?
  2. The residual hydrogen peroxide limit for cotton coil is 50 ppm. What safety considerations was this figure based on?
Rayon & Polyester Pharmaceutical Coil

Original Text

Rayon Pharmaceutical Coil

Rayon pharmaceutical coil is a fibrous form of bleached, regenerated cellulose, to be used as a filler in bottles of solid oral dosage forms to prevent breakage. It consists exclusively of rayon fibers except for a few isolated foreign fibers that may be present.

"[Note—Rayon pharmaceutical coil has been found to be a potential source of dissolution problems for gelatin capsules or gelatin-coated tablets resulting from gelatin cross-linking.]"

Identification

  • A. When treated with Iodinated zinc chloride solution, the fibers become violet.
  • B. Add 10 mL of Zinc chloride–formic acid solution to 0.1 g of fibers, heat to 40°, and allow to stand for 2 h: the fibers dissolve completely (except for mat rayon fibers where titanium particles remain). [Key difference from cotton: cotton does NOT dissolve.]
  • C. Fibers become blue–green with DuPont Stain No. 4 solution. [Cotton becomes green; rayon becomes blue-green.]

Acceptance Criteria (Rayon):

  • Loss on Drying 〈731〉: NMT 11.0%
  • Residue on Ignition 〈281〉: NMT 1.50% (5-g specimen)
  • Acid-insoluble ash: NMT 1.25%
  • Water-soluble substances: NMT 1.0%
  • Fatty matter: Residue NMT 0.5% (sample weight 5 g)

Polyester Pharmaceutical Coil

Polyester pharmaceutical coil is a white odorless material, to be used as a filler in bottles of solid oral dosage forms to prevent breakage.

Identification

  • A. IR spectrum matches USP Polyethylene Terephthalate RS (4000 to 650 cm⁻¹). FTIR or ATR per 〈197〉; Method 1 (KBr disc) or Method 2 (melt film).
  • B. Fibers become pale orange with DuPont Stain No. 4.

Acceptance Criteria (Polyester):

  • Acidity or alkalinity: Proceed as directed under Cotton Pharmaceutical Coil
  • Loss on Drying 〈731〉: NMT 1.0%
  • Residue on Ignition 〈281〉: NMT 0.5% (5-g specimen)
  • Finish on fibers: Shall comply with FDA food contact regulations

Test Methods — Infrared Spectroscopy (for Polyester)

Apparatus: FTIR or a double-beam spectrophotometer capable of scanning from 4000 to 650 cm⁻¹ (2.5–15 µm).

Specimen preparation: ATR per 〈197〉 may be used as alternative. Method 1 (KBr disc): cut fibers into short lengths (<1 mm), mix with 200 mg KBr, grind 1–2 min, form disc. Method 2 (melt film): press fibers between PTFE sheets between heated plates.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Major Risk of Rayon

Gelatin cross-linking problem: Unusually, USP adds a Note directly in the main text warning that rayon coil may cause dissolution problems in gelatin capsules. This cross-linking phenomenon hardens the capsule shell, slowing drug release and affecting bioavailability.

Practical implication: For a CDMO, if the client's product is a hard or soft gelatin capsule, selecting rayon coil requires particular caution. A compatibility study should be performed, and it is best to switch to cotton or polyester coil.

Analogy

The identification of polyester coil moves up from "chemical reaction" to "IR fingerprint comparison", like moving from "recognizing someone by sight" to "DNA matching". The molecular fingerprint from FTIR is the most precise means of identification and cannot be faked, which is why USP uses IR rather than staining tests for synthetic polymers.

Key Notes: FDA Food Contact Regulations for Polyester

"Finish on fibers" requirement: Lubricants (finish) are used during polyester coil manufacturing to aid processing, and these lubricants must comply with FDA food contact regulations (21 CFR). This means the supplier must provide a compliance statement for such additives, and the CDMO must confirm the supplier's FDA compliance documentation during supplier audits.

Desiccants: Overview and Bentonite

Original Text

DESICCANT

Desiccants are used to remove moisture from air in containers in order to protect drug products, particularly solid oral dosage forms. They are supplied in a number of different packaging materials including cotton, Kraft paper, rayon and polyester cloth bags, perforated plastic, polymer films, or polymer housings.

The most common types of commercial desiccants are: bentonite; calcium chloride; calcium oxide; carbon, activated; molecular sieves; and silica gel. Other desiccants are subject to appropriate testing to ensure suitability for the intended application.

"Where desiccants are incorporated directly into the wall or cap of packaging containers or bound by a carrier material, use unincorporated desiccant in the test methods. For desiccants that are loaded with a predetermined moisture content, perform the testing before the moisture has been loaded or after the desiccant has been regenerated."

Bentonite

Bentonite clay (also referred to as montmorillonite clay) is a native, colloidal, hydrated aluminum silicate.

Appearance: Grayish-white powder or pellets with a yellowish or pinkish tint

Identification — X-Ray Powder Diffraction 〈941〉:

  • Sample preparation A: Largest peak corresponds to d value between 15.0 and 17.2 Å
  • Sample preparation B: Major peak between 1.492 and 1.504 Å

Identification — Precipitation: Formation of a gelatinous white precipitate

Inorganic Impurities:

  • Arsenic: NMT 10 mg/kg
  • Lead: NMT 15 mg/kg

Specific Tests:

  • pH 〈791〉: 4.5–10.5 (disperse 4.0 g in 200 mL water)
  • Loss on Drying 〈731〉: NMT 3.0% (5–10 g at 110° to constant weight) [Note: conduct assay immediately after opening original container]

Moisture adsorption capacity:

  • NLT 13% at 40% ± 5% RH and 25 ± 2°
  • NLT 23% at 80% ± 5% RH and 25 ± 2°

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Classification and Principles of Desiccants

Desiccant: A material that removes moisture from inside a container by physical adsorption or chemical reaction; it is the most commonly used auxiliary component for protecting moisture-sensitive solid oral dosage forms.

Six USP-recognized desiccants:

  • Bentonite: Natural aluminum silicate, physical adsorption, pH 4.5–10.5
  • Calcium Chloride: Chemical moisture absorption, strong adsorption capacity, ≥28% at 80% RH
  • Calcium Oxide: High adsorption capacity, reacts with water to form Ca(OH)₂
  • Carbon, Activated: Also serves as an odor adsorbent, ≥21% at 80% RH
  • Molecular Sieves: Precisely controlled pore size, types 3A/4A/5A/13X
  • Silica Gel: The most common, pH 4–8, ≥19% at 40% RH

Key Notes: Critical Pretreatment Before Testing

Two important pretreatment principles:

1. Incorporated desiccants: For desiccants embedded directly in the bottle wall or cap, the test must use the same desiccant in unincorporated form rather than extracting it by destroying the original packaging.

2. Desiccants preloaded with moisture: These must be tested before the moisture is loaded or after regeneration, to ensure that the test measures the desiccant's original capacity rather than a saturated state.

These two provisions avoid a systematic bias that would underestimate the desiccant's actual capacity in the test results.

Analogy: The Special Nature of Bentonite

Bentonite is a "smart sponge": it swells in volume after absorbing water, just like a sponge. In X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) testing, a d-value of 15–17 Å represents the lattice spacing; water molecules inserting between the layers increase the spacing. Scientists use XRD to "measure" how much water the sponge has absorbed and to confirm that it is true bentonite rather than another clay mineral.

Key Notes: Importance of Heavy Metal Limits

Arsenic (As) ≤10 mg/kg, lead (Pb) ≤15 mg/kg: Bentonite is a natural mineral, and natural minerals inevitably contain heavy metal impurities. USP sets these two limits because (1) arsenic and lead are the most common and most toxic inorganic contaminants; and (2) heavy metals in pharmaceutical packaging may migrate into the drug product under certain conditions.

For a CDMO procurement team, this means every batch of bentonite supplied requires a CoA demonstrating heavy metal compliance.

Section Summary

  • USP <670> defines auxiliary packaging components as items that "support or enhance the container-closure system", including pharmaceutical coil, desiccants, and odor adsorbents, which must meet the requirements of both this chapter and the other specified USP chapters
  • The three types of pharmaceutical coil (cotton, rayon, polyester) each have their own identification tests: cotton uses staining (green) plus insolubility in zinc chloride-formic acid; rayon uses staining (blue-green) plus solubility in zinc chloride-formic acid; polyester uses FTIR comparison against the RS. Rayon carries a special warning that it may cause dissolution problems in gelatin capsules
  • The six USP-recognized desiccants (bentonite, calcium chloride, calcium oxide, activated carbon, molecular sieves, silica gel) each have their own chemical properties, adsorption capacity specifications, and heavy metal limit requirements; before testing, attention must be paid to the pretreatment rules for incorporated desiccants
  • The adsorption capacity test for all desiccants must be performed under two standard humidity conditions (40% RH and 80% RH, 25±2°C) and immediately after opening the bottle, to avoid pre-absorbed moisture causing falsely low results

Bentonite Test Methods in Detail

Original Text

Bentonite — Test Methods (continued from Section 0a)

Identification — X-ray Diffraction

Sample preparation A: Add 2 g in small portions to 100 mL of water with intense agitation. Allow to stand for 12 h to ensure complete hydration. Place 2 mL of the mixture on a suitable glass slide, and allow to air-dry at room temperature to produce an oriented film. Place the slide in a vacuum desiccator over a free surface of ethylene glycol. Evacuate the desiccator, and close the stopcock so that ethylene glycol saturates the desiccator chamber. Allow the slide to stand for 12 h.

Sample preparation B: Prepare a random powder specimen of the sample.

Analysis: Sample preparation A and Sample preparation B. Record the X-ray diffraction pattern of the samples, and determine the d values.

Identification — Precipitation

Sample: 5 g

Analysis: Add 1 g of potassium nitrate and 3 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate to the Sample in a metal crucible, heat until the mixture has melted, and allow it to cool. Add 20 mL of boiling water to the residue, mix, filter, and wash the residue with 50 mL of water. Add 1 mL of hydrochloric acid and 5 mL of water to the residue, and filter. Add 1 mL of 10 N sodium hydroxide to the filtrate, filter, and add 3 mL of 2 M ammonium chloride.

Arsenic — Sample solution: Transfer 8.0 g of dried sample into a 250-mL beaker containing 100 mL of dilute hydrochloric acid (1 in 25), mix, and cover with a watch glass. Boil gently for 15 min. Pass the hot supernatant liquid through a rapid-flow filter paper into a 200-mL volumetric flask, and wash with four 25-mL portions of hot dilute HCl. Cool, dilute to volume.

Arsenic trioxide stock solution: Accurately weigh 132.0 mg of arsenic trioxide, previously dried at 105° for 1 h, and dissolve in 5 mL of sodium hydroxide solution (1 in 5) in a 1000-mL volumetric flask. Neutralize with 2 N sulfuric acid, add an additional 10 mL of 2 N sulfuric acid, and bring to volume.

Standard arsenic solution: Dilute the stock solution to suitable concentrations within the linear working range of the instrument. Keep in all-glass container, use within 3 days.

Analysis: Proceed according to Elemental Impurities—Procedures 〈233〉.

Lead — Sample solution: Transfer 3.75 g of sample into 250-mL beaker containing 100 mL of dilute HCl (1 in 25), boil for 15 min, cool, pass through filter paper into 400-mL beaker. Concentrate to ~20 mL; if precipitate forms, add 2–3 drops nitric acid. Pass through filter into 50-mL volumetric flask, dilute to volume.

Lead nitrate stock solution: Dissolve 159.8 mg of lead nitrate in 100 mL of water to which has been added 1 mL of nitric acid, dilute with water to 1000 mL. Store in glass containers free from soluble lead salts.

Standard lead solution: On the day of use, dilute stock solution to suitable concentrations within the linear working range.

Analysis: Proceed according to 〈233〉.

Moisture Adsorption Capacity — General Test Method

Equipment: Temperature-humidity chambers capable of controlled humidity at 40% ± 5% RH and 80% ± 5% RH at 25 ± 2°, or a desiccator containing water-saturated salts that provide the required %RH, plus an oven capable of maintaining 25 ± 2°.

Method: 5–10 g. Remove the sample from the packaging material. Where absorbents are incorporated directly into the wall or cap of packaging containers, use unincorporated desiccant. Add the sample to the humidity chamber or desiccator and measure the weight gain over time until this reaches equilibrium when two successive consecutive weighings do not differ by more than 3 mg/g of substance taken, the second weighing following an additional 3 ± 1 h of storage at the required temperature and humidity conditions.

Calculate the adsorption capacity as percentage weight gained over the initial sample weight.

"Where two absorbents are packaged in combination, the moisture adsorption capacity specification must be calculated in proportion of the mix."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Use of XRD in Desiccant Identification

X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD): Identifies crystal structure from the diffraction intensity of X-rays at different angles. After absorbing water, the d-value (lattice spacing) of bentonite expands to 15–17.2 Å, which is direct evidence of the swelling of its layered structure on water uptake.

Differences between the two sample preparations:

  • Preparation A (ethylene glycol-saturated treatment): Allows molecules to insert between the montmorillonite layers and measures the lattice spacing after maximum expansion, verifying the swelling property of bentonite
  • Preparation B (random powder): Measures the overall crystalline characteristics of the powder itself, verifying the consistency of the mineral composition

Analogy: XRD Is Like a Molecular Fingerprint Scanner

Imagine that every mineral has a unique "barcode": when X-rays strike the crystal, each atomic layer acts like a line in the barcode, reflecting light at a specific angle. The "barcode" signature of bentonite is a d-value of 15–17.2 Å (after swelling with water).

The two sample preparations are like taking a "front view" (Prep A, showing the maximum feature) and a "side view" (Prep B, showing the overall structure) of the same person; only the two photos together fully confirm the identity.

Key Notes: Standardization of Heavy Metal Testing

The test methods for both arsenic and lead refer to USP <233> Elemental Impurities-Procedures: This means the heavy metal testing of bentonite does not use the old-style limit test (colorimetric comparison) but modern quantitative ICP-MS or ICP-OES methods. This is consistent with the modern elemental impurities management framework of ICH Q3D.

Preparation of the arsenic trioxide standard: 132.0 mg As₂O₃ in 1000 mL gives 100 mg/L As₂O₃; it must be dissolved in an alkaline medium (NaOH) and then neutralized with sulfuric acid. All-glass containers must be used and the solution used within 3 days, to avoid arsenic adsorbing onto plastic containers and lowering the standard concentration.

Formula: Adsorption Capacity

Adsorption capacity calculation:
% Adsorption Capacity = (Weight Gain / Initial Sample Weight) × 100

Equilibrium criterion:
Difference between two consecutive weighings ≤ 3 mg/g
The second weighing is made after an additional 3 ± 1 h

Blended desiccant calculation example (USP original):
60% molecular sieves × 15% adsorption capacity = NLT 9.0%
40% silica gel × 19% adsorption capacity = NLT 7.6%
Minimum requirement for the blend = 9.0% + 7.6% = NLT 16.6%

  2. The heavy metal standard "must be used within 3 days". What is the chemical reason behind this time limit?

Calcium Chloride, Anhydrous

Original Text

Calcium Chloride, Anhydrous

Specifications Summary:

  • Identification—calcium: Passes tests
  • Identification—chloride: Passes tests
  • Assay: NLT 93.0% and NMT 100.5% of calcium chloride (CaCl₂)
  • Arsenic: NMT 3 ppm
  • Fluoride: NMT 0.004%
  • Lead: NMT 5 ppm
  • Magnesium and alkali salts: NMT 25 mg of residue (NMT 5.0%)
  • pH 〈791〉: 4.5–11.0 (1:20 aqueous solution)
  • Moisture adsorption capacity: NLT 28% at 80% ± 5% RH and 25 ± 2°

Identification — Calcium: Insoluble oxalate salts are formed when solutions of calcium salts are treated: neutralize with 6 N ammonia, add 2.7 N HCl until acid. A white precipitate of calcium oxalate forms upon addition of ammonium oxalate TS — insoluble in acetic acid but dissolves in hydrochloric acid.

Identification — Chloride: Solutions of chlorides yield with silver nitrate TS a white, curdy precipitate that is insoluble in nitric acid but soluble in a slight excess of 6 N ammonia.

Assay (EDTA Titration):

Sample: 1.5 g. Transfer into a 250-mL volumetric flask, dissolve in 100 mL water + 5 mL of 2.7 N HCl, dilute to volume. Transfer 50 mL into suitable container, add 50 mL water. While stirring, add ~30 mL of 0.05 M disodium EDTA from a 50-mL buret. Add 15 mL of 1 N NaOH and 300 mg of hydroxy naphthol blue indicator. Continue titration to a blue endpoint.

"Each milliliter of 0.05 M disodium EDTA is equivalent to 5.55 mg of calcium chloride (CaCl₂)."

Fluoride — Ion-Selective Electrode Method:

Sodium fluoride solution (5 µg/mL): Transfer 2.210 g of sodium fluoride (dried at 200° for 4 h) into a 400-mL plastic beaker, add 200 mL water, stir until dissolved. Quantitatively transfer to 1000-mL volumetric flask, dilute to volume. Store in plastic bottle. On day of use, transfer 5.0 mL to 1000-mL flask, dilute to volume.

Calibration curve: Transfer 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 10.0, and 15.0 mL of sodium fluoride solution into separate 250-mL plastic beakers. Add 50 mL water, 5 mL of 1 N HCl, 10 mL of 1 M sodium citrate, and 10 mL of 0.2 M disodium EDTA to each beaker and mix. Transfer each to 100-mL volumetric flask, dilute to volume. Measure potential of 50-mL portions with fluoride ion-selective electrode. Plot on two-cycle semi-logarithmic paper or with a calculator/spreadsheet.

Analysis: Transfer 1.00 g sample into 150-mL glass beaker, add 10 mL water, slowly add 20 mL of 1 N HCl while stirring. Boil rapidly for 1 min, transfer to 250-mL plastic beaker, cool in ice water. Add 15 mL of 1 M sodium citrate and 10 mL of 0.2 M disodium EDTA, adjust pH to 5.5 ± 0.1. Transfer to 100-mL volumetric flask, dilute to volume. Measure 50-mL portion with fluoride ISE.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Principle of EDTA Titration of CaCl₂

EDTA complexometric titration: EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) forms stable 1:1 chelates with metal ions. Ca²⁺ reacts with EDTA to form a stable CaEDTA complex, and each mL of 0.05 M EDTA corresponds exactly to 5.55 mg of CaCl₂.

Hydroxy naphthol blue indicator: Under strongly alkaline conditions (after adding NaOH), it binds Ca²⁺ and appears purple-red; when all the Ca²⁺ has been titrated by EDTA, the free indicator turns blue, giving a clear endpoint.

CaCl₂ content limit (93.0%–100.5%): An upper limit above 100% is reasonable because the sample may contain slight excesses of the CaCl₂·nH₂O form, while the calculation is based on anhydrous CaCl₂ (MW 110.98).

Analogy: EDTA Titration Is Like a Precise "Metal Catcher"

EDTA is like a six-clawed trap: every calcium ion that touches it is firmly grasped, one to one, no more and no less. Hydroxy naphthol blue acts as the "alarm": as long as free Ca²⁺ remains in the solution, it keeps the red alarm on; only when the last Ca²⁺ has been captured (bound by EDTA) does the alarm turn blue, announcing that the titration is complete.

Formula: Fluoride Calculation

Fluoride content calculation:

Result = (C / W_S) × F × 100

C  = amount of fluoride found from the calibration curve (µg)
W_S = sample weight (g)
F  = conversion factor µg → g = 0.000001

Example: if C = 38 µg and W_S = 1.00 g
Result = (38 / 1.00) × 0.000001 × 100
       = 0.0038%
Acceptance criterion: NMT 0.004% ✓

Key Notes: Importance of the Fluoride Test

Why does calcium chloride need a fluoride test? CaCl₂ is often manufactured from limestone (CaCO₃), and limestone deposits usually contain fluorite (CaF₂) impurities. At high concentrations (>4 mg/L, the drinking water standard), fluoride is toxic to bones and teeth. Even though the amount migrating from pharmaceutical packaging is small, USP still sets a strict upper limit of 0.004%.

Advantages of the electrode method: The ion-selective electrode (ISE) method is faster and more accurate than traditional distillation and can directly determine free fluoride ion concentration in complex matrices; EDTA and sodium citrate serve as "ionic strength adjusters" to ensure consistent test conditions.

Plastic containers must be used: Silicates from glass containers interfere with ISE measurement of fluoride ions, so plastic containers must be used throughout the fluoride test.

Practical Application

In the QA department of a CDMO, you receive a batch of anhydrous calcium chloride desiccant, and the supplier's CoA shows Assay = 95.5%, pH = 7.2, Moisture adsorption = 30.1% at 80% RH. Does this material comply with USP <670>? Which data need further attention?

Analysis: The Assay (95.5%) meets 93–100.5%; the pH (7.2) meets 4.5–11.0; the adsorption capacity (30.1%) meets NLT 28%. However, the CoA data for arsenic (≤3 ppm), fluoride (≤0.004%), lead (≤5 ppm), and magnesium and alkali salts (≤5.0%) must also be checked.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Loss on Ignition vs. Loss on Drying

Loss on Drying (LOD): Drying at a relatively low temperature (typically 105°–145°), mainly removing water and volatile substances; it measures an approximation of the "moisture content".

Loss on Ignition (LOI): Igniting at a very high temperature (calcium oxide: 1100 ± 50°!), removing water, CO₂ (CaCO₃→CaO + CO₂), and other volatile components; it measures the "loss on ignition" of the material.

Why does CaO need LOI rather than LOD? Because CaO rapidly absorbs water from the air to form Ca(OH)₂, which then reacts with CO₂ to form CaCO₃. LOI at 1100° ensures that these conversion products are fully restored to CaO, so the true content of pure CaO is measured.

Key Notes: The Dual Identity of Activated Carbon

Activated carbon has two roles in USP <670>:

  • As a desiccant: it adsorbs moisture, and the tests include LOD, ROI, adsorption capacity, water extractables, and higher aromatic hydrocarbons
  • As an odor adsorbent: it must additionally pass the Butane Activity test, described in detail in Section 01

"Made by carbonization of plant material": Activated carbon must come from plant-based raw materials (not mineral-based), which affects its pore structure and adsorption characteristics.

Analogy: The Higher Aromatic Hydrocarbons Test for Activated Carbon

If carbonization is incomplete during the manufacture of activated carbon, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs, such as benzo[a]pyrene) may remain, and these are known carcinogens. The "higher aromatic hydrocarbons" test is like a "carcinogen residue screen" for activated carbon.

Quinine fluoresces strongly blue under UV light and serves as the standard reference. If the fluorescence of the sample under UV does not exceed that of the quinine reference, the residues of carcinogenic aromatic hydrocarbons are within a safe range. The concentration of only 83 ng/mL (ppb level) shows that this is an extremely sensitive safety protection test.

Procedure:
- Sample: about 5 g (accurately weighed, recorded as W)
- After refluxing, take 25.0 mL (of 100 mL total filtrate)

Key Notes: Comparing the Adsorption Capacity of CaO and CaCl₂

Calcium oxide (CaO) adsorbs water by chemical reaction, not physical adsorption:

CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (slaking of lime); this reaction is exothermic and irreversible (it cannot be regenerated). This is also why the adsorption capacity of CaO desiccant (NLT 28% at 80% RH) is the same as that of CaCl₂, yet it cannot be regenerated after use and must be discarded.

Comparison of desiccant "regenerability": Silica gel, bentonite, and molecular sieves can be regenerated by heating; activated carbon is partially regenerable; CaCl₂ is regenerable to a limited extent; CaO is not regenerable at all.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Why Molecular Sieves Are Tested for LOD at 575°

Why must the Loss on Drying of molecular sieves be performed at 575 ± 25°?

Ordinary LOD (105°) removes only free surface water, but the adsorbed water in the crystalline channels of molecular sieves (channel water) needs 500°+ to be fully desorbed. The "weight loss" measured at low temperature does not truly reflect the amount of water the molecular sieves can adsorb, so USP specifically prescribes the high-temperature LOD condition.

This also explains why the LOD limit for molecular sieves is NMT 4.5%: this figure means "fails only when close to saturation", not "fails if it contains a small amount of moisture".

Analogy: The Conductivity Test for Silica Gel

The "soluble ionizable salts" test for silica gel is like measuring the "TDS (Total Dissolved Solids)" of mineral water. The higher the conductivity, the more ions are dissolved in the water and the more impurities, such as sodium sulfate, remain in the silica gel.

Na₂SO₄ is used as the control solution (standard): if the conductivity of the sample does not exceed that of the control (1 mg/mL Na₂SO₄), the soluble salts are within an acceptable range. This is a quick, indirect screening method for "ionic impurities".

W₂ = final residue weight after volatilizing the SiO₂ by HF treatment

Silica gel adsorption capacity is tested at both humidity conditions: Silica gel is a physical adsorbent whose adsorption capacity increases linearly with humidity, so specifications are set at both low (40%) and high (80%) humidity. For molecular sieves, the adsorption capacity at low humidity (40%) is actually more important, because this is their key advantage over silica gel (they still dry effectively in a low-humidity environment).

pH 4–8 of silica gel: Silica gel is mildly acidic and may have a slight effect on certain pH-sensitive drug products (for example, drugs that need a mildly alkaline environment), so compatibility should be evaluated at selection.

Key Concepts: Silicate Reaction Mechanism of Ammonium Molybdate TS

Chemistry of silica gel identification: Under acidic conditions, silicate ions (SiO₄⁴⁻) react with molybdate (MoO₄²⁻) to form "silicomolybdic acid" (H₄SiMo₁₂O₄₀), giving a vivid deep yellow color. This is a color reaction specific to silicon.

Key Notes: GMP Implications of Solution Management

"Protect from light, and if a precipitate forms use only the clear solution": Under light and oxidizing conditions, ammonium molybdate is easily reduced to dark blue molybdenum blue (MoO₂) and loses its yellow color-forming function. This preparation and storage requirement directly affects the reliability of the test results.

Good documentation practice (GDocP) requirements under GMP: All laboratory solutions must have a preparation date, expiry date, preparer's signature, and storage conditions. The "48-hour standing period before use" for ammonium molybdate TS is a required procedure under USP and must not be shortened. During QA audits, solution preparation records are a key item checked.

Analogy: Test Solutions Are Like "Perishable Ingredients"

In laboratory management, such solutions need a designated person responsible for regular preparation and expiry control, and this is a common audit check point in QA inspections.

Volatiles Organic Compounds (VOC) Adsorption Power

Butane Activity

This method determines the ability of carbon, activated to adsorb butane from dry air under controlled conditions and provides an indication of the pore size of carbon, activated for air/vapor applications. Method:

Dry the sample of charcoal, activated as per 〈731〉 and determine the bulk density (Bulk Density and Tapped Density of Powders 〈616〉).   • D = mass of butane-saturated carbon, sample tube, and stoppers
  • C = mass of carbon, sample tube, and stoppers (before butane exposure)
  • M = mass of sample tube and stoppers alone (tare weight)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Scientific Principle of Butane Activity

Butane Activity: Uses n-butane (n-C₄H₁₀) as a surrogate molecule to measure the adsorption capacity of activated carbon for volatile organic compounds (VOCs). The butane molecule (kinetic diameter about 4.3 Å) can enter the mesopores (2–50 nm) and macropores (>50 nm) of the activated carbon, and so represents the overall adsorption performance of the activated carbon for VOCs in general.

Why sample by "volume" rather than "weight" (16.70 ± 0.05 mL)? Because the density of activated carbon can differ between batches. Using a fixed volume (rather than a fixed weight) ensures the same bed height in every test, so that flow rate and contact time are reproducible and the test results are comparable.

Cross-reference to ASTM standards: This test method is based on ASTM D5742-95, "Standard Test Method for Determination of Butane Activity of Activated Carbon". USP directly adopts this ASTM industry standard, showing the harmonization of the two standards systems.

Formula: Butane Activity Calculation

Butane activity calculation:

% Butane Activity = [(D − C) / (C − M)] × 100

D = weight of (carbon + tube + plug) after adsorption to saturation (g)
C = weight of (carbon + tube + plug) before adsorption (g)
M = weight of the empty tube + plug (tare weight) (g)

Numerator: D − C = mass of butane adsorbed
Denominator: C − M = mass of the activated carbon sample itself

Acceptance criterion: NLT 17.6%

Calculation example:
M = 28.50 g (empty tube + plug)
C = 42.35 g (carbon + tube + plug, before adsorption)
D = 44.78 g (after saturation)

Carbon mass = C − M = 42.35 − 28.50 = 13.85 g
Butane adsorbed = D − C = 44.78 − 42.35 = 2.43 g
% BA = (2.43 / 13.85) × 100 = 17.55%
→ Fails (NLT 17.6%, short by 0.05%)

Analogy: Butane Activity Is Like a "Sponge Oil-Absorption Test"

The butane activity test is like placing a kitchen sponge in an oily environment to see how many times its own weight in oil it can absorb. The "pore structure" of the sponge determines its oil absorption capacity, just as the pore size structure of activated carbon determines its VOC adsorption capacity.

Testing with a "fixed volume (16.70 mL)" is like using a "fixed-size piece of sponge" every time: only by keeping the geometry consistent can the oil absorption of different sponge brands be compared fairly. The design of repeating the test at 10-minute intervals until it is "saturated" (weight no longer changes) ensures that the true upper limit of adsorption capacity is measured.

Key Notes: Safety Is the Biggest Challenge of This Test

Hazards of n-butane: Highly flammable (LEL: 1.8%), can act as an anesthetic, and can cause ventricular fibrillation and asphyxiation. USP specifically flags it with a [Caution] and requires work in a fume hood and away from ignition sources.

Laboratory safety precautions:

  • Must be carried out in a well-ventilated fume hood
  • No open flames or electrical sparks nearby
  • A CO₂ or dry powder fire extinguisher must be available
  • The 1.9 m heat exchange coil ensures the butane reaches the water bath temperature (25°C) before entering the sample, avoiding result bias caused by uneven temperature

Significance of the accelerated 2024 revision: The lower limit for butane activity was updated from the previous version to NLT 17.6% (with the revision location marked by the ▲ symbol), indicating that the USP expert committee tightened the specification in line with the latest industry practice.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Harmonization of ASTM and USP

USP <670> cites several ASTM standards:

  • ASTM D276: Identification of textile fibers (cotton, rayon, polyester)
  • ASTM D5745: Determination of butane activity of activated carbon (odor adsorbent test)
  • ASTM D5742-95: The earlier version of the standard for butane activity of activated carbon

This cross-referencing reflects the openness of the pharmaceutical industry: USP does not rewrite test methods for which industry standards already exist, but cites them directly and, where necessary (for example for drug safety reasons), adds or strengthens requirements. This greatly reduces the cost of standard setting and also ensures the methods are recognized by industry.

Key Notes: Flexibility of "Suitable" Equipment

"A suitable [equipment]... may be used" appears many times in USP (footnotes 4–9), and this is an important flexibility in the design of USP:

  • Use of a specific brand of instrument is not mandatory
  • Companies may use any suitable equipment that achieves the same result
  • But the suitability of the equipment used must be demonstrated (method validation)

For a CDMO laboratory, when equipment is changed, the equivalence of performance must be evaluated and the evaluation documented; this is a core regulatory compliance requirement.

Analogy: Footnotes Are the "Technical Support Phone Number"

The footnotes of a USP chapter are like the "technical support contact information" on the last page of an instruction manual: the main text tells you "how to do it", while the footnotes tell you "where to buy the materials/equipment" and "where to look for more information".

Key Concepts: Using the Quick-Reference Table in Decisions

Key Notes: Distinctive Specification Points of Each Desiccant

Points of difference that need particular attention:

  • The lead limit for CaO (NMT 2 mg/kg) is the strictest of all desiccants, almost half that of CaCl₂ (5 ppm)
  • The fluoride limit for CaO (NMT 0.015%) is 3.75 times looser than that for CaCl₂ (NMT 0.004%)
  • The LOD of molecular sieves is at 575°, far higher than for the other desiccants (105–145°), and it is the only desiccant that uses a high-temperature LOD
  • Silica gel is the only desiccant with an Assay requirement (NLT 94% SiO₂); the others have only identification and no content specification
  • Activated carbon is the only desiccant that measures both organic contaminants (higher aromatic hydrocarbons, water extractables) and inorganic contaminants (heavy metals)

Analogy: The Regulatory Framework Is a "Multi-Layer Filter"

The acceptance criteria system of USP <670> is like a multi-layer filter: the first layer (identification tests) confirms it is the right material; the second layer (purity/impurity tests) ensures the material is sufficiently pure; the third layer (functional tests) verifies that the material has sufficient working capacity.

None of the three layers can be omitted: a desiccant that passes identification but has insufficient content cannot protect the drug product; a desiccant that passes content but exceeds heavy metal limits may harm patients.

Practical Application: CDMO Procurement Audit Scenario

Suppose you are responsible for auditing a desiccant supplier that supplies molecular sieves (type 4A) for the packaging of your oral solid dosage form product. The following is your audit checklist (based on USP <670>):

  • Are there test records within the last 1 year for Identification A (silicic acid residue) and Identification B (amphoteric precipitation of aluminum)?
  • Is the LOD tested at 575 ± 25° (rather than the ordinary 105°), with results ≤4.5%?
  • Is the adsorption capacity tested under both 40% RH (≥15%) and 80% RH (≥16.5%)?
  • Is the lead content (≤5 ppm) determined using the USP <233> method, with the standard prepared within 3 days?
  • Is the pH within the range 6.5–12 (200 mg/mL in CO₂-free water)?
  • Do the adsorption capacity test records include the raw weighing data and documentation of the equilibrium criterion (≤3 mg/g difference)?

Complete Chapter Summary: USP <670> Auxiliary Packaging Components

  • Ammonium molybdate TS: Must stand for 48 hours before use, be protected from light, and be verified for activity with dibasic sodium phosphate TS before use (a yellow precipitate forms immediately); it is the specific reagent for the identification of SiO₂ in silica gel
  • Butane activity test for activated carbon as an odor adsorbent: Based on ASTM D5742-95, using a fixed volume (16.70 mL) of sample, a butane flow rate of 250 mL/min, and a 25°C water bath, repeated at 10-minute intervals until constant weight (±0.02 g); calculation formula = [(D-C)/(C-M)] × 100, acceptance criterion NLT 17.6% (updated in 2024)
  • Dual citation of USP <670> and ASTM: ASTM D276 (fiber identification), D5745, and D5742-95 (butane activity) reflect the spirit of standards harmonization; the principle that "suitable equipment may be used" gives companies flexibility in equipment selection, but method validation is required
  • The most important differences across the chapter: The three coil types are distinguished by staining color (green / blue-green / light orange); the six desiccants each have their own LOD temperature (105°–575°) and adsorption specifications; activated carbon as an odor adsorbent must additionally pass the butane activity test; rayon coil carries a warning about the risk of gelatin cross-linking
