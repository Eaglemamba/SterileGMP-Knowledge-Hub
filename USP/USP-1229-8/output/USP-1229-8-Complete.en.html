Learning Objectives

  • Understand the principle of dry heat sterilization: hot air (≥160°C) serves as the heat-transfer medium, suitable for heat-resistant items that cannot be steam sterilized

  • Understand the four validation steps: equipment qualification, empty chamber temperature distribution, load mapping, heat penetration + microbial challenge
  • Know the rationale for selecting Bacillus atrophaeus (ATCC 9372) as the biological indicator and how to use it
  • Understand the functional overlap and differences between dry heat sterilization and depyrogenation under ⟨1228.1⟩

Introduction

Original Text

Dry heat sterilization is a process utilized for heat-stable items (glass, stainless steel, nonaqueous liquids, powders, etc.) that are unsuited for steam sterilization because of either an absence of water (nonaqueous liquids and powders) or requirements for absolute dryness following processing (product contact parts for nonaqueous products). Because dry heat relies on air for the transfer of heat to and from the load items, the process takes longer than a steam process for a comparable size item or load. Lengthy heating and cooling periods require that the load items be unaffected by heat over a long period of time and also require the use of the overkill method for cycle development and validation.

Dry heat sterilization is typically performed in the range of 160°–190° where the objective is sterilization rather than depyrogenation. (Depyrogenation will be covered separately in Dry Heat Depyrogenation ⟨1228.1⟩). In dry heat sterilization, hot air is in direct contact with the load items (whether wrapped or unwrapped) and transfers some of its thermal energy. Unlike steam sterilization, in dry heat sterilization there is no phase change of the heating medium, and thus heat transfer is less efficient. The items can be stainless steel, glass, ceramic, or other heat-stable materials and may be wrapped or covered with aluminum foil to protect them during pre- and postprocess handling. Dry heat sterilization is commonly used for heat-stable materials (e.g., petrolatum or powders).

The limited heat transfer capacity of air requires that items in the oven be placed in locations that were confirmed to be acceptable during the validation effort. Manufacturers should exercise caution with varying load sizes because in some instances (resulting from system design and control probe positioning) minimum load sizes may present a worst case.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dry heat sterilization: a sterilization method that kills microorganisms with high-temperature air (rather than steam). Typical applications include:

  • Glass containers — vials, ampoules, syringes
  • Stainless steel parts — filling needles, funnels, tubing connectors
  • Non-aqueous liquids — petrolatum, mineral oil
  • Powders — active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) powders (must be sterilized in the dry state)

Why not use steam? Steam sterilization (121°C, 15 min) is more efficient, but it leaves moisture on the item surface. For product-contact surfaces used with anhydrous formulations, even a trace of residual moisture can compromise product stability.

Key Notes

Dry heat vs steam — the physical basis of the efficiency difference:

Steam sterilization relies on the latent heat of phase change: each gram of steam releases about 2,260 joules as it condenses, delivering that energy directly to the item surface. Dry heat relies only on convective heat transfer through air, and the specific heat capacity of air is far lower than that of steam, so a higher temperature and a longer time are needed to achieve the same lethality.

Minimum load = worst case: this point is easily overlooked. In an oven, when the load is too small, the control probe may not detect the true temperature change inside the chamber (because the load absorbs too little heat), so the system may judge the cycle complete too early. Validation must test both the maximum and the minimum load.

CDMO Practical Perspective

The dual function of a depyrogenation tunnel: on a CDMO aseptic filling line, the depyrogenation tunnel operates at ≥250°C, well above the dry heat sterilization range (160–190°C). This means the tunnel achieves sterilization + depyrogenation at the same time. However, ⟨1229.8⟩ focuses on dry heat sterilization, not depyrogenation; the validation requirements for depyrogenation (≥3 log endotoxin reduction) are covered separately in ⟨1228.1⟩.

Sterilization Cycle Control

Original Text

Process equipment for dry heat sterilization is controlled by calibrated temperature sensors. During the exposure portions of the cycle, attainment of a minimum dwell time at a predefined temperature is used to document process lethality. Cycle efficacy for dry heat sterilization customarily is measured using FH, which typically is defined as the amount of time the load receives the equivalent of exposure at 170°. The FH approach is used to compare sterilization processes that operate at varying temperature conditions to a single standard. The process lethality at temperatures other than 170° can be calculated to determine lethality equivalent to that provided at 170°.

Sterilizer control systems must deliver conditions within a predefined time–temperature or FH range. Simple mathematics can be used to calculate the total lethality over the course of the process.

Accumulation of the lethality (FH) for the sterilization process across the entire cycle (heat-up and cool-down segments included) includes the contribution of those segments and allows the cycle to be defined by a targeted lethality rather than by a time at a defined minimum temperature.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: FH Value

FH (dry heat lethality): the measure of lethality for dry heat sterilization. Conceptually it is similar to F0 for steam sterilization, but the reference conditions differ:

What a z-value of 20°C means: for every 20°C increase in temperature, the sterilization rate increases 10-fold. For example, 1 minute at 190°C is equivalent to 10 minutes at 170°C.

FH Calculation — Lethality Formula

Original Text

For the specific reference temperature of 170° and a z-value (for definitions see Steam Sterilization by Direct Contact ⟨1229.1⟩) of 20°, the FH calculation can be determined by the following equation:

FH Equation (Integral Form)

         t2
F_H  =  ∫   10^((T - 170) / 20) dt
         t1

Discrete summation form:

         t2
F_H  =  ∑   10^((T - 170) / 20) × Δt
         t1

Where:

  • FH = accumulated lethality (equivalent minutes at 170°C)
  • t1 = start time
  • t2 = end time
  • T = temperature (°C) at each time interval
  • Δt = time interval (typically 1 minute)

Tutorial Commentary

Formula Breakdown

Core logic: the temperature reading for every minute of the entire dry heat cycle (including the heat-up and cool-down phases) is converted into equivalent "minutes at 170°C", and these are then summed.

Worked example:

Suppose the temperature in a given minute is T = 180°C:
  L = 10^((180 - 170) / 20) = 10^(0.5) = 3.16

Meaning: 1 minute at 180°C
     = 3.16 minutes at 170°C

Suppose the temperature in another minute is T = 160°C:
  L = 10^((160 - 170) / 20) = 10^(-0.5) = 0.316

Meaning: 1 minute at 160°C
     = only 0.316 minutes at 170°C

Key Notes

Contribution of the heat-up and cool-down phases: the original text states explicitly that the FH calculation covers the entire cycle (heat-up and cool-down segments included). This means the heat-up and cool-down phases also deliver lethality and can be counted toward the total. This is especially important for oven operation, because the heat-up and cool-down times of an oven may account for 40–60% of the total cycle.

Difference from FH for depyrogenation: depyrogenation under ⟨1228.1⟩ uses the same form of formula, but the reference temperature is 250°C, the z-value is 46.4°C, and the acceptance criterion is different (≥3 log endotoxin reduction). Do not confuse the two.

Validation of Dry Heat Sterilization

Original Text

Because dry air has limited heat capacity and dry heat conditions are more variable than those encountered with other thermal sterilization methods, analysts routinely validate their dry heat sterilization procedures using the overkill method as defined in Sterilization of Compendial Articles ⟨1229⟩.

Overkill sterilization can be defined as a method in which the destruction of a high concentration of a resistant microorganism supports the destruction of reasonably anticipated bioburden that could be present during routine processing. That objective can be demonstrated by attaining any of the following: a defined minimum lethality; a defined set of method conditions; or confirmation of minimum log reduction of a resistant biological indicator.

The validation requirements for the overkill method are less onerous than those of the other sterilization approaches. Because the load items can withstand substantial amounts of heat without adverse consequence, the greater lethality provided by the overkill method clearly is justifiable.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Overkill Method

Overkill method: dry heat sterilization is almost always validated using the overkill method, for a simple reason: the items being sterilized (glass, metal) are extremely heat resistant and can easily withstand far more heat than the minimum required. This contrasts with steam sterilization of heat-sensitive products, which must be protected from excess heat.

Three ways to demonstrate overkill:

  • Define a minimum lethality — for example, FH ≥ 30 minutes (170°C equivalent)
  • Define the process conditions — for example, 170°C / 120 minutes
  • Confirm a minimum log reduction — for example, ≥12 log kill of the biological indicator (6 log BI population + 6 log safety margin)

Analogy

Using a cannon to kill a mosquito: the logic of overkill is like using a cannon to kill a mosquito. A fly swatter might be enough, but since the cannon will not damage your furniture (the heat-resistant items), why not use it to be absolutely safe? This "excess assurance" strategy greatly simplifies the validation documentation, because you do not need to determine the bioburden count and resistance precisely.

Equipment Qualification

Original Text

Equipment qualification is a predefined program that focuses on the sterilizing equipment to confirm that it has been properly installed and operates as intended before evaluation of the sterilization process. In some companies, equipment qualification is separated into installation qualification and operational qualification or is lumped together under a joint terminology of installation/operational qualification. The major use of qualification of the sterilizing equipment is to provide a baseline for preventive maintenance and change control, ensuring reproducibility of operation over time and assurance that the sterilization process is constantly and accurately performed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: IQ/OQ Framework

Purpose of equipment qualification: it is not to prove that "sterilization is effective" (that is the job of PQ), but to establish the operating baseline of the equipment and ensure it is installed correctly and operates according to design specifications.

IQ (Installation Qualification): confirms the equipment is installed according to specifications — power supply, piping connections, safety devices, control system software version, and so on.

OQ (Operational Qualification): confirms the equipment operates properly across its operating range — temperature control accuracy, heating rate, air velocity uniformity, alarm functions, and so on.

Why establish a baseline? The data generated during equipment qualification become the reference for later preventive maintenance (PM) and change control. For example, if the original OQ record shows a heating rate of 5°C/min and a later PM test finds it has dropped to 3°C/min, this triggers an investigation.

Empty Chamber Temperature Distribution

Original Text

The equipment should be evaluated for empty chamber temperature distribution. The oven or tunnel should be evaluated to determine the range of temperatures within the system, and the cycle parameters should be determined to ensure adequate lethality across the expected load. The acceptance criteria for empty chambers can vary with the equipment capabilities and customary use, but it is typically less uniform than observed in steam sterilizers. Biological indicators are not required during the evaluation of empty chamber temperature distribution.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

"Less uniform than steam sterilizers": this is a reality the original text acknowledges directly. In a steam sterilizer, saturated steam spreads quickly through the chamber, and temperature uniformity is usually within ±0.5°C. A dry heat oven relies on air convection, and uniformity may be ±5°C or even wider. This is also why dry heat sterilization uses the overkill approach — the larger temperature variation requires a larger safety margin to compensate.

BIs are not needed: the empty chamber test measures only physical parameters (temperature distribution), so no biological indicators are placed. BIs are used later, in the heat penetration phase.

CDMO practice — tunnel vs oven:

  • Tunnel: the empty chamber test focuses on the temperature gradient of each heating zone and the relationship between conveyor speed and temperature
  • Oven: focuses on temperature differences between locations in the chamber (corners, center, upper and lower shelves)
Component Mapping

Original Text

Load items that are complex and feature enclosed volumes and product contact surfaces should be subjected to component mapping to determine internal cold spots. This is particularly important in powder sterilization. For each load item, manufacturers should establish the ability of heat to penetrate the items or containers and to bring them to the required temperature. These studies can be performed in a laboratory setting and need not be repeated when the same item is sterilized in other equipment. Thermocouples should be placed into direct contact with the item(s) being evaluated. During component mapping load items should be prepared and oriented in a manner that is consistent with how they will be processed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Component Mapping

Component mapping: a heat penetration study of a single item, whose purpose is to find the cold spot inside the item — that is, the location that is slowest to reach the target temperature.

When is it needed? When the item has a complex structure, enclosed spaces, or product-contact surfaces. For example:

  • Powder containers — powders conduct heat poorly, so the temperature at the center of the container may be far lower than at the surface
  • Multi-layer wrapped metal parts — filling needle assemblies wrapped in aluminum foil
  • Complex geometries — stainless steel parts with internal cavities

Transferability: the original text notes that component mapping results can be used across equipment, because the study measures the heat penetration characteristics of the item itself, independent of any particular equipment. This is very valuable for a CDMO: one study can apply to multiple production lines.

Load Mapping

Original Text

Fixed loading patterns for dry heat sterilization in batch ovens are preferable because the limited heat capacity of the air allows substantial temperature differences across the load. It may be possible to validate maximum and minimum loads as determined by either the number of items or their mass within the oven. Loading in a continuous tunnel process is typically well defined by the limitations of the conveying system. Load and component mapping ensures that all load items attain the required temperature. Information from the load mapping is used to adjust cycle timing to ensure appropriate lethality. System control must consider the relationship between load position and size relative to temperature control locations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

Fixed loading pattern: the original text emphasizes that an oven should use a fixed loading pattern. In CDMO practice, this means a loading SOP must be established, with photographs or diagrams that clearly define the position and quantity of items for each batch. If operators load items arbitrarily, the validation results are meaningless.

Key Concepts: Load Mapping

Load mapping: tests the temperature distribution of the entire load in the equipment to identify the worst-case condition for the load as a whole.

Biological Indicators

Original Text

The biological indicator (BI) for dry heat sterilization is Bacillus atrophaeus (ATCC 9372), a thermophilic spore-former with high resistance to dry heat. The spore challenge is placed on a substrate positioned within the load or on a load item. If spores are used as intended by the BI manufacturer, the population and resistance information provided by the vendor can be used. End users should determine the population and resistance of their biological indicator used when inoculating their own items.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

Supplier BIs vs self-inoculation: the original text distinguishes two ways of using BIs:

  • Using supplier-provided BIs — the population and resistance data provided by the supplier (D-value, population) can be used directly, without your own determination
  • Self-inoculation onto items — you must determine the actual population and resistance after inoculation yourself, because a change of substrate may affect the dry heat resistance of the spores

CDMO practical recommendation: unless there is a special need, use commercially available supplier BIs (such as spore strips or spore discs), which greatly reduce testing effort and documentation burden.

Key Concepts: BI Selection

Bacillus atrophaeus (ATCC 9372): the biological indicator strain dedicated to dry heat sterilization. It was chosen because its spores have extremely high resistance to dry heat and are among the hardest of the microorganisms likely to be encountered in the natural environment to kill.

Heat Penetration & Microbiological Challenge

Original Text

The core of the validation activity is the confirmation of acceptable heat penetration using temperature measurements and microbial challenges. Thermocouples and BIs are placed within the load items at the locations determined during the component and load mapping to present the worst case. Thermocouples should be placed into direct contact with the item(s) being monitored. Proof of cycle efficacy is provided by replicate studies in which the BIs are killed and the physical measurements correspond to the expected values of time–temperature or FH.

If the microbial and physical measurements do not correlate, manufacturers should conduct an investigation and should take corrective action to rectify the discrepancy. This study customarily is performed slightly subminimal to the lower specification limits for time, temperature, and/or cumulative lethality.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Validation Core

Heat penetration + microbiological challenge = the core of validation: this is the essence of PQ (Performance Qualification). The two data sets must be collected simultaneously and cross-checked:

  • Physical data (thermocouples) — demonstrate that every test location reaches the target temperature and FH
  • Microbiological data (BIs) — demonstrate that all BIs are killed under worst-case conditions

If the physical data show the criteria are met but a BI is not killed, or the physical data show the criteria are not met but all BIs are dead, an investigation must be started. Such an inconsistency may indicate incorrect thermocouple placement, improper BI placement, or an unknown problem in the process.

Key Notes: Subminimal Approach

"Slightly subminimal": this is the key validation strategy. Rather than testing under normal conditions, you deliberately shorten the time or lower the temperature to near (but slightly below) the lower operating specification. If all BIs are still killed under this "half-dose" condition, it demonstrates an adequate safety margin under normal operating conditions.

Why not simply test under normal conditions? Because normal conditions deliver excess lethality, which may mask cold-spot problems in the load. A half-cycle test is more sensitive for detecting uneven temperature distribution.

Routine Process Control

Original Text

As with all sterilization processes, after the dry heat sterilization process has been validated, it must be subject to formalized controls that keep it in a validated state over time. General chapter Sterilization of Compendial Articles ⟨1229⟩ details the general practices that are appropriate for all sterilization systems. This is accomplished by a number of related practices that are essential for the continued use of the process over an extended period of time. The essential practices to maintain validated status include calibration, physical measurements, physical integrators and indicators, ongoing process control, change control, preventive maintenance, and periodic reassessment and training.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Maintaining the Validated State

Validation is not a one-time event: passing PQ is only the starting point. The original text lists seven elements for maintaining the validated state:

  1. Calibration — periodic calibration of temperature sensors to ensure accurate readings
  2. Physical measurements — time–temperature data recorded for every run
  3. Physical integrators/indicators — chemical indicator tape, temperature indicator labels, and so on
  4. Ongoing process control — monitoring trends and detecting drift
  5. Change control — any change to equipment or process must be assessed for its impact on the validated state
  6. Preventive maintenance — performed as scheduled, not deferred until the equipment fails
  7. Periodic reassessment & training — periodic review of process performance and ongoing training of operators

CDMO Practical Perspective

Change control pitfalls: in a CDMO environment, situations in which change control is especially easy to overlook include:

  • Changing the vial supplier — different glass materials may have different heat-transfer characteristics
  • Changing the BI lot or supplier — the D-value of a new lot may differ
  • Changing heating elements or fans — affects temperature distribution and air circulation patterns
  • Modifying the loading pattern — even a change in item spacing

Any of the above changes should trigger the change control process to assess whether revalidation is needed.

Chapter Summary

  • Scope of dry heat sterilization: heat-resistant items that are unsuitable for steam sterilization (glass, metal, powders, non-aqueous liquids), operating at 160–190°C
  • FH calculation: reference temperature 170°C and z-value 20°C; the calculation covers the cumulative lethality of the entire cycle, including heat-up, hold, and cool-down
  • Validation strategy: because the items are highly heat resistant, the overkill method is almost always used, which simplifies validation requirements
  • Four validation steps: equipment qualification (IQ/OQ) → empty chamber temperature distribution → component mapping + load mapping → heat penetration + microbiological challenge (PQ)
  • BI selection: Bacillus atrophaeus (ATCC 9372) — the indicator organism dedicated to dry heat sterilization, highly resistant to dry heat
  • Subminimal testing: validation performed under conditions slightly below the lower specification limit, to ensure an adequate safety margin in normal operation
  • Relationship to ⟨1228.1⟩: a depyrogenation tunnel (≥250°C) achieves sterilization + depyrogenation at the same time; this chapter covers only sterilization at 160–190°C
  • Maintaining the validated state: calibration, process monitoring, change control, preventive maintenance, and periodic reassessment — all seven elements are essential
