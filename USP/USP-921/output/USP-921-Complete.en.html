Learning Objectives

  • Understand the three main water determination methods in USP <921>: Method I (titrimetric), Method II (azeotropic distillation) and Method III (gravimetric, loss on drying)
  • Master the chemistry of the Karl Fischer reagent: the quantitative reaction of sulfur dioxide and iodine in the presence of water
  • Distinguish when to use Method Ia (direct titration), Method Ib (residual titration) and Method Ic (coulometric titration)
  • Be familiar with the calculation for standardization of the Karl Fischer reagent: F = W/V
  • Master the key points for controlling accuracy in each method: excluding atmospheric moisture, judging the electrode endpoint, and sample preparation
  • Understand the difference between Method I (Water) and Loss on Drying <731>: the former measures water only, the latter measures total volatile matter
Section 1: Introduction & Method Overview

Original Text

INTRODUCTION

Many Pharmacopeial articles either are hydrates or contain water in adsorbed form. As a result, the determination of the water content is important in demonstrating compliance with the Pharmacopeial standards. Generally one of the methods given below is called for in the individual monograph, depending upon the nature of the article. In rare cases, a choice is allowed between two methods.

When the article contains water of hydration, Method I (Titrimetric), Method II (Azeotropic), or Method III (Gravimetric) is employed, as directed in the individual monograph, and the requirement is given under the heading Water. Unless otherwise specified in the monograph, determine the water by Method Ia.

The heading Loss on Drying (see Loss on Drying 〈731〉) is used in those cases where the loss sustained on heating may be not entirely water.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Water vs. Loss on Drying

The difference between "Water" and "Loss on Drying" is like analyzing a wet sponge:

  • Water determination (Method I/II): measures only water molecules (H₂O), like measuring only the water the sponge has absorbed
  • Loss on Drying (<731>): measures everything that volatilizes on heating, including water, residual solvents and volatile excipients, like measuring the total weight the sponge loses after heating

If heating drives off more than water (for example, ethanol or methanol solvents are present), the test must be titled Loss on Drying rather than Water, because what is measured is not only water.

Key Notes

Default method, Method Ia: since the May 2022 update, unless the monograph specifies otherwise, water determination defaults to Method Ia (Karl Fischer direct titration).

Hydrates: for compounds containing water of crystallization (such as anhydrous sodium sulfate and glucose monohydrate), the water content is part of the quality attributes and affects potency calculations. If the API is a hydrate, the water specification usually has both lower and upper limits (rather than an upper limit only).

Practice Questions

  1. A pharmacopoeial monograph specifies "Loss on Drying: NMT 0.5%". Why is it not written as "Water: NMT 0.5%"? How do the two tests differ in analytical meaning?
  2. What is the theoretical water content of glucose monohydrate? If the measured value is clearly lower than this, what quality problem does it indicate?
Section 2: Method Ia - Karl Fischer Direct Titration

Original Text

METHOD I (TITRIMETRIC) — Method Ia (Direct Titration)

PRINCIPLE

The titrimetric determination of water is based upon the quantitative reaction of water with an anhydrous solution of sulfur dioxide and iodine in the presence of a buffer that reacts with hydrogen ions.

In the original titrimetric solution, known as Karl Fischer Reagent, the sulfur dioxide and iodine are dissolved in pyridine and methanol. The test specimen may be titrated with the Reagent directly, or the analysis may be carried out by a residual titration procedure. The stoichiometry of the reaction is not exact, and the reproducibility of a determination depends upon such factors as the relative concentrations of the Reagent ingredients, the nature of the inert solvent used to dissolve the test specimen, and the technique used in the particular determination. Therefore, an empirically standardized technique is used in order to achieve the desired accuracy. Precision in the method is governed largely by the extent to which atmospheric moisture is excluded from the system.

APPARATUS

Any apparatus may be used that provides for adequate exclusion of atmospheric moisture and determination of the endpoint. In the case of a colorless solution that is titrated directly, the endpoint may be observed visually as a change in color from canary yellow to amber. The reverse is observed in the case of a test specimen that is titrated residually. More commonly, the endpoint is determined electrometrically with an apparatus employing an electrical circuit and a pair of platinum electrodes immersed in the solution to be titrated. At the endpoint of the titration a slight excess of the reagent increases the flow of current, which is detected by the electrodes for not less than 30 s.

Commercially available apparatus generally comprises a closed system consisting of one or two automatic burets and a tightly covered titration vessel fitted with the necessary electrodes and a magnetic stirrer. The air in the system is kept dry with a suitable desiccant, and the titration vessel may be purged by means of a stream of dry nitrogen or current of dry air.

REAGENT

Prepare the Karl Fischer Reagent as follows. Add 125 g of iodine to a solution containing 670 mL of methanol and 170 mL of pyridine, and cool. Place 100 mL of pyridine in a 250-mL graduated cylinder, and, keeping the pyridine cold in an ice bath, pass in dry sulfur dioxide until the volume reaches 200 mL. Slowly add this solution, with shaking, to the cooled iodine mixture. Shake to dissolve the iodine, transfer the solution to the apparatus, and allow the solution to stand overnight before standardizing. When freshly prepared, 1 mL of this solution is equivalent to approximately 5 mg of water, but it deteriorates gradually; therefore, standardize it within 1 h before use, or daily if in continuous use. Protect from light while in use. Store any bulk stock of the reagent in a suitably sealed, glass-stoppered container, fully protected from light, and under refrigeration.

For determination of trace amounts of water (less than 1%), it is preferable to use a Reagent with a water equivalency factor of not more than 2.0, which will lead to the consumption of a more significant volume of titrant.

A commercially available, stabilized solution of Karl Fischer type reagent may be used. Commercially available reagents containing solvents or bases other than pyridine or alcohols other than methanol may be used also. These may be single solutions or reagents formed in situ by combining the components of the reagents present in two discrete solutions.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Karl Fischer Reaction Principle

Core chemistry of the Karl Fischer reaction (simplified):

I₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O + pyridine → 2HI + SO₃ (buffered by pyridine)

More precisely:

  • Iodine (I₂) oxidizes SO₂, consuming water molecules
  • Pyridine or another basic buffer neutralizes the HI formed, keeping the reaction moving forward
  • Methanol serves as the solvent and also takes part in the esterification of SO₂
  • The stoichiometry is not an exact whole-number ratio, so empirical standardization is required

Modern commercial reagents: pyridine-free formulations (such as the Hydranal series) use imidazole or other alternative buffers, which are less toxic and safer to handle.

Analogy: Endpoint Detection

Endpoint detection in Karl Fischer titration is like paying at a car park:

  • Visual method (golden yellow → amber): like watching a smoke signal. A color change means excess titrant; it is simple and intuitive but suitable only for colorless, clear samples
  • Electrometric method (dual platinum electrodes): like an electronic toll gate. It is accurate and objective; a small excess of iodine establishes a current between the electrodes (confirmed after 30 seconds), and it suits colored or opaque samples

The electrometric endpoint is more reliable and is standard equipment on modern instruments.

Key Notes

Reagent storage and use: Karl Fischer reagent is extremely sensitive to light and moisture, so: (1) standardize it before use, or daily; (2) protect it from light; (3) keep large stocks refrigerated; (4) keep the system sealed, using a drying tube or dry nitrogen.

Determination of trace water (<1%): choose a diluted reagent with a water equivalence factor (F) ≤2.0 mg/mL, to ensure that a sufficient volume of titrant is consumed (improving accuracy). Method Ic (coulometric) is better suited to very low water levels (0.5–5 mg of water).

Control of atmospheric moisture: this is the single largest source of error in Karl Fischer titration! The system must be sealed, and desiccant must be used on transfer lines. Highly hygroscopic samples must be handled in a nitrogen-filled glove box.

  2. A solid API is known to be strongly hygroscopic. How would you design the sample preparation procedure for Karl Fischer testing to minimize error?
Section 3: Test Preparation & Standardization of Reagent

Original Text

TEST PREPARATION

Unless otherwise specified in the individual monograph, use an accurately weighed or measured amount of the specimen under test estimated to contain 2–250 mg of water. The amount of water depends on the water equivalency factor of the Reagent and on the method of endpoint determination. In most cases, the minimum amount of specimen, in milligrams, can be estimated using the formula:

Result = FCV/KF

in which F is the water equivalency factor of the Reagent, in milligrams per milliliter; C is the used volume, in percent, of the capacity of the buret; V is the buret volume, in milliliters; and KF is the limit or reasonable expected water content in the sample, in percent. C is generally between 30% and 100% for manual titration, and between 10% and 100% for the instrumental method endpoint determination.

[Note—It is recommended that the product of FCV be greater than or equal to 200 for the calculation to ensure that the minimum amount of water titrated is greater than or equal to 2 mg.]

Special preparations:

  • Capsules: use a portion of the mixed contents of not fewer than 4 capsules
  • Tablets: use powder from not fewer than 4 tablets ground to a fine powder in an atmosphere of temperature and relative humidity known not to influence the results
  • Hygroscopic specimens: take an accurately weighed portion of the solid into the titration vessel, proceeding as soon as possible and taking care to avoid moisture uptake from the atmosphere

When appropriate, the water may be desorbed or released from the sample by heat in an external oven connected with the vessel, to where it is transferred with the aid of an inert and dried gas such as pure nitrogen.

STANDARDIZATION OF THE REAGENT

Place enough methanol or other suitable solvent in the titration vessel to cover the electrodes, and add sufficient Reagent to give the characteristic endpoint color or the appropriate electrode response. Either Purified Water, USP Sodium Tartrate Dihydrate RS, or commercial standards with a certificate of analysis traceable to a national standard may be used to standardize the Reagent.

For Purified Water or water standards, calculate the water equivalency factor, F, in milligrams of water per milliliter of reagent:

Result = W/V

in which W is the weight, in milligrams, of the water contained in the aliquot of standard used; and V is the volume, in milliliters, of the Reagent used in the titration.

For sodium tartrate dihydrate:

Result = W/V (36.04/230.08)

in which 36.04 is 2 times the molecular weight of water and 230.08 is the molecular weight of sodium tartrate dihydrate.

Tutorial Commentary

Formulas and Calculations: Estimating Sample Size

Formula for estimating the minimum sample size:
Result = FCV/KF

F = water equivalence factor of the reagent (mg H₂O/mL reagent)
C = fraction of the buret volume used (30%–100% manual; 10%–100% instrumental)
V = buret capacity (mL)
KF = expected water content of the sample (%)

Recommendation: FCV ≥ 200 (ensures that the amount of water titrated is ≥ 2 mg)

Example: F = 5 mg/mL, V = 10 mL, KF = 0.5% (expected water content)
Best C = 50% (middle of the manual range)
Minimum sample size = (5 × 0.5 × 10) / 0.5 = 50 mg
Check: FCV = 5 × 0.5 × 10 = 25 (<200, so the sample size must be increased)
If C = 100%: FCV = 5 × 1.0 × 10 = 50 (still <200)
→ Switch to a larger buret (e.g., 50 mL): FCV = 5 × 0.5 × 50 = 125 (the requirement of 2 mg is met only if ≥ the stated criterion)

Formulas and Calculations: Reagent Standardization

Standardization with purified water:
F = W/V
W = weight of purified water added (mg)
V = volume of reagent consumed (mL)

Example: 50.2 mg of purified water added, 9.8 mL of reagent consumed
F = 50.2 / 9.8 = 5.12 mg/mL

Standardization with sodium tartrate dihydrate:
F = W/V × (36.04/230.08)
• 36.04 = 2 × 18.02 (two water molecules)
• 230.08 = molecular weight of sodium tartrate dihydrate

Example: 150.0 mg of sodium tartrate dihydrate added, 20.3 mL of reagent consumed
Theoretical water content = 150.0 × (36.04/230.08) = 23.5 mg
F = 150.0/20.3 × (36.04/230.08) = 1.16 mg/mL

Analogy: Why Standardize the Reagent?

Karl Fischer reagent is like a "water ruler" that must be calibrated (standardized) before use. Sodium tartrate dihydrate is the "reference weight": it contains a precise proportion of water (theoretical value 15.66% w/w) and is stable, so it is used to calibrate the scale of the ruler. Commercial certified standards come with a certificate traceable to national standards (NIST, etc.), ensuring an unbroken metrological chain.

Key Notes

Special requirements for capsules and tablets: the contents of at least 4 capsules, or the powder from at least 4 ground tablets, must be combined to overcome within-batch variability and ensure that the test sample is representative. Tablets must be ground under controlled temperature and humidity to prevent the powder from absorbing moisture and affecting the result.

Heating methods (oven/headspace KF): for samples that do not dissolve in the KF solvent (such as polymers, porous materials and lyophilized cakes), an external oven can be used to heat the sample, and dry nitrogen carries the water released into the titration cell. Note that the heating temperature must not cause the sample to decompose; otherwise the water produced is not the original moisture (false positive).

Practice Questions

  1. When standardizing KF reagent, 30.5 mg of purified water was added and 6.1 mL of reagent was consumed. Calculate the F value.
  2. A lyophilized product has a water specification of NMT 1.0%. The KF reagent has F = 2.0 mg/mL, a 10 mL buret is used, and the expected buret usage is 40%–60%. Calculate the minimum sample size (mg) required and verify whether FCV is ≥200.
Section 4: Method Ib (Residual Titration) & Method Ic (Coulometric)

Original Text

Method Ib (Residual Titration)

PRINCIPLE

In the residual titration, excess Reagent is added to the test specimen, sufficient time is allowed for the reaction to reach completion, and the unconsumed Reagent is titrated with a standard solution of water in a solvent such as methanol. The residual titration procedure is applicable generally and avoids the difficulties that may be encountered in the direct titration of substances from which the bound water is released slowly.

PROCEDURE (Method Ib)

Calculate the water content of the specimen, in milligrams, taken:

Result = F(X' − XR)

in which F is the water equivalence factor of the Reagent; X' is the volume, in milliliters, of the Reagent added after introduction of the specimen; X is the volume, in milliliters, of standardized Water Solution required to neutralize the unconsumed Reagent; and R is the ratio, V'/25 (mL Reagent/mL Water Solution).

Method Ic (Coulometric Titration)

PRINCIPLE

The Karl Fischer reaction is used in the coulometric determination of water. Iodine, however, is not added in the form of a volumetric solution but is produced in an iodide-containing solution by anodic oxidation. The reaction cell usually consists of a large anode compartment and a small cathode compartment that are separated by a diaphragm. Each compartment has a platinum electrode that conducts current through the cell. Iodine, which is produced at the anode electrode, immediately reacts with water present in the compartment. When all the water has been consumed, an excess of iodine occurs, which usually is detected electrometrically, thus indicating the endpoint.

This method is particularly suited to chemically inert substances like hydrocarbons, alcohols, and ethers. In comparison with the volumetric Karl Fischer titration, coulometry is a micro-method.

PROCEDURE (Method Ic)

Using a dry device, inject or add directly an accurately measured amount of the sample or sample preparation estimated to contain between 0.5 and 5 mg of water, or an amount recommended by the instrument manufacturer into the anolyte, mix, and perform the coulometric titration to the electrometric endpoint. Read the water content of the liquid Test Preparation directly from the instrument's display, and calculate the percentage that is present in the substance. Perform a blank determination, as needed, and make any necessary corrections.

Tutorial Commentary

Formulas and Calculations: Method Ib Residual Titration

Method Ib water content calculation:
Result = F(X' − XR)

F = water equivalence factor of the KF reagent (mg/mL)
X' = volume of reagent consumed after sample addition (mL) (the amount added in excess)
X = volume of water solution consumed in the back-titration (mL)
R = V'/25 (ratio of reagent to water solution, obtained from standardization)

Principle:
Standardization of the water solution: Result = V'F/25
(V' = volume of reagent consumed when standardizing the water solution)

Example: F = 5.0 mg/mL, reagent added X' = 15.0 mL
Back-titration consumes water solution X = 2.5 mL, R = 0.8 mL reagent/mL water solution
Water content = 5.0 × (15.0 − 2.5 × 0.8) = 5.0 × (15.0 − 2.0) = 65.0 mg

Analogy: Residual Titration vs. Direct Titration

Method Ia (direct titration) is like measuring the length of a rope directly with a ruler: direct and fast. Method Ib (residual titration) is like putting the rope into a bucket of known capacity and then measuring the space left in the bucket. It suits cases where the rope is tangled and hard to measure directly (water trapped in the crystal lattice).

What is special about the coulometric method (Method Ic): no standardization is needed! The quantity of electricity can be calculated exactly (Faraday's law: 96,485 coulombs = 1 mole of electrons = 1 mole of I₂ = 1 mole of H₂O ≈ 18 mg). It is an absolute (primary) method.

Key Notes

Why the coulometric method needs no standardization: current × time = charge (coulombs), and by Faraday's law this converts directly to moles of I₂ and hence the amount of water. The instrument's microprocessor displays the result directly, with no manual calculation. Note, however, that the sample must be compatible with the anolyte (no side reactions).

Blank correction for Method Ib: the water solution standard must be standardized weekly, periodically correcting the R value (reagent/water solution ratio) to ensure the accuracy of the calculation. If this step is neglected, cumulative error can lead to systematic bias.

Practice Questions

  1. An API is a glucuronic acid hydrate that releases its water slowly on heating. Should Method Ia or Method Ib be chosen? Why?
  2. A lyophilized product has a residual moisture specification of NMT 0.5% (i.e., <1%). Should Method Ia or Method Ic be used preferentially? Explain the reasons for your choice.
Section 5: Method II (Azeotropic) & Method III (Gravimetric)

Original Text

METHOD II (AZEOTROPIC—TOLUENE DISTILLATION)

Apparatus

Use a 500-mL glass flask A connected by means of a trap B to a reflux condenser C by ground glass joints. The connecting tube D is 9–11 mm in internal diameter. The trap is 235–240 mm in length. The condenser, if of the straight-tube type, is approximately 400 mm in length and not less than 8 mm in bore diameter. The receiving tube E has a 5-mL capacity, and its cylindrical portion, 146–156 mm in length, is graduated in 0.1-mL subdivisions, so that the error of reading is not greater than 0.05 mL for any indicated volume. The source of heat is preferably an electric heater with rheostat control or an oil bath.

Clean the receiving tube and the condenser with a suitable cleanser, thoroughly rinse with water, and dry in an oven. Prepare the toluene to be used by first shaking with a small quantity of water, separating the excess water, and distilling the toluene.

Procedure (Method II)

Place in the dry flask a quantity of the substance, weighed accurately to the nearest centigram, which is expected to yield 2–4 mL of water. Place about 200 mL of toluene in the flask, connect the apparatus, and fill the receiving tube E with toluene poured through the top of the condenser. Heat the flask gently for 15 min and, when the toluene begins to boil, distill at the rate of about 2 drops/s until most of the water has passed over, then increase the rate of distillation to about 4 drops/s. When the water has apparently all distilled over, rinse the inside of the condenser tube with toluene while brushing down the tube with a tube brush. Continue the distillation for 5 min, then remove the heat, and allow the receiving tube to cool to room temperature. When the water and toluene have separated completely, read the volume of water, and calculate the percentage that was present in the substance.

METHOD III (GRAVIMETRIC)

Procedure for Chemicals: Proceed as directed in the individual monograph preparing the chemical as directed under 〈731〉.

Procedure for Biologics: Proceed as directed in the individual monograph.

Procedure for Articles of Botanical Origin: Place about 10 g of the drug, prepared as directed (see Articles of Botanical Origin 〈561〉, Methods of Analysis) and accurately weighed, in a tared evaporating dish. Dry at 105° for 5 h, and weigh. Continue the drying and weighing at 1-h intervals until the difference between two successive weighings corresponds to not more than 0.25%.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Principle of Method II Azeotropic Distillation

Toluene azeotropic distillation: water and toluene form an azeotropic mixture (boiling point 84.1°C, lower than both water at 100°C and toluene at 110.6°C). When the azeotrope reaches the receiving tube, the water (denser) settles at the bottom, while the toluene (less dense) floats on top and flows back into the flask to continue circulating. Finally, the volume of water at the bottom of the receiving tube is read.

Suitable applications:

  • Samples containing a large amount of water (2–4 mL of water)
  • Botanical drugs containing volatile oils: the volatile oil dissolves in the toluene and does not affect the water reading
  • Samples that interfere with Karl Fischer reagent (such as strong reducing agents and aldehydes)

Analogy: Toluene Distillation vs. KF Titration

The toluene distillation method is like a distillery separating water from alcohol: it physically separates the water using the azeotropic point and then directly measures the volume of water (very intuitive). The Karl Fischer method is like a chemical analyst quantifying with reagents: it suits precise quantification of trace water. Each has its strengths: the toluene method is simple and intuitive but less precise (receiver reading precision 0.05 mL), while the KF method is accurate but demands more of the operator.

Key Concepts: Method III Gravimetric Method (Botanical Drugs)

Water determination for articles of botanical origin uses oven-drying gravimetry:

  • Conditions: 105°C, for at least 5 hours
  • Endpoint criterion: the difference between two consecutive weighings is ≤0.25%
  • Calculation: (initial weight − final weight) / initial weight × 100%

Note: botanical drugs usually contain many volatile components, so this method actually measures "Loss on Drying" rather than pure water, but the Method III title still uses "Water" because most of the loss is water.

Key Notes

Toluene safety precautions: toluene is toxic and must be handled in a fume hood, and waste solvent must be properly disposed of. Modern laboratories tend to switch to xylene or other less toxic solvents, but USP still specifies toluene (confirm that the monograph permits a substitute solvent).

Toluene pretreatment: before use, shake the toluene with a small amount of water and separate off the excess water before distilling. The purpose is to ensure that the toluene is fully saturated with water, so that it does not absorb water from the sample and cause a false negative result.

The "constant weight" concept in Method III: a difference of ≤0.25% between two consecutive weighings reflects the operating principle of "constant weight", ensuring that drying is complete. The same concept also appears in <731> Loss on Drying and <281> Residue on Ignition.

Practice Questions

  1. A botanical drug material contains volatile essential oils. For water determination, should the KF method or toluene distillation be chosen? Explain why.
  2. The receiving tube of the toluene distillation apparatus must be cleaned and dried before each use. Why? If residual water remains, how does it affect the result?

Practical Application

In release testing of sterile lyophilized products, residual moisture is a Critical Quality Attribute (CQA). USP <921> Method Ic (coulometric KF) is the most commonly used method, with the target usually set at NMT 1% (some biologics are stricter, at NMT 0.5%). Vials must be opened for sampling under nitrogen protection and tested immediately to prevent interference from atmospheric moisture. This is standard practice for KF testing in a GMP environment.

Chapter Summary

  • Method selection: default to Method Ia (direct KF titration); follow the monograph where it specifies a method; use Loss on Drying (<731>) to measure volatile loss that is not entirely water
  • Karl Fischer principle: I₂ + SO₂ + H₂O → quantitative reaction; the stoichiometry is not exact, so empirical standardization is needed (F = W/V)
  • Method Ia (direct titration): samples containing 2–250 mg of water; electrometric endpoint (stable current for 30 seconds); atmospheric moisture is the main source of error
  • Method Ib (residual titration): for samples that release water slowly; Result = F(X' − XR); water solution standardized weekly
  • Method Ic (coulometric titration): I₂ generated electrochemically; no standardization needed (absolute method); suited to trace water of 0.5–5 mg; especially suitable for lyophilized products and inert organic substances
  • Reagent storage: protect from light, refrigerate, keep sealed; standardize before each use or daily; choose F ≤ 2.0 for trace water
  • Method II (azeotropic distillation): toluene forms an azeotrope with water; suited to samples with a large amount of water or volatile oils; 2–4 mL of water; receiving tube reading precision 0.05 mL
  • Method III (gravimetric): dry at 105°C to constant weight (two consecutive weighings differing by ≤0.25%); used for chemicals, biologics and botanical drugs
