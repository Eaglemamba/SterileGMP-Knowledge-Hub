Section 1 — Introduction: Seal Quality Tests vs. Leak Tests

Original Text

"Package seal quality tests" are checks used to characterize and monitor the quality and consistency of a parameter related to the package seal, providing some assurance of the package's ability to maintain integrity. Seal quality tests ensure that seal attributes, package materials, package components, and/or the assembly process are consistently kept within established limits, thus further supporting package integrity.

Seal quality tests differ from leak tests in that they provide no information relative to actual package integrity; thus, a package that meets the requirements of a seal quality test may still be defective and leak. For example, a flexible pouch package that passes a seal strength test may leak through a puncture in the pouch face. A bottle that meets closure application and removal torque tests may have a scratch on the bottle finish surface that allows product leakage.

In contrast, a pouch or bottle that is poorly assembled could pass leak tests at the time of product manufacture, yet develop leaks later, before reaching the end user.

Therefore, seal quality tests and leak tests work together to ensure package integrity.

Unlike package leak tests, seal quality tests are qualified for use rather than being fully validated. Qualification includes a demonstration of instrument performance and in some cases proof of appropriate instrument set-up specific for the package to be tested.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Nature of Seal Quality Testing

Seal quality testing is a surrogate indicator of process consistency, not direct proof of integrity. The logic chain:

  • Consistent seal quality → process under control → product-package system is very likely intact
  • But "good seal quality" ≠ "no leak" (for example, a puncture defect on the container body is not in the seal area)
  • "Poor seal quality" may also pass a leak test (a freshly made seal may not yet show obvious leakage)

The two types of test therefore complement each other in time and in space, and neither can be dispensed with.

Key Point: Qualification vs. Full Validation

USP asks for "qualification" of seal quality tests, not the "full validation" required of leak tests.

  • Qualification: demonstrates that the instrument works properly and is set up appropriately for the package system → relatively light effort
  • Full validation: demonstrates that the method can reliably detect leaks near the MALL → requires extensive positive/negative control data

This distinction has a direct impact on documentation: the SOP for a seal quality test does not need to meet the full requirements for method validation under USP <1225>.

Analogy

Seal quality testing is like the torque wrench on an automobile assembly line: it makes sure every bolt is tightened to the specified torque. Correct torque usually means a secure joint, but it cannot guarantee that the threads themselves are free of defects. True integrity confirmation (a leak test) is like pressure-testing the whole system with water afterwards. Only the two together assure quality.

Sections 2 & 3 — Closure Torque & Package Burst

2. Closure Application and Removal Torque

The closure application torque test measures the force exerted during the application of a screw-thread cap onto a threaded container. The closure removal torque test measures the force required to initiate screw-cap removal.

Application torque is kept within an optimum range to prevent leakage from loose caps and to preclude component distortion and compromised seals from over-torqued caps.

Removal torque is less than application torque due to stress relaxation and closure "back off" that may occur as a function of time and other environmental variables. Caps that back off excessively during shipping, storage, or distribution increase the risk of product leakage.

For multiple-dose packages, cap design and application forces should be such that the end-user population may be able to open and reclose the package.

Reproducibility is improved when tests are performed using automatic instrumentation that applies and removes caps at uniform speeds. Referenced standards: ASTM D2063, D3198, D3469, D3470, D3472, D3810, D3968.

3. Package Burst

The package burst test involves inserting a pressure source into a test package and applying pressure until the package seal(s) burst open. Applicable to flexible bags and pouches formed by heat-sealing processes.

The burst strength result (in pressure units) provides an indication of relative seal strength and evaluates the most likely location and mode of package failure. However, this test cannot provide a measure of package seal uniformity or overall package integrity.

ASTM F2054 (with restraining plate): stress uniformly applied to all seal areas → identifies weakest point. ASTM F1140 (without restraining plate): simulates real-world pressure events (sterilization cycle, distribution); highest stress at package middle, may not find weakest seal.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point: Burst Testing Cannot Prove Integrity

USP states explicitly that burst testing does not provide information on seal uniformity or overall package integrity. A package that withstands a high burst pressure may still leak outside the seal area (for example, through damage to the package face). Burst testing is mainly used to:

  • Confirm the minimum strength requirement of the sealing process
  • Compare seal strength under different sealing parameters
  • Simulate pressure-differential challenges during transport or sterilization

Analogy

Package burst testing is like testing how much air a balloon can hold before it pops: you learn the burst pressure, but not whether the balloon surface has a tiny pinhole. A high burst pressure shows that the seal joint is strong, but a pinhole will let air out slowly at any pressure. This is why burst testing must be paired with leak testing.

Sections 4 & 5 — Package Seal Strength & Residual Seal Force

4. Package Seal Strength (Peel Test)

The package seal strength test (peel test) measures the force required to peel apart two bonded surfaces. Applicable for: flexible pouch/bag seals (flexible-to-flexible), or flexible-to-rigid material seals (e.g., lidded tray).

Force results provide a measure of seal strength between bonded surfaces. Maximum seal force, as well as average force to open the seal, may be determined. Results are also useful for monitoring the consistency of package assembly.

The test is performed using a universal stress-strain instrument in extension force mode. The seal strength test is described in ASTM F88. Test results are affected by:

  • Peel test fixture design
  • Peel angle
  • Pull direction
  • Pull speed
  • Properties of the test sample itself

5. Residual Seal Force (RSF)

The residual seal force (RSF) test provides an indirect measure of the compressive force exerted by an elastomeric closure onto a parenteral vial finish after package assembly (capping). RSF tests can be performed on glass or plastic vial packages of all dimensions.

RSF is linearly related to closure compression: more tightly capped vials yield higher RSF values. A consistent and sufficiently significant RSF value provides a useful indicator of capping process consistency.

The RSF test is performed using a universal stress-strain instrument in compression force mode, with a metal tool called a cap anvil. A constant rate of compressive strain is exerted onto the anvil. The point in the stress-response curve immediately before the terminal slope change corresponds to the RSF value (in newtons or pound-force units).

RSF tests are nondestructive in terms of package integrity. Test results are influenced by:

  • Cap anvil tooling design
  • Test compression rate
  • Closure viscoelastic properties
  • Closure dimensions
  • Aluminum seal plastic top (remove for more reproducible results)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Two Values from a Peel Test

A peel test provides two useful values:

  • Maximum seal force: the peak force at which the seal joint begins to fail → reflects seal strength
  • Average peel force: the average force over the whole peeling process → reflects seal uniformity

A consistent average peel force profile (with no sudden drops) indicates a uniform seal and a stable process. A sudden drop may point to a local channel defect.

Core Concept: The Physical Meaning of RSF

RSF measures the residual compressive force that the stopper exerts on the vial neck after it has been pressed into the neck finish. This force is what maintains the seal:

  • RSF too low → insufficient force from the stopper on the vial → reduced seal force → higher risk of leakage
  • RSF too high → the stopper may be over-compressed → long-term material fatigue → seal failure

RSF is a direct monitoring indicator for the crimping process and is closely tied to the capping force setting. It is commonly used for process capability analysis (Cpk) in process validation.

Key Point: Regulatory Use of RSF

RSF has an important place in the regulatory documentation for aseptic vial filling:

  • Crimping process validation: an acceptable RSF range must be established (usually expressed in N or lbf)
  • Capper IQ/OQ/PQ: RSF is monitored as a critical output parameter
  • Routine manufacturing monitoring: RSF can be measured on samples from each batch to confirm process consistency
  • There are FDA 483 observations citing "inadequate control of stopper compression force"

Practical Application

When a CDMO takes on a new client's lyophilized vial product, crimping process validation typically includes: (1) RSF values at different capping force settings → establishing a correlation curve between RSF and capping force; (2) CCI testing in parallel (such as vacuum decay) to confirm the RSF range within which the package passes CCI; (3) using this to set the in-production RSF specification (upper and lower limits) as an IPT indicator for the crimping process.

Section 6 — Airborne Ultrasound

6. Airborne Ultrasound

The airborne ultrasound test checks seal quality by passing an ultrasound signal through the sealed area of a package or item. The signal strength transmitted through the package seal is compared to that previously established for "good" package seals. Poorly sealed areas will not transmit as much ultrasonic energy as properly sealed areas.

Detectable seal defects include:

  • Incomplete seal
  • Channel defect (seal channel)
  • Wrinkle in the seal
  • Extraneous material inclusion in seal
  • Package seal bond weakness

The method may be used:

  • Off-line: x–y coordinate scan of an entire seal area (detailed map of seal quality)
  • On-line: linear scan of seals during manufacturing

Airborne ultrasound is a noncontact testing technology that requires no package preparation and no coupling media (liquid or gel). Therefore, it is nondestructive to the package under test.

The method is described in ASTM F3004. The test system consists of: transmitter transducer → air gap → package seal → receiver transducer → signal intensity comparison.

Detection capability varies based on: package material of construction, seal structure (smooth vs. textured surface), scanning speed, and scanning signal strength.

Applicable to: flexible pouch seals and rigid tray seals made of metallic or plastic laminates.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Physics of Ultrasonic Seal Inspection

How airborne ultrasound works:

  • Good seal: the two material layers are tightly fused, so the ultrasound can travel from the transmitter across the seal area to the receiver → strong signal
  • Defective seal: channels, wrinkles or trapped foreign matter → voids in the seal area → ultrasound is attenuated or reflected by air → weak signal

"Airborne" (air-coupled) means the ultrasound travels through air and needs no liquid or gel as a coupling medium (unlike conventional contact ultrasonic flaw detection).

Key Point: Application Boundaries of Ultrasonic Seal Testing

Important limitations of ultrasonic seal testing:

  • Cannot detect: package integrity problems outside the seal area (such as punctures in the pouch face)
  • Cannot quantify: leak rate or defect size cannot be quantified
  • Many influencing factors: material texture, thickness and scan speed all affect the establishment of the signal baseline
  • Works well for quality monitoring of tray sealing for lyophilized products, because large seal areas can be scanned quickly

Analogy

Airborne ultrasound is like speaking toward the seal from one side of a wall while someone on the other side listens to how loud it is. If the wall (the seal) is intact, the sound is clear; if there is a gap in the wall (a channel defect), the sound is absorbed or altered. The technique is especially good at finding channel defects: a peel test may miss very fine channels, but ultrasound can scan them out precisely.

Practice Questions

  1. Airborne ultrasound seal testing is described as "non-destructive", but it is a seal quality test, not a leak test. If an ultrasound scan shows the seal area to be intact, can you conclude that the package does not leak? Explain why.
  2. Is ultrasonic seal testing suitable for a flexible pouch containing an aluminum foil layer (such as an IV bag)? What are the respective advantages compared with HVLD?

Practical Application

In the quality system for aseptic flexible bag filling (such as large-volume infusion bags), ultrasonic seal testing is an ideal in-line process monitoring tool: it can scan the seal area of every bag in real time on the filling line, immediately detect process abnormalities such as uneven heat sealing and channel defects, and trigger in-line rejection or a line-stop alarm. Combined with periodic sampling for CCI leak testing (such as vacuum decay), it forms a complete package integrity assurance system.

Technology Comparison Summary

Chapter Summary

  • Seal quality test ≠ leak test: the former is a surrogate indicator for process control, while the latter directly measures package integrity; the two complement each other and neither can be omitted
  • Qualification is sufficient: seal quality tests need only qualification (instrument performance plus suitability of the setup), not full analytical method validation
  • Each of the five technologies has its own role: torque (screw caps) → burst (flexible pouch strength) → peel (seal uniformity) → RSF (glass vial crimping) → ultrasound (locating defects in the seal area)
  • RSF is central to aseptic vials: it correlates linearly with capping force and is the standard tool for crimping process validation and routine monitoring, with a basis in both PDA TR27 and USP <1207.3>
  • The unique strengths of ultrasound: non-contact, non-destructive, usable in-line, and able to locate defects, making it an ideal choice for in-line quality monitoring on flexible bag filling lines
  • Seal quality tests cannot prove integrity: a package that passes any seal quality test may still leak through defects outside the seal area (punctures, cracks); this is the fundamental limitation of seal quality testing
  • The three USP <1207> chapters as a whole: <1207.1> method selection + <1207.2> leak technologies + <1207.3> seal quality technologies = a complete CCI body of knowledge; only the three combined can build a scientifically sound package integrity assurance program
