Learning Objectives

  • Understand the biology of mycoplasma: the smallest self-replicating prokaryotes, with no cell wall
  • Understand the two compendial test methods required by USP ⟨63⟩: (A) the culture method and (B) the indicator cell culture method

  • Recognize why and how to test for inhibitory substances
  • Understand the formulation logic and scope of use of the recommended media (Hayflick, Frey, Friis)
0.1 Introduction

Original Text

The genus Mycoplasma represents a group of minute bacteria which have no cell walls. The genus comprises more than 120 species. They are the smallest self-replicating prokaryotic organisms. The cells vary in size and morphology and cannot be Gram stained, but impressions of colonies on solid agar can be stained with methylene blue or equivalent stain. Mycoplasma are parasites and commensals, and some may be pathogenic to a variety of animal and plant hosts.

In humans, Mycoplasma are usually surface parasites that colonize the epithelial lining of the respiratory and urogenital tracts. Mycoplasma are common and may cause serious contamination in cell and/or tissue cultures used to generate compendial articles. They may also cause contamination of filtered sterilized soybean casein digest broth. A cell culture infection may persist for an extended period of time without causing apparent cell damage. Infection of cells in a culture can affect nearly every pathway of cell metabolism, including alteration of the cells' phenotypical characteristics and normal growth. The presence of Mycoplasma species does not always result in turbid growth in cultures or visible alteration of the cells.

Testing for Mycoplasma is a necessary quality control requirement to assure reliably pure biotechnological products and allied materials used to generate these products. This general test chapter describes two methods required to detect Mycoplasma contamination of test articles, tissues and/or cell cultures used to produce test articles, digest broth, or any other material in which Mycoplasma contamination is suspected. These are: (A) the agar and broth media procedure and (B) the indicator cell culture procedure.

These tests require careful aseptic technique and suitable laboratory conditions. In order to ensure appropriate testing and interpretation of results, personnel should be properly trained and qualified. A validated nucleic acid amplification technique (NAT) or an enzymatic activity based method may be used to detect Mycoplasma, provided such a method is shown to be comparable to both methods (A) and (B). Alternative methods must be suitably validated.

"Testing for Mycoplasma is a necessary quality control requirement to assure reliably pure biotechnological products and allied materials used to generate these products."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

What is mycoplasma?

  • The smallest self-replicating prokaryotes — much smaller than ordinary bacteria (0.2–0.3 µm), and able to pass through some sterilizing-grade filters
  • No cell wall — this is the defining feature. Because there is no cell wall: (1) they cannot be detected by Gram stain; (2) they are naturally resistant to antibiotics that act on the cell wall (such as penicillins); (3) their morphology is highly variable, so they are hard to recognize in routine microbiological testing
  • More than 120 species — different species have different nutritional requirements and host preferences, which is why more than one medium is needed for detection

Why is mycoplasma especially dangerous for a CDMO?

  • Infection can persist "silently" — with no obvious cell damage or turbidity in the culture
  • It affects almost every cellular metabolic pathway — altering cell phenotype and growth characteristics, and directly affecting biological product quality
  • Once a cell bank is contaminated, the entire product line may be affected

Analogy

The invisible roommate:

Imagine your cell culture flask is an apartment, home to the CHO cells you have carefully nurtured. Mycoplasma is like an invisible roommate who has quietly moved in — it does not make a mess (no obvious turbidity) and does not make noise (no obvious cell damage), but it quietly helps itself to your food (consuming medium nutrients), adjusts the thermostat (changing pH), and even rearranges the furniture (altering cell metabolism and phenotype).

The scariest part is that you may have no idea it is there — until your "tenant" (the cell product) develops a quality problem. This is why you need regular "inspections" (mycoplasma testing).

Key Notes

The legal status of USP ⟨63⟩: This is not a "recommendation" document; it is an official General Chapter of the United States Pharmacopeia. For biological products marketed in the US, the mycoplasma testing described in this chapter is a compendial requirement.

Both methods are indispensable: USP ⟨63⟩ requires the use of both the culture method (A) and the indicator cell culture method (B). If an alternative method (NAT or an enzyme activity-based method) is to be used, it must be shown to be "comparable" to both standard methods.

Commercial impact for a CDMO: A mycoplasma contamination event is one of the most serious quality incidents in biopharmaceutical manufacturing. A single contamination can lead to rejected batches, production line shutdown, destruction of cell banks, FDA 483 observations, and even product recalls.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can mycoplasma pass through some 0.2 µm sterilizing filters? What does this imply for your aseptic manufacturing operations?
  2. The source text notes that mycoplasma infection does not necessarily cause turbid culture fluid. In day-to-day CDMO quality control, is visual inspection alone sufficient to rule out microbial contamination?
  3. If your client asks you to use a NAT method in place of the traditional culture method, what validation data must you provide to meet the requirements of USP ⟨63⟩?
0.2 Culture Method — Choice of Media

Original Text

The test is carried out using a sufficient number of both solid and liquid media to ensure growth in the chosen incubation conditions of small numbers (approximately 100 colony-forming units, cfu; or 100 color-changing units, ccu) of Mycoplasmas that may be present in the test article/material. Liquid media must contain phenol red. The range of media chosen is shown to have satisfactory nutritive properties for at least the microorganisms shown in Quality Control Test Strain Organisms (below).

When testing for Mycoplasmas include in each test at least two known Mycoplasma species or strains (listed in Quality Control Test Strain Organisms) as positive controls, one of which should be a dextrose fermenter (i.e., M. pneumoniae or equivalent species and strain) and one of which should be an arginine hydrolyzer (i.e., M. orale or equivalent species and strain). Only when testing insect cell lines should one include a Spiroplasma control strain.

Quality Control Test Strain Organisms

Positive control cultures should be not more than 15 passages from isolation. Mycoplasma species or strains suitable for use are listed below:

  • Acholeplasma laidlawii — vaccines and/or cell-derived materials/cultures for human and veterinary use when an antibiotic has been used during production
  • M. gallisepticum — when avian material has been used during production or when the vaccine or cell culture is intended for use in poultry
  • M. hyorhinis — nonavian veterinary vaccines or cell cultures
  • M. orale — vaccines for human and veterinary use
  • M. pneumoniae — vaccines or cell banks for human use (or another suitable species of d-glucose fermenter such as M. fermentans)
  • M. synoviae — when avian material has been used during production or when the vaccine or cell bank is intended for use in poultry

Table 1. Type Cultures for Identifying Field Isolates Used as Test Strains

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why are both solid and liquid media needed?

  • Liquid medium (broth) — provides a richer nutrient environment that favors the multiplication of low concentrations of mycoplasma; it contains phenol red as a pH indicator, so mycoplasma metabolism changes the pH and causes a color change
  • Solid medium (agar) — used to observe the typical "fried-egg" colonies of mycoplasma, which is key to confirmatory identification

Positive control strains for the two major metabolic types:

  • Dextrose fermenter — such as M. pneumoniae, which lowers the medium pH (phenol red turns yellow)
  • Arginine hydrolyzer — such as M. orale, which raises the medium pH (phenol red turns pink/magenta)

These two metabolic types are chosen as positive controls to ensure that the medium supports the growth of mycoplasma with different nutritional requirements.

Analogy

Menu variety at a restaurant:

Imagine you have opened a restaurant and need to prove that your kitchen can satisfy all kinds of guests. You cannot validate it with a taster who only likes steak — you also need a vegetarian. The dextrose fermenter is like the "meat lover" and the arginine hydrolyzer is like the "vegetarian." If both of these very different guests can eat well and be satisfied at your restaurant, you can be confident that your medium (the menu) supports the growth of a wide range of mycoplasma.

Key Notes

The 15-passage limit: Positive control strains must not exceed 15 passages from isolation. This is because mycoplasma may undergo phenotypic or genotypic changes during repeated passaging, so that their growth characteristics no longer represent the wild type. Storage should be frozen (−20°C or colder) or freeze-dried.

Link between strain selection and product type: Note that the applicability of each strain is directly tied to the source or use of the product. For example, if your CDMO manufactures vaccines using avian-derived raw materials, you must include M. gallisepticum and M. synoviae as quality control strains.

CDMO practice: Most CDMO biologics production lines use mammalian cells (CHO, Vero, HEK293), and the most common positive control combination is M. pneumoniae (or M. fermentans) + M. orale.

  1. Your CDMO manufactures both human and veterinary vaccines. Which quality control strains should your mycoplasma testing program include?
  2. Why must liquid medium contain phenol red? If it were omitted, how would that affect the interpretation of test results?
  3. How would you manage and track the "15-passage limit" for positive control strains in your QC laboratory?
0.3 Incubation Conditions & Nutritive Properties

Original Text

Incubation Conditions

Incubate liquid media in tightly stoppered containers at 36 ± 1°. Incubate solid media in microaerophilic conditions (hydrogen atmosphere containing < 0.5% oxygen and/or nitrogen containing 5%–10% carbon dioxide in nitrogen). Sufficient humidity should be available to prevent desiccation of the agar surface at 36 ± 1°.

Nutritive Properties

Carry out the test for nutritive properties for each new batch of medium. Inoculate the chosen media with the appropriate test microorganisms; use not more than 100 cfu per plate containing at least 9 mL of solid media and per 100-mL container of liquid medium; use a separate plate and container for each species of microorganism.

Incubate the media and make subcultures from 0.2 mL of liquid medium to solid medium at the specified intervals. The solid medium complies with the test if a count within a 0.5-log unit range of the inoculate amount is found for each test microorganism. The liquid medium complies with the test if growth is found on agar plates subcultured from the broth, for at least 1 subculture for each test microorganism. The use of a microscope at 100× or greater may be helpful.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Microaerophilic conditions:

  • Mycoplasma are neither strict anaerobes nor aerobes — they require a low-oxygen environment
  • Solid medium requires an atmosphere of < 0.5% oxygen + 5%–10% CO2
  • Liquid medium is incubated in tightly closed containers, where a low-oxygen environment forms naturally above the liquid surface

Meaning of the 0.5-log range:

When 100 cfu is inoculated, the recovered count must fall within a 0.5-log range. This means the recovery should be between ~32 and ~316 cfu (101.5 to 102.5). This criterion ensures that the medium provides enough nutrients to support growth, without containing components that promote growth excessively and distort the result.

Analogy

"Quality acceptance" of media:

Just as you would not weigh a critical raw material on an uncalibrated balance, you cannot test for mycoplasma with unqualified medium. The growth promotion test is the "calibration procedure" for the medium — you load a known quantity of "reference standard" (100 cfu of a QC strain) and see whether the medium can "read it accurately" (recovery within the 0.5-log range). If recovery is too low, the medium is nutritionally deficient; if it is too high, there may be a problem that needs investigation.

Key Notes

Every batch of medium must be verified: The source text explicitly requires the nutritive properties test for "each new batch of medium." For a CDMO, this means your QC laboratory must have a complete medium batch management procedure — after receiving a new batch of medium, a growth promotion test must be passed before it can be used for formal mycoplasma testing.

Temperature precision of 36 ± 1°C: This temperature range is somewhat higher than the 30–35°C used for general bacterial culture. Incubator temperature calibration and routine monitoring are basic conditions for ensuring test validity.

Practice Questions

  1. Your QC laboratory receives a new batch of Hayflick medium. Describe the complete procedure you would follow to perform the growth promotion test.
  2. If the recovery count on solid medium falls outside the 0.5-log range (for example, 100 cfu inoculated but 500 cfu recovered), how should you investigate and handle it?
0.4 Inhibitory Substances

Original Text

The test for inhibitory substances is carried out once for a given product and is repeated whenever there is a change in production method that may affect the detection of Mycoplasma. To demonstrate absence of inhibitory substances, carry out the test for nutritive properties in the presence and absence of the test article/material.

If growth of a test microorganism occurs more than 1 subculture sooner in the absence of the test article/material than in its presence, inhibitory substances are present. The same is true if plates directly inoculated with the test article/material are not within a 0.5-log unit range of the number of colonies of those inoculated without the test article/material.

In both cases, inhibitory substances must be neutralized or their effect otherwise countered, by an appropriate method, for example, by passage in substrates not containing inhibitors or dilution in a larger volume of medium, before the test. If dilution is used, larger medium volumes may be used or the inoculums' volume may be divided among several 100-mL flasks. The effectiveness of the neutralization or other process is checked by repeating the test for inhibitory substances after neutralization.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why test for inhibitory substances?

Your product (for example, a biologic supernatant or cell lysate) may contain substances that inhibit mycoplasma growth, such as residual antibiotics, preservatives, or high concentrations of salt or protein. If these substances inhibit mycoplasma growth, your test may give a "false negative" — contamination is present but not detected.

Acceptance criteria (two indicators):

  • Time difference — if growth appears in the control without product more than one subculture earlier than in the test with product, the product is inhibitory
  • Count difference — on directly inoculated plates, the difference in colony counts between the product-containing and product-free groups falls outside the 0.5-log range

Analogy

Interfering-substance testing for a pregnancy test strip:

Imagine you are validating a pregnancy test strip — you must not only confirm that it correctly shows positive for a known hCG-positive sample, but also confirm that nothing in the subject's urine interferes with the result (for example, some medications could cause a false negative). Inhibitory substances testing does the same thing: it confirms that your product does not "kill" the mycoplasma in the medium, leading you to wrongly believe there is no contamination.

Key Notes

Performed once; repeat when the process changes: The inhibitory substances test only needs to be performed once for a given product. However, if the process changes in a way that could affect mycoplasma detection (for example, changing medium components, adding an antibiotic step, or changing the purification method), it must be repeated.

Common neutralization strategies:

  • Dilution — dilute the product in a larger volume of medium to lower the effective concentration of the inhibitory substance
  • Passaging — passage first in a substrate without the inhibitory substance, allowing the cells to metabolize it away
  • Key point: Whichever method is used, the inhibitory substances test must be repeated to confirm that the neutralization is effective

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO is manufacturing a biologic containing a high concentration of penicillin. Penicillin is effective against ordinary bacteria but not against mycoplasma (because mycoplasma have no cell wall). In this case, do you need to perform the inhibitory substances test? Why?
  2. If the inhibitory substances test shows that your product is indeed inhibitory and you choose dilution to neutralize it, how would you determine the most appropriate dilution factor?
0.5 Test for Mycoplasma in the Test Article/Material

Original Text

Inoculate no less than 10 mL of the test article/material per 100 mL of each liquid medium. If a significant pH change occurs upon the addition of the test article/material, the liquid medium is restored to its original pH value by the addition of a sterile solution of either sodium hydroxide or hydrochloric acid.

Inoculate 0.2 mL of the test article/material on each plate of each solid medium. Incubate liquid media for 20–21 days. Incubate solid media for not less than 14 days, except those plates corresponding to the 20–21 day subculture, which are incubated for 7 days.

Concurrently, incubate an uninoculated 100-mL portion of each liquid medium and agar plate, as a negative control. On days 2–4 after inoculation, subculture each liquid medium by inoculating 0.2 mL on at least 1 plate of each solid medium. Repeat the procedure between days 6 and 8, again between days 13 and 15, and again between days 19 and 21 of the test.

Observe the liquid media every 2 or 3 days and if a color change occurs, subculture. If a liquid medium shows bacterial or fungal contamination, the test is invalid. The test is valid if at least 1 plate per medium and per inoculation day can be read.

Include in the test positive controls prepared by inoculation of not more than 100 cfu of at least 1 test microorganism on agar medium or into broth medium. Where the test for Mycoplasmas is carried out regularly, it is recommended to use the test microorganisms in regular rotation.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

A 28-day detective investigation:

The culture method is like sending a team of detectives on a four-week surveillance operation. You do not look once on the first day and draw a conclusion — you repeatedly look for clues at different time points (Days 2–4, 6–8, 13–15, 19–21) from the "signals" (color changes in the liquid medium), and periodically collect "evidence" (subculturing onto solid medium to see whether typical colonies grow). This 28-day wait is the biggest drawback of the culture method, and it is why industry keeps developing faster alternative methods (such as NAT/PCR).

Key Notes

The minimum inoculation ratio of 10 mL per 100 mL: This ratio ensures that enough test article enters the culture system. If the inoculum is too small, a low-level contamination may be missed.

pH adjustment: Some biological products may be acidic or alkaline and will change the pH when added to the medium. Because the color change of phenol red depends on pH, the pH must first be adjusted back to its original value; otherwise you cannot distinguish a pH change caused by the product from one caused by mycoplasma metabolism.

Impact on CDMO scheduling: A 28-day test cycle means you must allow sufficient QC testing time in the product release schedule. For cell therapy products that need rapid release, this timeline is often unacceptable — which is exactly the commercial value of rapid NAT/PCR methods.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO is evaluating whether to adopt a NAT (qPCR) method to shorten the release time for mycoplasma testing. Compared with the 28 days of the culture method, NAT can deliver results within hours. Analyze the pros and cons of this decision.
  2. On Day 7 of the test, you observe obvious turbidity in the liquid medium. What does this indicate? What should you do next?
  3. The source text says positive control strains should be used in "regular rotation." Why is rotating positive control strains better than always using the same strain?

Key Concepts

Test timeline (21-day process):

The whole test takes about 28 days from start to result.

0.6 Interpretation of Results (Culture Method)

Original Text

At the end of the prescribed incubation period, examine all inoculated solid media for the presence of Mycoplasma colonies. The product complies with the test if growth of typical Mycoplasma colonies has not occurred. The product does not comply with the test if growth of typical Mycoplasma colonies has occurred on any of the solid media.

The test is invalid if 1 or more of the positive controls do not show growth of Mycoplasmas on at least 1 subculture plate. The test is invalid if 1 or more of the negative controls show growth of Mycoplasmas.

If suspect colonies are observed, use a suitable validated method to determine whether they are due to Mycoplasmas.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Regular testing of an alarm:

Think of the smoke detector at home — you regularly press the test button (positive control) to confirm it sounds the alarm properly. You also confirm that it does not go off when there is no smoke (negative control). Only when the detector passes both tests can you trust its judgment in a real fire. The positive/negative control logic of mycoplasma testing is exactly the same.

Key Notes

Handling suspect colonies: If you see "suspect" but atypical colonies on solid medium, you cannot report them directly as positive or negative — you must use a validated confirmatory method (such as PCR or special staining) for further identification. This step requires an SOP and must be recorded in the batch record.

The cost of an invalid test: For a CDMO, an invalid test means restarting the entire 28-day process, which can seriously delay the product release schedule. Laboratory environmental control, medium quality management and positive control strain management are therefore critical.

Practice Questions

  1. Your QC team reports: the positive control shows growth, the negative control shows no growth, but small colonies that do not look quite typical appear on the solid medium for the test article. As QC head, what steps do you take?
  2. If growth of mycoplasma appears in the negative control (invalid test), what lines of investigation would you pursue? What CAPA would be needed?

Key Concepts

Four possible outcomes:

Key logic: The positive control must show growth (proving the test system is capable of detecting mycoplasma), and the negative control must show no growth (ruling out laboratory environmental contamination). Only when both conditions are met is the test article result meaningful.

0.7 Recommended Solutions and Media

Original Text

[Note — This section is provided for information.]

Hayflick Media (General detection of Mycoplasmas)

Liquid Medium:

Adjust to a pH of 7.8. Solid medium: replace beef heart infusion broth with beef heart infusion agar containing 15 g/L of agar.

Frey Media (Detection of M. synoviae)

Liquid Medium:

Adjust to a pH of 7.8. Mix β-NAD and cysteine HCl solutions, wait 10 min before adding to other ingredients.

Friis Media (Detection of nonavian Mycoplasmas)

Liquid Medium:

Adjust to a pH of 7.40–7.45. Solid medium: Add DEAE-dextran (200 mg) and purified agar (15.65 g) to 200 mL Hanks' BSS, sterilize by autoclaving, cool to 100°, add to 1740 mL liquid medium.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Specialized bait for different kinds of fish:

Hayflick medium is like a general-purpose bait — most fish are attracted to it. Frey medium is a special bait designed to catch one particularly picky fish (M. synoviae), and it needs the nutrient that fish especially likes (β-NAD). Friis medium is like an "ultimate formula" that mixes all the best baits together, ensuring that all kinds of non-avian mycoplasma are lured out.

Key Notes

"This section is provided for information": The source text specifically notes "[Note — This section is provided for information.]" This means you may use other media that meet the nutritive properties requirements and are not obliged to use these three. In practice, however, these three media (especially Hayflick) have become the "gold standard" formulations in industry.

CDMO practical considerations:

  • Most CDMOs buy commercially prepared ready-made media (such as BD PPLO Agar/Broth) rather than preparing their own
  • The advantage of in-house preparation is full control over ingredient quality, but it requires stricter batch management
  • Whether commercial or in-house, the medium must pass a growth promotion test

Practice Questions

  1. If your CDMO mainly manufactures human monoclonal antibodies (using CHO cells), which medium combination would you choose for mycoplasma testing, and why?
  2. Hayflick medium contains horse serum (unheated), whereas Frey medium uses swine serum (inactivated at 56°C for 30 minutes). Why are the two sera treated differently? What is the scientific rationale?

Key Concepts

Design logic of the three media:

Why add penicillin?

Penicillin acts on cell wall synthesis — mycoplasma have no cell wall and so are unaffected, whereas ordinary bacteria are killed by penicillin. Penicillin is therefore added to suppress contamination by common bacteria, allowing mycoplasma to grow without competitors and improving test sensitivity.

Section Summary

  • Mycoplasma are the smallest self-replicating prokaryotes, with no cell wall, and can cause "hidden" cell culture contamination — with no obvious turbidity or cell damage
  • The compendial requirement of USP ⟨63⟩ is to use two test methods together: the culture method (A) and the indicator cell culture method (B); alternative methods (NAT/PCR) must be shown to be comparable to both
  • Positive control strategy for the culture method: at least two strains — one dextrose fermenter (such as M. pneumoniae) + one arginine hydrolyzer (such as M. orale) — to ensure the medium supports mycoplasma of different metabolic types
  • The inhibitory substances test confirms that the product does not interfere with mycoplasma growth (which would cause false negatives); performed once and repeated when the process changes
  • The culture method takes about 28 days: including multiple subculture observations and a solid medium incubation period of at least 14 days
  • Interpretation logic: the positive control must show growth and the negative control must show no growth for the test article result to be valid; suspect colonies require further confirmation
  • Recommended media: Hayflick (general), Frey (avian), Friis (non-avian) — the design logic is based on the nutritional requirements of different mycoplasma species

1.1 Indicator Cell Culture Method

Original Text

Cell cultures are stained with a fluorescent dye that binds to DNA. Mycoplasmas are detected by their characteristic particulate or filamentous pattern of fluorescence on the cell surface and, if contamination is heavy, in surrounding areas. Mitochondria in the cytoplasm may be stained but are readily distinguished from Mycoplasmas.

For viral suspensions, if the interpretation of results is affected by marked cytopathic effects, neutralize the virus using a specific antiserum that has no inhibitory effects on Mycoplasmas, or use a cell culture substrate that does not allow growth of the virus. To demonstrate the absence of inhibitory effects of serum, carry out the positive control tests in the presence and absence of the antiserum.

"Mycoplasmas are detected by their characteristic particulate or filamentous pattern of fluorescence on the cell surface and, if contamination is heavy, in surrounding areas."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why is a second method needed?

The culture method (Method A) introduced in Section 0 takes 21–28 days, and some mycoplasma species (especially M. hyorhinis) grow slowly or not at all on traditional media. The indicator cell culture method (Method B) works on a completely different principle — mycoplasma are allowed to multiply on living cells and are then detected by DNA fluorescent staining.

Detection principle:

  • The DNA fluorescent dye (bisbenzimide / Hoechst 33258) binds to all DNA, including nuclear DNA, mitochondrial DNA and mycoplasma DNA
  • Normal cells: only large bright nuclei and tiny mitochondrial dots in the cytoplasm are seen
  • Mycoplasma contamination: characteristic particulate or filamentous fluorescence appears on the cell surface and in the intercellular spaces — this is the signal of mycoplasma DNA

Distinguishing mitochondria from mycoplasma: Mitochondrial fluorescence is located inside the cytoplasm and is fairly evenly distributed, whereas mycoplasma fluorescence appears on the cell membrane surface or in the spaces between cells. An experienced microbiologist can readily tell the two apart.

Analogy

Checking banknotes under UV light:

Imagine you are checking the hand stamps of people entering a nightclub with a UV black light. A genuine stamp (nuclear DNA) gives off the expected large bright glow. But if someone has picked up a fluorescent substance they should not have (mycoplasma DNA), you will see extra tiny bright dots scattered over the skin surface — particulate or filamentous. These abnormal fluorescence patterns are the evidence of an "uninvited guest."

Mitochondria are like a fluorescent watch you are wearing — it also glows under UV light, but because it sits on your wrist (inside the cytoplasm), its position and shape are clearly different from the stamp (mycoplasma).

Key Notes

Special considerations for viral products: If the test sample is a virus suspension, the virus may cause obvious cytopathic effect (CPE) that interferes with the fluorescence reading. USP ⟨63⟩ offers two solutions: (1) neutralize the virus with a specific antiserum, but it must be shown that the antiserum does not inhibit mycoplasma; or (2) use a cell line that does not support virus propagation.

CDMO practical impact: This consideration is especially important if your CDMO undertakes viral vector or vaccine manufacturing. You may need to prepare neutralizing antiserum in addition and verify that it does not inhibit mycoplasma.

  2. Why does USP ⟨63⟩ require positive control testing both with and without antiserum? What is the purpose of this dual design?
1.2 Verification of the Substrate

Original Text

Use Vero cells or equivalent cell culture (for example, the production cell line) that is equivalent in effectiveness for detecting Mycoplasmas. Test the effectiveness of the cells to be used by applying the procedure shown below and inoculating not more than 100 cfu or ccu microorganisms of suitable reference strains of M. hyorhinis and M. orale. The cells are suitable if both reference strains are detected.

The indicator cells must be subcultured without an antibiotic before use in the test.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why specify Vero cells?

  • Vero cells (African green monkey kidney cells) are the most widely used indicator cell line because they: (1) are sensitive to many mycoplasma species; (2) grow adherently and form a clear monolayer; (3) are easy to observe under a fluorescence microscope
  • USP allows the use of an equivalent cell culture (including the production cell line itself), but its detection performance must be verified

Logic behind the choice of positive control strains for verification:

  • M. hyorhinis — one of the most common sources of cell culture contamination, yet extremely difficult to grow on traditional media. This is the core reason the indicator cell method exists
  • M. orale — a mycoplasma commonly found in the human oral cavity, representing the risk of contamination introduced by personnel handling
  • Both strains must be detected; otherwise the substrate verification fails

Requirement for passage without antibiotics: Antibiotics may inhibit mycoplasma (although most mycoplasma are resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics, some antibiotics such as gentamicin may have a partial inhibitory effect), causing false negatives. The residual effect of antibiotics must be removed before testing.

Analogy

Qualifying a sniffer dog:

Indicator cells are like the sniffer dogs at an airport. Before going on duty, you must test whether the dog can truly detect a known "sample" (M. hyorhinis and M. orale). If it cannot even find a low-level (100 cfu) reference standard, it is not fit for the job. Also, you cannot let the dog work in an environment "disturbed by perfume" (antibiotic residues), or its sense of smell will be impaired.

Key Notes

Meaning of 100 cfu/ccu: As in the culture method, the verification inoculum is set at about 100 cfu/ccu, which represents a relatively low challenge. If the indicator cells can detect mycoplasma at this low level, they will also detect it effectively in real contamination (usually much higher).

CDMO cell bank testing: For a biologics CDMO, the indicator cell method is indispensable in mycoplasma release testing of master cell banks (MCB) and working cell banks (WCB). Some clients even require this test on end-of-production cells.

Practice Questions

  1. If your CDMO's QC laboratory uses CHO cells as indicator cells (instead of Vero cells), what additional validation work is required?
  2. How many passages without antibiotics are enough? Does USP ⟨63⟩ specify this explicitly? If not, how would you design the verification plan?
1.3 Test Method — Eight-Step Procedure

Original Text

Solutions

  • Phosphate Buffered Saline (pH 7.4) — Combine 3.6 mL of 2.0 M Monobasic Potassium Phosphate, 16.4 mL of 2.0 M Dibasic Potassium Phosphate, 8 g of sodium chloride, and 1 L of water. Adjust pH if necessary.
  • Bisbenzimide Stock Solution — Dissolve 5 mg of bisbenzimide in water, and dilute with the same solvent to 100 mL. Store in the dark.
  • Bisbenzimide Working Solution — Immediately before use, dilute 100 µL of Bisbenzimide Stock Solution with PBS (pH 7.4) to 100 mL.
  • Phosphate-Citrate Buffer Solution pH 5.5 — Mix 56.85 mL of 28.4 g/L Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub> and 43.15 mL of 21 g/L citric acid.

Method (8 Steps)

  1. Seed the indicator cell culture at suitable density (2 × 104 to 2 × 105 cells/mL, 4 × 103 to 2.5 × 104 cells/cm2) that will yield confluence after 3 days of growth. Inoculate 1 mL of the product to be examined into the cell culture vessel, and incubate at 36 ± 1°.
  2. After at least 3 days of incubation, when cells have grown to confluence, make a subculture on cover slips in suitable containers or on some other surface (e.g., chambered slides). Seed cells at low density so that they reach 50% confluence after 3–5 days. Complete confluence impairs visualization of Mycoplasmas after staining and must be avoided.
  3. Remove medium and rinse the indicator cells with PBS (pH 7.4), then add a suitable fixing solution (freshly prepared 1:3 acetic acid : methanol is suitable when bisbenzimide is used).
  4. Remove fixing solution and wash cells with sterile Purified Water. Dry slides completely if staining more than 1 hour later (particular care is needed for staining after drying owing to artifacts).
  5. Add a suitable DNA stain and allow standing for a suitable time (bisbenzimide working solution and 10 minutes are suitable).
  6. Remove the stain and rinse the monolayer with Purified Water.
  7. Mount each coverslip, where applicable (equal volumes of glycerol and Phosphate-Citrate Buffer pH 5.5 is suitable). Examine by fluorescence (for bisbenzimide: 330–380 nm excitation filter and LP 440 nm barrier filter) at 400× magnification or greater.
  8. Compare the microscopic appearance of the test cultures with that of the negative and positive controls, examining for extranuclear fluorescence. Mycoplasmas produce pinpoints or filaments over the indicator cell cytoplasm. They may also produce pinpoints and filaments in the intercellular spaces. Multiple microscopic fields are examined according to the protocol established during validation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Critical control points of the eight-step procedure:

  • Step 1 — Inoculation density control: If the density is too high, the cells become over-confluent after 3 days and cannot be observed; if it is too low, the cells are not healthy enough, which may affect mycoplasma multiplication
  • Step 2 — The 50% confluence requirement: This is one of the most critical steps. If the cells reach full confluence (100%), the spaces between cells are filled and you cannot see the fluorescence of mycoplasma on the cell surface and in the intercellular spaces — the equivalent of "covering up the evidence"
  • Step 3 — Fixative ratio: Glacial acetic acid:methanol = 1:3 (a variant of Carnoy's fixative), which must be prepared fresh
  • Step 4 — Risk of drying: If slides are allowed to dry before staining, artifacts may appear and lead to false positive readings
  • Step 7 — Fluorescence microscope settings: 330–380 nm excitation + LP 440 nm barrier filter is the standard optical setup for Hoechst 33258 (bisbenzimide)
  • Step 8 — Multiple fields of view: You cannot draw a conclusion from a single field; multiple microscope fields must be examined according to the protocol established during validation

Analogy

UV search at a crime scene investigation:

The whole eight-step procedure is like a CSI crime scene investigation. Steps 1–2 are "setting up the scene" (preparing the indicator cell substrate); Steps 3–4 are "preserving the evidence" (fixation and washing, so the evidence is not destroyed); Steps 5–6 are "spraying luminol" (DNA staining); Steps 7–8 are "searching with UV light" (fluorescence microscopy).

The 50% confluence requirement is like saying the crime scene must not be too crowded — if the floor is piled with furniture (over-confluent cells), you cannot see the bloodstains (mycoplasma fluorescence).

Key Notes

Storage requirements for bisbenzimide: The stock solution must be protected from light (a light-sensitive dye), and the working solution must be prepared immediately before use. This must be stated explicitly in the QC laboratory SOP and emphasized in training.

Shelf life of the fixative: The glacial acetic acid/methanol fixative must be freshly prepared. Using old fixative may lead to incomplete fixation, affecting staining quality and the accuracy of interpretation.

The central role of validation: Note that Step 8 refers to "according to the protocol established during validation" — this means your QC laboratory must establish the number of fields, the observation criteria and the interpretation criteria clearly at the method validation stage. This cannot be decided by simply looking at a few random fields.

Fluorescence Microscopy Parameters

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dilution factors for bisbenzimide (Hoechst 33258):

  • Stock solution concentration: 5 mg / 100 mL = 50 µg/mL
  • Working solution: 100 µL stock / 100 mL = 0.05 µg/mL (a 1,000-fold dilution)
  • This very low working concentration is enough to bind DNA and produce fluorescence without causing excessive background fluorescence

The science of the fixative:

  • Methanol: denatures proteins and dehydrates and fixes the cells, while keeping the DNA stainable
  • Glacial acetic acid: helps remove histones, making it easier for the DNA to bind the dye
  • The 1:3 ratio is a validated optimum — enough to fix without damaging the DNA structure

Analogy

Exposure settings in photography:

Setting up a fluorescence microscope is like setting the exposure on a camera. The excitation filter (330–380 nm) is like choosing the right flash wavelength, the barrier filter (LP 440 nm) is like setting which colors of light are allowed into the lens, and the magnification (400×) is like choosing the right focal length. No parameter may be changed arbitrarily — just as you cannot use red light to photograph evidence that only shows up under UV light.

Key Notes

Reagent management in the QC laboratory: Protecting the bisbenzimide stock solution from light is critical. Brown containers or containers wrapped in aluminum foil are recommended, and the opening date and expiry date should be recorded. The requirement to prepare the working solution immediately before use means it cannot be prepared in bulk in advance.

Equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ): The fluorescence microscope must undergo appropriate equipment qualification, including verification of the spectral characteristics of the filters and periodic calibration of light source intensity. These are basic requirements for equipment management in a GMP environment.

1.5 Interpretation of Results

Original Text

The product to be examined complies with the test if fluorescence typical of Mycoplasmas is not present.

The test is invalid if the positive controls do not show fluorescence typical of Mycoplasmas.

The test is invalid if the negative controls show fluorescence typical of Mycoplasmas.

Summary of Indicator Cell Culture Method Results

"The product to be examined complies with the test if fluorescence typical of Mycoplasmas is not present."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The interpretation logic is identical to the culture method:

  • The positive control must be positive — if the positive control (indicator cells inoculated with a known mycoplasma) does not show typical fluorescence, the system has failed (possibly a staining problem, microscope problem, cell problem or strain problem) and the entire test is invalid
  • The negative control must be negative — if the negative control (uninoculated indicator cells) shows typical fluorescence, there is cross-contamination in the laboratory environment and the entire test is invalid
  • Only when both the positive and negative controls pass is the test article result meaningful

Criteria for "typical fluorescence":

  • Mycoplasma positive: extranuclear fluorescence in the form of pinpoints or filaments on the cell surface, which may extend into the intercellular spaces
  • Not mycoplasma: large uniform bright nuclei, and scattered mitochondrial dots in the cytoplasm (inside the cell, not on the surface)

Analogy

Three traffic lights:

Imagine an intersection with three traffic lights:

  • Positive control light (green = fluorescence): must light up, proving the signal system works properly
  • Negative control light (red = no fluorescence): must stay off, proving there is no false triggering
  • Only when both lights are normal can you trust the result of the test article light — off means pass, on means a problem

If the positive control light does not come on (the system is broken) or the negative control light comes on (there is interference), you cannot trust any signal at all. The whole system must be restarted (repeat the test).

  1. In your QC report, if the indicator cell method result is "suspected positive" (an atypical fluorescence pattern, between mitochondrial and mycoplasma), what should your procedure be?
  2. If the culture method (A) result is negative but the indicator cell method (B) result is positive, does the product pass? How would you handle such a discrepancy?
  3. From a CDMO commercial perspective, the indicator cell method takes 6–10 days vs 28 days for the culture method. Under time pressure for product release, how would you design the testing schedule?

Key Notes

Complementarity of the indicator cell method and the culture method:

Only the combination of the two methods provides complete coverage for mycoplasma detection, which is the fundamental reason USP ⟨63⟩ requires both methods to be used

1.6 NAT-Based Alternative Methods

Original Text

A validated nucleic acid amplification technique (NAT) or an enzymatic activity based method may be used to detect Mycoplasma, provided such a method is shown to be comparable to both methods (A) and (B). Alternative methods must be suitably validated. Validation requirements for alternate methods will not be addressed in this chapter.

"A validated nucleic acid amplification technique (NAT) or an enzymatic activity based method may be used to detect Mycoplasma, provided such a method is shown to be comparable to both methods (A) and (B)."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

USP ⟨63⟩ position on alternative methods:

  • Permitted, but with a strict precondition: the method must be comparable to both the culture method (A) and the indicator cell method (B)
  • Validation requirements are "not discussed" in this chapter — meaning laboratories must refer to other regulatory guidance (such as EP 2.6.7, PDA TR 50, FDA guidance) to design the validation plan
  • This passage appears in the "Introduction" (also covered in Section 0), but its practical significance is critical to the structure of the whole chapter

Common types of alternative methods:

  • PCR / qPCR — amplifies mycoplasma-specific gene fragments (such as 16S rRNA); the most commonly used NAT method
  • Enzyme activity methods such as MycoAlert™ — detect enzyme activities specific to mycoplasma (such as adenylate kinase); fast (~20 minutes) but possibly less sensitive
  • LAMP (loop-mediated isothermal amplification) — does not require a PCR thermal cycler and is simpler to operate

Analogy

Certifying a new security scanner:

Imagine airport security has two standard procedures: X-ray scanning (the culture method) and a manual pat-down (the indicator cell method). Now someone has developed a new full-body scanner (the NAT/PCR method) and claims it can replace the old methods. USP ⟨63⟩ says: "You may use it, but you must prove that the new machine can find everything that both the X-ray and the pat-down can find." And as for "how to do this comparability validation? We will not teach you here — go and look at another manual."

Key Notes

The huge time advantage of NAT methods: PCR/qPCR methods can give results within hours, compared with 28 days for the culture method and 6–10 days for the indicator cell method, which has a revolutionary effect on the speed of product release. However, the speed advantage must rest on rigorous validation.

The high bar of "comparable to both": Note that USP ⟨63⟩ requires comparability with "both methods," not just one. This means the NAT method must be able to detect the species that grow in the culture method and the species detected by the indicator cell method (such as the hard-to-culture M. hyorhinis).

Strategic considerations for CDMOs: Many advanced CDMOs already use NAT as a rapid screening tool for in-process testing, while retaining the traditional methods as the compendial methods for release testing. This "dual-track" approach balances speed and compliance.

Section Summary

  • The indicator cell culture method (Method B) uses DNA fluorescent staining (Hoechst 33258 / bisbenzimide) to detect the characteristic fluorescence pattern of mycoplasma on the surface of living cells — particulate or filamentous extranuclear fluorescence
  • Vero cells are the standard indicator cell line and must pass performance verification with M. hyorhinis and M. orale; they must be passaged without antibiotics before testing
  • Critical control points in the eight-step procedure: control of 50% confluence (to avoid over-confluence masking the fluorescence), freshly prepared fixative, control of staining time, and examination of multiple fields
  • Result interpretation is consistent with the culture method: the positive control must show typical fluorescence and the negative control must not, otherwise the test is invalid
  • The core value of Method B is its ability to detect species that grow poorly on culture media (such as M. hyorhinis), complementing Method A
  • NAT (such as PCR) alternative methods may be used, but comparability with both Method A and Method B must be demonstrated — the validation details are outside the scope of this chapter
  • CDMO best practice: adopt a "dual-track" approach — NAT rapid screening (hours) + traditional methods for final release (28 days), balancing speed and compliance
