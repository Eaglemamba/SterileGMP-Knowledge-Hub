Learning Objectives for This Chapter

  • Understand the position of USP <1117> as an informational chapter: guidance on best laboratory practices for microbiology laboratories
  • Master the key steps of media preparation: weighing, dissolving, sterilization, and pH confirmation
  • Understand media storage limits on temperature, humidity, and remelting
  • Learn the requirements for QC testing: growth promotion, inhibition testing, and shelf-life verification
  • Learn the convention for expressing microbiological incubation times: hours vs. days
Introduction

Original Text

Good practices in a microbiology laboratory consist of activities that depend on several principles: aseptic technique, control of media, control of test strains, operation and control of equipment, diligent recording and evaluation of data, and training of the laboratory staff in related competencies.

Because of the inherent risk of variability in microbiology data, reliability and reproducibility are dependent on the use of accepted methods and adherence to good practices in the laboratory. This chapter does not cover the unique and specific laboratory activities for virology and mycoplasma recovery and detection.

"Good practices in a microbiology laboratory consist of activities that depend on several principles: aseptic technique, control of media, control of test strains, operation and control of equipment, diligent recording and evaluation of data, and training of the laboratory staff."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Six Pillars of the Microbiology Laboratory

USP <1117> opens by listing the six foundations of good microbiological laboratory practice:

  • Aseptic technique - preventing extraneous contamination from entering the test system
  • Media control - lifecycle management from preparation through storage to quality control
  • Control of test strains - ensuring consistent strain identity, purity, and viability
  • Equipment operation and control - qualification, calibration, and maintenance
  • Data recording and evaluation - complete, accurate, and traceable
  • Personnel training - a competency-based training system

These six pillars are the backbone of the chapter; everything that follows elaborates on these principles.

Analogy

The kitchen analogy: A microbiology laboratory is like the kitchen of a high-end restaurant. You need clean ingredients (media), proven standard recipes (strains), calibrated ovens and stoves (equipment), rigorous records of every dish served (data), and well-trained chefs (personnel). If any link is missing, the dish you serve (the test result) cannot be relied on.

Key Points

Informational vs. mandatory chapters: USP <1117> is numbered above <1000>, which makes it an informational chapter: it provides best-practice guidance but is not enforceable. However, FDA investigators often cite this chapter as a benchmark when assessing laboratory practices. In a CDMO setting, following the recommendations of <1117> is a basic expectation for demonstrating laboratory capability.

Scope limitation: The chapter does not cover the specialized practices of virology and mycoplasma testing; more specific guidance documents exist for those areas.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Why does microbiological data carry an "inherent risk of variability"? Consider it from two angles: the randomness of microbial growth and the representativeness of sampling.
  2. If an inspector asked, "How does your laboratory ensure the reliability and reproducibility of microbiological data?", how would you structure your answer?
Media Preparation

Original Text

Culture media are the basis for most microbiological tests. Culture media may be either prepared in-house or obtained commercially. The term “prepared media” used in this chapter covers both these types of media. Safeguarding the quality of the media is therefore critical to the success of the microbiology laboratory.

It is important to choose the correct media or components in making media based on the use of accepted sources or references for formulas. The manufacturer’s formula and instructions for preparation routinely accompany dehydrated and ready-made media.

Water is the universal diluent for microbiological media. Purified Water is most often used for media preparation, but in certain cases the use of deionized or distilled water may be appropriate. Water of lesser quality should not be used.

Consistent preparation of media requires accurate weighing of dehydrated media or media constituents. A calibrated balance with the appropriate weight range for the ingredients should be used. Clean weighing containers and tools (such as spatulas) should be used to prevent foreign substances from entering the formulation.

Dehydrated media should be thoroughly dissolved in water before dispensing and sterilization. If heating is necessary to help dissolve the media, care should be taken not to overheat the media because all culture media, to a greater or lesser extent, are heat-sensitive. Darkening of media (Maillard-type reaction or nonenzymatic browning) is a general indication of overheating.

Sterilization of media should be performed within the parameters provided by the manufacturer or validated by the user. Autoclaving by moist heat is the preferred sterilization technique. Typically, manufacturers recommend an autoclave cycle of 121° for 15 min using a validated autoclave. These conditions apply to time at temperature of the media.

The pH of each batch of medium should be confirmed after it has cooled to room temperature (20°–25°). The pH of media should be in a range of ±0.2 of the value indicated by the manufacturer unless a wider range is acceptable by the validated method.

Prepared media should be checked by appropriate inspection for the following quality and integrity parameters:

  • Cracked containers or lids
  • Unequal filling of containers
  • Dehydration resulting in cracks or dimpled surfaces
  • Hemolysis
  • Excessive darkening or color change
  • Crystal formation from possible freezing
  • Excessive number of bubbles
  • Status of redox indicators (if appropriate)
  • Lot number and expiration date
  • Sterility
  • Cleanliness of plates (lid should not stick to dish)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Five Key Steps in Media Preparation

Culture media are the foundation of microbiological testing. Media of unacceptable quality lead to false negatives (missed organisms) or false positives (extraneous contamination), and both are serious failures.

  1. Select the correct formulation - based on a recognized source or the manufacturer's instructions
  2. Weigh accurately - use a calibrated balance and record the volume of water and the weights of ingredients
  3. Dissolve properly - dissolve completely but avoid overheating (Maillard reaction = darkened medium = compromised quality)
  4. Sterilize appropriately - 121°C / 15 min is the standard condition, but it must be adjusted for container size and load
  5. Confirm pH - measure after cooling to room temperature, allowing a deviation of ±0.2

Analogy

The baking analogy: Preparing media is like baking: the ingredient proportions must be exact (weighing), the batter must be fully mixed (dissolving), the oven temperature and time must be right (sterilization), and the finished product must be checked when it comes out (pH and appearance). An overbaked cake burns (Maillard reaction) and an underbaked one is raw (not sterilized). In either case the cake (the medium) cannot be used.

Key Points: CDMO Practical Considerations

Water quality: USP explicitly states that Purified Water should be used, and water of lower quality must not be. In a CDMO, the water used for media preparation usually comes from the same purified water system as manufacturing, and it must be supported by ongoing water quality monitoring data.

Sterilization cycle validation: "121°C / 15 min" refers to the temperature and time of the medium itself, not the autoclave set-point. Large volumes or heavy loads need a longer heat-up time, so the actual sterilization cycle may be much longer than 15 minutes. This is frequently examined in audits.

Appearance inspection checklist: The 11 appearance checks listed in USP should be turned into a standard inspection SOP in your laboratory. Every batch of prepared media should have records showing that these checks were completed.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Suppose you find that a batch of TSA is clearly darker than usual, while the pH is still within specification. Would you release it for use? Why or why not? What additional tests would you need to perform?
  2. Why does USP require pH to be measured only after the medium has cooled to room temperature? If pH were measured while the medium was still at 50°C, what bias would result?
Media Storage

Original Text

It is prudent to understand how the manufacturer or supplier transports and stores media before distribution to the end user. Manufacturers of media should use transport and storage conditions that minimize the loss of moisture, control the temperature, prevent microbial contamination, and provide mechanical protection to the prepared media.

Media should be labeled properly with batch or lot numbers, preparation and expiration dates, and media identification. Media should be stored according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Media prepared in-house should be stored under validated conditions.

Do not store media plates containing agar at or below 0°, as freezing could damage the gel structure. Protect stored media from exposure to light, excessive temperature, and temperature changes, as this may cause condensation.

Remelting of an original container of solid media, if not containing heat-labile components, should be performed only once to avoid media whose quality is compromised by overheating or potential contamination.

It is recommended that molten agar medium be held in a monitored water bath until it has been tempered to 45°–50°, and not further held for more than 8 h, or as determined appropriate for the medium composition.

Disposal of used cultured media (as well as expired media) should follow local biological hazard safety procedures.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Key Limits on Media Storage

  • Lower temperature limit: Agar-containing media must not be stored below 0°C - freezing destroys the gel structure and affects microbial recovery
  • Number of remelts: Solid media may be remelted only once. Each heating degrades nutrients and selective agents
  • Molten-hold time: Molten agar held in a 45°-50°C water bath should not exceed 8 hours - beyond this, media quality deteriorates

Analogy

The ice cream analogy: Have you ever let ice cream melt after taking it out of the freezer, and then put it back to refreeze? When you take it out again the texture is completely different: the ice crystals are bigger and the texture is coarse. Media behave the same way: freezing and thawing change the microstructure of the agar and affect surface flatness and the ability of microorganisms to attach. That is why USP says "remelt at most once", for the same reason you should not repeatedly freeze and thaw ice cream.

Key Points

Supplier transport conditions: USP specifically notes the need to understand how suppliers ship and store media. In CDMO supplier oversight this is an important audit point that is often overlooked. If summer heat during transport degrades the media, no amount of good laboratory practice can make up for it.

Water bath risk when pouring media: When a container is taken out of the water bath to pour media, water on the outside wall can get into the sterile medium. USP recommends wiping the outside of the container dry before pouring - a simple detail that is often overlooked.

Questions for Reflection

  1. A technician in your laboratory melts media on Friday afternoon and leaves it in the water bath, and pours the plates only on Monday morning. This exceeds the 8-hour limit. How would you handle this batch of media? Does it require a deviation investigation?
  2. How would you establish a shelf-life verification program for media? Which parameters would you test, and at which time points would you sample?
Quality Control Testing

Original Text

Many laboratories, for convenience, use dehydrated media or purchase commercially prepared media. Manufacturers of media attempt to standardize raw materials from biological sources but must constantly deal with unavoidable differences in raw materials obtained from natural sources, and therefore lot-to-lot variability of media must be considered.

Quality control tests should be performed on all prepared media, including media associated with swabs or media in strips. Tests routinely performed on in-house prepared media should include pH, growth promotion, inhibition, and indicative properties (as appropriate).

A satisfactory growth-promotion outcome includes growth that is visibly comparable to that obtained with a previously tested and approved batch of medium. This is for a qualitative comparison. If quantitative comparison is desired, follow guidance in Validation of Microbial Recovery from Pharmacopeial Articles 〈1227〉; the medium should be of the same formula, consistency, and volume.

When a batch of media does not meet the requirements of growth-promotion testing, an investigation should be initiated to identify the cause. Any batch of media that fails growth-promotion testing is unsuitable for use. [Note—Failed growth-promotion test results may not be used to negate positive test results.]

Special care should be taken with media that are used in sterility tests (see 〈71〉) and in environmental monitoring studies. Media used for environmental monitoring of critical areas should preferably be double wrapped and terminally sterilized. If terminal sterilization is not performed, media should be subjected to 100% pre-incubation and inspection before use within a critical area.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Four QC Tests for Media

  1. pH test - confirm it is within the manufacturer's specified range of ±0.2
  2. Growth promotion - demonstrate that the medium supports the growth of the target microorganisms
  3. Inhibition - selective media should inhibit non-target microorganisms
  4. Indicative properties - differential media should correctly show the differentiating reactions

Key principle: Media that fail growth promotion testing must never be used. Moreover, a failed growth promotion result cannot be used to negate positive results already obtained with that batch of media. The implication is profound: even if the medium is "defective", if microorganisms have grown, that result remains valid.

Analogy

The thermometer analogy: Imagine you take your temperature with a thermometer and it reads 39°C, a fever. Later you discover that the thermometer failed its quality check. Can you then say "I don't have a fever"? Of course not - the thermometer may read inaccurately, but the high reading is still a warning sign. Likewise, a medium that fails growth promotion may have poor recovery (missing organisms), but if something still grew on it, that positive result is even less to be ignored.

Key Points: Special Requirements for Environmental Monitoring Media

Media used for environmental monitoring of critical areas have stricter requirements:

  • Preferred: Double wrapped & terminally sterilized
  • If not terminally sterilized: 100% pre-incubation and inspection for sterility are required before the media enter the critical area
  • Additional requirement: Verify that pre-incubation does not affect recovery (growth-promotion testing after pre-incubation)

In a CDMO, control of environmental monitoring media is a frequent focus of FDA inspections. Make sure your SOP clearly defines how media are handled before they enter the cleanroom.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Your laboratory uses commercially available prepared media, and the supplier provides a CoA showing that the media passed growth promotion testing. Do you still need to perform growth promotion testing on receipt? Why?
  2. In a batch of TSA contact plates for environmental monitoring, 2 plates show contamination after pre-incubation. How would you handle this batch of media? Which departments need to be notified?
Microbiological Media Incubation Times

Original Text

Incubation times for microbiological tests with a duration of less than 3 days should be expressed in hours: e.g., “Incubate at 30°–35° for 18–72 h”. Tests with a duration longer than 72 h should be expressed in days: e.g., “Incubate at 30°–35° for 3–5 days”.

For tests with incubation times expressed in hours, incubate for the minimum specified time and exercise good microbiological judgment when exceeding that time. For tests with incubation times expressed in days, incubations started in the morning or afternoon should generally be concluded at that same time of day in the morning or afternoon, respectively.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The cooking-timer analogy: It is like cooking: for boiled eggs you time in minutes (6 minutes for soft-boiled, 10 minutes for hard-boiled), but for braised pork you say "braise for a day" and do not specify hours and minutes. Microbial incubation works the same way: short tests need to be specified to the hour, while long tests are expressed in days, but you must stay consistent in starting and ending at the same time of day.

Key Points

Why distinguish hours from days? This rule looks simple, but it solves a very practical problem: if an SOP says "incubate for 3-5 days" and an analyst starts incubation at 9 a.m. on Monday, does "day 3" end at 9 a.m. on Wednesday or at the end of Wednesday? USP's answer is: if incubation starts in the morning, it ends in the morning (results are read on the morning of day 3-5). This removes differences in interpretation between analysts.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Your SOP specifies an incubation time of "3-5 days, 30°-35°C" for environmental monitoring media. An analyst starts incubation at 3 p.m. on Wednesday. What is the earliest time the results can be read, and what is the latest time they should be read?
  2. If an analyst reads the results of a test expressed in hours before the minimum incubation time (for example, reading an 18-72 h test at 16 hours), does this constitute a deviation? What action is needed?

Section Summary

  • Six pillars of the microbiology laboratory: aseptic technique, media control, strain control, equipment control, data recording, and personnel training - the overall framework of USP <1117>
  • Media preparation: select the correct formulation, weigh accurately, dissolve fully (avoid overheating), validate the sterilization cycle (121°C/15 min is the medium temperature), and confirm pH after cooling (±0.2 range)
  • Media storage: do not freeze (≤0°C destroys the gel structure), remelt at most once, and hold molten agar in a water bath for no more than 8 hours
  • QC testing: pH + growth promotion + inhibition + indicative properties. GP failure = must not be used. A failed GP cannot negate a positive result
  • Environmental monitoring media: double-wrapped, terminally sterilized media are preferred; otherwise 100% pre-incubation + verification that pre-incubation does not affect recovery
  • Incubation times: <3 days expressed in hours (incubate at least the minimum time); >72 h expressed in days (start and end at the same time of day)

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the ATCC strain equivalency requirement and the minimum requirements for identity confirmation
  • Understand the limits on subculturing (passage) and the risk of phenotypic alteration
  • Know the qualification and calibration requirements for key laboratory equipment (autoclaves, incubators, biological safety cabinets)

Maintenance of Microbiological Cultures

Original Text

Biological specimens can be the most delicate standards to manage because their viability and characteristics are dependent on adequate handling and storage. Standardizing the handling and storage of cultures by the user laboratory should be done in a way that will minimize the opportunity for contamination or alteration of growth characteristics.

Cultures for use in compendial tests should be acquired from a national culture collection or a qualified secondary supplier and have documented equivalency to relevant ATCC strains. They can be acquired frozen, freeze-dried, on slants, or in ready-to-use forms. Confirmation of the purity of the culture and the identity of the culture should be performed before its use in quality control testing. The confirmation of identity for commonly used laboratory strains ideally should be done at the level of genus and species.

Preparation and resuscitation of cultures should follow the instructions of the supplier or a validated, established method. The “seed-lot technique” is recommended for storage of stock cultures.

The original sample from the national culture collection or a qualified secondary supplier is resuscitated and grown in an appropriate medium. Aliquots of this stock culture (the first transfer or passage) are suspended in a cryoprotective medium, transferred to vials, and frozen at −30° or below until use. If stored at −70° or below, or in lyophilized form, strains may be kept indefinitely.

Once opened, do not refreeze unused cell suspensions after culturing a working suspension. The unused portion should be discarded to minimize the risk of loss of viability and contamination of the stock.

The number of transfers of working control cultures should be tracked to prevent excessive subculturing that increases the risk of phenotypic alteration or mutation. One passage is defined as the transfer of organisms from a viable culture to a fresh medium with growth of the microorganisms. Any form of subculturing is considered to be a transfer/passage.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Seed-Lot Technique

This is the gold-standard method for managing reference microbial strains, and it follows the same principle as the cell bank system in vaccine manufacturing:

National culture collection (ATCC, etc.)
↓ Revival
Stock Culture (passage 1 / P1)
↓ Aliquot and freeze (below −30°C)
Working Culture (routine use)

Key rules:

  • Below −30°C, cultures can be kept for months to years
  • Below −70°C, or freeze-dried, cultures can be kept "indefinitely"
  • Thawed cultures must not be refrozen; discard whatever is not used
  • Each time, take one vial from the stock culture and revive it for use as the working culture

Analogy

A family heirloom analogy: Imagine you have the original handwritten manuscript of a family recipe (the original ATCC strain). You make a few photocopies and keep them in a safe (the stock culture), then take one out and copy it again for everyday use (the working culture). You would never use the original manuscript day to day (it would wear out), and you would never use a blurry copy that has been copied many generations over for anything important (the risk of phenotypic alteration). Whenever you need one, you go back to the safe and take out a clear copy.

Key Points

ATCC equivalency requirement: The 2022 revision added an important requirement: strains must have "documented equivalency to relevant ATCC strains". This means that if you buy strains from a non-ATCC supplier, you need documentation proving the strain is equivalent to the ATCC reference strain.

Level of identity confirmation: USP recommends that identity confirmation of commonly used strains be done to at least the genus and species level. In a CDMO setting, this usually means using molecular methods (such as 16S rRNA sequencing) or mass spectrometry (such as MALDI-TOF) to confirm strain identity, rather than relying on biochemical identification alone.

Tracking passage number: Excessive subculturing can cause phenotypic changes in a strain (for example, reduced antibiotic resistance or altered growth rate), making it unsuitable as a reference strain for QC testing. The permitted number of passages is usually specified in the individual USP test chapters (typically no more than 5 passages).

Questions to Consider

  1. Your laboratory buys a ready-to-use Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 6538 strain from a European supplier. What confirmation tests do you need to perform on receipt? What is the minimum requirement?
  2. A technician accidentally puts a strain that was already thawed and used back into the −70°C freezer. How should you handle it once you find out? Does this require a deviation investigation?
  3. Why does USP stress that "any form of subculturing counts as one passage"? If you inoculate a culture suspension onto an agar slant and then pick colonies from the slant to inoculate a liquid medium, how many passages is that?
Laboratory Equipment

Original Text

Most equipment (incubators, water baths, and autoclaves) are subject to standard validation practices of installation qualification, operational qualification, and performance qualification. Some useful guidance can be found in Analytical Instrument Qualification 〈1058〉. Additionally, periodic calibration (generally annually or on a frequency based on a risk assessment) is commonly required.

New equipment, critical to the operation of the laboratory, should be qualified according to a protocol approved by the quality assurance unit (QAU). In addition, regular cleaning and sanitization of equipment such as incubators, refrigerators, and water baths should be performed to minimize the potential for contamination in the laboratory. Door seals of incubators and refrigerators should be cleaned and checked for state of repair. These activities should be incorporated into the equipment preventative maintenance program.

Instruments (pH meters and spectrophotometers) used in a microbiology laboratory should be calibrated on a regular schedule and tested to verify performance on a routine basis. Laboratory equipment with software should be evaluated for maintaining data integrity (21 CFR, Part 11).

Equipment that is difficult to sanitize (such as refrigerators and incubators) should be segregated from aseptic operations (such as storage of media for testing and incubation of sterility test samples) and live culture operations to minimize the potential for inadvertent contamination of the tests.

Autoclaves are central to the operation of the laboratory and must have proper validation in place to demonstrate adequate sterilization for a variety of operations. Autoclave resources must be available (and validated) to sterilize waste media (if performed in that laboratory) as well as the media prepared in that laboratory. The choice of one or several autoclaves is not driven by a need to separate aseptic and live operations (everything in the properly maintained autoclave is sterile after the cycle), but rather driven by resource considerations.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

A car maintenance analogy: When you buy a new car it needs to be inspected (IQ/OQ/PQ); after that it needs regular servicing and an annual inspection (calibration); and day to day you wash it and check the tire pressure (routine maintenance). Equipment management in a microbiology laboratory follows the same three layers. If any layer is missing, the reliability of the equipment cannot be assured, and neither can the data it produces.

Key Points: Equipment Segregation Principle

USP <1117> sets out an important principle for equipment use:

  • Equipment that is difficult to disinfect (refrigerators, incubators): equipment used for "sterile work" must be separated from equipment used for "viable culture work"
  • Autoclaves: no separation needed, because after a properly validated sterilization cycle all contents are sterile

This distinction matters: the interior surfaces of incubators and refrigerators can be contaminated by viable samples placed there earlier, and that contamination can be transferred to sterile test media placed there later. Autoclaves do not have this problem, because they are thoroughly sterilized with every use.

21 CFR Part 11: The 2022 revision specifically added requirements for data integrity of equipment software. In a CDMO setting, this means that incubator temperature recorders, autoclave control software and the like must comply with electronic records regulations (audit trails, electronic signatures, access control, etc.).

Questions to Consider

  1. Your laboratory has only one incubator, so sterility test samples and environmental monitoring samples cannot be incubated separately. What alternative measures can you think of to reduce the risk of cross-contamination?
  2. USP says autoclaves do not need to distinguish between "clean" and "viable" operations. Yet some companies still use a dedicated autoclave for biological waste. Do you think this is over-control, or is there a reasonable justification?
  3. The calibration frequency of an incubator is currently set at once a year. Based on a risk assessment, under what circumstances would you recommend increasing the calibration frequency?
Using Modern Technologies

Original Text

The use of new technologies in a microbiology laboratory requires new learning and training to ensure a proper implementation in the laboratory (for examples, see Validation of Alternative Microbiological Methods 〈1223〉, Microbial Characterization, Identification, and Strain Typing 〈1113〉, and Rapid Microbial Tests for Release of Sterile Short-Life Products 〈1071〉).

In addition to the laboratory specialists, the laboratory supervisors or quality assurance personnel evaluating qualification of systems or deviations must also have the skills to analyze and interpret the complex data that may be generated by new techniques.

For each new technology, it is valuable to develop rules and principles that will build consistency and accuracy when laboratory personnel are using that technology to perform the relevant procedure. The rules and principles should be included in the written procedure.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Key Elements of Introducing New Technology

USP added this entire section in the 2022 revision, reflecting the rapid evolution of microbiology laboratory technology:

  • MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry identification: the mainstream technique for rapid microbial identification, replacing traditional biochemical identification
  • 16S rRNA sequencing: a molecular identification method used for final confirmation of difficult strains
  • Rapid microbiological methods (RMM): such as ATP bioluminescence and flow cytometry, used to accelerate release testing

Introducing new technology requires not only training of operators but also the ability of management and QA staff to interpret complex data. This is often overlooked: when a deviation occurs, a supervisor who does not understand the MALDI-TOF scoring system cannot make a sound judgment.

Analogy

A manual-to-automatic transmission analogy: Introducing new technology is like switching from a manual car (traditional methods) to an automatic (new technology). The driver (the operator) must learn a new way of operating, but the driving instructor (the supervisor) and the vehicle inspector (QA) also need to understand how an automatic works in order to judge whether something is wrong. If only the driver knows how to drive it and the instructor does not, nobody can make a sound judgment when a problem arises.

Key Points

Rules and principles in SOPs: USP specifically emphasizes that each new technology should have its own "rules and principles" built into SOPs. For example, a MALDI-TOF identification SOP should cover: What is the minimum acceptable score threshold? What is the procedure when the score is insufficient? What are the standardized sample preparation steps? These are key to ensuring that different operators obtain consistent results.

Questions to Consider

  1. Your CDMO is evaluating the introduction of a MALDI-TOF system to replace traditional biochemical identification. How would you plan the validation protocol? Which parameters would you need to compare?
  2. While reviewing a MALDI-TOF identification report, a QA manager finds a score of 1.85 (a score of ≥2.0 is generally considered reliable for species-level identification). How should she handle this result?

Section Summary

  • Seed-lot technique: national culture collection → stock culture (below −30°C) → working culture. Do not refreeze after thawing, and passage number must be tracked
  • ATCC equivalency: strains from non-ATCC sources must have documentation proving equivalency to the ATCC reference strain (new requirement in 2022)
  • Identity confirmation: commonly used strains must be confirmed to at least the genus and species level
  • Three layers of equipment control: qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) + calibration (annual or risk-based) + maintenance (routine PM)
  • Equipment segregation: incubators and refrigerators must be separated for sterile work and viable culture work; autoclaves need not be, because everything that comes out is sterile
  • Introducing new technology: not only operators need training; management and QA must also be able to interpret complex data
  • Data integrity: equipment software must meet 21 CFR Part 11 requirements (new in 2022)

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the design principles for separating the "clean area" from the "viable area" in a microbiology laboratory, and how to prevent cross-contamination
  • Master the segregation requirements between PCR/DNA/RNA testing areas and traditional microbiology areas (new in 2022)
  • Understand the key time and temperature limits for sample handling: start testing as soon as possible after sampling, and never freeze microbiological samples
  • Understand the environmental control requirements for sterility testing: isolators are preferred over cleanrooms
  • Learn the principles for assessing and handling incubation temperature excursions
Laboratory Layout and Operations

Original Text

Laboratory layout and design should carefully consider the requirements of good practices in a microbiology laboratory and laboratory safety. It is essential that cross-contamination of microbial cultures or DNA/RNA samples for PCR testing be minimized to the greatest extent possible.

In general, a laboratory should be divided into clean or aseptic areas and live culture areas. Areas in which environmental and other microbiological test samples are handled and incubated should be maintained completely free of live cultures, if possible. If complete separation of live and clean culture zones cannot be accomplished, then other barriers and aseptic practices should be employed to reduce the likelihood of accidental contamination.

When growth is detected, the sample should be taken from the clean section of the laboratory to the live culture section without undue delay. Subculturing, staining, microbial identification, or other investigational operations should be undertaken in the live culture section.

Staff engaged in sampling activities should not enter or work in the live culture handling section unless special precautions are taken, including wearing dedicated protective clothing that should not be worn outside the laboratory and gloves and carefully washing and sanitizing hands upon exiting.

All testing in laboratories used for critical testing procedures—such as sterility testing of final dosage forms, bulk product, seed cultures for biological production or PCR testing of cell cultures—should be performed under controlled conditions. Sterility tests should preferably be carried out in an isolator with ISO 5 classification.

For less-critical microbiological testing, such as with nonsterile product or intermediate bioburden samples, testing under a unidirectional airflow (UDAF) cabinet, or as applicable is preferable. During qualification, the UDAF air must meet the air quality grade for which it is being qualified.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

A hospital infection-control analogy: Just as a hospital separates infectious-disease wards from general wards, a microbiology laboratory needs this kind of "zone control." If you have just cared for a contagious patient in the infectious ward (the viable area), you cannot walk into the operating room (the clean area) in the same clothes to perform sterile surgery. Likewise, laboratory personnel leaving the viable area must change protective garments and wash and disinfect their hands before entering the clean area.

Key Points

PCR area segregation (new in 2022): The 2022 revision specifically added segregation requirements for DNA/RNA samples and PCR testing. PCR is extremely sensitive to DNA contamination: even a trace amount of DNA can be amplified and produce a false positive. In a CDMO environment, if you use a PCR method for mycoplasma testing, the PCR work area must be completely segregated from the routine microbiology laboratory.

Environmental requirements for sterility testing: USP explicitly recommends using an isolator for sterility testing, because the environmental contamination level in an isolator is lower than in a cleanroom operated by personnel. For a CDMO, investing in an isolator not only reduces the risk of false positives but also demonstrates a commitment to sterility assurance during inspections.

Practice Questions

  1. Your laboratory space is limited and you cannot achieve complete physical separation between the clean area and the viable area. What "alternative barrier measures" would you design to reduce the risk of cross-contamination?
  2. After collecting settle plates from the sterile processing area, an environmental monitoring sampler must return the plates to the laboratory for incubation. On the way, he has to pass through the corridor of the viable work area. Do you think this is a risk? How would you improve it?

Core Concept: The Two-Area Separation Principle in the Laboratory

The core of a microbiology laboratory layout is to divide the work areas into two distinctly different zones:

One-way flow principle: Once growth is found in a sample in the clean area, the sample moves "one way" to the viable area for further analysis and does not return to the clean area. Samples containing growing colonies must never be opened in the clean area.

Sample Handling

Original Text

Viable microorganisms in most microbiology samples—particularly water, environmental monitoring, and bioburden samples—are sensitive to handling and storage conditions. Critical parameters in these conditions include product (or sample) composition, container composition, time of storage, and temperature of storage.

It is important to minimize the amount of time between the sampling event and the initiation of testing and to control, as much as possible, the conditions of storage. Microbiological samples should never be stored in a frozen state before testing because this practice leads to loss of cell viability.

When monitoring water or bioburden prior to bioburden-reducing steps or sterilizing steps, samples may be held at 2°–8° for up to 24 h from the time of sample collection until the start of the analysis. When testing within 24 h is not possible (e.g., when contract laboratories are used), the actual maximum hold time between collection and testing should be supported by experimental studies (e.g., challenge tests).

If environmental monitoring samples cannot be incubated within a reasonable time frame (e.g., due to the use of an off-site testing laboratory), the time from sample collection to the start of incubation should be supported with experimental data.

All microbiological samples should be taken using aseptic techniques, including those taken in support of nonsterile products.

Samples submitted to the microbiology laboratory should be accompanied by documentation detailing source of the sample, date the sample was taken, date of sample submission, person or department responsible, storage conditions, and any potentially hazardous materials associated with the sample.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Time and Temperature Control in Sample Handling

The number of viable microorganisms in a microbiological sample changes with time and storage conditions. Delayed testing or improper storage can lead to:

  • Decreased counts - some microorganisms die under unfavorable conditions, leading to low counts
  • Increased counts - in nutrient-rich samples, storage at room temperature promotes microbial growth
  • Changes in microbial flora - different microorganisms tolerate storage conditions differently, so prolonged storage changes the composition of the population

Standard practice: Store water and bioburden samples at 2°–8°C and begin testing within 24 hours. Storage beyond 24 hours requires supporting challenge-test data.

Strictly prohibited: Freezing microbiological samples. The freeze/thaw process kills some microorganisms and seriously compromises counting accuracy.

Analogy

A fresh-food analogy: Microbiological samples are like fresh ingredients. A fish bought at the market keeps for about a day in the refrigerator (2°–8°C), but not indefinitely. Left at room temperature, it starts to spoil within hours. Frozen fish can be stored for a long time, but after thawing the taste and texture are completely different (the cell structure has been damaged). Similarly, freezing a microbiological sample causes cell death and makes your counts seriously low.

Key Points: Challenges of Outsourced Testing at a CDMO

Sample transport to contract laboratories: The 2022 revision specifically addresses the challenges of using contract laboratories: when testing cannot begin within 24 hours, challenge-test data must be available to justify the extended holding time. For a CDMO that sends environmental monitoring samples or water samples to an external laboratory, controlling the time and temperature during transport becomes critical.

Sample accompanying documentation: USP requires that every sample sent to the laboratory be accompanied by complete documentation. This is especially important in a CDMO environment: when samples from multiple product lines and multiple clients are handled together, a sound labeling and documentation system is the only safeguard against mix-ups.

Practice Questions

  1. Your CDMO project outsources the incubation of environmental monitoring samples to a contract laboratory a 2-hour drive away. What kind of validation program would you establish to ensure that transport does not affect the test results?
  2. A sampler collected a batch of water samples at 5 p.m. on Friday, but the laboratory had already closed. The samples were kept in a refrigerator (2°–8°C) until testing began on Monday morning (about 63 hours later). Is this consistent with USP recommendations? What data would you need to support this practice?
Incubation Temperature Excursions

Original Text

Incubation equipment qualification and ongoing calibration provide the acceptable range for temperature conditions relative to the expected procedural incubation temperatures or range.

When an excursion occurs outside of the procedural incubation temperature(s), an assessment should be performed to determine the potential impact on the test samples and what, if any, action should be taken with the test samples.

The effect of a higher or lower temperature on microbial recovery from the test samples for the recorded period of time should be evaluated.

An investigation of the excursion should be documented along with the disposition of the test samples. For instance, a slightly lower temperature excursion (e.g., 1°–2°) for a brief time could be compensated with an increase in incubation time; or, for a higher temperature excursion (e.g., greater than 40° for a total aerobic microbial count or greater than 30° for a total yeasts and molds count) consider any negative impact on recovery.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

An oven-temperature analogy: If your oven runs a little low while baking a cake (say it is set to 180°C but is actually at 178°C), baking a few extra minutes usually fixes it. But if the temperature spikes to 250°C, the surface of the cake burns, and extending the time does not help because the damage is done. The same is true for microbial incubation: a low-temperature excursion can be compensated for by extending the incubation time, but a high-temperature excursion may already have killed some of the microorganisms, and this cannot be reversed.

Key Points

Different temperature sensitivity of TAMC vs TYMC: USP gives two different high-temperature thresholds: for TAMC (total aerobic microbial count) the temperature of concern is >40°C, while for TYMC (total combined yeasts and molds count) it is >30°C. This is because molds and yeasts are usually incubated at 20°–25°C and are more sensitive to high temperatures.

Documentation requirements: Every temperature excursion must have a written investigation record, including the duration and magnitude of the excursion, an assessment of the impact on the samples, and the final disposition decision for the samples. These records are frequently reviewed during FDA inspections.

Practice Questions

  1. Your 30°–35°C incubator had a brief compressor failure over the weekend, and the temperature rose to 38°C for 4 hours before returning to normal. The incubator held environmental monitoring samples and sterility test samples in progress. How would you assess the impact on each of the two types of samples?
  2. Your laboratory monitors incubator temperature with a continuous temperature recorder. The recorder shows a brief low-temperature excursion (down to 28°C for 30 minutes), but a later calibration finds that the recorder itself has an error of ±1°C. How would you handle this situation?

Core Concept: Framework for Assessing Temperature Excursions

Incubation temperature excursions are among the most common types of deviation in a microbiology laboratory. USP provides a practical assessment framework:

Section Summary

  • Two-area separation: The laboratory is divided into a clean area (handling unknown samples) and a viable area (handling known/viable organisms). Samples with growing colonies move one way from the clean area to the viable area.
  • PCR segregation (new in 2022): DNA/RNA samples and PCR testing areas must be segregated from the viable area to prevent DNA contamination.
  • Personnel flow control: Sampling personnel should not enter the viable area; those who do must wear dedicated protective garments and wash and disinfect their hands upon leaving.
  • Sterility test environment: An isolator (ISO 5) is preferred, followed by a cleanroom. UDAF is suitable for non-critical testing.
  • Sample time and temperature: Store at 2°–8°C and test within 24 hours. Never freeze. Storage beyond 24 hours requires challenge-test data.
  • Temperature excursion assessment: A small low-temperature excursion can be compensated for by extending incubation; high-temperature excursions (TAMC >40°C, TYMC >30°C) require an assessment of the impact on recovery.
  • Sample documentation: Each sample must be accompanied by complete records of its source, date, submitter, storage conditions, and so on.

Competencies and Training of Personnel

Original Text

Each person engaged in each phase of pharmaceutical manufacture should have the education, training, and experience to do his or her job. The demands of microbiological testing require that the core educational background of the staff, supervisors, and managers be in microbiology or a closely related biological science.

A coherent system of standard operating procedures (SOPs) is necessary to run the microbiology laboratory. These procedures serve two purposes in a training program. Firstly, these SOPs describe the methodology that the microbiologist will follow to obtain accurate and reproducible results, and so serve as the basis for training. Secondly, by tracking the procedures in which a particular microbiologist has demonstrated proficiency, the procedure number or title also serves to identify what training the microbiologist has received.

Training records should be current, documenting the microbiologist’s training in the current revision to the particular SOP.

Periodic performance assessment is a wise investment in data quality. This performance testing should provide evidence of competency in core activities of the microbiology laboratory such as hygiene, plating, aseptic technique, documentation, and others as suggested by the microbiologist’s job function.

Microbiologists with supervisory or managerial responsibilities should have appropriate education and in-house training in supervisory skills, laboratory safety, scheduling, budgeting, investigational skills, technical report writing, relevant SOPs, and other critical aspects of the company’s processes.

It is expected that laboratory supervisors and managers have a demonstrated level of competence in microbiology at least as high as those they supervise.

It should be noted that microbiology is a scientifically based discipline that deals with biological principles substantially different from those of analytical chemistry and engineering disciplines. Many times it is difficult for individuals without specific microbiological training to make the transition.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The pilot's license analogy: Just as a pilot needs formal training from a flight school (education), accumulated flight hours (experience), and regular medical exams and simulator checks (performance evaluation), microbiology laboratory personnel need all three elements to demonstrate competence. And just as an airline would not let someone who has only driven cars fly a plane (a chemistry analyst moved directly into microbiology), USP states explicitly that people moving from a chemistry or engineering background into microbiology often struggle.

Key Points

Supervisor competency is taken seriously: USP requires that "supervisors and managers have microbiological competence at least equal to that of the personnel they supervise." This means you cannot assign a supervisor with a chemistry background to run a microbiology laboratory unless he or she has demonstrated microbiological competence through adequate training and experience.

Training on the SOP version: Training records must show that the employee has been trained on the "current version" of the SOP. When an SOP is revised, all relevant personnel must be retrained. This is a common audit finding in CDMO environments.

Microbiology is not chemical analysis: USP emphasizes that microbiology and chemical analysis are "fundamentally different" disciplines. The background is that many companies try to have chemistry QC analysts "double up" on microbiological testing, but microbiology deals with living biological systems, statistical variability, and unique concepts such as logarithmic thinking. It is not a simple cross-disciplinary switch.

Discussion Questions

  1. Your CDMO plans to expand its microbiology laboratory, but qualified microbiologists are hard to find on the market. How would you design a training program that converts chemistry QC analysts to microbiology so that they can perform basic microbiological testing competently within a reasonable time?
  2. A microbiology team leader's performance evaluation shows that she excels at aseptic technique, but the quality of her investigation reports has been consistently poor. Based on USP's competency requirements, how would you help her improve?

Qualification of Analysts

Original Text

The hiring of experienced personnel combined with periodic training and qualification of analysts, including regular vision checks, should limit to an acceptable level the variability of microbiological test results produced by each analyst’s handling and reading of test results, as well as ensure that the defined good documentation practices are understood and followed.

These also apply to personnel who may be involved in visual inspection of media fill units.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Elements of Analyst Qualification

  • Hire experienced personnel - the starting point is the right people
  • Periodic training and qualification - to maintain competence over time
  • Periodic vision testing - to ensure colonies on the culture media can be read accurately

Although this section is short, it contains a key insight: much of the variability in microbiological test results comes from the individual analyst's technique and the way results are read.

Analogy

The gemologist analogy: A microbiology analyst is like a gemologist: they rely on the naked eye to judge the number, morphology, and color of tiny colonies on the culture medium. If a gemologist's eyesight declines, small diamonds may be missed (missed colonies) or specks of dust mistaken for diamonds (particulates misread as colonies). Periodic vision testing is therefore not a formality; it is a basic safeguard for the accuracy of test results.

Key Points

The link to media fill: USP specifically notes that analyst qualification also applies to personnel who visually inspect media fill units. In a CDMO environment, reading positive results in a media fill is an extremely critical task. If an inspector overlooks a turbid vial, it can lead to a wrong conclusion and compromise the validation result of the aseptic process.

Good documentation practices (GDP): Ensuring that analysts understand and follow GDP is also part of qualification. The integrity of microbiological data directly affects batch release decisions.

Discussion Questions

  1. How would you design an annual analyst qualification program for microbiology analysts? What assessment items should it include?
  2. If a senior analyst's annual vision test shows that their myopia has worsened by 100 degrees (1.0 diopter), what action would you take?
Considerations for Microbiological Risk Assessments

Original Text

A microbiological risk assessment can be beneficial and provide information to assist in decision-making about the impact of microbiological quality concerns.

Some examples of when a microbiological risk assessment might be used are: 1) elevated bioburden counts (but within specification); 2) growth of atypical colonies after enrichment on selective agar plates; 3) exceeded alert/action levels; and 4) identification of a recovered species of concern.

The following is a list of example topics that could be included as part of the risk assessment:

  • Scope of the Event — number of product lots directly or indirectly impacted
  • Microorganism Identification — identifying to the species level whenever possible
  • Characterization and Source — natural habitat, physiological states (spore vs. vegetative, planktonic vs. biofilm), potential pathogenicity, and number recovered
  • Manufacturing Process — evaluating microbial ingress/growth-control points or reducing steps
  • Target Patient Base — potential health effects to probable user groups
  • Route of Administration — oral, topical, inhalant, etc.
  • Intrinsic Product Characteristics — water activity, pH, antimicrobial activity
  • Assessment Conclusion — scientifically-based determination of objectionable or not objectionable

Writing and approval of a microbiological risk assessment should be performed by personnel with specialized education and experience in microbiology.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Seven Considerations of a Microbiological Risk Assessment

USP provides a structured risk assessment framework, which is especially useful in a CDMO environment, where you must make science-based decisions on microbiological deviations for different clients and different products:

  1. Extent of the event - How many batches are affected? Which raw materials or equipment are shared?
  2. Microbial identification - Identify to the species level whenever possible
  3. Microorganism characteristics and source - Natural habitat, spore or vegetative state, pathogenicity
  4. Process evaluation - Are there subsequent microbial control or removal steps?
  5. Target patient population - Immunocompromised patients? Infants and young children?
  6. Route of administration - Injectables carry far higher risk than oral solids
  7. Intrinsic product characteristics - Water activity, pH, and whether antimicrobial activity can inhibit microorganisms

Analogy

The detective analogy: A microbiological risk assessment is like a detective investigation. You need to answer "who" (which microorganism), "where from" (source investigation), "how far did it spread" (extent of the event), "what harm could it cause" (risk to patients), and "are there safeguards" (control steps in the process). Only by putting these clues together can you reach a scientific conclusion: is this microorganism a "harmful suspect" or a "harmless bystander"?

Key Points

Four triggering situations: The four situations that USP lists as triggers for a risk assessment are very common in day-to-day CDMO operations:

  • Elevated bioburden that is still within specification - no OOS investigation is required, but a risk assessment is worthwhile
  • Atypical colonies growing on selective media - further identification and assessment are needed
  • Alert or action limit excursions - the most common trigger in environmental monitoring
  • Identification of organisms of concern - such as Burkholderia cepacia and Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Requirement for qualified personnel: USP explicitly requires that a risk assessment be written and approved by "personnel with education and experience in microbiology." QA or Production cannot do this on their own; a microbiology SME must be involved.

Discussion Questions

  1. Your CDMO detects Burkholderia cepacia at 5 CFU/100 mL in a batch of water for injection (WFI) (specification <10 CFU/100 mL). The result is within specification, but the organism is of concern. Using USP's seven-consideration framework, draft the key points of a risk assessment.
  2. A client's oral liquid product shows E. coli (a specified microorganism under USP <62>) in finished-product bioburden testing. Do you need to perform a risk assessment, or do you reject the batch outright? Why?
Laboratory Resources & Method Transfer

Original Text

The laboratory management is responsible for ensuring that the laboratory has sufficient resources to meet the existing testing requirements. This requires some proficiency in budget management and in determining appropriate measures of laboratory performance.

A measure of laboratory performance is the number of investigations performed on tests conducted by the laboratory, but this measure alone is not sufficient. In addition to tracking investigations, the period of time between sample submission and initiation of testing should be tracked, as well as the period of time between end of test and report release.

Oversight of Contract Laboratories: It is the company’s responsibility to thoroughly investigate the reputation and quality control performance of any new or unfamiliar contract laboratory. Before signing an agreement, a manufacturer should confirm that the contract laboratory complies with current GMP regulations. Reports written by the contract laboratory should include complete analytical worksheets from the analysis performed. A certificate of analysis with a summary of analytical results does not provide all the information needed to verify sample test data.

Oversight of Suppliers: The impact of the microbiological quality of materials from a supplier can be significant for the laboratory (media, reagents, cultures) or for manufacturing (excipients, components). An experienced microbiologist should initiate ongoing communications in this type of customer–supplier relationship.

Method Transfer: Performance of validated methods can change from one laboratory to another. Differences in media, reagents, equipment, procedures, and in levels of experience of personnel can all affect the outcome. For method transfers, comparative testing of the same sample may not be relevant for microbiological methods due to the non-homogeneity of microbial cells in the sample. Carrying out a method suitability verification would provide greater assurance that the transfer-receiving unit may adequately perform the tests.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Indicators for Managing Laboratory Resources

USP provides three practical laboratory performance indicators:

  1. Number of investigations - More investigations (deviations/OOS) may indicate poor laboratory operating quality or insufficient resources
  2. Time from sample submission to start of testing - Delays can lead to inaccurate microbiological sample results
  3. Time from end of testing to report issuance - Delays slow down batch release

Key insight: USP explicitly states that "insufficient resources" is an important cause of testing delays and an increased number of investigations. This gives laboratory managers a science-based argument for requesting budget from senior management.

Analogy

Quality control for the outsourced restaurant dessert: Outsourcing microbiological testing to a contract laboratory is like a restaurant outsourcing its desserts to a bakery. You cannot judge only by whether the cake looks good when it arrives (the CoA summary); you must be able to see the complete production records: what flour was used (culture media lot numbers), what the oven temperature was set to (equipment calibration records), and whether the baker washed his or her hands (analyst training records). USP is clear: a CoA alone is not enough.

Key Points: What Is Special About Method Transfer in Microbiology

Why microbiological method transfer differs from chemical method transfer:

  • Microorganisms are not evenly distributed in a sample - even with identical methods, two laboratories testing the same batch of samples may obtain different results
  • Many microbiological test results are "zero counts" - comparing two zeros is meaningless
  • USP therefore recommends "Method Suitability Verification" in place of the traditional "side-by-side comparative testing of the same samples"

In a CDMO environment, this is especially important when you transfer a test method to a new client-designated laboratory, or accept a method that the client has moved over from another CMO. Do not simply apply the template for chemical method transfer; microbiological method transfer needs a different acceptance strategy.

Key Points: Oversight of Outsourced Laboratories

USP's oversight requirements for outsourced laboratories are very specific:

  • GMP compliance must be confirmed before the contract is signed
  • Reports must include Complete Analytical Worksheets, not just the CoA
  • Raw data should be reviewed periodically by a qualified microbiologist on the client side
  • Both positive and negative control results must be included in the final report
  • The microbiological qualifications of laboratory management personnel must be documented

Discussion Questions

  1. The number of investigations in your CDMO's microbiology laboratory has recently increased by 40%, and sample waiting times before testing have also lengthened noticeably. How would you report this to management and make the case for more resources? Which provisions of USP <1117> support your argument?
  2. You are evaluating a new outsourced laboratory. Beyond the requirements listed in USP, what other aspects would you check? Design a brief audit checklist.
  3. A client asks you to transfer their bioburden test method for their product from another CMO. The other party has provided the validation report and SOPs. How would you design the microbiological method transfer plan? Why can't you simply run a comparative test on the same batch of samples?

Section Summary

  • Personnel competency model: Education + training + experience, all three together. A microbiology background is the core requirement. SOPs serve as both operating instructions and training records
  • Supervisor competency requirement: Managers' microbiological competence must be at least equal to that of their subordinates - a microbiology laboratory must not be managed by non-specialists
  • Analyst qualification: Periodic training + periodic vision testing + GDP compliance. Also applies to personnel who visually inspect media fill units
  • Seven risk assessment considerations: Extent of the event, microbial identification, characteristics and source, process evaluation, target patients, route of administration, intrinsic product characteristics → scientific conclusion
  • Laboratory performance indicators: Number of investigations + sample waiting time + report issuance delay - a simultaneous rise in all three is a warning sign of insufficient resources
  • Outsourced laboratories: Complete analytical worksheets > CoA summary. Raw data should be reviewed periodically by a client-side microbiologist
  • Method transfer: Microbiological methods are not suited to "same-sample comparative testing" - use Method Suitability Verification instead
