Section 0: Introduction & Glass Classification

p1-p2

USP General Chapter 〈660〉 | Pages 1–2 (Description & Overview)

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the scope of USP <660>: which pharmaceutical glass containers must comply with this standard.
  • Distinguish the chemical composition and applications of the four glass performance types (Type I, II, III, NP).
  • Explain hydrolytic resistance as the scientific basis of glass classification.
  • Understand how USP <660> and USP <1660> divide responsibilities, and how SRMs are cited to ensure test accuracy.
  • Know the specification requirements for colored glass (light protection) and how they relate to regulatory compatibility.
Description

Original Text

Glass containers for pharmaceutical use are intended to come into direct contact with pharmaceutical products.

The following recommendations can be made as to the suitability of a glass container for pharmaceutical products, based on the tests for hydrolytic resistance.

Glass containers meeting Type I performance are suitable for most products for parenteral and nonparenteral uses (e.g., borosilicate). Glass containers meeting Type II performance are suitable for most acidic and neutral aqueous products for parenteral and nonparenteral uses (e.g., treated soda-lime silica). Glass containers meeting Type II performance may be used for alkaline parenteral products where stability data demonstrate their suitability. Glass containers meeting Type III performance (e.g., soda-lime silica) usually are not used for parenteral products or for powders for parenteral use, except where suitable stability test data indicate that containers meeting Type III performance are satisfactory.

The inner surface of glass containers may be treated to improve hydrolytic resistance. The outer surface of glass containers may be treated to reduce friction or for protection against abrasion or breakage. The outer surface treatment is such that it does not contaminate the inner surface of the container.

Information on chemical composition of glass types, the formation of glass containers, and factors that influence inner surface durability of glass containers is provided in Evaluation of the Inner Surface Durability of Glass Containers 〈1660〉. This chapter also contains recommended approaches to evaluate the potential of a drug product to cause the formation of glass particles and delamination.

Glass may be colored to provide protection from light by the addition of small amounts of metal oxides and is tested as described in Spectral Transmission for Colored Glass Containers. A clear and colorless container that is made light resistant by means of an opaque enclosure (see Packaging and Storage Requirements 〈659〉) is exempt from the requirements for spectral transmission.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Glass Type Classification

Type I (borosilicate glass): Highest hydrolytic resistance; suitable for almost all parenteral and non-parenteral products. It contains B₂O₃ and has strong chemical stability, making it the first-choice material for injectables.

Type II (treated soda-lime silica): The inner surface is dealkalized, making it suitable for acidic or neutral aqueous injectables. For alkaline injectables, supporting stability data are required.

Type III (soda-lime silica): Generally not used for parenteral products unless adequate stability data exist. Mostly used for oral liquids or topical preparations.

NP (Not for Parenteral use): General-purpose glass; not suitable for any parenteral product.

Analogy

Think of glass containers as kitchen cookware: Type I is a high-quality cast-iron pot (heat resistant, non-reactive); Type II is a non-stick pan (a protective coating on the surface, suited to specific uses); Type III is an ordinary steel pot (fine for everyday cooking, but not for delicate dishes). Putting the drug into the pot and "cooking" it simulates the interaction between drug product and glass. Choose the wrong pot and the product gets "contaminated".

Key Notes

Inner surface treatment can improve the hydrolytic resistance of Type II glass. However, outer surface treatment (for example, silica coating to prevent breakage) must not contaminate the inner surface; this is a core GMP requirement for container compatibility.

When Type II is used for alkaline injectables, it must be supported by stability data. This means that in a CDMO client's CTD, the container compatibility section must contain this justification.

Practical Application

When a CDMO takes on a new client's formulation, the first question in selecting a glass container is: Is this product an injectable? What is its pH? For an alkaline protein drug (biologic) at pH 7–9, Type I is almost the only option; Type II requires additional stability assessment, which adds development time and cost.

Specific Tests — Overview & Test Selection Tables

Original Text

The Glass Grains Test combined with the Surface Glass Test for hydrolytic resistance determines glass performance. The hydrolytic resistance is determined by the quantity of alkali released from the glass under the conditions specified. This quantity of alkali is extremely small in the case of the more resistant glasses, thus calling for particular attention to all details of the tests and the use of apparatus of high quality and precision.

Conducting these tests in conjunction with a glass standard reference material (SRM) on a routine basis will help to ensure the accuracy of the method. Reference materials are available for both borosilicate glass (SRM 623) and soda-lime-silica glass (SRM 622) from the National Institute of Standards and Technology. The tests should be conducted in an area relatively free from fumes and excessive dust.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Two Hydrolytic Resistance Tests

Glass Grains Test: The glass is ground to a powder of defined particle size, heated in an autoclave, and the amount of alkali released is determined. Because it tests the "bulk glass composition", it can distinguish Type I from Type II/III.

Surface Glass Test: Intact containers are extracted directly in an autoclave and the alkali released from the inner surface is determined. It reflects the condition of the "surface that actually contacts the drug product" and is closer to clinical use.

The difference is that the Glass Grains Test represents the "bulk glass" while the Surface Glass Test represents the "container inner surface"; for Type II glass (surface-treated), the two results differ significantly.

Analogy

The Glass Grains Test is like blending an apple into juice to test its composition; the Surface Glass Test is like licking the apple skin to sense its surface. For a waxed apple (Type II), the wax makes up a very small share when the apple is blended, but a surface test reveals the wax. This is the point of the Surface Etching Test: strip off the surface "wax layer" (the surface treatment), retest, and confirm the source of the resistance.

Key Notes

Importance of the SRM (Standard Reference Material): The amount of alkali released in hydrolytic resistance testing is extremely small, so test error can seriously affect the result. USP requires that NIST SRM 622 (soda-lime silica) or SRM 623 (borosilicate) be used alongside routine testing as a method verification tool. This is key to ensuring consistent results between laboratories (inter-lab).

Extractable arsenic: Applies only to containers of Type I or Type II for aqueous injectable products; it is a baseline heavy-metal safety requirement (limit: 0.1 µg/g).

Test Selection Logic

Objective: determine the container performance type

Step 1: Glass Grains Test
  → Low result (≤0.1 mL 0.02M HCl/g) → possibly Type I

Step 2: Surface Glass Test
  → Type I / II both judged on resistance

Step 3 (if Type I vs II must be distinguished):
  Surface Etching Test, or
  comparison of Glass Grains + Surface Glass data
  → Type I: values before and after etching are similar
  → Type II: values rise markedly after etching

Note: the arsenic test applies only to Type I / II containers for aqueous injectables

Hydrolytic Resistance Apparatus & Reagents

Original Text

Autoclave: For these tests, use an autoclave capable of maintaining a temperature of 121 ± 1°, equipped with a thermometer, or a calibrated thermocouple device, allowing a temperature measurement independent of the autoclave system; a suitable recorder; a pressure gauge; a vent cock; and a tray of sufficient capacity to accommodate the number of containers needed to carry out the test above the water level. Clean the autoclave and other apparatus thoroughly with purified water before use.

Mortar and pestle: Use a hardened-steel mortar and pestle, made according to the specifications in Figure 1.

Other apparatus: Also required are a set of three square-mesh stainless steel sieves mounted on frames consisting of US Sieve Nos. 25, 40, and 50 (see Particle Size Distribution Estimation by Analytical Sieving 〈786〉); a mechanical sieve-shaker or a sieving machine; a tempered, magnetic steel hammer; a permanent magnet; weighing bottles; stoppers; metal foil (e.g., aluminum, stainless steel); a hot air oven, capable of maintaining 140 ± 5°; a balance, capable of weighing up to 500 g with an accuracy of 0.005 g; a desiccator; and an ultrasonic bath.

Carbon dioxide-free water: This is purified water that has been boiled vigorously for 5 min or more and allowed to cool while protected from absorption of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, or purified water that has a resistivity of not less than 18 Mohm-cm.

Methyl red solution: Dissolve 50 mg of methyl red in 1.86 mL of 0.1 M sodium hydroxide and 50 mL of ethanol (96%), and dilute with purified water to 100 mL. To test for sensitivity, add 100 mL of carbon dioxide-free water and 0.05 mL of 0.02 M hydrochloric acid to 0.1 mL of the methyl red solution. The resulting solution should be red. NMT 0.1 mL of 0.02 M sodium hydroxide is required to change the color to yellow. A color change from red to yellow corresponds to a change in pH from pH 4.4 (red) to pH 6.0 (yellow).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why an Autoclave Is Used

Maintaining a precise temperature of 121°C in the autoclave is critical. This temperature comes from standard drug product sterilization conditions and also represents glass extraction under "worst-case conditions of use". 121°C is an accelerating condition for the glass/water interface reaction, and in a short time (30–60 minutes) it simulates the chemical reactions that occur over the entire lifecycle of a glass container.

The use of carbon dioxide-free water is essential: CO₂ dissolves in water to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), which affects pH and the titration endpoint, causing a false "acidification" that interferes with the determination of released alkali.

Analogy

Preparing carbon dioxide-free water is like a barista using demineralized water to brew coffee: the "purity" of the water determines the purity of the flavor. Any foreign ion in the test (including CO₂) is "noise" that must be removed so that the tiny amount of alkali released by the glass itself can be measured accurately.

Key Notes

Methyl red indicator changes color over a pH range of 4.4 (red) to 6.0 (yellow). This slightly acidic titration endpoint is chosen deliberately: it can detect a very small amount of acid (0.02M HCl), which means the very small amount of neutralized alkali (from the glass) can be back-calculated. The sensitivity test ensures the reagent is prepared correctly and is a necessary QC step in the SOP.

Use of a permanent magnet: When grinding glass, steel grinding equipment may introduce iron particles. Iron particles (a strongly acidic oxide) interfere with the titration result, so they must be removed with a magnet before testing. This detail reminds us that every step in analytical chemistry has a scientific basis.

Practice Questions

  1. If ordinary purified water containing dissolved CO₂ is used for the test, what effect will it have on the result? Will it be biased high or low?
  2. The autoclave requires an "independent temperature measuring device" (calibrated thermocouple). Which GMP equipment qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) requirement does this correspond to?

Section Summary

  • USP <660> classifies glass by hydrolytic resistance into three performance types: Type I (borosilicate), Type II (treated soda-lime silica) and Type III (soda-lime silica); NP is not for parenteral use.
  • Performance is determined by a two-step test approach: the Glass Grains Test assesses bulk composition; the Surface Glass Test determines inner surface resistance; the Surface Etching Test distinguishes Type I from Type II.
  • The tests use carbon dioxide-free water and methyl red indicator for acid-base titration, sensitively detecting very small amounts of released alkali; NIST SRMs are used alongside to ensure method accuracy.
  • Containers of Type I or Type II for aqueous injectable products must also undergo the extractable arsenic test (limit 0.1 µg/g).
  • The choice of glass container directly affects drug product stability and patient safety, and is the core starting point of CDMO container compatibility assessment.

1.1 Glass Grains Test — Sample Preparation

Original Text

The Glass Grains Test may be performed either on the canes used for the manufacture of tubing glass containers or on the containers.

Sample Preparation

Rinse the containers to be tested with purified water, and dry in the oven. Wrap at least three of the glass articles in clean paper, and crush to produce two samples of about 100 g each in pieces NMT 30 mm across. Place in the mortar 30–40 g of the pieces between 10 and 30 mm across taken from one of the samples, insert the pestle, and strike it heavily with the hammer once only. Alternatively, transfer samples into a ball mill-breaker, add the balls, and crush the glass.

Transfer the contents of the mortar or ball mill to the coarsest sieve (No. 25) of the set. Repeat the operation until all fragments have been transferred to the sieve. Shake the set of sieves for a short time by hand, and remove the glass that remains on sieves No. 25 and No. 40. Submit these portions to further fracture, repeating the operation until about 10 g of glass remains on sieve No. 25. Reject this portion and the portion that passes through sieve No. 50. Reassemble the set of sieves, and shake for 5 min. Transfer to a weighing bottle the glass grains that passed through sieve No. 40 and are retained on sieve No. 50. Repeat the crushing and sieving procedure with the second glass sample until two samples of grains are obtained, each of which weighs more than 10 g.

Spread each sample on a piece of clean glazed paper, and remove any iron particles by passing the magnet over them. Transfer each sample into a beaker for cleaning. Add 30 mL of acetone to the grains in each beaker, and scour the grains, using suitable means such as a rubber-tipped or plastic-coated glass rod. After scouring the grains, allow to settle, and decant as much acetone as possible. Add another 30 mL of acetone, swirl, decant, and add a new portion of acetone. Fill the bath of the ultrasonic vessel with water at room temperature, then place the beaker in the rack, and immerse it until the level of the acetone is at the level of the water; apply the ultrasound for 1 min. Swirl the beaker, allow to settle, and decant the acetone as completely as possible; then repeat the ultrasonic cleaning operation. If a fine turbidity persists, repeat the ultrasonic cleaning and acetone washing until the solution remains clear. Swirl, and decant the acetone. Dry the grains, first by putting the beaker on a warm plate, then by heating at 140° for 20 min in a drying oven. Transfer the dried grains from each beaker into separate weighing bottles, insert the stoppers, and cool in a desiccator.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Scientific Basis of Particle Size Selection

The Glass Grains Test uses glass powder between US Sieve No. 40 (opening about 420 µm) and No. 50 (opening about 300 µm). This particle size range is chosen in order to:

  • Standardize the specific surface area: The same particle size gives the same area of contact with water, so results from different batches are comparable.
  • Avoid extreme values: Powder that is too fine (passes No. 50) has an excessive specific surface area and is over-extracted; particles that are too coarse (retained on No. 25) are under-extracted.

Each of the two independent (duplicate) samples must exceed 10 g. This is the statistical minimum and the basis for calculating the Table 3 permissible range.

Analogy

Preparing glass powder is like grinding coffee beans: you need a specific grind (neither too coarse nor too fine) to extract a standard amount of caffeine. The sieves are the "grader", ensuring the contact area is the same for every "extraction". Ultrasonic cleaning to remove residual dust is like a barista rinsing the filter with clean water so that residue from the previous batch does not contaminate this one.

Key Notes

Removing iron particles with a magnet: Iron particles from steel grinding equipment are a strong oxide (Fe₂O₃); dissolved in water they are acidic and neutralize the alkali released from the glass, giving falsely low results (false negatives). This step looks minor but is key to data accuracy.

Acetone washing + ultrasonication: The purpose is to remove organic contaminants from the glass surface (such as lubricant residue from grinding). Organic matter can interfere with the color judgment of the methyl red indicator and affect the titration endpoint.

Drying at 140°C for 20 minutes: Removes residual moisture. If the sample contains water, the 10.00 g weighing is inaccurate, which directly affects the calculated result.

Practice Questions

  1. Why should the sample be prepared from "at least three glass containers" rather than just one? How does this relate to sampling representativeness?
  2. If the acetone is still slightly cloudy after ultrasonic cleaning, what are the consequences of continuing to use the sample?
1.2 Glass Grains Test — Method, Titration & Limits

Original Text

Method — Filling and Heating

Weigh 10.00 g of the cleaned and dried grains into two separate conical flasks. Pipet 50 mL of carbon dioxide-free purified water into each of the conical flasks (test solutions). Pipet 50 mL of carbon dioxide-free purified water into a third conical flask that will serve as a blank. Distribute the grains evenly over the flat bases of the flasks by shaking gently. Close the flasks with neutral glass dishes or aluminum foil rinsed with purified water or with inverted beakers so that the inner surfaces of the beakers fit snugly down onto the top rims of the flasks.

Place all three flasks in the autoclave containing the water at ambient temperature, and ensure that they are held above the level of the water in the vessel. Carry out the following operations:

  1. Insert the end of a calibrated thermometric device in a filled container through a hole of approximately the diameter of the thermocouple and connect it to an external measuring device.
  2. Close the autoclave door or lid securely but leave the vent-cock open.
  3. Start automatic recording of the temperature versus time, and heat the autoclave at a regular rate such that steam issues vigorously from the vent-cock after 20–30 min, and maintain a vigorous evolution of steam for a further 10 min.
  4. Close the vent-cock, and raise the temperature from 100° to 121° at a rate of 1°/min within 20–22 min.
  5. Maintain the temperature at 121 ± 1° for 30 ± 1 min from the time when the holding temperature is reached.
  6. Cool down to 100° at a rate of 0.5°/min, venting to prevent formation of a vacuum, within 40–44 min.
  7. Do not open the autoclave until it has cooled to 95°.
  8. Remove the hot samples from the autoclave using appropriate safety precautions, and cool the samples cautiously down to room temperature within 30 min, avoiding thermal shock.

Titration

To each of the three flasks add 0.05 mL of Methyl red solution. Titrate the blank solution immediately with 0.02 M hydrochloric acid, then titrate the test solutions until the color matches that obtained with the blank solution. Subtract the titration volume for the blank solution from that for the test solutions. Calculate the mean value of the results in mL of 0.02 M hydrochloric acid per g of the sample. Repeat the test if the highest and lowest observed values differ by more than the permissible range given in Table 3.

Tutorial Commentary

Formula & Calculation — Result Calculation

Glass Grains Test result calculation:

Result (mL/g) = (V_test - V_blank) / W

where:
V_test = volume of 0.02M HCl used to titrate the test solution (mL)
V_blank = volume of 0.02M HCl used to titrate the blank (mL)
W = weight of glass powder = 10.00 g

The mean of the two parallel samples is taken.

Acceptance criteria (Table 4):
  Type I: mean ≤ 0.1 mL/g
  Type II/III: mean ≤ 0.85 mL/g

If the difference between the two results exceeds the Table 3 permissible range
→ repeat the test (result not reliable)

Core Concept: Precise Control of the Autoclave Procedure

The heating, holding and cooling rates of the autoclave are strictly specified, for the following reasons:

  • Venting step (vent-cock): Letting steam escape freely for 10 minutes first removes the air from the chamber (air pocket). Residual air leads to uneven steam pressure distribution and inconsistent temperature.
  • Heating rate of 1°C/min (100→121°C): Avoids thermal shock to the samples and ensures uniform extraction.
  • Cooling rate of 0.5°C/min: The slower cooling prevents the glass from cracking because of thermal gradients, and also prevents the aqueous solution from boiling over on rapid pressure release (preventing "false positive" spattering).
  • Open the door only at 95°C: Protects the operator and prevents injury from hot steam.

Analogy

The autoclave heating and cooling program is like an aircraft taking off and landing: both takeoff (heating) and landing (cooling) need a steady rate of climb or descent, with no sudden pull-up or dive, otherwise the passengers (samples) are uncomfortable. The "venting" step is like equalizing cabin pressure before opening the aircraft door; without it the door would be "blown open" by the pressure (vacuum or overpressure).

Key Notes — Significance of the Table 3 Permissible Range

The Table 3 permissible range is designed as a relative value (not an absolute one): for high-resistance glass (Type I, result ≤0.1 mL/g), a 25% variation is allowed because the values themselves are very small and measurement precision is limited; for low-resistance glass (result ≥0.2 mL/g), only 10% variation is allowed because the values are larger and the precision required is higher.

This reflects statistical "proportional precision": USP does not require the same absolute error in every situation, but adjusts the acceptable range according to the size of the value.

Practical Application

In a CDMO's incoming material control for containers, the Glass Grains Test should be performed on each batch of glass containers before receipt into stock (or, based on the CoA and supplier qualification records, a decision may be made to skip it). If the CoA from a Type I glass supplier shows 0.08 mL/g but the CDMO's own test gives 0.12 mL/g, the difference (50%) far exceeds the Table 3 permissible range (25%), and an investigation should be triggered and the supplier notified.

1.3 Surface Glass Test — Filling Volume & Procedure

Original Text

Determination of the Filling Volume

The filling volume is the volume of purified water to be added to the container for the purpose of the test. For vials, bottles, cartridges, and syringes, the filling volume is 90% of the brimful capacity. For ampules, it is the volume up to the height of the shoulder.

Vials and bottles: Select six dry vials or bottles from the sample lot, or three if their capacity exceeds 100 mL, and remove any dirt or debris. Weigh the empty containers with an accuracy of 0.1 g. Place the containers on a horizontal surface, and fill them with purified water to about the rim edge, avoiding overflow and the introduction of air bubbles. Adjust the liquid levels to the brimful line. Weigh the filled containers to obtain the mass of the water expressed to two decimal places, for containers having a nominal volume less than or equal to 30 mL, and expressed to one decimal place, for containers having a nominal volume greater than 30 mL. Calculate the mean value of the brimful capacity in mL, and multiply it by 0.9. This volume, expressed to one decimal place, is the filling volume for the particular container lot.

Cartridges and syringes: Select six dry syringes or cartridges, and seal the small opening (mouth of cartridges, Luer cone, or staked needle of syringes), using an inert material. Determine the mean brimful capacity and filling volume according to Vials and Bottles.

Ampules: Place at least six dry ampules on a flat, horizontal surface, and fill them with purified water from a buret until the water reaches point A, where the body of the ampule starts to decrease to the shoulder of the ampule (see Figure 2). Read the capacities, expressed to two decimal places, and calculate the mean value. This volume, expressed to one decimal place, is the filling volume for the particular ampule lot.

Table 5. Volume of Test Solution and Number of Titrations

Method — Cleaning, Filling, and Heating

Cleaning: Remove any debris or dust. Shortly before the test, rinse each container carefully at least twice with purified water, refilled, and allow to stand. Immediately before testing, empty the containers; rinse once with purified water, then with carbon dioxide-free water; and allow to drain. Complete the cleaning procedure from the first rinsing within 20–30 min. Closed ampules may be warmed in a water bath or in an air oven at about 40° for approximately 2 min before opening to avoid container pressure when opening. Do not rinse before testing.

Filling and heating: The containers are filled with carbon dioxide-free water up to the filling volume. Containers in the form of cartridges or prefillable syringes are closed in a suitable manner with material that does not interfere with the test. Each container, including ampules, shall be loosely capped with an inert material such as a dish of neutral glass or aluminum foil previously rinsed with purified water. Place the containers on the tray of the autoclave. Close the autoclave, and carry out autoclaving procedure steps 1–8 as described in the Glass Grains Test, except that the temperature is maintained at 121 ± 1° for 60 ± 1 min.

Titration

Carry out the titration within 1 h of the removal of the containers from the autoclave. Combine the liquids obtained from the containers, and mix. Introduce the prescribed volume (see Table 5) into a conical flask. Transfer the same volume of carbon dioxide-free water, to be used as a blank, into a second similar flask. Add to each flask 0.05 mL of Methyl red solution for each 25 mL of liquid. Titrate the blank with 0.01 M hydrochloric acid. Titrate the test solution with the same acid until the color of the resulting solution is the same as that obtained for the blank. Subtract the value found for the blank titration from that found for the test solution, and express the results in mL of 0.01 M hydrochloric acid per 100 mL of test solution.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Filling Volume = 90% of Brimful Capacity

The filling volume is set at 90% of the brimful capacity rather than 100%, for the following reasons:

  • It simulates the actual drug product fill volume (regulatory headspace requirement): injectables are usually not filled to the brim, and headspace is left for thermal expansion and lyophilization.
  • Prevents overflow: The water expands when heated in the autoclave; a 90% fill ensures it does not overflow and change the composition of the extract.

For ampoules, the filling volume is the "volume below the shoulder", because of their special geometry; the fill logic differs from that of vials and bottles.

Result (mL/100mL) = (V_test - V_blank) / V_aliquot × 100

where:
V_test = volume of 0.01M HCl used to titrate the test solution (mL)
V_blank = volume of 0.01M HCl used to titrate the blank (mL)
V_aliquot = aliquot volume (per Table 5: 25, 50 or 100 mL)

Analogy

The cleaning steps of the Surface Glass Test (completed within 20–30 minutes) are like the instrument cleaning procedure before surgery in a hospital: the time window is strictly controlled because instruments (containers) exposed to air for too long may be contaminated again (CO₂ absorption, dust deposition). The requirement to complete the titration within 1 hour is similar to a blood sample having to be analyzed within a specified time: the chemical state of the extract changes over time (CO₂ re-absorption), and a delay makes the result unreliable.

Key Notes

Difference in holding time: The Glass Grains Test holds for 30 minutes, the Surface Glass Test for 60 minutes. This is because the extraction surface of an intact container is the glass inner surface (intact surface), whose area-to-mass ratio is far lower than that of powder, so a longer time is needed to reach a similar degree of extraction.

Titration design in Table 5: Larger containers use a larger aliquot (100 mL) and require 2–3 titrations, to improve statistical representativeness, because larger containers show greater individual variation (manufacturing variability).

Combining the liquids: In the test, the extracts from all containers are combined and mixed, and an aliquot is then taken for titration. This is equivalent to taking the average of the batch rather than relying on the result of a single container, and reflects the hydrolytic resistance at batch level more accurately.

Surface Etching Test

The Surface Etching Test is used in addition to the Surface Glass Test when it is necessary to determine whether a container has been surface treated and/or to distinguish between Type I and Type II glass containers. Alternatively, the Glass Grains Test and Surface Glass Test may be used. The Surface Etching Test may be carried out either on unused samples or on samples used in the Surface Glass Test.

Method

Vials and bottles: The volumes of test solution required are shown in Table 5. Rinse the containers twice with purified water, fill to the brimful point with a mixture of one volume of hydrofluoric acid and nine volumes of hydrochloric acid, and allow to stand for 10 min. Empty the containers, and rinse carefully five times with purified water. Immediately before the test, rinse once again with purified water. Submit these containers to the same autoclaving and determination procedure as described in the Surface Glass Test. If the results are considerably higher than those obtained from the original surfaces (by a factor of about 5–10), the samples have been surface treated.

[Caution—Hydrofluoric acid is extremely aggressive. Even small quantities can cause life threatening injuries.]

Ampules, cartridges, and syringes: Apply the test method as described in Vials and Bottles. If the ampules, cartridges, and syringes are not surface treated, the values obtained are slightly lower than those obtained in the previous tests.

[Note—Ampules, cartridges, and syringes made from Type I glass tubing are not normally subjected to internal surface treatment.]

Distinction between Type I and Type II Glass Containers

The results obtained from the Surface Etching Test are compared to those obtained from the Surface Glass Test. For Type I glass containers, the values obtained are close to those found in the Surface Glass Test. For Type II glass containers, the values obtained greatly exceed those found in the Surface Glass Test; and they are similar to, but not greater than, those obtained for Type III containers of the same filling volume.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Design Logic of Table 6

Table 6 shows an important trend: the larger the container, the stricter the limit (the smaller the permitted mL/100mL). This seems counterintuitive at first, but the principle is:

  • Small containers (such as a 1 mL ampoule) have a much higher surface-area-to-volume ratio (S/V ratio) than large containers (a 500 mL bottle).
  • For the same inner surface quality, a small container extracts more alkali relative to its volume, so its limit is "more lenient" (expressed in mL/100mL).
  • A large container has a low S/V ratio; if a lot of alkali is still released per 100 mL, the surface quality is poor, so the limit is stricter.

The Type III limit is about 10 times that of Type I/II, clearly showing the difference in order of magnitude in hydrolytic resistance between them.

Analogy — Surface Etching Test

The Surface Etching Test is like "peeling an onion": a hydrofluoric acid/hydrochloric acid mixture first "peels off" the surface treatment layer of the glass (like removing the outer skin of an onion), and the "true composition" underneath is then tested. If the result rises sharply after peeling (5–10 times), the "onion skin" (the surface treatment) contributed a great deal of resistance, which is the characteristic of Type II glass; if there is little difference before and after peeling, the resistance comes from the "onion itself" (the glass composition), which is the characteristic of Type I.

Hydrofluoric acid (HF) warning: HF is an extremely hazardous chemical; after penetrating the skin it continues to cause damage in the bone. Any operation using HF must be performed in a fume hood, wearing special protective gloves (not ordinary rubber gloves).

Key Notes

The "5–10 times" criterion: After the Surface Etching Test, if the result is 5–10 times higher than the original Surface Glass Test, a surface treatment (dealkalization) is indeed present. This is not a precise calculated limit but an order-of-magnitude judgment, which allows the laboratory some operational flexibility.

Ampoules/cartridges/prefilled syringes are usually not given an inner surface treatment: These containers are made from Type I glass tubing, whose chemical stability is already sufficient, so no secondary surface treatment is needed. This shows that the Surface Etching Test is mainly used to differentiate Type I from Type II for vials/bottles.

Practical Application

When a CDMO accepts glass containers from a new supplier and the supplier's CoA states "Type II treated soda-lime", QC should perform the Surface Etching Test to confirm the presence of the surface treatment. If the surface treatment is missing for some reason during manufacture (for example, an abnormal dealkalization furnace temperature), the Surface Glass Test may still pass (because the sensitivity of the test itself is limited), but the Surface Etching Test will reveal the truth. This is a key item in supplier audits and annual requalification.

Practice Questions

  1. Explain why, in Table 6, the larger the filling volume, the smaller the limit value (mL/100mL). How is this related to the S/V ratio (surface-area-to-volume ratio)?
  2. A container gives 1.2 mL/100mL in the Surface Glass Test and 9.5 mL/100mL after the Surface Etching Test. Decide: (a) Which glass type is it? (b) Is there a surface treatment?
  3. Why can a hydrofluoric acid/hydrochloric acid mixture (ratio 1:9) "remove" the surface treatment layer of glass? What special chemical reaction does HF have with silicate glass?

Section Summary

  • The Glass Grains Test uses 10 g of powder (between sieve Nos. 40 and 50), autoclaved at 121°C for 30 minutes and titrated with 0.02M HCl; the limit is 0.1 mL/g for Type I and 0.85 mL/g for Type II/III.
  • The Surface Glass Test extracts intact containers (121°C for 60 minutes) at a filling volume of 90% of brimful capacity and titrates with 0.01M HCl; the limit is judged from Table 6 by filling volume and type, and the titration is completed within 1 hour.
  • The Table 6 limit design reflects the S/V ratio logic: the larger the container, the lower the permitted alkali release per 100 mL of extract.
  • The Surface Etching Test etches the surface with an HF/HCl mixture and then retests; a result 5–10 times higher indicates a surface treatment (a Type II characteristic), while a similar result indicates Type I.
  • Used together, the three tests fully identify the glass composition type and surface treatment status, providing a comprehensive basis for CDMO container quality control.

2.1 Impurities — Extractable Arsenic

Original Text

IMPURITIES — Arsenic 〈211〉

Use as the Test Preparation 35 mL of the water from one Type I or Type II glass container, or, in the case of smaller containers, 35 mL of the combined contents of several Type I or Type II glass containers, prepared as directed in the Surface Glass Test. The limit does not exceed 0.1 µg/g.

This test applies only to Type I and Type II containers intended for aqueous parenteral products.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Only Arsenic Is Tested

Among the heavy-metal impurities of concern for glass containers, arsenic (As) is singled out by USP <660>, mainly for these reasons:

  • Historical reasons: Traditional glass manufacturing (especially older soda-lime silica glass) used arsenic compounds as fining agents to make the glass more homogeneous and clear. Modern processes have greatly reduced this, but a residual risk remains.
  • Toxicity: Arsenic is a confirmed human carcinogen (IARC Group 1) with cumulative toxicity even at trace levels, an unacceptable risk for patients receiving injectables.
  • Release pathway: The ability of arsenic to dissolve in aqueous solution (especially under alkaline conditions) allows it to be extracted from the glass inner surface into the drug product, which is exactly the extract of the Surface Glass Test.

The 0.1 µg/g limit is calculated on the "weight of extract (g)" rather than volume (mL), because the mass of extract differs for solutions of different density, so a weight basis is more accurate.

Analogy

The arsenic test is like testing drinking water for heavy metals: when a water pipe (glass container) ages, lead or arsenic may dissolve into the water. For injectable drug products the quality requirement for this "pipe" is stricter than for a tap-water pipe, because the drug goes directly into the bloodstream without the filtering protection of the gastrointestinal tract. The 0.1 µg/g limit reflects the strictest safety standard for sterile injectable use.

Key Notes

Source of test sample: The extract from the Surface Glass Test (water from the Surface Glass Test) is used directly rather than prepared separately. This makes the arsenic test an "add-on test" to the Surface Glass Test with no additional autoclave procedure, saving a great deal of time and resources.

Applicability: Limited to Type I or Type II containers for aqueous injectable products. Type III containers are not intended for injectables and do not need this test. Type I/II containers for non-parenteral use are also not required to be tested.

Reference to chapter <211>: USP <211> Arsenic is the General Test for arsenic determination, and <660> cites its method directly, reflecting the modular design of the USP system: a dedicated test method is not rewritten but cross-referenced.

Practical Application

When a CDMO performs incoming quality control on glass containers, if the batch CoA includes Surface Glass Test data but does not explicitly list an arsenic result, QC should proactively ask the supplier to supplement it. Especially for large-molecule biologics (mAbs, proteins) in highly alkaline buffers (pH > 7), arsenic is released from glass at a higher rate, making this a necessary test item in container compatibility studies (CCS studies). The ICH Q3E document (Container Closure System Extractables) also stresses the obligation to evaluate heavy metals as extractables.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is the arsenic limit expressed in µg/g (weight/weight) rather than µg/mL (weight/volume)? What is the difference between the two in actual calculation?
  2. If the arsenic result in the Surface Glass Test extract of a batch of Type II glass containers is 0.08 µg/g, does it meet the specification? If the result is 0.12 µg/g, what actions should be taken?
  3. Why do Type III glass containers not need the arsenic test, instead of being given a more lenient limit?
2.2 Functionality — Spectral Transmission for Colored Glass Containers

Original Text

FUNCTIONALITY — Spectral Transmission for Colored Glass Containers

Apparatus

A UV-Vis spectrophotometer, equipped with either a photodiode detector or a photomultiplier tube coupled with an integrating sphere

Sample Preparation

Break the glass container or cut it with a circular saw fitted with a wet abrasive wheel, such as a carborundum or a bonded diamond wheel. Select sections representative of the wall thickness, and trim them as suitable for mounting in a spectrophotometer. After cutting, wash and dry each specimen, taking care to avoid scratching the surfaces. If the specimen is too small to cover the opening in the specimen holder, mask the uncovered portion of the opening with opaque paper or tape, provided that the length of the specimen is greater than that of the slit. Before placing in the holder, wash, dry, and wipe the specimen with lens tissue. Mount the specimen with the aid of wax, or by other convenient means, taking care to avoid leaving fingerprints or other marks.

Method

Place the specimen in the spectrophotometer with its cylindrical axis parallel to the slit and in such a way that the light beam is perpendicular to the surface of the section and the losses due to reflection are at a minimum. Measure the transmission of the specimen with reference to air in the spectral region of 290–450 nm, continuously or at intervals of 20 nm.

Limits

The observed spectral transmission for colored glass containers for products for nonparenteral use does not exceed 10% at any wavelength in the range of 290–450 nm, irrespective of the type and capacity of the glass container.

The observed spectral transmission in colored glass containers for parenteral products does not exceed the limits given in Table 7.

Table 7. Limits of Spectral Transmission for Colored Glass Containers for Parenteral Products

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Spectral Transmission Testing Is Needed

Many drugs are sensitive to ultraviolet (UV) light, especially in the range of 290–450 nm (UV-A to near-visible light):

  • Vitamins (such as riboflavin and folic acid): UV exposure causes degradation.
  • Protein biologics: light can induce aggregation and oxidation.
  • Certain antibiotics and antineoplastic drugs: photochemical degradation produces toxic by-products.

Colored glass (amber glass) gets its brown/amber color from added metal oxides such as iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) and manganese oxide (MnO₂), and can absorb UV light. However, its protective effect varies with container wall thickness and depth of color, so quantitative testing is required, which is the purpose of the spectral transmission test.

Analogy

Spectral transmission testing is like UV-protection testing of sunglasses: not every dark lens blocks UV effectively. Depth of color is a visual judgment, but real protective ability needs instrument measurement (transmission ≤10%). A pharmaceutical amber glass vial is the drug's "sunglasses", and USP <660> ensures that these sunglasses really do block the "UV rays" that damage the drug.

The role of the integrating sphere: Curved glass (a section cut from the container wall) scatters light, and an ordinary spectrophotometer cannot collect all of the transmitted light. The integrating sphere collects all the scattered light and sends it to the detector, ensuring the measurement represents the "actual transmission" rather than only the directly transmitted component.

How to Read the Table 7 Limits

Transmission limits for colored glass containers for parenteral products (Table 7):

Key observations:
1. The larger the container → the lower the permitted transmission (stricter limit)
   Reason: larger containers have thicker walls and should in theory protect better;
        if transmission is still high, the glass quality is insufficient.

2. Difference in closure method:
   Flame-sealed (ampoules): more lenient limit
   Containers with closures (vials/cartridges): stricter limit

   Reason: the neck wall of containers with closures is usually thinner (≤ the flame-sealed part of an ampoule),
        and light may penetrate through the thin wall, so tighter overall control is needed.

Non-parenteral: single limit ≤ 10%, regardless of volume and closure type
(because the gastrointestinal barrier gives the drug a larger safety margin)

Key Notes

Measurement range of 290–450 nm: UV below 290 nm is absorbed by the ozone layer in the atmosphere and does not actually reach the drug product; above 450 nm is the visible range (blue light), which has no significant photodegradation effect on most drugs. 290–450 nm is the most dangerous "photochemically active window".

"Not more than 10% at any wavelength": This is the strictest form of wording. If transmission is 8% at 320 nm but 12% at 380 nm, the container fails, because exceeding the limit at any one wavelength is a failure. An interpretation of "the average passes" is not allowed.

Exemption when an opaque outer package is used: A clear glass container placed in an opaque outer package (such as a carton) can be exempted from the spectral transmission test requirement under the definition of "light-resistant container" in <659>. This gives a CDMO an alternative path in material selection decisions.

Practical Application

When a CDMO develops a 2 mL injectable for a light-sensitive biologic (such as an ADC, antibody-drug conjugate) and selects a colored glass vial (with closure), Table 7 requires transmission ≤ 15% (2–5 mL, container with closure). If the test result on the supplier CoA is "18% at 350 nm", it does not meet the specification, and the supplier must be changed or asked to improve the formulation (increasing the metal oxide content).

In addition, in stability studies (ICH Q1B, photostability), using an amber vial that does not meet the specification may cause the photostability study to fail, which in turn affects the timeline of the marketing application (NDA/BLA). This is a real-life scenario in CDMO container material selection risk management.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does spectral transmission testing need an "integrating sphere" rather than the standard integration of an ordinary spectrophotometer? How is this related to the geometry of glass containers?
  2. A 15 mL amber glass vial for injectables (with closure) shows 11% transmission at 400 nm and below 12% at all other wavelengths. According to Table 7, is the container acceptable?
  3. If a pharmaceutical company decides to use a clear glass vial with an opaque carton to package a light-sensitive drug, what validation or evaluation is needed for such a design within the GMP system?
  4. Why is the limit for flame-sealed containers (ampoules) in Table 7 more lenient than for containers with closures? Explain from a container design perspective why this difference is reasonable.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Position of USP <660> in the Overall GMP System

USP <660> is the "basic compliance tool" for pharmaceutical glass containers. It is important to understand its relationship to other documents:

  • <660> vs <1660>: <660> is compulsory testing against specifications; <1660> is informational/guidance, covering delamination risk assessment, which is especially important for container compatibility studies of modern biologics.
  • <660> vs ICH Q3E: ICH Q3E (guideline on extractables and leachables) has a broader scope and covers organic extractables; the chemical tests in <660> mainly address inorganic alkali and heavy metals. The two are complementary.
  • <660> vs FDA Container Guidance: FDA's "Container Closure Systems for Packaging Human Drugs and Biologics" guides firms on how to use <660> data to support NDA/ANDA/BLA applications.

Analogy — The Complete Structure of Glass Testing

The test structure of USP <660> is like a three-layer screening system: the first layer is "material identification" (Glass Grains Test: what material are you using?); the second is "finished product surface quality" (Surface Glass Test: what is the actual contact surface of your container like?); the third is "functional verification" (Spectral Transmission: can your container protect the drug from damaging light?). The arsenic test is the "safety baseline", confirming that the container will not actively harm the patient.

Key Notes — About the IRA Revision

The mark "▲ (IRA 1-Oct-2023)" that appears repeatedly in the text of this chapter denotes an Interim Revision Announcement, officially effective from 1 October 2023. These revisions are mainly textual clarifications that unify the wording "glass type" into "glass performance", emphasizing that test results classify glass by "performance" rather than purely by "chemical composition". This subtle difference is particularly important for Type II glass: the "high performance" of Type II comes from the surface treatment, not from the composition of the bulk glass.

Section Summary

  • Extractable arsenic test (Arsenic <211>): only for Type I/II containers for aqueous injectable products, using the Surface Glass Test extract, limit ≤ 0.1 µg/g, to ensure patient safety.
  • Spectral transmission test for colored glass: UV-Vis spectrophotometer with integrating sphere, measuring the 290–450 nm range; non-parenteral ≤10%, parenteral per Table 7 (larger volume, closure type → stricter limit).
  • The two-level design of Table 7 (volume × closure type) reflects the effect of actual container geometry on light protection capability.
  • USP <660> is a compulsory specification standard; <1660> provides supplementary guidance (delamination, inner surface durability); the two must be used together to fully evaluate the glass container risk of modern biologics.
  • The three-layer structure of the whole chapter (chemical resistance → impurity safety → functional protection) forms the complete USP compliance framework for pharmaceutical glass containers and is the core basis for CDMO container material selection decisions.
