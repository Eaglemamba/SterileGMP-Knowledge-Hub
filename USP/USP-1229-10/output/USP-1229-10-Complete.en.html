Learning Objectives

  • Understand the three ionizing radiation modalities (gamma, e-beam, X-ray) and their key differences in penetration, dose rate, and source type
  • Understand the differences among the three ionizing radiation modalities (gamma rays, electron beam, X-rays) in penetration, dose rate, and radiation source
  • Learn the ISO 11137 dose-setting methods (Method 1, 2A, 2B, VDmax) and when to apply each
  • Master the selection logic and applicable situations of the ISO 11137 dose-setting methods
  • Recognize why biological indicators are inappropriate for radiation sterilization and how dosimetry serves as the primary process control
  • Recognize why biological indicators are not used in radiation sterilization, and how dosimeters serve as the process control tool
  • Apply material compatibility assessment and dose mapping principles to CDMO RTU tub and single-use component sterilization scenarios
  • Apply the principles of material compatibility assessment and dose mapping to the sterilization of RTU containers and single-use components at a CDMO

Introduction

Original Text

Radiation sterilization utilizes the lethal effect of various forms of radiation as a means of microbial destruction. Ionizing radiation (gamma, x-ray, or beam) sterilization is used extensively for the sterilization of medical devices and for a variety of other materials and products. Nonionizing sterilization methods such as microwave, infrared, x-ray, and ultraviolet light may be useful but have more restricted application, and are outside the scope of this chapter. This chapter provides an overview of sterilization using ionizing radiation and its validation, including dose setting, material compatibility, and dose verification.

The effects of radiation on materials can be substantial and are a major consideration when manufacturers select radiation as a processing method. The advantages of sterilization by irradiation include simplicity, absence of mechanical complexity, reproducibility, and overall efficiency. In fact, radiation sterilization is unique because the basis of control essentially is the absorbed radiation dose, which can be precisely measured.

Methods used to establish appropriate radiation doses to achieve the desired sterility assurance level are defined in ISO 11137-1 Sterilization of Health Care Products; ISO 11137-2 Sterilization of Health Care Products—Radiation—Part 2: Establishing the Sterilization Dose; and ISO TS 13004:2013 Sterilization of Health Care Products—Radiation—Substantiation of Selected Sterilization Dose: Method VDmaxSD. These methods include Method 1, Method 2A, Method 2B, and Method VDmax, which differ in the specific testing scheme and the number of articles that are needed for testing and are based on certain assumptions about bioburden.

The use of a biological indicator is inappropriate during radiation sterilization validation because (a) there are accurate correlations between dose measurement and microbial destruction for a wide range of microorganisms and (b) the established dose setting methods are based on the material’s bioburden in its natural state. These correlations have been developed by the medical device industry and provide a direct methodology for process control.

Dosimetry plays a central role in radiation sterilization and serves as a direct means for affirming process lethality. The radiation dose measured in kGy (formerly MRads) is directly related to the lethal effects of the radiation on microorganisms. The measured dose has the same utility as F0 in steam sterilization. Routine process control for radiation sterilization is provided by one or more reference dosimeters on the exterior of the packages (after dosimeters have been correlated during validation with dose measurement inside the package). The robustness and reliability of the absorbed dose of the article to be sterilized can support parametric release, as described in Terminally Sterilized Pharmaceutical Products—Parametric Release 〈1222〉, for many items.

“Radiation sterilization is unique because the basis of control essentially is the absorbed radiation dose, which can be precisely measured.”

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The unique advantage of radiation sterilization: Compared with steam sterilization (which requires control of temperature, pressure, and time) or ethylene oxide sterilization (which requires control of concentration, temperature, humidity, and time), radiation sterilization essentially has one control parameter: the absorbed dose. This dose can be measured accurately, which makes radiation the most "quantifiable" of all sterilization methods.

kGy (kilogray): The SI unit of absorbed radiation dose. 1 kGy = 1000 J/kg. The old unit, the Mrad, is no longer used (1 Mrad = 10 kGy).

Analogy to F0: In steam sterilization, F0 is the core measure of process lethality; in radiation sterilization, the dose in kGy plays exactly the same role. Both are measurable physical parameters that can be directly related to microbial kill.

Key Notes

Biological indicators are not applicable: This is a major difference between radiation and steam sterilization. Steam sterilization validation uses biological indicators (BIs) to confirm process lethality, but radiation sterilization does not need them, because:

  • Physical measurements from dosimeters are more reliable and more reproducible than biological systems
  • Precise data already exist correlating dose with microbial kill
  • Dose-setting methods are based on the natural bioburden of the material, not on artificial indicator organisms

Parametric release: Because dose measurement is highly reliable, radiation-sterilized products can be released on the basis of process parameters rather than sterility test results. This is clearly described in USP <1222>.

CDMO Practical Applications

RTU containers and single-use components: For CDMOs that use prefilled syringes (PFS), RTU vials, and RTU tub systems, these components are usually terminally sterilized by the supplier using radiation (usually gamma). The CDMO needs to:

  • Verify that the supplier's certificate of sterility (CoS) meets ISO 11137 requirements
  • Confirm the minimum and maximum dose range and the material compatibility data
  • Include an assessment of the effect of radiation exposure in stability studies
Gamma Sterilization

Original Text

Gamma sterilization entails the use of a specifically designed facility where items to be sterilized are exposed to a Co60 radiation source in a manner that ensures uniform dosing. Highly penetrating photons (gamma rays) are emitted from Co60 as it decays to Ni60. The half-life for this isotope is 5.27 years, which means that over the course of each year the source loses about 12% of its radioactivity. This steady reduction in radioactivity requires that radiation process operators adjust their process controls (typically exposure time) to maintain the established dose required. Periodically, additional Co60 is required to maintain practical throughput.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Co60 source: Cobalt60 is the most commonly used gamma source. As it decays to nickel60 (Ni60), it emits high-energy gamma photons. Key characteristics:

  • Very high penetration: Gamma rays can penetrate thick, dense packaging and large pallet loads without unpacking
  • Half-life of 5.27 years: Roughly 12% of the radioactivity is lost each year, so exposure time must be adjusted periodically or the source replenished
  • Continuous irradiation: Products travel around the source on a conveyor system and receive a continuous dose

Key Notes

Significance for CDMOs: Most RTU container suppliers (such as Schott, Gerresheimer, and SCHOTT Pharma) use gamma sterilization for prefilled glass vials, syringes, and tub systems. When receiving these components, the CDMO should confirm that the supplier's sterilization certificate includes: the actual irradiation dose range, the dosimeter type, and the dose mapping reference for that batch.

X-Ray Sterilization

Original Text

X-ray sterilizers generate highly penetrative photons similar to the gamma photons from Co60 irradiators. X-ray photons are generated when accelerated electrons impact a target such as tantalum. These systems rely on scanning of materials with x-ray photons in order to sterilize them. Properly maintained, these systems are able to deliver a constant dose over time. No local radioactive source is required for x-ray sterilization systems.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

X-ray vs gamma: X-ray sterilization generates photons with physical properties similar to gamma rays (both are highly penetrating photons), but the sources are entirely different:

  • Gamma: From the nuclear decay of the radioisotope Co60
  • X-ray: From accelerated electrons striking a metal target such as tantalum; it is electrically generated

Main advantages: No on-site radioactive source is needed and the system can be switched on and off at will; the dose does not decay over time; and penetration comparable to gamma can be provided.

E-Beam Sterilization

Original Text

Electron beam systems rely on scanning of objects with focused electrons to sterilize the items within a defined radiation field. Properly maintained and controlled, these systems deliver a constant dose, so there is no change in dose with respect to time. The principal advantages of electron beam sterilization are a much higher dose rate and the absence of a localized radioactive source. These systems can be installed and operated by the end user.

Electron beam penetration is substantially less than that obtained with photons, and therefore dose mapping is critical to ensure that items of varying density and complexity are properly sterilized. Because of the high dose rates used with electron beam sterilization, some materials can experience significantly higher temperatures than the same materials would experience in Co60 irradiation.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Characteristics of electron beam:

  • High dose rate: Processing is extremely fast, with product exposure measured in seconds (gamma is measured in hours)
  • Low penetration: Electrons penetrate far less than photons, so the method suits only thin or low-density items
  • Temperature effect: The high dose rate may cause the material to heat up, so the compatibility of heat-sensitive materials must be evaluated
  • No radioactive source: Can be installed and operated by the end user

Key Notes

Dose mapping is even more critical: Because electron beam penetration is limited, variations in item density and geometry significantly affect the dose distribution. Dose mapping is therefore more important for e-beam sterilization than for gamma.

Validation of Radiation Sterilization

Original Text

Cycle development for radiation requires the identification of an appropriate radiation dose for the objects and confirmation that the dose does not adversely affect the material’s essential quality attributes. In other words, analysts should identify the minimum sterilization dose as well as the maximum dose the material can withstand without negative effects. With this information analysts can set the dose for a specific radiation sterilization application.

Dose setting or dose establishment typically is achieved by following one of the ISO methods. These are Method 1, Method 2A, Method 2B, and Method VDmax. The choice of the most appropriate method depends chiefly on production batch size, knowledge of the normal bioburden, and the material’s sensitivity to radiation. Dose mapping plays an important role through the cycle development and dose-setting exercise.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dose window: The core objective of radiation sterilization validation is to establish a "dose window":

  • Minimum dose (Dmin): The lowest dose sufficient to achieve the target SAL (usually 10−6)
  • Maximum dose (Dmax): The highest dose the material can tolerate without affecting its quality attributes

The larger the Dmax / Dmin ratio, the wider the process window and the greater the operating flexibility.

Key Notes

Three key factors in choosing a dose-setting method:

  1. Batch size: Large batches suit Method 1; for small batches, consider VDmax
  2. Knowledge of bioburden: Use Method 1 when the bioburden can be enumerated; use Method 2 when it cannot
  3. Radiation sensitivity of the material: Sensitive materials need more precise dose control, which suits the refined approach of Method 1
Dose Setting

Original Text

Method 1 is based upon the assignment and verification of a sterilization dose based on a microbial population. The resistance of the microbial population is not determined, and dose setting is based on a standard radiation resistance assigned to the microbial population, derived from data obtained from medical device manufacturers and from the literature. This analysis assumes that the distribution of standard resistance represents a more severe challenge than the natural microbial population on the material to be sterilized. A verification dose study should confirm the relative resistance assumption.

The VDmax method is similar to Method 1 (it requires both bioburden and dose verification testing) but relies on bioburden ranges (e.g., <1000 CFUs per item for a 25-kGy sterilization dose and, for example, 0.1–1.5 CFUs per item for a 15-kGy sterilization dose).

The more complex Method 2 does not require the enumeration of the microbial population for the purpose of setting the sterilization dose (although it is required for routine monitoring and control) but uses a series of incremental dose exposures to establish a dose at which approximately 1 out of 100 samples irradiated at that dose will be nonsterile. This is not the sterilization dose, but it provides the basis to determine the sterilization dose by extrapolation from this information.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

The unique logic of Method 2: Method 2 does not measure bioburden directly. Instead, it uses incremental dose experiments to find the dose at which about 1 in 100 samples is not sterile. From that point, it extrapolates upward to calculate the sterilization dose that achieves a PNSU of 10−6. This is a "bottom-up" approach, the opposite direction from Method 1, which "starts from a known bioburden."

Key Concepts: Dose Setting Methods

Where 25 kGy comes from: Many people believe 25 kGy is the "standard" sterilization dose, but it is actually the dose set by the VDmax method for items with a bioburden of <1000 CFU. If the bioburden is lower, 15 kGy may be enough. It is not a "universal number" but the result of a calculation based on specific conditions.

Material Compatibility

Original Text

Once the required dose level has been established, the maximum dose should be established. Analysts typically establish the maximum dose by evaluating the highest likely dose that might be seen during the sterilization process, adding a safety factor, and evaluating the item for immediate and long-term effects of the radiation exposure. Some materials may appear unchanged initially, and the effects may become evident only over time.

The evaluation should consider all of the materials exposed to the radiation processing, especially the drug product and its primary container. Product stability, safety, and functionality should be confirmed over the product’s intended use period.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three levels of material compatibility assessment:

  1. Immediate effects: Changes detected right after irradiation (appearance, color, odor, mechanical strength)
  2. Long-term effects: Changes that appear only after storage (embrittlement, yellowing, release of extractables)
  3. Safety factor: Dmax should exceed the highest dose that could occur in the process, plus an appropriate safety margin

CDMO Practical Applications

Material compatibility of RTU containers and single-use system (SUS) components:

  • Glass containers (Type I borosilicate): Extremely stable to radiation; material compatibility is usually not a concern
  • Plastic components (such as bag films, tubing, and connectors): Radiation may cause polymer chain scission (embrittlement) or crosslinking (hardening), and the generation of extractables (E&L)
  • Rubber closures (stoppers, plungers): Radiation may affect elasticity, sealing performance, and extractables test results
  • The drug product itself: If radiation sterilization is applied to the final product, drug product stability must be evaluated
Dose Verification

Original Text

The methods for cycle development and dose setting rely on the bioburden approach. Analysts use defined presterilization bioburden controls and periodic evaluation of the process effects on the bioburden to maintain cycle efficacy. Establishing the required dose for microbial destruction during cycle development uses the bioburden’s natural resistance; analysts then extrapolate the dose-setting algorithms to establish a dose that is capable of delivering a probability of a nonsterile unit (PNSU) of 1 × 10−6.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Bioburden approach: Dose setting for radiation sterilization is based entirely on the natural microbial contamination on the product, not on artificial indicator organisms. This is because:

  • Extensive research data already exist on the lethality of radiation against different microorganisms
  • The radiation resistance of natural bioburden is usually lower than the assumed value for indicator organisms
  • The verification dose study confirms whether this assumption holds

PNSU 10−6: That is, at most one non-sterile unit is allowed per million sterilized units. This is the same as the SAL 10−6 target for steam sterilization.

Analogy

The meaning of Figure 1: Imagine two castles. One has 1000 soldiers (high bioburden), all in heavy armor (high radiation resistance), and needs a powerful siege force (high dose). The other has just 1 sentry (low bioburden) with no armor (low resistance), and a small patrol (low dose) can take it. This is why bioburden and resistance together determine the required sterilization dose.

Validation Activities

Original Text

Equipment Qualification

The use of gamma sterilization requires initial and periodic assessment of equipment controls and parameters necessary to establish the system’s capability. Sterilization systems that deliver directed beams or rays have controls for scan speed, source intensity, and system timers. The other elements of radiation sterilization equipment largely are related to material transport and are easily qualified. Qualification of safety controls, devices, and software is required.

Empty Chamber Dose Mapping

This optional exercise entails mapping the target area for radiation dose in the absence of a load and is a possible means to evaluate a focused beam or ray system. It provides a baseline of performance that may be useful over time.

Load Dose Mapping

The arrangement of items in irradiation containers, carriers, or pallets is an essential part of the initial validation exercise. The goal of the mapping is to define the distribution of a dose throughout the load items and establish a configuration that minimizes dose variation across the materials. The items are mapped using multiple dosimeters positioned internally and externally. Identification of maximum dose location is important in evaluating the effects of the radiation on the load items. The location of minimum and maximum dose can be identified from the dose mapping data for monitoring in routine sterilization of materials.

Biological Indicators

The use of biological indicators for radiation sterilization is not indicated because the physical and dosimetric measurements employed are more reliable, reproducible, and robust than biological systems.

Dosimetry

Process control for radiation sterilization relies heavily on dosimetry for both initial development and ongoing verification. For guidance on the selection and use of a dosimetry system for use in radiation sterilization refer to ASTM E2628 Practice for Dosimetry in Radiation Processing. The dosimeters and the instruments used with them should be calibrated according to ISO/ASTM 51261 Practice for Calibration of Routine Dosimetry Systems for Radiation Processing.

Process Confirmation

The core of the validation activity is the confirmation of acceptable lethality using dosimeters that are positioned across the material as it is processed through the radiation-sterilizing equipment. Proof of sterilization cycle efficacy is provided in replicate studies in which the dosimetry results correspond to the required minimum value for sterility assurance and demonstrate that the maximum value has not been exceeded.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

Key outputs of dose mapping:

  • Dmin location: Where the routine monitoring dosimeter is placed, to confirm that the minimum dose is achieved
  • Dmax location: The basis for assessing material compatibility, to confirm that the maximum dose does not exceed the material's tolerance limit
  • Dose uniformity ratio (DUR): The Dmax/Dmin ratio; the closer to 1.0 the better, indicating a uniform dose distribution

Dosimetry standards: ASTM E2628 provides guidance on selecting dosimetry systems; ISO/ASTM 51261 specifies calibration requirements. Common dosimeter types include alanine dosimeters and film dosimeters.

CDMO Practical Applications

For CDMOs receiving RTU tubs and SUS components: Although sterilization is performed by the supplier, the CDMO should confirm that the supplier's validation documents include:

  • Complete dose mapping data and DUR values
  • Dosimeter calibration records and traceability
  • The actual irradiation dose range on the batch release certificate
  • Bioburden monitoring and periodic dose audit records
Routine Process Control

Original Text

Radiation sterilization should be subject to formal controls that maintain the validated status. The practices outlined in General Principles of Sterilization of Compendial Articles 〈1229〉 provide the general requirements appropriate for all sterilization systems. This is accomplished by a number of related practices that are essential for the continued use of the process over an extended period of time. The practices that are essential to maintain validated status for radiation include training, calibration, physical measurements, bioburden monitoring, change control, preventive maintenance, and periodic dose audits.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Seven practices for maintaining the validated state:

  1. Training: Confirmation of operator competence and continuing education
  2. Calibration: Periodic calibration of dosimeters and related instruments
  3. Physical measurements: Routine recording and review of process parameters
  4. Bioburden monitoring: Regular confirmation that the pre-sterilization microbial level of the product does not exceed the assumptions used in dose setting
  5. Change control: Management of any change in load configuration, materials, or process
  6. Preventive maintenance: A planned program of periodic equipment maintenance
  7. Periodic dose audits: Usually quarterly or annually, to reconfirm that the dose setting remains valid

Key Notes

Relationship to USP <1229>: USP <1229.10> is a subchapter of USP <1229> (General Principles of Sterilization). <1229> provides general principles for all sterilization methods, while <1229.10> addresses radiation sterilization specifically. Other subchapters include:

Chapter Summary

  • Radiation Sterilization uses ionizing radiation (gamma, e-beam, X-ray) to destroy microorganisms; control is based on the precisely measurable absorbed dose (kGy), making it the most quantifiable sterilization method
  • Three modalities offer different trade-offs: gamma (high penetration, isotope source), X-ray (high penetration, electrical), e-beam (highest dose rate, limited penetration)
  • Dose setting follows ISO 11137 methods (1, 2A, 2B, VDmax), chosen based on batch size, bioburden knowledge, and material sensitivity
  • Biological indicators are not used — dosimetry provides more reliable, reproducible process control than biological systems
  • Material compatibility must assess both immediate and long-term radiation effects on drug product and primary container
  • Dose mapping identifies Dmin and Dmax locations for routine monitoring; the dose uniformity ratio (DUR, Dmax/Dmin) characterizes load configuration quality
  • Routine control requires training, calibration, bioburden monitoring, change control, preventive maintenance, and periodic dose audits
  • CDMO relevance: RTU tubs and SUS components are typically gamma-sterilized by suppliers; CDMO must verify supplier certificates, dose ranges, and material compatibility data
