1. Introduction

Original Text

It is the intent of USP general test chapters Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉 and Particulate Matter in Injections 〈788〉 that systems, packaging, and formulation be selected and developed with regard to commercial product cleanliness and stability. Monitoring and limiting particulate levels are an important effort in ensuring product quality and controlling the risk of adverse effects or loss of drug efficacy.

A related chapter, Measurement of Subvisible Particulate Matter in Therapeutic Protein Injections 〈1787〉, categorizes particulate matter as intrinsic, extrinsic, or inherent. Process streams, equipment, and other factors related to the manufacturing process may contribute intrinsic particulate matter. Formula components and container/closure systems may contribute to immediate and even long-term generation of intrinsic particles. Poor selection or cleaning of components and systems can exacerbate these problems. The production environment, including personnel gowning and behavior, may contribute extrinsic particulate matter to the filled product. Finally, some products may have a small level of unintended particulate matter that is inherent to the product, even after careful formulation.

Historically, the first USP requirements for determination of subvisible particulate matter used the microscopic membrane (MM) method. Membrane filtration has the advantage of collecting a wide size range of particles and retaining these for later analysis, but MM is labor intensive and requires special facilities and operator training. Because it is automated and robust, the light obscuration (LO) method was introduced in USP standards in 1985. By 1995, both methods had been optimized and applied in 〈788〉 to both large and small volume products.

Several USP chapters are related to subvisible particles, including three compendial chapters, Subvisible Particulate Matter in Therapeutic Protein Injections 〈787〉, 〈788〉, Subvisible Particulate Matter in Intraocular Solutions 〈789〉, and the aforementioned informational chapter 〈1787〉. This informational chapter is meant to complement the others and focuses on the description of the LO, MM, and flow imaging (FI) methods.

Chapters 〈788〉 (a harmonized chapter) and 〈789〉 provide an LO test (Method 1) for sizing and counting of subvisible particles (generally 2–100 µm) for nearly transparent, low viscosity ("waterlike") product samples, and an MM particle count test (Method 2) for manual particle sizing and counting as an alternate method. 〈787〉 also includes LO and is especially pertinent to protein therapeutics. FI is recommended as a third method to complement LO and MM, especially for particle characterization, and during product development.

In the LO method, a laser beam shines through the liquid sample as it is drawn through an optical flow cell. Any particles, air bubbles, or liquid droplets will scatter or absorb light to some degree, briefly reducing the total transmitted light. Each transient drop in detected light is counted as one particle. The MM method consists of filtering the liquid sample, inspecting the dried filter through a microscope, and manually counting particles above stated size thresholds. For the FI method, the sample is drawn through a flow cell and a sequence of photographic images obtained of particles in the sample. Subsequent automated analysis gives image parameters such as size, morphology, and image intensity, which are useful to categorize particle images and provide improved understanding of the different particle types observed.

Analogy: The Three Particle Types as Foreign Objects in the Kitchen

Imagine foreign matter in a pot of soup (the drug product):

  • Extrinsic particles: A fly that flew in from outside. It comes from the external environment and signals a failure of the manufacturing environment or of aseptic control.
  • Intrinsic particles: Flakes shed from the pot or container. They come from the process, the equipment or the container closure system.
  • Inherent particles: The soy pulp in soy milk. It is a characteristic of the product formulation itself, such as protein self-aggregation.

The three carry different levels of risk, so the control strategies differ as well.

Key Point: The Fundamental Differences Between the Three Analytical Methods

  • LO (light obscuration): Automated and high-throughput, but it cannot distinguish solids from droplets or bubbles, and low-refractive-index particles (such as protein aggregates) are undercounted.
  • MM (membrane microscopy): Particles are retained for later identification, but the method is labor-intensive and time-consuming, and it is not suitable for silicone oil or protein aggregates.
  • FI (flow imaging): Captures an image of every particle and gives rich morphological information, but it has not yet been incorporated into the <788> compendial limits.

2. Scope

Original Text

This chapter is meant to expand upon the information in 〈787〉, 〈788〉, and Subvisible Particulate Matter in Intraocular Solutions 〈789〉 by including FI techniques and by providing additional guidance on the recommended use of LO and MM.

The use of the FI method for particle sizing and enumeration in this chapter constitutes a substantial update to past approaches. FI has become a valuable orthogonal method that provides morphology data. These data contribute to a better understanding of the particle population, help resolve discrepancies between other methods, and provide insight into further definition of the sources of the particle load, supporting risk assessment and evaluation of acceptability.

FI is not currently planned for inclusion in 〈788〉 because: 1) 〈788〉 is a harmonized chapter, and addition of a new technique would require new discussions for acceptance; 2) this is an emerging technique and there is both a lack of historical data from products that have patient exposure and an undetermined relationship of the particle size distribution obtained with FI relevant to that determined by LO or MM, making the establishment of specifications difficult; and 3) specifications (if/when set in the future) should be set by the stakeholder/company and should be product specific, based on prior knowledge and risk assessment.

This chapter directly supports 〈788〉 and 〈789〉; however, the chapter is also useful for 〈787〉, which addresses particulate matter testing for therapeutic protein products. Therapeutic protein products require special care in terms of particle measurement, and 〈787〉 provides modifications to 〈788〉 methods that are suitable for protein and other large-molecule formulations.

Tracking and identifying particles allow identification of the sources of the particle load and provide a means to evaluate product acceptability and drive product improvement. USP subvisible particle concentration limits apply to particle sizes ≥10 µm. Note that 〈788〉 carries limits for 10- and 25-µm particles, and 〈789〉 carries limits for 10-, 25-, and 50-µm particles. These limits were based on historical clinical exposure, not necessarily the demonstrated impact on patient safety.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Orthogonal Methods

Orthogonal methods: Analyzing a sample with methods that work on different principles and measure complementary attributes, in order to gain a more complete understanding. It is like photographing one object from the front, by X-ray and in cross-section: each method shows a different aspect.

In particle analysis, LO tells you how many particles there are, MM tells you what they look like, and FI tells you the shape and color of each individual particle. Only the three together give the complete picture.

Key Point: Why Is 10 µm the Critical Threshold?

The USP limits are based on ≥10 µm, which originates from clinical experience in the 1970s and 1980s. It is not direct proof of the effect on patients of particles smaller than 10 µm.

The modern view is that for biologics (especially protein drug products), subvisible particles of 2–10 µm may also trigger immunogenicity, which is why <787> and <1787> recommend extending the detection range to ≥2 µm.

Analogy: Why Not Simply Put FI into the Compendial Limits?

Think of a new subject in the driving test, such as a night-time road test. It would technically assess driving ability better, but making it part of the formal rules requires nationwide uniform standards, a large body of historical data and trained examiners. FI is currently at exactly this stage: very promising but not yet standardized.

3. Background

Original Text

3.1 Testing Considerations

LO is the preferred first-pass method for 〈787〉, 〈788〉, and 〈789〉, although certain solution formulations may not be easily analyzed using this technique. The LO method may encounter problems with a product that does not have clarity or viscosity approximating that of water. Further, formulation characteristics (e.g., color or viscosity) or inherent formulation properties (e.g., shear-induced changes) may generate erroneous LO data. Similarly, products that produce air or gas bubbles when drawn through the LO sensor, such as bicarbonate-buffered formulations, may generate erroneous high-bias data. LO has an inherent bias for sizing larger or fibrous particles (Feret's diameter ≥50 µm).

The LO method can also be applied to particle sizes considerably smaller than the 〈788〉 lower limit of 10 µm with good reproducibility and to particle sizes much larger than the upper limit of 25 µm, with proper sensor selection. Thus, valuable information on the product for visible and subvisible particle content is enhanced by including a few more size categories in the subvisible methods such as ≥2 µm and ≥5 µm, the compendial thresholds ≥10 µm and ≥25 µm, and perhaps ≥50 µm and ≥100 µm.

The MM method is useful for testing formulations that are not amenable to LO, such as those with color and viscosity affecting optical detection and those that are emulsions. MM isolates particles as a filter retentate, which a trained and qualified operator then uses to determine the count and size population. While the operator counts particles on the membrane surface that meet counting criteria (≥10 µm and <25 µm or ≥25 µm), there is also a concurrent, streaming view of particle appearance that provides an impression of the particle types or categories that are present. MM methods will not be useful for evaluation of silicone oil droplets or protein aggregates, because these flexible, amorphous particles are difficult to detect on the membrane and can pass through the pores as well.

The FI method has the advantage that all particles, liquid droplets, and air bubbles are captured in microscopic images. The images can be classified by morphological and image-intensity parameters, as well as by particle size. The classification can be useful in determining the relative proportions of different particle types, such as silicone oil droplets and protein aggregates.

3.2 The Nature of Particulate Matter

Particles arise from many different sources. Over time, with growing experience in manufacturing and administration of therapeutics, it has become apparent that not all particles are equivalent in terms of their effects on safety or indications for product stability. One way to capture this difference in the risk associated with particles is to classify them into three broad categories: extrinsic, intrinsic, and inherent (See 〈1787〉). However, within these categories, different particle types can pose different patient safety risks.

Ophthalmic Products—Quality Tests 〈771〉, Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉, 〈787〉, and 〈1787〉 have all adopted a unified definition for particulate matter. Two categories, extrinsic and intrinsic, describe the traditionally held concept of particles as foreign matter. A third category, inherent, describes particles that represent the character of the drug formulation and typify acceptable product.

Heterogeneous particle populations are common (e.g., more than one chemical entity with many variations of density, refractive index, color, and shape). Heterogeneous particles are classified as either extrinsic or intrinsic based on the non-proteinaceous component that presents the greatest risk. Orthogonal techniques must be used to fully understand this particle type. As with all quality attributes, the ultimate decision on what levels are allowed should be tied to risk assessment and the clinical history of the product.

In the case of protein-based pharmaceutics, there is an additional source of particles and associated product safety concerns. Proteins self-associate based on the inherent molecular properties of the specific molecule and can form aggregates in a broad size range, including 1–100 µm and larger. Proteinaceous particles in this size range can be quantified using LO and FI. Aggregated proteins have the potential to be immunogenic, adding another level of complexity to the monitoring and control of subvisible particles in protein therapeutics.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Systematic Bias of LO

LO measures the cross-sectional area with which a particle blocks light, so:

  • Spherical particles: Measured accurately.
  • Fibrous particles: The reported equivalent circular diameter reflects the fiber width rather than its length (overestimated).
  • Low-refractive-index particles (such as protein aggregates): Poor light obscuration, so they are undercounted.
  • Silicone oil droplets/air bubbles: Counted as particles even though they are not solid foreign matter.

These biases are not instrument malfunctions. They follow from the physical principle of the LO method and must be assessed during method validation.

Formula Concept: Refractive Index (RI) and Detection Sensitivity

Detection sensitivity ∝ |RI particle - RI solution|

RI of water ≈ 1.33
RI of silicone oil ≈ 1.40
RI of protein aggregates ≈ 1.35 (difference only ~0.02)

→ The LO signal of protein aggregates is weak,
  so they are easily undercounted or fall below the detection limit

Analogy: The Practical Meaning of the Three Particle Categories

Think of food safety management in a restaurant:

  • Extrinsic: Dirt brought in by a customer (environmental contamination). It signals that the GMP environment is out of control and is the most serious.
  • Intrinsic: Rust scraped off a pot by the cook (equipment/container contamination). It signals a process design problem that needs engineering improvement.
  • Inherent: The protein foam on a stew (a product characteristic). It is unavoidable, so its acceptability must be justified scientifically.

Key Point: The Regulatory Challenge of Protein Immunogenicity

Aggregates of protein drug products (such as monoclonal antibodies and fusion proteins) can trigger an immune response in patients (anti-drug antibodies, ADA). This can affect efficacy and may even be life-threatening (severe hypersensitivity reactions).

This is exactly why <787> sets stricter requirements for protein drug products, and why a comprehensive particle analysis strategy must be established early in drug development.

4. Detection and Enumeration of Particles | 5. Applicability of Methods

Original Text

Particle formation often occurs across a size continuum with the lower end of the visible range near the upper region of the detection methods described herein. The size range detected depends on the particle type and method used, and care should be taken to choose the appropriate method for the population being tested.

The techniques reviewed in this chapter are applicable for the determination of unwanted particle content, such as contamination, solids from instability, and insufficient package cleaning, as well as particles that arise from lubricants, product contact, formulation components, and the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) itself. These particles vary in size from tens of nanometers to hundreds of micrometers.

Pharmaceutical products come in various forms with large differences in particle size distributions and in concentrations of particle types. Drug forms may include nanoparticulate suspensions, solid drug suspensions, and emulsions. Extremes of drug concentration and factors such as color and transmission variability, along with a wide array of active ingredient sources that have small to very large molecular weights, may all play a role in particulate formation.

Evaluation, test method development, and control for particles in the formulation in the <10-µm range can be important for some of these products. For biologics and vaccines, detection below 10 µm is important to capture protein aggregate contents (see 〈787〉 and 〈1787〉). Ophthalmic solutions require particle determination at an additional 50-µm threshold, as specified by 〈789〉.

No single method should be used during development, but rather orthogonal testing should be employed for full characterization and understanding of the expected particle population for that particular product. During production, if the LO lot release method fails, these methods can be deployed to determine root cause and other factors.

All three methods produce appropriate counts in size thresholds when used properly and applied to appropriate sample formulations and particle populations. But each can be misused, and each has inherent drawbacks to consider. Calibration and operator training are imperative.

5. Applicability of Methods

All sterile solutions for injection or parenteral infusion are included within the scope of these test regimens. However, some solutions are amenable to the methods while others are not. All will require qualification of the methods for specific product forms and formulation families.

Clear, aqueous-solution formulations with low to moderate concentrations of the API, and simple vehicles, will be the most suitable (with minimal method development or changes) and amenable to all 〈1787〉 and 〈1788〉 methods. Of greatest concern for all techniques are formulations that have competing solids, suspensions (either as-is or after resuspension), emulsions, and nanodispersions.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Particle Size Continuum

By size, particles fall into four zones:

  • Nanoparticles: <2 µm. These require special techniques such as DLS and NTA.
  • Subvisible: 2–100 µm. The main detection range of LO/MM/FI.
  • Visible gray zone: 50–200 µm. Detection by the human eye is probabilistic and unreliable.
  • Visible: >100–200 µm. Covered by visual inspection, <790>.

The compendial limits focus on ≥10 µm and ≥25 µm, but a complete quality control strategy should cover a wider size range.

Key Point: Decision Logic for Method Selection

Decision flow for selecting a particle analysis method:

  1. Is the product clear, low-viscosity and colorless? → Yes: use LO as the primary method.
  2. Is the product colored or highly viscous? → Consider MM or a method modification.
  3. Is it an emulsion or a suspension? → It may not be possible to test it directly; separation is needed first.
  4. Is it a protein drug product? → Add FI as an orthogonal method to evaluate aggregates.

Analogy: Comparing the Methods Is Like Different Types of Security Screening

Airport security uses several kinds of equipment: an X-ray machine (scans baggage quickly, like LO), a manual bag search (careful examination, like MM) and a 3D body scanner (a complete image, like FI). No single method is universal; only a combination gives the highest level of assurance.

Practical Application: CDMO Method Development Strategy

When taking on a new product from a client, a CDMO should define the analytical method strategy according to the product characteristics:

  • Clear small-molecule product: use the standard LO method directly, for fast release.
  • Protein drug product: establish both LO and FI methods and evaluate the effect of the aggregate RI.
  • Ophthalmic solution: must meet the stricter limits of <789> (including the ≥50 µm threshold).
  • Suspension: method development studies (dilution, phase separation) are needed before any method can be applied.

6. Particulate Material Evaluation

Original Text

For all particle determination efforts, and ultimately to reach a comprehensive control strategy, it is necessary to understand the category, if not the identity, of the particle population. As described in this chapter, extrinsic and intrinsic particles may be quantified (using LO), quantified and described (using MM and FI), and isolated (using MM) for further analysis.

For extrinsic and intrinsic particle varieties, it is recommended to use a database consisting of an internal particle library that provides particle references. The database is a key component of particle classification, risk assessment, and the ultimate identification. Some public domain and vendor databases are available, but the best database comes from compilation of particle species from your own operations.

Evaluation of the inherent particle-population baseline will utilize 〈788〉 techniques but also those described in 〈1787〉. For protein therapeutic drugs, establishment of the inherent baseline of proteinaceous particles is necessarily important and has been discussed in detail.

LO provides only particle concentration for specified size bins. Although these data are valuable for tracking product quality and manufacturing consistency, LO does not differentiate among particles based on particle types. Both MM and FI provide additional information on particle attributes other than size. FI reveals the images of all particles streaming by the camera system and thus allows classification according to morphology and image intensity. MM not only tabulates counts but also reveals the particle image and provides an isolate for analysis after counting.

However, much more can be done to fully characterize and possibly identify particulate matter with an integrated approach using light microscopy, vibrational spectroscopy, elemental analysis, and electron microscopy. These references describe a forensic pathway of identification.

Applicability of any of these tests for specific products should be evaluated using appropriate qualification steps. Qualification of the instrument should be based on frequency of use, maintenance schedule, and accuracy requirements. General guidance on performing qualification is provided in Validation of Compendial Procedures 〈1225〉 and Verification of Compendial Procedures 〈1226〉.

6.1 Final Filter Exemption

General chapter 〈788〉 provides the following: "Parenteral products for which the labeling specifies use of a final filter prior to administration are exempt from the requirements of 〈788〉, provided that scientific data are available to justify this exemption." The purpose of this exemption is to offer a mechanism to reduce the subvisible particulate burden inherent in the final drug product when the amount would exceed acceptable limits and cannot otherwise be reduced through formulation adjustments or the manufacturing process prior to use.

The utilization of an in-line filter should be driven by risk benefit evaluation of the potential clinical implication of subvisible particulates for the intended user population, but not be recommended to mitigate a Current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP)-related product quality issue (e.g., the presence of extrinsic particles). The application of this exemption should be supported by scientific evidence sufficient to justify its adoption and generally include the composition and mechanism of formation of the particles in addition to demonstrating that process-optimization strategies failed to reduce the particle load.

Labeled recommendations of filter type and size should also be supported by appropriate drug product compatibility assessments, consistency in particle removal, and clinical data as necessary. Demonstration that drug potency is not significantly affected is essential. An associated risk assessment is strongly encouraged to evaluate the potential clinical implications to the intended user populations if a final in-line filter is not utilized.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Importance of Building a Particle Library

Internal particle library: A reference library built by collecting and analyzing, over the long term, the various particles that appear in your own manufacturing process, with photographs, chemical composition, size characteristics and source traceability.

This library is a core tool for quality investigations. When a new batch shows an abnormal particle count, you first compare against the library to determine whether the particle is a known, acceptable one or an unknown particle that signals a new problem.

How to build it in practice: particles isolated by MM → microscope photographs → identification by infrared spectroscopy (IR) → record the source and the disposition outcome

Key Point: The Strict Conditions for the Final Filter Exemption

Use of the Final Filter Exemption must satisfy the following:

  1. Process optimization measures have been exhausted and the particle load cannot be reduced further
  2. There is sufficient scientific evidence explaining the composition and formation mechanism of the particles
  3. There are compatibility data for the filter specification and data on the consistency of particle removal
  4. It is confirmed that filtration does not affect potency (potency testing)
  5. Strictly prohibited: The exemption must not be used to mask GMP compliance problems (such as extrinsic contamination).

Analogy: The Final Filter Exemption Is Not a Cure-All

Imagine a restaurant whose food occasionally contains grains of sand (extrinsic contamination). Telling customers to "filter it yourself before eating" is not a solution; it only hides a hygiene problem in the kitchen. The Final Filter Exemption applies only where "the ingredients themselves contain natural particles that are difficult to remove completely" (inherent, or in part intrinsic, particles). It cannot be used to cover up environmental or equipment contamination.

7. USP Subvisible Particle Limits

Original Text

Current limits for 〈787〉 and harmonized 〈788〉 methods are set at maximum allowable particles per container or particles per unit volume at size thresholds ≥10 and ≥25 µm. These limits are historical and have changed over time based on method development and species of concern. Chapter 〈789〉 applies the 10- and 25-µm thresholds as well, plus a 50-µm threshold. In 〈789〉, the limits are provided only on a per milliliter basis.

The calculation of allowable particle content is based on product volume (as determined by the manufacturer) for the SVI (small-volume injection) market, and on a particles-per-milliliter basis for the LVI (large-volume injection) market. However, these limits are generous, and actual particle loads are often an order of magnitude below them. All efforts to reduce particle load and maintain a consistent low-particle contribution are encouraged.

Table 2. 〈788〉 Particle Content Limits. [Note: 〈787〉 limits total LVI particles to 6000 ≥10 µm and 600 ≥25 µm. For ophthalmic products, consult 〈771〉]

No limits have been established for the use of FI or for the collection of data with size thresholds <10 µm for 〈787〉, 〈788〉, and 〈789〉. As Table 2 shows, the registration limits vary according to technique and injection product volume. Furthermore, as discussed previously, the technique capabilities are certainly not equivalent across a broad range of sizes.

Particle concentration should be monitored even for samples that give results below the Table 2 limits. Although technically within USP specifications, particle content in a single container near the limit may be a concern and may indicate problems in product quality. Results that are out of trend with what is normal for a product, even if well below the specification limits, would be of concern as well and should also be investigated.

The Table 2 particle concentration limits are often significantly higher than the particle concentrations observed with many commercial products. The limits apply both at time of release and at the end of shelf life, and they apply to a range of product types, from the simplest to the most complex formulas with stability issues. These limits are historical and based on historical clinical exposure; internal target choices allow for consideration of product histories, product types, therapeutic uses, and patient populations.

The intent of these tests is to provide a control strategy framework for designing the most stable formula, selecting the most stable and inert package, and employing an elegant fill/finish in a high-quality, state-of-the-market factory. The limits are imposed as a minimal standard of control, with the intent that the innovator, contract manufacturing operation, or generic producer will meet and exceed this standard across all injectable dosage forms.

Core Concept: Why Are the LO Limits Less Strict Than the MM Limits?

LO counts include solid particles, silicone oil droplets and air bubbles, so its counts are inherently higher. To keep the two comparable, USP gives LO limits twice as generous (for example, SVI ≥10 µm: LO 6000 vs MM 3000).

This is a methodological compensation, not an indication that the LO standard is lower. In practice, LO and MM should lead to consistent conclusions for the same sample.

Key Point: "Meets Specification" ≠ "High Quality"

The USP Table 2 limits are the "minimum acceptable standard", not a quality target. The original text states clearly that:

  • The actual particle load of many commercial products is an order of magnitude lower than the limits
  • Even when the specification is met, a trend that departs from normal values should be investigated
  • CDMOs and pharmaceutical companies should set their own (stricter) "internal target values" rather than merely comply with the USP limits

Analogy: The speed limit on a highway is 110 km/h, but in rain or dense fog, "complying with the regulation" does not mean "driving safely".

Analogy: The Calculation Logic of SVI vs LVI

Imagine two kinds of restaurants:

  • SVI (small volume): The total bacterial count in each meal must not exceed the standard. What matters is the safety of the whole serving.
  • LVI (large volume): The bacterial concentration per liter of food must not exceed the standard. Because an infusion bag is large, concentration is used to make comparison uniform.

8. Control of Particulate Matter

Original Text

It is the intent of 〈1〉 that in the design of formulations, selection of packages, and final assembly of packages, a stable final product is attained. Using the methods and limits in 〈788〉 and 〈789〉, high-quality commercial product cleanliness and stability are verified. This state can be described as "clean" (few particles) and "stable" (avoiding changes that promote particle growth). Process streams, equipment, filling lines, pumps, staging tanks, personnel gowning, packaging cleanliness, and environmental quality all may contribute particulate matter to the product.

8.1 Development

Due diligence using particle detection methods endorsed by USP or alternate method strategies for the best product design is recommended during product and process development and may be continued in selected evaluations during commercial production. The use of many methods early in development is an important consideration for limiting the number of particles in the product and for full understanding and characterization of the particle population.

To accomplish this, start early and conduct analyses often, using methods appropriate to the product development phase. Analysis using only lot release methods will give a limited understanding of the particle population. As the development cycle progresses, the use of orthogonal techniques provides greater confidence in particle assessment. It is important to explore the incidence of change in the number of particles during early phases of development to provide the optimal selection of formulation, manufacturing process, and packaging. Also, prior to commercialization, the character of inherent solids must be fully explored to establish the range of its acceptability.

Particles may form during manufacturing or over time due to any one or more of the following factors:

  • Formulation instability
  • Package cleanliness
  • Package selection (quality/stability)
  • Inorganic extracts
  • Organic extracts
  • Product-package interaction
  • Process contributions
  • Stresses throughout the process (e.g., pH swings, surface exposures)
  • Silicone content

The chosen target specifications unique to a product are an important extension of the USP limits in Table 2. In the implementation of a target-specification strategy, several tenets apply:

  • Understand and minimize inherent particle load when appropriate
  • Minimize intrinsic particle load
  • Identify and characterize particles during development, and establish a particle database
  • Correlate particle load to visible particle rejects
  • Track the particle population within product families and across historical batches to reveal anomalous trends and initiate remediation
  • Establish targets for particle content relevant to product family, including considerations of manufacturing arena, packaging, and formulation complexity

8.2 Statistical Sampling Plan

Chapter 〈788〉 provides the sampling plan according to product volume, while 〈787〉 emphasizes a statistically relevant sample plan independent of volume. For all products, regardless of volume, comprehensive experience regarding the integrity and consistency of the batch is gained throughout development by analyzing a number of samples throughout each batch.

The sampling plan should consider the statistical precision of the obtained counts, the likely heterogeneity within a vial or other vessel, the likely heterogeneity between vials or other vessels, and variations in particle concentration with time or other variables.

Conditions that promote heterogeneity in particle loading include:

  1. Insufficient or inconsistent container washing
  2. Particle load on the stoppers
  3. Variations in silicone coater performance
  4. Particles contributed by the filling equipment (particle load may vary from pump to pump)
  5. Particles contributed by the drying/sterilization system
  6. Failure of aseptic filling conditions
  7. Self-association of active ingredient or formulation components
  8. Particles generated over time, due to formulation instability or product-package interaction

Chapter 〈788〉 provides a sampling plan according to product volume. This plan is based solely upon the limitations of traditional optical counting instruments for adequate, multiple-sample aliquot draws. Originally, the sample volume for the LO method was set at 25 mL. Due to the current capability of MM to process any liquid aliquot regardless of volume, a wide range of sampling volumes can now be accommodated.

Unit variability is an important quality parameter 1) during development of the formulation; 2) after process changes; and 3) when changing package vendors. Evaluation of the non-pooled vials can give a better measure of inter-vial variability and also can eliminate the introduction of particles in the pooling process.

8.3 Risk-Based Approach

The USP limits in Table 2 mirror the historical approach of obtaining particle concentrations above given size thresholds and treating all particles as equivalently deleterious to product quality. However, the increased variety of particle types in modern pharmaceuticals, each with different risk factors, suggests that a better approach would be to identify the particle concentrations of the component subtypes of the total particle load and to then assess the risk to product quality posed by each particle type.

For example, the presence of extrinsic particles could be evidence of a breach in sterility, with a high product risk. Increases in intrinsic particle load may be associated with the production process and/or package-related sources, which can be indicative of systemic problems. Conversely, the presence of air bubbles is not a concern.

Risk for elevated final product particulate levels will be reduced when there is deep product knowledge and when systems are utilized to control the process stream in the reduction of particle load (type). Consideration should also be given to the following:

  • Package selection with regard to formulation (stability)
  • Package source and preparation quality (removal and addition)
  • Product-contact surfaces (particle load and extractable substances)
  • Filling arena cleanliness and practices (addition)
  • Formulation design (stability)

Successful risk analysis may require considerable particle characterization, beyond what can be achieved by LO, MM, and FI, and utilization of prior knowledge.

9. Silicone Droplets and Air Bubbles Versus Non-Silicone Particles

With the historical use of a single optical particle counting approach (LO), there was high reliability, reproducibility, and throughput for the quality control lab analysis of particle load. However, the technique does not provide differentiation between solid, liquid, and gaseous matter initiating the counting pulse. Both FI and MM can be useful adjuncts to LO in distinguishing air bubbles and silicone oil droplets from other particle types.

Certainly, the content of gas bubbles identified as counts by a particle counter may be considered an artifact of the formulation and/or sample preparation and may be alleviated or negated by sampling or preparation methodology or data manipulation. The counts by optical methods of immiscible liquids, which are largely composed of the ubiquitous silicone lubricants in pharmaceutical articles, must be considered. For some dosage forms such as cartridges, syringes, and some devices, lubrication (usually by silicone oil) is necessary.

There is a difference of opinion regarding the relative risk posed by silicone oil droplets. These opinions range from "no risk"—based on the droplet sterility and on the long history of medical use and patient exposure to silicone oil—to "potential concerns" about the increased immunogenicity of biologic drugs. Immunogenicity would arise from possible interactions between the droplets and the protein, resulting in an increase in protein aggregation or the silicone oil acting as an adjuvant.

Historically, MM was used as a "referee" method to resolve the occurrence of elevated LO particle counts by verifying whether the counts were due to solid particles rather than gases and liquids. With the addition of FI, it is now possible in many cases to begin to resolve and differentiate gases and liquids counted as particles from the semisolid to solid particle fraction.

Air and immiscible liquids do not affect the membrane count. Membrane surface artifacts and semisolid particles can, however, complicate this analysis, while many protein samples spread out on the surface such that they are not detectable. This procedure is also time consuming and difficult to reproduce, requiring extensive operator training.

10. Challenging Samples

Alternate methods may be necessary for dosage forms for which 〈788〉 cannot be directly applied. This includes some of the sample types described in 5. Applicability of Methods (e.g., process streams, vehicles, or components). Alternate methods and/or evaluation should be considered for dosage forms and process samples for which optical detection is not practical.

For example, some products have inherent properties that affect or preclude particle counting so 〈788〉 methods cannot be used as written. Direct evaluation of container–closure rinses and product vehicles may be used for these suspension systems by the 〈788〉 methods. When the product is not a solution or is viscous or will otherwise confound the 〈788〉 methods, it must be altered or accommodated to facilitate the assay. Examples of alternate methodologies are:

  • Particle determination of product after treatment with solvent to dissolve all product solids
  • Use of centrifugation to separate product solids from the vehicle
  • Use of selected screens or sieves to separate product solids from the product vehicle to minimize interference
  • Evaluation of the product filling system by using an alternate, clear fluid such as sterile media or saline

Use the methods that are best adapted to the particle type and volume of sample. Selected sampling requires careful statistical evaluation of the batch population when using small sample volumes to evaluate product acceptability; this applies to all methods. Regardless of the sample size, the number of samples tested should be determined statistically based on acceptable risk and development history.

11.1 Guidelines for the Use of Calibrator Spheres

11.1.1 General

Polystyrene spheres of stated diameter, or stated diameter and concentration, are commercially available for calibrating particle counting instruments. All such standards are only valid if the spheres are predominantly unaggregated. When measuring particle mean diameter, particle concentration, or instrument resolution, the user should inspect the particle size distribution to confirm the absence of microsphere aggregates. Aberrant calibration results may be due to instrument or method problems or calibrant quality. If there is evidence of aggregation, the bottle of calibrator spheres used should be discarded. Any results obtained from a vial that has demonstrated aggregation of spheres should be disregarded, as long as a satisfactory result with a new source of spheres is obtained. If the instrument is running properly but the calibration results deviate from the manufacturer's specifications, and the calibrator spheres show no indication of aggregation, the manufacturer of the instrument should be consulted.

11.1.2 Specific Standards

Chapter 〈788〉 specifies the use of mono-sized dispersions of traceable spherical particles with known diameters that include the 10- and 25-μm required sizes for particulate-matter testing. This must also include an expanded range of sizes to cover the calibrated range between 2 and 100 µm. The calibration curve should include additional sizes such as 2, 5, 10, 15, 25, and 50 µm, especially when particle load data are expected outside the traditional 〈788〉 thresholds. All particles are suspended in particle-free water (do not use Water for Injection), with small amounts of surfactant and preservative allowed.

Each calibrator sphere size should have a mean diameter within 5% of the nominal diameter and should be traceable to National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) standard reference materials or certified reference materials from other national metrology institutes. The concentration of calibrator microspheres may be certified by the manufacturer or by the user via MM or other methods. For an MM determination of particle concentration, pipet a volume of the suspension containing 250–500 particles into a filter funnel. It is very important to calibrate for both size and number of particles, as the accuracy of one can influence the other.

11.2 Elements of Measurement Quality Control

Quality control of measurements is ensured by a hierarchy of activities. See 11.3 Validation Considerations for All Techniques. This topic can be generally summarized as the following:

  • Method validation ensures that the chosen method will reliably measure the quantity of interest at known levels of accuracy, precision, and specificity (see 〈1225〉)
  • Instrument qualification verifies that a particular instrument is fit for purpose (see Analytical Instrument Qualification 〈1058〉)
  • Instrument standardization tests (ISTs) are the historical parameters of control for LO and are described in this chapter
  • System suitability tests (SSTs) are routine and usually single-pass measurements performed to demonstrate a high degree of confidence that the instrument is performing correctly in use. The SST contained in this chapter for LO is a series of tests that demonstrates that each parameter complies with the manufacturer requirements.

Elements of method validation will be discussed for each technique; however, each method should include an assessment of the following:

  • Recovery
  • Accuracy
  • Precision
  • Specificity
  • Lower detection limit
  • Upper detection limit
  • Sampling volume
  • Linearity/range

For all the methods, there are general laboratory and sample considerations. First, remember that the main objective is to determine the level of unwanted particulate matter in a final parenteral product. The handling of the article during setup, selection, preparation, and testing should not significantly affect the results. As described in 〈788〉, determine the particulate matter in 5 samples of filtered water, each with a volume of 5 mL (or a blank volume that is equivalent to the sample volume for your specific method). For the 5-mL blank volume, if the number of particles that are ≥10 µm in size exceeds 25 for the combined 25 mL (NMT 1/mL), then this indicates that the precautions taken for the tests were not sufficient.

11.3 Validation Considerations for All Techniques

Although the 〈788〉 methods are deemed robust for most product forms, deviation from clear, noncolored, and waterlike formulations may need further study. At the very least, an alternative particle counting strategy and a recovery study with known particle size standards will demonstrate the performance of the method for a specific medium.

Chapter 〈1225〉 and ICH Q2(R1) provide comprehensive guidance for validation of analytical procedures. The aspects of assay validation in these guidelines are as follows:

  • Chapter 〈1225〉: Accuracy, Precision, Specificity, Detection limit, Quantitation limit, Linearity, Range, Robustness
  • ICH Q2(R1): 1. Specificity, 2. Linearity, 3. Range, 4. Accuracy, 5. Precision, 6. Detection limit, 7. Quantitation limit, 8. Robustness, 9. System Suitability Testing
  • IST for LO: 〈1788.1〉, 3. Instrument Standardization Tests — Sample Volume Accuracy, Sample Flow Rate, Diameter Calibration, Sensor Size Resolution, and Qualification Considerations

11.3.1 Specificity

As discussed previously, LO instruments will count air bubbles, immiscible liquid droplets, and semisolid and solid particles. Thus, LO has low specificity, and the specificity for detecting the particles of interest should be qualified by other methods. MM is insensitive to air bubbles and liquid droplets. FI will capture images of all bubbles, droplets, and particles. The specificity of FI can be assessed by measurement of mixtures of known particle concentrations followed by analysis with the algorithm that will also be used for the specific product.

11.3.2 Recovery

The use of microspheres of lower refractive index than polystyrene (such as polymethylmethacrylate or silica) or other particles of low refractive index may be appropriate to model particles of low refractive index difference for both LO and FI. In all cases, the ability of the method to accurately report the number of particles within the assigned sizing bins should be determined.

11.3.3 Linearity and Range

Linearity and range can be assessed by the measurement of microsphere mixtures at different dilutions followed by comparison of the measurement result with the particle concentration expected from the known dilution factor. All three methods are subject to coincidence errors in which two particles appear as a single particle. LO method has typical coincidence errors of 10% at particle concentrations ranging from several thousand to a hundred thousand particles per milliliter, depending on the sensor choice. Typical FI instruments have much lower coincidence errors than MM or LO. The range extends from the level of background counts observed to the highest concentration with acceptable coincidence error.

11.3.4 Accuracy

For particle counters, there are two types of accuracy that must be assessed: accuracy of reported diameter and accuracy of reported particle count. Both may be assessed at a basic instrumental level by measuring microsphere suspensions of known diameter and/or concentration. However, LO, FI, and MM will all report different diameters, even if the instruments have been properly calibrated to give accurate diameters with microspheres, because of the different dependencies of the instruments on particle morphology and refractive index.

While there is no consensus method for adjusting the reported diameter to a corrected diameter, there are some methods that can be helpful. Silica and polymethylmethacrylate microspheres have lower refractive index than commonly used polystyrene microspheres. Currently, there are two fundamental limitations on correcting errors of reported diameter: First, the refractive index of certain common particles such as protein aggregates is not yet established. Second, there is no accepted method for correcting diameter errors for heterogeneous mixtures of particles.

11.3.5 Precision

Precision may be assessed by considering the repeatability of multiple assays. Assays performed with microspheres or microsphere mixtures may be useful for assessing the fundamental precision limit of a method. Assays performed with samples representative of actual particles will provide a more accurate assessment of the precision that is possible in regular use.

11.3.6 Detection Limit

The detection limit is usually defined as the particle concentration corresponding to a 3:1 signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio. Particle counters may be theoretically capable of counting single particles without ambiguity, but noise is introduced in the form of background counts seen with blank or filtered buffer samples. The detection limit will vary with the size of the particle.

11.3.7 Robustness

Assay results for particle counts can be sensitive to sample preparation, sample variability, and instrument cleanliness for all methods. MM is especially dependent on operator judgment.

12. Test Environment and Preparation

Original Text

The nature of the sample preparation and the analytical method used can substantially alter the environmental requirements. Especially for MM, the pooling of aliquots, and other procedures requiring sample manipulation or samples exposed to the environment, will require more attention to cleanliness than procedures that only require drawing samples out of a vial as for LO. In all cases, perform the test in an environment that minimizes background particulate matter. Glassware must be cleaned to the extent that the level of particles added by testing has a negligible effect on the outcome of the test.

12.1 Sample Handling

Best practices for minimizing particle contamination from packaging or the testing environment should be followed at every step. Remove tertiary and secondary packaging outside of the test area and ensure that the exterior of the product containers has been cleaned. Rinse the exterior of containers with filtered water after removing all retention hardware (e.g., ferrules, clips, bands) and as directed in 12.2 Sampling Practices for each technique. Protect the containers from environmental contamination until analyzed.

12.1.1 Liquid Preparations

Prepare the container(s) as directed in 〈788〉, Method 1 Light Obscuration Particle Count Test, either pooling units to get the 25-mL test aliquot when using 〈788〉 or testing individual units if using 〈787〉. When using 〈788〉 for containers of 25 mL or more, simply open the container, insert the counter probe in the middle of the solution and measure. For all liquid samples, gently mix the samples by hand swirling, gentle inversion, or mechanical means. Care should be taken to avoid introduction of air bubbles or contaminants and to avoid disruption of particles in all cases.

12.1.2 Dry or Lyophilized Preparations

Prepare the containers as directed under 〈788〉, Method 1 Light Obscuration Particle Count Test. Open each container, taking care not to contaminate the opening or cover. Reconstitute as directed by the product insert instructions, allowing time for the sample to stand if so directed. Replace the closure and manually agitate the container sufficiently to ensure dissolution of the drug, being careful not to agitate too vigorously and cause particle formation. For some dry or lyophilized products, it may be necessary to let the containers stand for a suitable interval and then agitate again to achieve complete dissolution.

12.1.3 Products Packaged with Dual Compartments

Prepare the units to be tested as directed under 〈788〉, Method 1 Light Obscuration Particle Count Test and according to product insert directions. Mix each unit as directed in the labeling, activating and agitating it to ensure thorough mixing of the separate components and to ensure drug dissolution. Prepare as described in 〈1788.1〉, 6. Blank and Sample Handling, and proceed as appropriate.

12.1.4 Products Labeled as "Pharmacy Bulk Package Not for Direct Infusion"

Proceed as directed for Liquid Preparations where the volume is 25 mL or more. Calculate the test result on a portion that is equivalent to the maximum dose given in the labeling. For example, if the total bulk package volume is 100 mL and the maximum dose volume is 10 mL, then the average LO particle count per milliliter would be multiplied by 10 to obtain the test result based on the 10-mL maximum dose. [Note—For the calculations of test results, consider this maximum dose portion to be the equivalent of the contents of one full container.]

12.2 Sampling Practices

After proper mixing, open the container(s) and pour or withdraw by syringe the contents under test to transfer in a manner least likely to generate particles. The number of product samples is determined by the fill category (small volume or large volume) or the intent of the determination.

  • Direct sampling—Contents of containers with removable stoppers may be poured out directly after removing the closures for MM. Sampling devices that have a needle to penetrate the unit closure may also be used for all three methods.
  • Pooling—If there is insufficient volume for the test method, the contents of multiple containers may be pooled from freshly opened product into clean glassware (cleanliness verified by testing).
  • Reconstitution—Dry or lyophilized products must be constituted using either their insert diluent or particle-free water. The closure is removed to add the fluid, which is injected via hypodermic syringe.
  • Dilution—Some products will require dilution for LO or FI analysis due to insufficient volume or due to suboptimal fluid properties, such as color or viscosity. The diluent must be particle-free water (or filtered product vehicle) and dilution vessels must be scrupulously cleaned and verified. The impact of dilution must be evaluated for acceptability through feasibility studies.

12.2.1 Mixing

For reference standards, homogenization of the mixture may be attained by agitating the preparation for 20 s and sonicating for up to 30 s. Gently mix the contents of the sample just prior to analysis by hand-swirling, tipping of the vial, or mechanical means. Take care not to introduce air bubbles, or if open, contamination. Mixing of samples is necessary to resuspend settled particles; however, mixing must be done carefully, because agitation may alter fragile or shear-labile particles. Swirling is gentler than tipping the vial but may not resuspend all particles.

12.2.2 Degassing

The handling of microspheres and some blanks is different from the handling of some test samples. Microspheres will not degrade under sonication; however, sonication will strongly affect some particle types and is never acceptable for protein-containing solutions. Use a consistent degassing procedure appropriate for the samples.

Recommended degassing procedures include:

  1. Sonication at 20–80 watts for about 30 s
  2. Standing and equilibrating at room temperature for 10 min
  3. Applying a mild vacuum, e.g., 75 torr, for 10 min

There are caveats to sonication and standing. With sonication, much energy is dissipated as heat, resulting in denaturation of the biologics; this could also affect certain particles in the sample. Allowing the sample to stand to dissipate gas is effective, but stratification and settling of particulate matter occurs during standing. For this reason, use of the standing approach must be accompanied by sufficient gentle agitation to bring all particles into the sampling zone. Sometimes, allowing the sample to stand is not sufficient to remove microbubbles (e.g., if the solution is viscous or if the air bubbles associate with the particles). Gentle vacuum can often remove these entrained bubbles without disrupting the inherent particle population.

12.2.3 Blank Samples

For the 〈788〉 method, an environment blank verifies system cleanliness. To collect blank counts, use a cleaned vessel of the type and volume to be used in the test. Place a defined volume of filtered water consistent with the volume of sample to be counted in the vessel and agitate the water sample in the cleaned glassware by inversion or swirling. Using the same method as for product samples is recommended.

Best practice includes running additional blank samples, especially between product families. Measurement of additional blanks consisting of filtered formulation buffer may be useful for assessing the introduction of air bubbles or desorption of particles from wetted surfaces in surfactant-containing formulations.

Core Concept: The Golden Rules of Sample Handling

The greatest risk in particle analysis is that "the testing process itself introduces particles". Every step of sample handling can be a point of contamination:

  • Unclean glassware → contributes extrinsic particles
  • Excessive agitation → false counts from air bubbles, or shear-induced particle formation
  • Environmental exposure when the container is opened for sampling → dust contamination
  • Foam from pipetting → false-positive counts

Therefore, a test environment (usually a HEPA-protected area) and strict adherence to operating procedures (SOPs) are prerequisites for reliable results.

Analogy: The Challenge of Reconstituting Lyophilized Products

Reconstituting a lyophilized drug product is like making instant coffee: you need to dissolve it fully (no undissolved powder) but not stir so hard that you create foam. For protein drug products, overly vigorous shaking can also cause proteins to unfold and aggregate at the water-air interface, so use gentle swirling rather than vigorous shaking.

Practical Application: The Calculation Logic for a Pharmacy Bulk Package

Particle testing of a pharmacy bulk package (a 100 mL bottle with a maximum single dose of 10 mL):

  • Test the whole bottle by LO to obtain an average count of C particles/mL
  • Calculate on the basis of the maximum dose volume: reported value = C × 10 mL
  • Compare against the SVI (≤100 mL) limit: ≤6000 (LO ≥10 µm)

This logic reflects risk-assessment thinking: the total amount of particles to which the patient is exposed in a single dose is the real safety risk indicator.

Formula Concept: Correcting Test Results for Dilution

If the sample must be diluted because of high viscosity (dilution factor DF):

Reported count = measured count × DF

Note: Before diluting, verify that:
  1. The diluent does not dissolve the particles being tested
  2. The particle background of the diluent itself is low enough
  3. The dilution ratio is within the linear range of the method
  4. The dilution operation does not introduce additional particles

Key Point: The Selection Logic for the Three Degassing Methods

Ultrasonication is strictly prohibited for protein drug products, because the heat and shear forces it generates can denature the protein and form aggregates, creating the very problem you are trying to detect.

Section Summary

  • Measurement quality control has four tiers: method validation → instrument qualification → IST (periodic) → SST (every test); each has a different purpose and frequency.
  • Calibrator spheres must be NIST-traceable, with mean diameter deviation ≤5%, and suspended in particle-free water (not WFI); aggregated spheres must be discarded.
  • LO has low specificity (it counts air bubbles and droplets), so the nature of the counts must be confirmed by MM or FI before formal use.
  • The degassing method must be chosen according to the sample properties: ultrasonication is strictly prohibited for protein formulations; after standing, the sample must be gently remixed to overcome particle settling.
  • Reconstitution of lyophilized products must be gentle to avoid protein aggregation caused by excessive agitation; dual-chamber packages must be fully activated according to the label.
  • Operations such as dilution and pooled sampling require prior feasibility validation to confirm that they do not introduce or dissolve the particles being tested.

13. Instrument Standardization Tests

Original Text

ISTs are used to verify that the instrument performs in accordance with the manufacturer's specifications upon calibration and continues to provide accurate and consistent results between calibrations. ISTs, as described in this section, are equivalent to the performance qualification (PQ) in 〈1058〉 and are intended to verify the acceptable performance of the instrument for its intended use. See 〈1058〉 for an in-depth description of PQ, operational qualification (OQ), and system suitability. These tests are also applicable for MM but in a much-simplified approach (see Membrane Microscope Method for the Determination of Subvisible Particulate Matter 〈1788.2〉).

ISTs for LO and FI include several tiers. Instrument calibration is the most comprehensive set of tests, and PQ tests provide assurance that the instrument meets all key performance requirements. Other verification tests provide daily assurance that the instrument performance is acceptable.

13.1 Diameter Calibration

Perform instrument calibration according to the instrument manufacturer's instructions. The count accuracy of an instrument can be significantly affected by the size accuracy of the instrumental particle counter. Count accuracy is also dependent upon the sensor used and may vary with individual sensors of the same model, as described previously.

Perform a system suitability test upon completion of the instrument calibration to ensure that the particle sizes of interest for the application (i.e., 10 and 25 µm) and other sizes (e.g., 5 and 15 µm), are accurately reported and that the count accuracy is verified. Count accuracy should be performed during or after the diameter accuracy is confirmed. Particle count accuracy may be assessed using commercial microsphere count standards. For LO and FI, corrections are incorporated into instrument software to give accurate particle diameters of the calibration microspheres.

13.2 System Suitability Tests

The key attributes of a particle counting system are that it counts particles accurately and that the level of background counts is acceptably low. Count accuracy may be assessed by routine measurement of microsphere count standards. Background counts may be assessed by routine measurement of blanks or filtered formulation. These two types of tests are the core system suitability tests.

A few factors influence how often a system suitability test should be run:

  • Do the test samples tend to clog flow cells in the LO and FI methods?
  • Do the particles in the test sample tend to adhere to wetted surfaces, thereby causing increased counts in subsequent blanks or test sample runs?
  • What are the consequences if assay failure is detected at a date later than when the assay was run?

13.2.1 System Suitability Failure

Instruments that fail system suitability tests can often be remediated by thorough cleaning or by evaluating the particle standard used (these can aggregate over time). If failure of the standard is ruled out, the manufacturer's directions should be followed for thoroughly cleaning all fluid lines and the sensor head. Back-flushing to remove partial clogs may also be beneficial. The instrument is deemed to be suitable for use if it passes the system suitability tests after cleaning. Flush the system to remove traces of the particle standard prior to routine use.

Depending upon the dosage form being tested, proceed as directed under the appropriate category in 14. Calculations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: IST vs SST

IST (instrument standardization test): Equivalent to PQ, a comprehensive performance confirmation that includes:

  • Sample volume accuracy
  • Sample flow rate confirmation
  • Diameter calibration
  • Sensor size resolution
  • Qualification considerations

Usually performed after installation of a new instrument, major repair or recalibration, and at a lower frequency (monthly/quarterly).

SST (system suitability test): A routine confirmation before/after each test, built around two elements: "counting accuracy" and "background count level".

Key Point: Factors That Determine SST Frequency

USP does not specify a fixed SST frequency; it requires the frequency to be determined by risk assessment. The key questions are:

  1. Risk of flow cell blockage: High-viscosity or high-particle samples → test before and after every sample.
  2. Risk of particle adhesion and carry-over: Samples that adhere easily, such as proteins → add blank tests between samples.
  3. Consequences of late detection of failure: If instrument failure is discovered only later, the affected batches may already have been released → higher-risk products need more frequent SST.

Analogy: The Order of Troubleshooting an SST Failure

It is like a thermometer giving an odd reading: first suspect the thermometer itself (use a fresh calibrator sphere standard), then the measurement environment (clean the instrument), and finally contact the manufacturer. Start from the simplest and most likely cause rather than immediately declaring the instrument faulty.

The troubleshooting order given by USP: check whether the calibrator sphere standard has aggregated → clean the flow path and sensor head → back-flush to remove local blockages → contact the manufacturer.

14. Calculations

Original Text

14.1 Pooled Samples

Average the counts from the aliquot portions analyzed. Calculate the number of particles in each container using the following formula:

PΣ≥10 × VT / VA / n   (for ≥10 µm threshold)
PΣ≥25 × VT / VA / n   (for ≥25 µm threshold)
PΣ≥50 × VT / VA / n   (additionally for 〈789〉)

where P is the average particle count per threshold obtained from all portions analyzed; VT is the volume (mL) of pooled sample; VA is the volume (mL) of each portion analyzed; and n is the number of containers pooled.

14.2 Individual Samples

Average the counts obtained for the aliquot portions from each separate unit analyzed and calculate the number of particles in each container:

PΣ≥10 × V / VA   (for ≥10 µm threshold)
PΣ≥25 × V / VA   (for ≥25 µm threshold)
PΣ≥50 × V / VA   (for 〈789〉)

where P is the average particle count per threshold from all portions analyzed; V is the volume (mL) of the tested unit; VA is the volume (mL) of each portion analyzed. For individual unit samples measured directly, the results are reported as P per unit volume (counts per milliliter).

14.3 Individual Unit Samples

Average the counts obtained for the aliquot portions taken from the solution unit. Calculate the number of particles in each milliliter of product solution taken:

PΣ≥10 / VA   (for ≥10 µm threshold)
PΣ≥25 / VA   (for ≥25 µm threshold)
PΣ≥50 / VA   (for 〈789〉)

where P is the average particle count per threshold from all portions analyzed; VA is the volume (mL) of the portion taken.

For all types of product, if the tested material has been diluted to decrease the viscosity, the dilution factor must be accounted for in the calculation of the final test result.

[Note—For all methods, when reporting particle load the particle counts must be reported in cumulative mode. If the instrument has been configured to count in differential bins (e.g., ≥10–24 µm, >25–50 µm, >50 µm), all bins ≥10 µm must be added to yield a total ≥10-µm count and all bins ≥25 µm need to be added to yield a total 25-µm count. Reports should clearly state the diameter limits, the units of particle counts (e.g., counts per container or counts per milliliter), and that the particle loads are for all particles greater than or equal to the stated diameter limits.]

Tutorial Commentary

Formula Analysis: Which Calculation Mode Applies to Which Situation

14.1 Pooled samples:
  Applies to: several small-volume containers pooled and then tested
  Result expressed as: particles per container
  Calculation: mean count × total volume / volume per sampling / number of containers

14.2 Individual samples:
  Applies to: each container tested separately (corresponds to the <787> strategy)
  Result expressed as: particles per container
  Calculation: mean count × container volume / volume per sampling

14.3 Individual unit samples:
  Applies to: direct measurement of the container contents, with the result expressed as a concentration
  Result expressed as: particles per milliliter (counts/mL)
  Calculation: mean count / volume per sampling

Note: If any dilution is used in any of the methods → multiply by the dilution factor

Core Concept: Cumulative Count vs Differential Count

Cumulative mode: The count at ≥10 µm = the sum of all particles of size ≥10 µm.

Differential mode: The instrument may be set to count in bins such as 10–24 µm, 25–49 µm and ≥50 µm.

USP requires that results be reported in cumulative mode. If the instrument records in differential mode, the bins must be added together in the calculation:

  • Reported value at ≥10 µm = (10–24 µm) + (25–49 µm) + (≥50 µm)
  • Reported value at ≥25 µm = (25–49 µm) + (≥50 µm)

Key Point: The Strategic Choice Between Individual and Pooled Sampling

<788> has traditionally used pooled sampling, whereas <787> recommends individual sampling:

  • Advantages of pooled sampling: Suitable for small-volume containers, simple statistics and sufficient sample volume.
  • Advantages of individual sampling: Allows evaluation of within-batch heterogeneity (inter-vial variability) and avoids particles introduced by the pooling operation.

The modern trend (especially for protein drug products) is to use individual sampling wherever possible, to obtain a truer picture of batch quality.

Practical Application: Format Requirements for Reporting Calculations

USP explicitly requires that the report include:

  • The counting diameter thresholds (e.g., ≥10 µm, ≥25 µm)
  • The counting unit (per container or per milliliter)
  • A statement that the count is cumulative (all particles ≥ stated diameter)

In CDMO practice, the CoA (certificate of analysis) should state all of the above clearly, to avoid customer misunderstanding of the result units (per container vs per mL), especially in product lines that mix SVI and LVI.

Diameter bias:
Deviation of a derived particle diameter from the actual diameter due to technique parameters and limitations.
Diascopic illumination:
Transmitted light illumination for transparent to translucent samples.
Episcopic illumination:
An internal illumination path coincident with the optical path, providing top-reflected light for the detection of thin, flat particles and films.
Equivalent circular diameter:
The diameter of a standard sphere that produces an equivalent electronic pulse or other signal to an analyte particle.
Feret's diameter:
The dimension of an object along a specified direction, also known as caliper diameter.
Flow imaging:
An instrumental system configured to collect digital images of solids in sample fluid flowing between a light source and a camera.
Fouling:
Plugging or coating of a particle counting system sensor or flow cell; may be a full blockage, partial blockage, or an extraneous substance affecting the analysis.
Image analysis, dynamic:
A software program used to collect images from a flowing display, store the images, and retrospectively analyze the collected data.
Image analysis, static:
A software application used to collect images and discriminate the images from a live, unchanging image.
Light obscuration:
An optical particle-sensing technique that records impulses on a photodetector from obscuration of a source illumination by analyte particles. Particle size correlates with the response curve of a known spherical standard.
Longest chord:
The longest dimension that can be measured across the perimeter of an object.
Membrane microscopy:
A particle determination method that isolates solids from product samples by filtration and then examines them with a light microscope to count the collected material in size thresholds.
Operational qualification (OQ) / Performance qualification (PQ):
Essential elements of method validation: OQ specifies the principal operational elements of the device in both hardware and method controls; PQ verifies the acceptable performance characteristics for a specified instrument OQ.
Optical particle counter:
An instrument that provides detection, sizing, and enumeration of substances in a fluid medium using visible spectrum illumination and an electronic detector.
Particle concentration:
Number of particles (count) per unit volume.
Particle count accuracy:
An evaluation of the capability of an instrumental system to collect particle counts for evaluation against a standard.
Particle size:
Visible: In the context of pharmaceutical products, the size at which one can visualize a single particle within a final product package. USP has set a conservative threshold at 100 µm. Subvisible: 2–100 µm. Nanoparticles: <2 µm.
Refractive index (RI):
The numerical ratio of the speed of light through a vacuum over the speed of light through a substance. The RI of water is 1.33, with fluid oils ranging to 1.50 and solids spanning a wide range upwards from 1.34. RI is wavelength specific.
Relative standard deviation / Coefficient of variation:
A measure of dispersion of a probability distribution. It is the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean and is expressed as percentage.
Schlieren effects:
These are regions of varying refraction in a transparent medium that are often caused by differences in temperature or pressure and are detectable by optical means, generally by the passage of a beam of light through the medium.
Sheath flow:
A technique using a "wall" of higher-viscosity fluid encasing a lower-viscosity analyte sample flowing through it, thereby producing a limited focal plane due to hydrodynamic channels.
System suitability:
A measurement of the system output with a known standard that demonstrates acceptable performance of the system, often the result of several operational parameters.
Validation:
Validation of an analytical procedure is the process by which it is established, by laboratory studies, that the performance characteristics of the procedure meet the requirements for the intended analytical applications. This typically includes measurement of method accuracy, precision, specificity, detection limit, linearity, range, and robustness. Described in 〈1225〉.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Equivalent Circular Diameter vs Feret's Diameter

Different measurement methods report different "sizes", and understanding their definitions is essential to interpreting results:

  • Equivalent circular diameter: Used by LO and FI. It is the diameter of a standard sphere that produces the same signal intensity. It has a systematic bias for non-spherical particles.
  • Feret's diameter: Used by MM. It is the maximum linear dimension of the particle measured from any direction, and is closer to the "actual" size of the particle.

Therefore, the "size" given by LO and MM for the same fibrous particle may differ greatly. This is not an error but a difference in how each method defines size.

Key Point: The Practical Impact of the Schlieren Effect

Schlieren effect: Temperature or composition gradients cause local differences in refractive index, so that the LO instrument sees "false particles" (actually optical interference).

Common triggering conditions:

  • Samples just thawed after freezing (temperature gradient)
  • High-concentration protein solutions (concentration gradient)
  • Bicarbonate buffers (air bubbles create local refractive index differences)

Remedy: Allow the sample to equilibrate fully to room temperature and mix thoroughly before testing.

Analogy: The Effect of Refractive Index on Detection

Imagine taking a photograph beneath the surface of a swimming pool: transparent jelly (refractive index close to water) is almost invisible, but an opaque stone (large refractive index difference) is clearly distinguishable. LO detection works on a similar principle: the greater the refractive index difference between the particle and the solution, the stronger the signal and the more accurate the detection. The refractive index of protein aggregates (~1.35) is very close to that of water (1.33), so LO is inherently less sensitive to them.

Appendix 1: Historical Development of USP Chapters on Subvisible Particulate Matter

The determination of subvisible particulate matter in injections was initiated in 1975 as an MM method for large-volume injections (LVI) in USP XV. This one method was quite effective at revealing the content of unwanted solid particles ≥10 µm in the product, because filtration could accommodate a wide range of product volumes and the microscopist could evaluate the retained solids. However, the microscope method was much slower per sample than corollary instrumental techniques and required high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) environmental hoods for isolation operations, with special microscope count training for the analysts.

Higher throughput with a robust electronic method was sought. Historically, the British Pharmacopoeia had utilized electrical sensing zone (Coulter counter) counting for all particles ≥2 µm; however, the method required dispersion of the product in an electrolyte solution, which did not work for certain products and formulations.

The current USP subvisible particle determination methods were established in 1986 for determination of particulate matter in sterile injections, and in 1995, an improved version of this methodology was adopted following extensive development by an expert volunteer group. The MM method was improved, and the combination of LO and MM was proposed for use as a two-stage test for both LVI and small-volume injection (SVI) products. In 〈788〉 and 〈789〉, "small-volume parenteral" is synonymous with SVI, and "large-volume parenteral" is synonymous with LVI.

Appendix 2: Cleaning Procedures to Limit Particle Contamination

Cleanse glassware, closures, and other required equipment, preferably by immersing and cleaning the items using warm, nonionic detergent solution. Rinse in flowing tap water, and then rinse again in flowing filtered water. Organic solvents may also be used to facilitate cleaning. [Note—These steps describe one way to clean equipment; alternatively, particle-free equipment may be obtained from a suitable vendor.] Preferably, the test specimen, glassware, closures, and other required equipment are then given a final rinse with filtered water using a hand-held pressure nozzle with final filter or other appropriate filtered water source within an environment protected by HEPA cabinets. While conducting the assay, non-shedding garments and powder-free gloves are worn within the HEPA environment.

For example, prepare the test specimens in the following sequence. Outside of the unidirectional airflow cabinet (UAFC) or HEPA filters to be used for the test, remove outer hardware or sealing bands. Rinse the exteriors of the containers with filtered water. Protect the containers from environmental contamination until analyzed.

Within the UAFC, open the containers and withdraw, pour, or otherwise sample the contents of the containers for testing in a manner least likely to generate particles that could enter the test specimen. Contents of containers with removable stoppers may be withdrawn directly by removing the closures. A sampling device with a needle to penetrate the unit closure may also be used. Products packaged in flexible plastic containers may be sampled by cutting the medication or administration port tube or a corner from the unit with a suitably cleaned razor blade or scissors.

Core Concept: The Historical Context of the USP Particle Chapters

Understanding the history helps explain why the current standards are designed as they are:

  • 1975: Only the MM method existed, aimed at LVI, because large-volume infusion packs were the main products at the time.
  • 1985: The LO method was introduced, an automation revolution that greatly improved QC efficiency.
  • 1986: LO+MM officially became the USP standard.
  • 1995: The current <788> framework was established, with the two-stage LO+MM test applying to both LVI and SVI.
  • 2024 (current version of <1788>): FI was formally incorporated into the informational chapter.

Practical Application: Key Points for a Glassware Cleaning SOP

The cleaning process in Appendix 2 is essential for establishing laboratory SOPs. The key steps are:

  1. Pre-cleaning: Soak and wash in warm water with a non-ionic detergent.
  2. Rinse 1: Running tap water.
  3. Rinse 2: Running filtered water.
  4. Final rinse: In a HEPA-protected environment, with a pressure nozzle and terminally filtered water.
  5. Personal protection: Non-shedding cleanroom garments and powder-free gloves.

Cleaning effectiveness is verified by the blank test, which closes the loop in the quality system.

Analogy: The Methodological Lesson from History

LO replaced MM as the preferred method in 1985, like a bank switching from counting banknotes by hand to using a counting machine: it is faster and more consistent, but the machine's own errors (for example, counting a foreign banknote as domestic currency) need to be verified periodically by manual spot checks. This is why MM is still retained as the "referee method": when LO results are abnormal, MM is used to confirm the truth.

USP <1788> Complete Chapter Summary

  • Method system: LO (preferred, automated), MM (referee method, retains particles) and FI (morphological information, orthogonal method) each have advantages and limitations; using them orthogonally is best practice.
  • Limits: <788> Table 2 is the minimum compliance standard; SVI are counted per container and LVI per mL; all counts must be reported in cumulative mode.
  • Calculation points: The three calculation modes (pooled/individual/individual unit) correspond to different sampling strategies; after dilution, the result must be multiplied by the dilution factor.
  • Measurement quality: IST (PQ equivalent) → SST (confirmation every time) → blank test (environment and operation confirmation): three layers that assure the reliability of results.
  • Terminology precision: Equivalent circular diameter vs Feret's diameter, the effect of refractive index on detection sensitivity, and recognition of the Schlieren effect: these concepts directly affect whether results are interpreted correctly.
  • Historical context: From MM in 1975 to LO in 1985 to FI in 2024, each evolution was a meeting point of technical capability and clinical need.
  • CDMO practice: Build an internal particle library, set internal target values stricter than USP, and introduce multiple analytical methods early in development. This is a necessary investment for high-quality manufacturing.
