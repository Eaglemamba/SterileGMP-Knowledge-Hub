Purpose & Scope

Original Text

PURPOSE

This general information chapter provides information about factors that affect the durability of the inner surface of glass containers. Recommended approaches are provided to evaluate the potential of a drug product to cause formation of glass particles and delamination of the inner surface. Screening methods are provided to detect glass particles and delamination, allowing a comparison to be made of glass durability on a lot-to-lot basis or between different glass manufacturers.

SCOPE

This chapter addresses bottles and vials manufactured by molding and ampuls, cartridges, vials, and prefillable syringes manufactured from tubing glass. Glass for pharmaceutical packaging is classified as Type I borosilicate glass, Type II treated soda-lime-silica glass, or Type III soda-lime-silica glass on the basis of the hydrolytic resistance of the glass, as defined in Containers—Glass 〈660〉.

Type I glass containers are suitable for most products for parenteral and nonparenteral use. Type II glass containers are suitable for most acidic and neutral aqueous products for parenteral and nonparenteral uses, and can be used for alkaline parenteral products when stability data demonstrate their suitability. Type III glass containers usually are not used for parenteral products or for powders for parenteral use, except when suitable stability test data indicate that Type III glass is satisfactory.

This chapter focuses primarily on Type I glass, because it is the most widely used in the pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical industry for parenteral products although the guidance can be equally applied to Type II and Type III glass used for parenteral products.

The chapter should be useful for the following:

  • Molded and tubular glass container manufacturers and converters
  • Pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical companies
  • Contract manufacturing and filling organizations
"Glass delamination may be described as the appearance of thin flexible flakes of glass (or lamellae) that can range in size from <50 µm to 200 µm in a drug product solution. This is a serious quality issue and can result in a product recall."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

What is glass delamination?

Delamination is the end-stage manifestation of glass surface corrosion. When the inner glass surface stays in contact with the drug product for a long time, the glass structure separates into layers and eventually sheds paper-thin glass flakes (lamellae). These flakes are typically <50 µm to 200 µm in size and fall within the range of subvisible particles.

Why is this a serious problem? Once glass flakes enter an injectable, they can cause: (1) vascular embolism in patients after intravenous injection; (2) non-compliance with particulate limits (USP <788>/<789>); and (3) product recalls, with serious consequences for the company's reputation and finances. There are a number of well-known historical cases of injectables recalled because of glass delamination.

Principles for selecting among the three glass types:

  • Type I (borosilicate): highest chemical resistance → first choice for injectables
  • Type II (soda-lime-silica treated with ammonium sulfate): acceptable for acidic/neutral injectables
  • Type III (soda-lime-silica): generally not used for injectables

Key Notes

Warning about the "lagging indicator": USP states explicitly that glass delamination (visible flaking of lamellae) is a "lagging indicator of strong glass-drug interactions". By the time you see delamination, the problem started a year ago or even earlier. This means that once delamination is found, it can no longer be prevented, only recalled. The key to prevention is early screening: identify the risk during product development.

Implication for CDMOs: when accepting a product whose formulation contains organic acid salts, high ionic strength or high pH, you must proactively assess the delamination risk at the early development stage, rather than wait until problems appear in stability studies.

Analogy

Glass delamination is like a rusting iron pipe. Rust does not appear overnight; it is the result of water molecules reacting with the iron for months or even years. By the time the pipe starts to rust and flake (delaminate), the internal corrosion is already severe. Prevention is to choose a stainless steel pipe (select glass with better chemical durability) or to run a compatibility test before installation (drug-glass compatibility screening).

Glass Types

Original Text

Glass in its pure form consists of silicon dioxide with a melting point in excess of 1700°. However, this is rarely used commercially because of the cost of working at these elevated temperatures. Added network modifiers, such as sodium, potassium, or boron oxide, lower the melting point and lower the chemical durability, whereas added network stabilizers, such as calcium and aluminum oxides, improve the durability of the glass. Colored glass (e.g., amber glass) is produced by transition metal oxides such as iron oxides. All additives to pure silicon dioxide, as well as silicon itself, can be viewed as potential extractables from glass containers.

Glass compositions do not exist as stoichiometric chemical compounds but rather are expressed by a range of compositions. Thus, there is allowable variation within a glass type, and glass types may vary slightly among glass producers.

Soda-lime-silica glass consists of silicon dioxide (60–75 wt%), sodium and potassium oxides (12–18 wt%), and smaller amounts of calcium, magnesium, and aluminum oxides (5–12 wt%). This glass has a relatively high coefficient of expansion (COE) of 80–90 × 10⁻⁷ per degree and is susceptible to breakage by thermic shock.

Borosilicate glass consists of silica (65–80 wt%), boric oxide (7–13 wt%), and smaller amounts of sodium, potassium, and aluminum oxides. The presence of boron provides greater resistance to thermal shock through a reduction in COE and to hydrolytic attack by increasing the connectivity of the glass network.

Type I glass is available in multiple formulations: tubular glass is available with a low COE, described as 32–33 expansion glass and with a relatively low COE (range, 48–56 expansion), for example 51 expansion glass. Molded glass has a higher COE in the region of 60–63 expansion.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Chemical structure of glass: network modifiers vs. network stabilizers:

Pure silicon dioxide (SiO₂) forms a dense three-dimensional network (silicon-oxygen tetrahedra) with excellent chemical stability, but its melting point is as high as 1700°C, making it difficult and expensive to process.

Network modifiers: Adding Na₂O, K₂O and similar oxides breaks Si-O-Si bonds and lowers the melting point (easier processing), but also lowers chemical durability, because sodium ions leach readily from the glass surface.

Network stabilizers: Adding CaO, Al₂O₃, B₂O₃ and similar oxides partially restores network connectivity and improves chemical durability. Boric oxide (B₂O₃) is the key component of borosilicate glass: it lowers the COE (greater thermal stability) and improves chemical durability at the same time.

Practical meaning of COE: The lower the COE (coefficient of thermal expansion), the less likely the glass is to break under thermal shock (for example, autoclaving). Tubular 32–33 expansion glass has the lowest COE and the best thermal stability, but is also the most difficult to manufacture.

Formulas and Calculations

Comparison of glass composition and properties:

Soda-lime-silica glass:
SiO₂: 60–75 wt%  Na₂O + K₂O: 12–18 wt%
CaO + MgO + Al₂O₃: 5–12 wt%
COE: 80–90 × 10⁻⁷ /°C
→ Type II / III glass, lower chemical resistance

Borosilicate glass:
SiO₂: 65–80 wt%  B₂O₃: 7–13 wt%
Na₂O + K₂O + Al₂O₃: small amounts
COE: 32–56 × 10⁻⁷ /°C (depends on composition)
→ Type I glass, high chemical resistance

Expansion coefficients of Type I tubular glass:
32–33 expansion: COE = 32.5 × 10⁻⁷ /°C (lowest, best thermal shock resistance)
51 expansion: COE = 51.0 × 10⁻⁷ /°C (more common)
Molded glass COE: 60–63 × 10⁻⁷ /°C (higher)

Key Notes

Impact of "glass composition is given as a permitted range": USP notes that glass composition is "expressed as ranges", so the chemical composition of Type I glass can differ slightly between suppliers. This means: (1) glass validated from Supplier A cannot be applied directly to Supplier B; (2) even within Type I, the delamination tendency can differ from batch to batch; and (3) when the glass supplier is changed, drug-glass compatibility must be re-evaluated.

Extractables: USP states explicitly that all additives in glass (Na, K, Ca, B, Al, etc.) are potential extractables. For biologics that must meet E&L (Extractables & Leachables) requirements, elemental leaching from the glass container must be evaluated.

Formation of Molded and Tubular Glass Containers

Original Text

Formation of molded and tubular glass containers requires a number of steps. The quality of the container used in packaging depends on the conditions and the quality control of each step. Both molded and tubular containers originate from a glass furnace, and different furnaces are dedicated to borosilicate or soda-lime-silica glass. The refractory bricks lining the furnace deteriorate with time and must be replaced. Worn bricks can contribute to cosmetic defects such as stones (inclusions in the glass).

Molded glass vials and bottles are manufactured in a one-step process whereby a stream of molten glass is cut into a gob, which then enters a mold where air or tooling is used to shape the container to the mold.

Tubular glass vials formation is a two-step process. Glass tubes of a specific diameter are formed from a stream of molten glass that exits the furnace, is cooled, and is sectioned into standard lengths. These tubes are subsequently converted into glass containers (ampuls, cartridges, syringes, or vials) by either the tubing glass manufacturer or by independent converters.

Gas flames are used to soften tubing glass to form the neck, to melt the glass to form the base of ampuls or vials, and to separate the container from the glass tube. Heating rate, maximum glass temperature, and production speed are critical parameters that can be adjusted for individual forming machines.

After formation, both tubular and molded containers pass through an annealing oven (lehr) that heats the containers to 20° to 30° above the transformation temperature (Tg) of the individual glass formulation (Tg for borosilicate glass is approximately 570°) and then gradually cools them in order to remove stresses in the container due to the manufacturing process.

The process of forming tubular vials and ampuls has an effect on the local surface composition of the glass. During formation of the neck and particularly the base, the temperature of the inner surface of the containers can exceed the evaporation point of some glass components such as alkali borates. Under certain time–temperature conditions, the glass can phase separate during forming, creating nonhomogeneous surface chemistry on the interior of the container. Evidence of this can be obtained by appropriately etching the glass with acid, after which an opaque ring will appear above the heel of the container.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Fundamental differences between molded and tubular containers:

  • Molded: One-step forming; the whole container is blown or pressed directly from a gob of molten glass. Advantage: uniform wall thickness. Disadvantages: lower dimensional accuracy, and unsuitable for small-volume containers (such as a 1 mL PFS).
  • Tubular: Two-step forming; glass is first drawn into a tube and then flame-worked into a container. Advantage: high dimensional accuracy, suited to precise small-volume containers. Disadvantage: the flame-working step can change the local glass surface composition and increase the delamination risk.

Importance of annealing: Annealing is a process of slowly heating the glass to above the glass transition temperature (Tg) and then cooling it, to relieve the internal stresses generated during manufacturing. Containers that are not adequately annealed have reduced chemical and mechanical durability.

Key Notes: Root Cause of Delamination in Tubular Glass

Alkali borate evaporation: This is the most important mechanism for understanding delamination in tubular glass. During forming of the bottom and neck of tubular glass, the temperature can exceed the volatilization point of certain glass components (especially alkali borates). After these components evaporate from the inner surface, they leave behind a thin silica-rich layer depleted of alkali elements.

This silica-rich surface looks very "chemically resistant" (silica is inherently stable), but its chemical bonding to the bulk glass is actually weak, making it a potential starting point for delamination. When it contacts certain drug formulations (especially organic acids and high pH), delamination occurs easily.

Sign: After the glass is etched with acid, an opaque ring appears above the "heel" of the container. This is characteristic of an alkali-depleted zone, and this location is exactly where delamination in tubular glass is most common.

Analogy

Alkali borate evaporation in tubular glass is like the Maillard reaction on the surface of baking bread: at high temperature the chemical composition of the surface layer changes and forms a structure different from the interior. If the bond between this "crust" and the "crumb" is not strong enough, it easily separates and flakes off. For glass vials, this "surface layer" (the silica-rich layer) is the hidden risk for delamination.

Practice Questions

  1. Why does tubular glass have a higher delamination risk than molded glass? Explain from the standpoint of the manufacturing process.
  2. Relationship between inadequate annealing and glass delamination: annealing mainly addresses mechanical stress. Does it also affect surface chemistry? Why?
Processing of Molded and Tubular Glass Containers

Original Text

At times, the inner surfaces of glass ampuls, vials, and bottles undergo additional treatments. As an example, heating glass propagates sodium oxide toward the inner surface of the container, but washing with water does not remove sodium oxide because of the latter's limited solubility.

One common treatment is the use of ammonium sulfate which converts the sodium oxide on the inner surface to a depth of approximately 10–100 nm into highly soluble sodium sulfate that then can be removed by washing. Although removal of sodium ions from the surface does reduce the propensity for pH shift, the treatment does remove structural elements, leaving a thin silica-rich inner surface layer.

The process originally was designed to raise the surface hydrolytic resistance of Type III soda-lime-silica glass to that of Type II glass in order to mimic the hydrolytic resistance of Type I glass. This process also can be applied to Type I glass.

In summary, the key factors that influence glass surface durability of containers manufactured from Type I glass are primarily the manufacturing conditions, such as the forming temperature, the time of exposure to heat, and the annealing conditions. The temperatures used for subsequent steps are lower than those used for forming and annealing, and do not pose an additional risk to the chemical durability of the glass from phase separation or volatilization.

Post-manufacturing operations such as storage in humid conditions and processing, such as depyrogenation in the presence of water vapor and terminal sterilization via autoclaving, can also impact glass surface chemical durability.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The double-edged effect of ammonium sulfate treatment:

The purpose of this treatment is to remove sodium oxide (Na₂O) from the glass surface, because Na⁺ readily leaches into the drug solution (raising pH) and lowers product quality.

Mechanism of action:

Na₂O (glass surface) + (NH₄)₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ (soluble, removed by water washing) + NH₃ (volatile)

After treatment, Na⁺ on the glass surface is reduced, alkali leaching decreases, and surface hydrolytic resistance increases.

The danger: After Na₂O is removed, only a "silica-rich layer" remains on the surface, and the structural integrity of this thin layer (10–100 nm) is weaker than that of normal glass. If the drug product that later contacts it has an aggressive formulation (organic acids, EDTA, high pH), this weakened surface layer is actually more prone to delamination.

Key Notes

Limitations of the Surface Glass Test <660>: The USP Surface Glass Test uses water as the extraction medium and can measure alkali leaching from the glass surface. The problem is that if the glass has been treated with ammonium sulfate, the test result is low (because Na⁺ has already been removed) and looks "good", but this low alkali value does not mean the glass resists delamination. Although the silica-rich layer has little Na⁺, it is actually more fragile against certain drug formulations. This is why USP 1660 says the Surface Glass Test is only a "first step" and additional screening methods are needed.

Hierarchy of process temperatures: The table shows that the most dangerous temperatures (1300–1500°C, bottom forming and cut-off) are far higher than all subsequent steps (except the furnace). Once surface chemistry changes occur in the glass at this stage (phase separation, alkali borate evaporation), none of the later steps, annealing (550–600°C), depyrogenation (250–350°C) or autoclaving (110–130°C), can "repair" the problem.

Analogy

Ammonium sulfate treatment is like polishing a knife: the surface looks shiny (low alkali leaching), but if the polishing is too deep and damages the steel structure of the blade (the silica-rich layer), the knife actually chips more easily. "Looks good" (low Na leaching) and "really good" (high chemical durability) are two different things. This is why glass quality cannot be evaluated by the Surface Glass Test alone.

Glass Surface Chemistry

Original Text

After manufacturers are assured of the quality and consistency of the glass containers they purchase, they can use the complex aqueous chemistry of surface glass to decide on potential drug product formulation and treatment steps that could increase glass stability.

The first reaction between the glass surface and an aqueous phase (water or water vapor) involves ion exchange between hydrogen ions (or hydronium ions H₃O⁺) from the aqueous phase and alkaline ions in the glass (Equation 1). This ion-exchange occurs in a short reaction time in acidic or neutral solutions. In basic solutions, the reaction occurs at the glass/water interface and dissolves the silica network.

Further reaction of the silica releases silicic acid into the solution, thereby lowering the pH (Equation 2). These reactions result in hydration of the glass surface and an alkali-depleted, silica-rich layer.

The presence of water in the leachate promotes hydrolysis of the Si–O bond forming a silica-gel layer (Equation 3). The mechanical properties of the surface gel that forms are different from those of bulk glass. Repeated hydration and dehydration of the layer leads to the cracking of the gel layer and eventual generation of particles. This process is worsened as the gel layer increases in thickness. This phenomenon is well known in glass exposed to ambient moisture (known as weathering).

At higher pH values, the mechanism of glass degradation changes from the leaching of alkali elements to the dissolution of the silicate network. At some point the limit of solubility is exceeded, and particles are formed via precipitation. If the solution is not buffered, a decrease in the solution pH will take place. These reactions and scenarios apply only to the reactions of glass with water; the presence of drug product formulations can complicate the situation considerably.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three-stage reaction of glass corrosion:

Stage 1: Ion exchange

H⁺ (drug solution) ↔ Na⁺ (glass surface)

Hydrogen ions (H⁺) in the liquid exchange with sodium ions (Na⁺) in the glass. Sodium leaches into the drug solution and H⁺ enters the glass surface. This raises the pH of the solution (if the formulation has insufficient buffering capacity).

Stage 2: Silica-gel layer formation

Hydrolysis of Si-O-Si bonds → formation of silanol groups (SiOH) → silica-gel layer

The mechanical properties of the silica-gel layer (elastic modulus, strength) differ from those of the underlying glass. Repeated hydration-dehydration cycles crack the silica-gel layer, which eventually breaks up into particles.

Stage 3 (high pH): Dissolution of the silicate network

At pH > 8–9, it is no longer just Na⁺ leaching: the entire Si-O network is attacked by OH⁻, dissolution of the whole glass accelerates, and once the dissolved silicic acid exceeds its solubility it reprecipitates to form particles.

Key Notes

pH is the most important formulation risk factor: Low pH (acidic) promotes ion exchange, so sodium and aluminum leach easily; high pH (> 8) attacks the silicate network directly and greatly accelerates the corrosion rate. Both extremes of pH are a problem, but high pH is more destructive. This explains why formulations with pH > 8 (such as certain buffer solutions) are particularly damaging to glass containers.

The "weathering" analogy: The cracking caused by repeated hydration-dehydration of the silica-gel layer, as described in USP, is similar to the weathering of glass surfaces exposed outdoors. It is the reason an "iridescent film" appears on old glass bottles. For injectables stored for a long time (2-3 years), even Type I glass may show similar problems with high-risk formulations.

Analogy

Glass corrosion is like tooth decay. Step 1: acid (from fermenting food residue) corrodes the enamel (ion exchange, silica-gel layer formation). Step 2: the enamel flakes off and exposes the dentin (cracking and shedding of the silica-gel layer). In a strongly corrosive environment (high pH), the entire tooth structure dissolves directly (dissolution of the silicate network). Prevention is to choose harder enamel (select better glass) and to reduce contact with acid (select a suitable formulation).

Factors that Influence Inner Surface Durability

Original Text

A number of factors have the potential to negatively influence the chemical durability of the inner surface of glass containers. These factors include glass composition, the conditions under which the containers were formed, subsequent handling and treatments, and the drug product in the container (Table 2). Not only can an aggressive drug substance corrode the inner surface, but excipients such as buffers, chelating agents and organic acids and high pH can also have a deleterious effect.

For example, neutral solutions of sodium citrate attack glass with a severity similar to that of substantially alkaline solutions. Organic acids, such as gluconic and malonic acids, also corrode glass through a proposed mechanism of an ion exchange reaction in which metal ions on the glass surface are replaced by hydrogen ions from the acid.

Not all listed factors negatively influence surface durability to the same degree, and can contribute to delamination either acting alone or in combination. Because of the range of variables, end users should examine all relevant variables for an individual drug product and assess the degree of risk for delamination and formation of subvisible and visible glass particles.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quick checklist of "high-risk formulation features": According to USP 1660, the following formulation features significantly increase the risk of glass delamination:

  • Organic acid salt buffers: acetate, citrate, phosphate, succinate, tartrate, malate, gluconate
  • High ionic strength: > 0.1 M alkaline salts
  • Chelating agents: EDTA (attacks the metal ions in the glass directly)
  • High pH: > 8.0
  • Long shelf life (24–36 months) + higher temperature (room-temperature storage corrodes faster than refrigerated storage)
  • Terminal sterilization (multiple autoclave cycles accelerate corrosion)

Warning about "neutral sodium citrate": USP specifically points out that a neutral (pH ~7) sodium citrate solution attacks glass as aggressively as a strongly alkaline solution. This overturns the intuition that "neutral pH = safe" and is an important point that CDMOs must understand.

Key Notes

Special hazard mechanism of EDTA: EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) is a strong chelating agent that attacks the metal ions on the glass surface (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, etc.). These ions are stabilizers of the glass network. EDTA "pulls them out" of the glass structure, causing the glass network to collapse and giving a very high delamination risk. Many eye drops and injectables contain EDTA as a preservative enhancer, and such products need particularly careful glass compatibility assessment.

Mechanism of high ionic strength: A high salt concentration raises the ionic strength of the solution and changes the water activity, which affects the structure of the hydrated layer on the glass surface and indirectly accelerates ion exchange and dissolution reactions.

Analogy

Assessing the glass delamination risk of a formulation is like assessing the "corrosion environment class" of a region: acid rain (organic acids), salt spray (high ionic strength) and strongly alkaline wastewater (high pH) are different corrosion factors. A factory facing acid rain, salt spray and strong alkali at the same time suffers far more corrosion damage than from any single factor. Likewise, if a formulation contains an organic acid buffer, EDTA and high pH together, the delamination risk is multiplied rather than simply added.

Practical Application

In a CDMO tech transfer review, when a customer formulation sheet is received, a "quick screen for glass delamination risk" should be done immediately: (1) check the pH (> 8.0 is flagged as high risk at once); (2) check the buffer type (citrate, acetate and succinate are all high risk); (3) check whether EDTA is present; (4) check the ionic strength (> 0.1 M alkaline salts); (5) check the shelf life and storage temperature. If any one high-risk factor is present, a glass compatibility screening study should be listed in the technical agreement.

Practice Questions

  2. Why does terminal sterilization (autoclaving at 121°C) increase the risk of glass delamination? Explain from a physical chemistry standpoint.

Section Summary

  • Glass delamination is the end-stage manifestation of corrosion of the glass inner surface. Lamellae of <50–200 µm are a serious quality problem that can lead to product recall; delamination is a "lagging indicator", and by the time it is seen it can no longer be prevented
  • Type I borosilicate glass (B₂O₃ added to increase network connectivity) is the first choice for injectables; tubular glass has a higher delamination risk than molded glass, because alkali borate evaporation during flame forming leaves a thin silica-rich layer
  • Glass corrosion occurs in three steps: ion exchange (Na⁺ leaching) → silica-gel layer formation (Si-O hydrolysis) → dissolution of the silicate network at high pH; the silica-rich layer left after ammonium sulfate treatment removes Na⁺ is actually more fragile with high-risk formulations
  • High-risk formulation factors (from Table 2): organic acid salt buffers (especially citrate), EDTA, high ionic strength (> 0.1 M), high pH (> 8), long shelf life, terminal sterilization, room-temperature storage
  • The Surface Glass Test <660> is only a first-step screen; for formulations with high-risk factors, additional screening of glass inner surface durability must be performed (see Section 1)

Glass Container Sourcing

Original Text

A pharmaceutical manufacturer has a range of choices when selecting a glass container for a drug product. These include the type of glass (I, II, or III), the production method (tubular or molded), surface treatments, as well as the size and neck finish of the container. It is important that the pharmaceutical manufacturers provide sufficient information on their requirements, such as the drug product formulation and the manufacturing and filling process, to allow the glass vendors to make informed judgments as to what containers to recommend.

Pharmaceutical manufacturers should consider the upstream provenance of the containers they purchase in that they should have sufficient knowledge of the glass manufacturing process and glass composition. This is essential to qualify a particular glass container type from a glass manufacturer for a particular drug product. The following knowledge is useful in this regard:

  • Glass formulation
  • COE for Type I tubular glass (32–33 or 48–56 expansion)
  • Whether the glass converter makes its own glass feedstock or sources the glass feedstock from a third party
  • The manufacturing site for the glass containers. If multiple sites manufacture glass containers for a given product, information to determine if the glass containers made at the different sites will perform comparably
  • Whether the glass surface has been modified through chemical treatment such as ammonium sulfate by the glass manufacturer or converter

The maker and user of a glass container should collaborate to assure that glass quality is monitored and maintained throughout the extent of the glass supply relationship. Glass quality should also be monitored and inferred by the user through observations made during storage throughout the product's use-by-date. The glass manufacturer and glass user quality management programs should include the following:

  • Quality audits of glass supplier (glass manufacturer and/or converter) by the glass user
  • Establishing mutually agreed upon acceptable quality levels for lots of glass containers
  • Monitoring and trending of the quality of glass batches, including but not necessarily limited to, monitoring the values obtained by the Surface Glass Test in 〈660〉
  • Monitoring and trending of glass quality and glass manufacturing process performance by the glass container manufacturer
  • Assurance that differences among different glass manufacturing sites do not significantly affect quality of a specified glass container sourced from multiple sites
  • Presence of a system to monitor and qualify changes made to the glass manufacturing process and to inform customers of such changes

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

"Informed selection" in glass container sourcing: USP expects pharmaceutical manufacturers to do more than "buy a vial that meets the Type I specification"; they must understand the supplier's manufacturing process in depth. The core information you need to know is as follows:

  • Glass formulation: The Type I borosilicate composition may differ between suppliers and directly affects chemical durability
  • COE: 32–33 vs. 51 expansion, which affects thermal shock resistance and delamination tendency
  • Whether ammonium sulfate treated: Directly affects how the Surface Glass Test results should be interpreted
  • Multiple-site issue: If the same model comes from different manufacturing sites, the glass quality may differ and equivalence must be evaluated

Role of the "converter": Some glass manufacturers produce only glass tubing, and a third-party converter processes it into vials/syringes. The converter's manufacturing conditions (temperature, speed) directly affect delamination risk and must be covered in vendor qualification.

Key Notes

Multiple-site risk is an FDA focus: In several inspections, FDA found that vials of the "same model" purchased from different sites of the same glass company showed significantly different delamination tendencies. The reason is that furnace condition, forming temperature settings, annealing profile and so on may differ between sites. If your supplier has several production sites, you need to evaluate each one separately and cannot assume that the "same model is necessarily equivalent".

Change notification system: USP expects glass manufacturers to establish a system that proactively notifies customers of process changes. This is very important for pharmaceutical manufacturers: if the supplier quietly changes the annealing profile or glass formulation, you may not find out until a stability problem appears. The supplier's change notification obligation must be clearly required in the supply agreement (quality agreement).

Analogy

Glass container sourcing is like buying certified organic food: you cannot rely on the "organic certified" label on the package alone (passing the Surface Glass Test <660>). You also need to know the soil composition of the farm (glass formulation), the growing methods (manufacturing process), whether pesticides were used (ammonium sulfate treatment), and which farm it came from (multiple-site issue). Real quality assurance comes from a thorough understanding of the whole supply chain.

Evaluation of the Inner Surface Durability

Original Text

Each lot of Type I, II, or III glass containers received by a pharmaceutical manufacturer must comply with the Surface Glass Test in chapter 〈660〉. This test provides an indication of inner surface chemical durability but does not appear to provide a clear direct correlation with the propensity to form glass particles or to delaminate.

The alkalinity value represents the sum of all the internal surfaces of the container, and although this is representative for molded containers, tubular glass vials can have different degrees of surface chemical durability, depending on the location (e.g., just above the heel versus the side wall). A low surface alkalinity value can be obtained from containers treated with ammonium sulfate but the treatment itself may reduce the inner surface chemical durability, dependent upon the drug product formulation used to fill the vial.

The most important variable that affects the surface durability is the drug product itself, and because it uses water as the extracting medium, the Surface Glass Test does not take this into consideration. Therefore, the Surface Glass Test represents only a first step in quality control of surface chemical durability, and additional screening methods should be used to demonstrate the suitability of vials for a formulation from a particular source before formal stability studies begin.

Predictive Screening Methods

Screening methods help evaluate glass containers from different vendors (molded or tubular), glass formulations (e.g., 32–33 or 48–56 expansion for tubular glass), and post-formation treatments. Screening also establishes lot-to-lot variation from individual vendors during the drug development process.

Screening methods can use a number of different technologies to examine three key parameters:

  • Visual examination and chemical profile of the inner surface layer
  • The amount and identity of extracted elements in solution
  • The number of subvisible and visible particles in solution

Table 3. Analytical Techniques for Screening Studies. DIC: Differential interference contrast microscopy. EM: Electron microscopy. SIMS: Secondary ion mass spectrometry. ICP-MS: Inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry. ICP-OES: Inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectrometry. SEM-EDX: Scanning electron microscopy–energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The three screening dimensions are complementary:

USP designed screening in three dimensions because each dimension detects a different stage of the delamination process:

  • Glass inner surface characteristics (DIC microscopy, SIMS): The earliest indicator. Before any delamination occurs, you can already see the degree of surface etching and the depth profile of the composition. This is an "advance warning".
  • Extracted elements in solution (ICP-MS, ICP-OES): The mid-stage indicator. It detects the amount of glass elements (Si, Na, K, Ca, B, etc.) leached into the drug solution. A rising SiO₂ concentration is an early signal of advancing corrosion.
  • Glass flakes/particles (SEM-EDX, visual inspection, particle size analysis): The late-stage indicator. It directly detects flakes that have already shed and confirms that delamination has occurred.

The ideal evaluation uses all three dimensions together, rather than relying only on particle counts (the last line of defense).

Analytical Techniques

ICP-MS (Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry):

The liquid sample is nebulized and passed into a high-temperature plasma (about 10,000 K), which atomizes and ionizes the elements; a mass spectrometer then analyzes the ions of each element by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Sensitivity is extremely high (ppb to ppt level), and dozens of elements (Si, Na, Al, B, Ca, Mg, etc.) can be quantified simultaneously. It is the "gold standard" for detecting elements leached from glass.

SEM-EDX (Scanning Electron Microscopy-Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy):

SEM provides micrometer-resolution morphology images (showing the shape, size and surface features of flakes); EDX provides the elemental composition of the flakes (confirming whether they are glass rather than other particles). In an OOS investigation, when particles are found, SEM-EDX can confirm whether they are of glass origin.

Key Notes

Specific limitations of the Surface Glass Test <660>:

  • The extraction medium is water, which does not reflect the aggressiveness of actual drug products (organic acid salts, EDTA)
  • Vials treated with ammonium sulfate can give artificially lowered alkali leaching values
  • Tubular glass is less durable near the heel (the most vulnerable area) than on the side wall, but the test measures the overall average
  • Passing the Surface Glass Test does not guarantee that the glass is suitable for a specific high-risk formulation

This is why USP 1660 says the Surface Glass Test is only a "first step" and not the end point.

  2. If SEM-EDX analysis finds that a particle contains high proportions of Si, O and Na and a small amount of B, what is the most likely source of the particle? How does your conclusion affect the direction of the follow-up investigation?
Aggressive Screening Conditions

Original Text

In selecting an appropriate primary glass container for pharmaceutical liquids, analysts should consider two approaches. The first is a series of accelerated temperature exposures using aggressive conditions that establish, in rank order, the chemical durability of the container without any specific reference to a given compound.

Such testing can be helpful when selecting a packaging system for which the most chemically durable glass is desired. This testing also can be helpful in determining if changes in glass quality have occurred or in assessing processing changes that have been made by the primary container manufacturer.

Screening Strategy for Drug Products

If the purpose of the glass screening is to determine the suitability of a given glass container for a specific product, the testing proposed in Table 4 is insufficient. The exposure conditions are too harsh and do not provide a direct link to the product itself.

In these instances, accelerated conditions are still relevant, but they must link to the relevant conditions for the given product. For example, if a product will be stored at 5° and the appropriate accelerated conditions are 30°, then testing should occur at 30°. Many products or formulations cannot withstand the elevated temperatures or high pH shown in Table 4.

Because lower temperatures are required for actual product testing, the duration of testing must be longer, ranging from weeks to months. A larger number of vials also is appropriate for this scenario because the goal of the testing is to ensure the results are representative of the quality of the glass that will be used for the drug product.

Table 5. Screening Strategy for Glass Vials

Indicators include the appearance of a pitted, fractured inner surface particularly around the heel of the vial instead of a smooth surface, as well as a number of changes in the test solution, especially increases in SiO₂ concentration, the number of subvisible particulates in the solution, and a change in pH.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The two types of screening conditions serve different purposes:

General aggressive screening (Table 4): Used for "ranking comparison". It is not directed at a specific product; instead, standard aggressive solutions (KCl, sodium citrate, glycine) are used under accelerated conditions to compare the relative chemical durability of different glass containers. It is suitable for supplier selection, comparison between glass batches and evaluation of process changes.

Product-specific screening (Table 5): The "real product" is tested under relevant accelerated conditions, because the complexity of real drug formulations (interactions among multiple excipients) cannot be simulated by general tests. Three sets of controls (stress test, water control and drug product control) must be set up together in order to interpret the results correctly.

Formulas and Calculations

Principles for selecting accelerated test conditions (Arrhenius acceleration concept):

Goal: use a short, high-temperature test to predict the effect of long-term, low-temperature storage

Typical acceleration factor (temperature dependence of chemical reaction rate):
For every 10°C increase in temperature, the reaction rate increases by about 2–3 times (Q₁₀ rule)

Example:
Product storage condition: 5°C, 24 months
Accelerated condition: 30°C, which approximately simulates:
(30-5)/10 = 2.5 → with Q₁₀ = 2, 30°C for 6 months ≈ 5°C for 24 months
(the actual acceleration factor must be calculated from the specific reaction mechanism)

Meaning of the three-control design in Table 5:
Stress test → confirms the test conditions are strong enough (able to induce observable delamination)
Water control → baseline (excludes the effect of water itself on the glass)
Drug product control → evaluates the effect of the actual formulation (= the source of the difference among the three sets)

Key Notes

Risk of "false positives": The general aggressive screening conditions in Table 4 are very severe (KCl at 121°C, sodium citrate at 80°C). Glass that shows delamination under these conditions will not necessarily delaminate in the actual product. If the Table 4 conditions are applied directly to a drug product, the product may be damaged by the excessive temperature, or the results may be meaningless because the conditions are not relevant. USP warns explicitly: "The Table 4 conditions are too severe to be used for evaluating the compatibility of a specific product with a specific glass."

"Indicators" of delamination: In a screening study, the appearance of any of the following should be taken very seriously: (1) pitting above the heel, the earliest signal of surface corrosion; (2) a rising SiO₂ concentration in solution, a sign of silicon dissolution; (3) an increase in the number of subvisible particles; (4) a change in solution pH (if buffering capacity is insufficient).

Analogy

The three controls in Table 5 are like the standard design of a material corrosion resistance test: (1) the stress test (immersion in salt water) confirms the method is sensitive enough; (2) the water control (immersion in distilled water) shows the baseline corrosion rate of the material itself; (3) the control in the actual environment (immersion in the real chemical) is the answer you really want. None of the three can be omitted, because a "seemingly neutral product + a specific temperature" can be far more corrosive than plain water, just as a neutral sodium citrate solution attacks glass as aggressively as a strong alkali.

Practical Application

Suggested CDMO glass compatibility screening workflow:

Step 1 (before tech transfer): Use the general aggressive screening in Table 4 to rank the candidate glass suppliers and screen out poor-quality suppliers at an early stage.

Step 2 (after the formulation is fixed, before stability): Use the three-control design in Table 5 and screen with the actual drug formulation under accelerated conditions (typically 30–40°C, 4–12 weeks). Monitor SiO₂ concentration, subvisible particles (USP <788>), visual appearance and pH.

Step 3 (during stability studies): Periodically monitor particles and leached elements in long-term stability samples (5°C or 25°C) to confirm that the accelerated screening results correlate with real conditions.

Conclusions & Mitigation Strategies

Original Text

CONCLUSIONS

Evaluation of the internal surface of glass containers begins with the Surface Glass Test, which uses water as the extracting medium. A low value is not always an indicator of a durable inner surface if the results are obtained using surface treatments (e.g., ammonium sulfate).

Such treatments can lead to a silica-rich inner surface layer that represents a weakened glass structure, and risk of delamination increases when the vial is filled with formulations that contain aggressive agents such as organic acids, EDTA, or solutions that have high ionic strength or high pH.

The screening methods and strategies described in this chapter can assist in the evaluation of glass containers from different suppliers or on a lot-to-lot basis and can provide an indication of the propensity of the selected formulation to cause delamination over time.

Selection of glass vials intended to contain a drug product with one or more of the formulation risk factors identified in Table 2 should undergo particular scrutiny.

Glass Container Sourcing Summary: The pharmaceutical manufacturer has a range of choices available, and the chapter's intent is to provide guidance to ensure the appropriate choice is made to protect the patient and the drug product from the consequences of glass delamination over the duration of a drug product's shelf life.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Summary of the core message of USP 1660:

Inadequate: Surface Glass Test <660> only
↓
Recommended: add general aggressive screening (supplier comparison)
↓
Best: add product-specific screening (3 controls) + stability monitoring

For high-risk formulations (organic acid salts, EDTA, high pH, high ionic strength), the highest level of evaluation must be reached. This is not just a USP recommendation but a necessary action to protect patients and the company's commercial interests.

Key Notes: Mitigation Strategies

If screening results show a high delamination risk, the following mitigation measures can be taken:

  • Container selection: Switch to molded glass (lower delamination risk than tubular glass); choose tubular glass with 32–33 expansion COE (more stable); consider plastic containers (COP/COC cyclic olefin polymers, which eliminate the glass delamination risk completely)
  • Formulation adjustment: Change the buffer type (for example, histidine instead of citrate); lower the buffer concentration; remove EDTA; adjust the pH to a safer range
  • Process adjustment: Avoid terminal sterilization (use sterile filtration and filling instead); optimize depyrogenation conditions (lower temperature or fewer cycles)
  • Glass coating: Some suppliers offer glass containers with an SiO₂ coating (such as Corning Valor Glass™), which have higher chemical durability

Analogy

Mitigation of glass delamination is like the three layers of defense in medical decision-making: the first layer is choosing the right material (choose more durable glass, or switch to a plastic container, equivalent to "moving to a healthier living environment"); the second layer is adjusting the formulation (remove aggressive factors, equivalent to "changing eating habits"); the third layer is optimizing the process (avoid process steps that add risk, equivalent to "avoiding risky behavior"). Ideally the first and second layers are put in place early in development, rather than remedied after problems arise.

Practical Application

Three-lines-of-defense decision framework for CDMOs:

Early development (pre-IND): Identify high-risk formulation factors → plan glass compatibility screening immediately → rule out unsuitable glass options as early as possible

Post-IND/clinical stage: Complete product-specific screening → establish trend monitoring of the glass supplier (batch-to-batch Surface Glass Test trend chart)

After commercialization: Annual glass batch evaluation → change management (a change of supplier or site must be re-evaluated) → include SEM-EDX confirmation of glass particles in the OOS investigation procedure

Container Selection Decision Framework

Original Text

In some situations, the accumulation of risk factors may indicate that the selection of a glass container for a particular formulation should be done following a predictive screening study to establish more stringent glass quality requirements or may indicate that a glass container should not be used for a formulation.

Each drug product must be considered individually. The factors that can contribute to glass delamination include: glass type and manufacturing method, container surface treatment, drug substance and excipient properties, pH and ionic strength, storage conditions and shelf life, and processing (terminal sterilization, multiple autoclaving cycles).

Because of the range of variables, the screening methods and strategies described in this chapter represent a scientific approach to minimizing the risk of glass delamination while providing flexibility in the choice of glass containers and formulations. The ultimate goal is to ensure patient safety by preventing glass particles from entering the drug product.

Historical case studies demonstrate that glass delamination can occur even with Type I glass when aggressive formulation conditions are combined with suboptimal glass manufacturing conditions. The consequences can include product recalls, regulatory actions, and patient harm. Early and thorough evaluation using the approaches described in this chapter can prevent these outcomes.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The importance of recognizing that "glass containers may not be suitable for some formulations": USP states explicitly that if risk factors accumulate to a certain degree, the right choice may be "not to use a glass container". This is a scientific decision that takes courage. Alternatives include:

  • COP/COC (cyclic olefin polymer containers): Completely eliminate the glass delamination risk, but have adsorption and specific leachable issues and require a separate E&L assessment
  • Stainless steel containers: Suitable for certain special formulations
  • Formulation redesign: Remove the aggressive excipients so that the formulation can be used safely in glass containers

Key Notes: Lessons from History

Lessons from real recall cases: Between 2010 and 2012, several pharmaceutical companies were forced to recall products because glass delamination flakes were found in injectables, involving millions of vials. The common features of these cases: (1) most were tubular glass containers; (2) the formulations contained high concentrations of organic acid salt buffers; (3) adequate glass compatibility screening was not performed before commercial production.

These recalls directly drove FDA and USP attention to the glass delamination problem and ultimately led to the development of USP 1660 (first published in 2013). A warning for CDMOs: "Lessons from history are not meant to be repeated."

Section Summary

  • The Surface Glass Test <660> is a mandatory starting screen but is not sufficient for high-risk formulations: ammonium sulfate treatment can artificially lower the alkali leaching value, and the test does not take into account the aggressiveness of the actual drug product
  • Predictive screening should evaluate three dimensions: glass inner surface characteristics (DIC microscopy/SIMS), extracted elements in solution (ICP-MS, especially SiO₂), and glass flakes/particles (composition confirmed by SEM-EDX)
  • There are two types of aggressive screening conditions: general aggressive screening (Table 4, for supplier ranking and comparison) and product-specific screening (Table 5, three-control design, reflecting real product risk); their purposes differ and they must not be mixed
  • Mitigation strategies for high-risk formulations: container replacement (molded glass, COP/COC containers), formulation adjustment (change the buffer, remove EDTA) and process optimization (avoid terminal sterilization); ideally decided early in development
  • CDMO responsibilities: identify high-risk formulation factors at tech transfer and proactively plan glass compatibility screening; establish a quality monitoring procedure for glass suppliers; include SEM-EDX confirmation of glass particles in OOS investigations
