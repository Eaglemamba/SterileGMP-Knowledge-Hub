USP General Chapter 〈381〉 | Pages 1–2 (Introduction, Scope, Test Samples, Procedures Overview)

Learning Objectives

  • Define the scope of elastomeric components and distinguish where the chapter applies and where it does not
  • Understand the three-tier chemical suitability framework: <381> baseline screening → <1663> extractables studies → <1664> assessment of leachables from drug products
  • Understand the classification principles for Type I and Type II elastomeric components and how they affect material selection for drug products
  • Understand the concept of indirect contact and its practical significance for defining scope
1. Introduction

Original Text

Packaging systems, also referred to as container–closure systems, are defined in Packaging and Storage Requirements 〈659〉; these systems are the sum of components that together contain and protect the drug product. Elastomeric components are formulated with elastomeric substances and can be either thermoset or thermoplastic in nature.

Every elastomeric component used in a pharmaceutical packaging/delivery system should be proven suitable for its intended use. The purpose of this chapter is to provide baseline chemical and biological reactivity requirements for the selection of elastomeric injectable packaging/delivery system components.

The establishment of the potential suitability of an elastomeric component does not rely on a single testing strategy. No single strategy can cover all elastomeric component attributes that have the potential to impact suitability. The chemical testing prescribed includes physicochemical tests. Extractable elements may also be relevant to the selection of a packaging system's materials of construction.

It is the material user's responsibility to evaluate the need for extractable elements testing and, if such testing is necessary, to establish and justify the means by which testing is accomplished, taking into account extraction conditions, target elements, and reporting requirements. The physicochemical tests are also augmented with biological reactivity tests.

If elastomeric components comply with the requirements outlined in this chapter, studies should follow to determine their suitability as recommended in Assessment of Extractables Associated with Pharmaceutical Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈1663〉 and Assessment of Drug Product Leachables 〈1664〉.

In summary, establishing chemical suitability involves multiple tests including:

  • Elastomeric component screening—baseline requirements for biological reactivity and physicochemical tests described in this chapter.
  • Controlled extraction studies—studies as described in 〈1663〉 to create extractables profile(s) of particular pharmaceutical packaging/delivery systems.
  • Pharmaceutical product assessment—Actual-case measurement of confirmed leachables in the pharmaceutical product in the packaging/delivery system intended for the commercial market. (For additional information, see 〈1664〉.)

Additional information about elastomeric components is found in 〈1381〉.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Elastomeric components: commonly called "rubber parts", they include vial stoppers, plungers, needle shields and similar items, and are among the most critical materials in injectable drug packaging systems.

Thermoset vs thermoplastic:

  • Thermoset (for example, conventional butyl rubber stoppers): cannot be softened again by heating after molding; the chemically cross-linked network is fixed. Relatively stable, but contains extractables such as vulcanizing agents and accelerators
  • Thermoplastic (for example, TPE): can be reheated and reshaped; usually contains fewer additives and is considered to carry a lower extractables risk, but its mechanical properties may differ

Analogy

The three-tier testing framework of <381> is like the three stages of building a house: foundation, structure and finishing:

  • <381> baseline screening = foundation test (can this plot of land support a house? The most basic safety confirmation)
  • <1663> extractables studies = structural design (what will this material release under defined conditions? Quantifying the risk)
  • <1664> leachables assessment = real-occupancy test (measuring actual leachables in the real product, as the final confirmation)

None of the three stages can be skipped, and they must be performed in sequence.

Key Notes

"User responsibility" is an important philosophy of <381>. The chapter states explicitly that assessing the need for extractable elements testing and establishing the test methods is the responsibility of the user, rather than USP providing a single universal method. This reflects the regulators' view that a drug manufacturer (or CDMO) must make a scientific judgment based on the characteristics of its own product, rather than apply a standard mechanically.

Practice Questions

  1. Why can a "single-test strategy" not fully confirm the suitability of an elastomeric component? Which attributes might a single test miss?
  2. A CDMO receives a lyophilized injectable formulation from a new client, and the client asks for the existing butyl rubber stopper to be used. Which tests do you need to complete to confirm that this stopper is suitable?
2. Scope

Original Text

Elastomeric components within scope are those used in the packaging systems of products described in Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉.

Elastomeric components utilized for injectable products within chapter scope include, but are not limited to, those used for:

  • vials and bottles (stoppers and cap liners)
  • prefilled syringes (plungers, needle shields, and tip caps)
  • cartridges (plungers and seal liners)
  • flexible bags (injection ports)
  • blow-fill-seal containers (cap liners)

The specified procedures and requirements outlined in this chapter are not applicable to elastomeric components used in products regulated by the Center for Devices and Radiological Health.

All elastomeric components in direct or indirect contact with the pharmaceutical product are within scope. An example of indirect contact is an elastomeric layer of a multilayer cap liner that does not directly contact the product but may leach into the product via migration through the product contact layer.

Also within scope are elastomeric components of systems or packages that are intended for transient product storage and/or product delivery intended for specific pharmaceutical products.

Elastomeric components outside of scope include those components of containers and closures that do not have direct or indirect contact with the pharmaceutical product or hold intermediate compounds, APIs, and excipients. Also outside chapter scope are elastomeric components of containment and/or transport systems used in product, intermediate compound, API, or excipient manufacturing. Although outside of the chapter scope, chapter tests and requirements may be applied.

Elastomeric component functional suitability tests described in this chapter are within scope until Elastomeric Component Functional Suitability in Parenteral Product Packaging/Delivery Systems 〈382〉 is fully implemented.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Direct vs Indirect Contact:

  • Direct contact: the component surface touches the drug product directly, for example the underside of a vial stopper or the plunger of a prefilled syringe
  • Indirect contact: the component itself does not touch the drug product, but its leachables can migrate to the drug product through other materials. For example, the inner rubber layer of a multilayer closure does not contact the drug, but chemicals can migrate through the outer LDPE film into the liquid in the container

The concept of indirect contact is a key extension in <381>: the testing obligation cannot be waived simply because the component "does not touch the drug directly".

Analogy

The difference between direct and indirect contact is like food packaging: in direct contact, a plastic bag holds the food directly; in indirect contact, ink from the outer carton seeps through the plastic bag into the food. Although the ink never "directly touched" the food, it can still contaminate it, which is why indirect contact also needs to be tested.

Key Notes

Chapter <382> transition provision: Functionality tests (that is, penetrability, fragmentation and so on) are currently in <381> but will in future be transferred to the new chapter <382>. Until <382> is fully implemented, they must still be performed as required by <381>. A CDMO's SOPs need to track this transition timeline.

Outside the scope but "may be applied": Rubber components of containers used for API manufacturing are outside the scope of the chapter, but "may be applied", which means a regulator or client may ask you to apply these standards voluntarily. This is a gray area and needs to be agreed explicitly in the quality agreement.

Practical Application

A CDMO receives a flip-off cap with multilayer liner from a client, in which the elastomeric layer does not contact the drug product directly but may pose a migration risk. Under the definition of indirect contact in <381>, this elastomeric layer is within the scope of the chapter and requires the corresponding physicochemical and biological reactivity testing, even though it is not a "vial stopper". Such cases need to be identified proactively during packaging material qualification.

Practice Questions

  1. Is a rubber injection port on a large-volume IV bag within the scope of <381>?
  2. A rubber gasket used on a storage drum at an API manufacturing site is outside the scope of <381>. But if your client asks you to test it according to <381>, how should you respond?
3. Test Samples

Original Text

Test samples should mimic finished elastomeric components after the completion of all manufacturing and processing steps (e.g., molding conditions, sterilization, etc.), and surface modifications (such as siliconization, chlorinated surface treatments, fluoropolymer coatings and films).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Types and effects of surface modifications:

  • Siliconization: application of silicone oil to reduce the coefficient of friction; widely used on prefilled syringe plungers. The silicone oil itself needs to be assessed for its effect on protein drugs (it may cause protein aggregation)
  • Chlorinated surface: surface treatment with chlorine gas to reduce extractables into the drug product, but it changes the surface chemistry
  • Fluoropolymer coating: for example Flurotec®, which forms an inert film on the rubber surface and greatly reduces extractables; commonly used for high-risk drug products (such as protein drugs and lipid formulations)

Key Notes

Test samples must represent the "finished product", including the state after all manufacturing steps and surface treatments. This means you cannot substitute a non-siliconized stopper for a siliconized one in testing, because surface treatment changes the extractables profile. The sample specification used in laboratory studies must be identical to that of the final commercial product, otherwise the validation results are invalid.

Practice Questions

  1. If a stopper was "uncoated" during development but the commercial product will use a "fluoropolymer-coated" version, can the development-stage test results be used for the commercial filing?
  2. What is the effect of siliconization on protein drugs (biologics)? Why does the siliconization method for prefilled syringes (spray-on vs baked-on) matter for product stability?

Section Summary

  • <381> sets out the baseline chemical and biological reactivity requirements for elastomeric components in injectable packaging/delivery systems, and must be combined with <1663> extractables and <1664> leachables studies to form a complete chemical suitability assessment framework
  • The scope covers all elastomeric components in direct contact and in indirect contact (affecting the drug product through migration); rubber components of containers used for API manufacturing are outside the scope but may be applied voluntarily
  • Test samples must be the finished product after all manufacturing steps and surface treatments (siliconization, chlorination, fluoropolymer coating, etc.), so that the results are representative
  • Functionality tests are currently in <381> and will be transferred once <382> is fully implemented; the user is responsible for assessing the need for extractable elements testing and establishing suitable test methods

USP General Chapter 〈381〉 | 4.2 Physicochemical Tests — Appearance, Color, Acidity/Alkalinity, Absorbance, Reducing Substances, Volatile Sulfides, Ammonium

Learning Objectives

  • Master how to prepare the Sample Solution, including surface area calculation, extraction conditions and blank correction
  • Understand the classification criteria for Type I and Type II elastomeric components and the different acceptance criteria of each physicochemical test
  • Be able to describe the purpose and principle of the seven tests: appearance (turbidity), color, acidity/alkalinity, absorbance, reducing substances, volatile sulfides and ammonium
  • Understand the preparation of the formazin turbidity standard reference suspension and the meaning of NTU
4.2 Physicochemical Tests — Sample Solution & Classification

Original Text

Sample Solution

Place whole, uncut elastomeric components corresponding to a surface area of 100 ± 10 cm² into a borosilicate glass, wide-necked flask (see Containers—Glass 〈660〉). If it is not possible to achieve the prescribed closure surface area (100 ± 10 cm²) using uncut elastomeric components, select the number of components that will most closely approximate 100 cm² and adjust the volume of water used to the equivalent of 2 mL/1 cm² of the actual elastomeric component's surface area.

Add 200 mL of Purified Water or Water for Injection to the elastomeric components, and weigh. Cover the mouth of the flask with a borosilicate glass beaker, or similar non-interacting container. Heat in an autoclave so that a temperature of 121 ± 2° is reached within 20–30 min, and maintain this temperature for 30 min. Cool to room temperature over a period of about 30 min. Add Purified Water or Water for Injection to bring it up to the original mass. Shake, and immediately decant and collect the solution.

[Note—This solution must be shaken before being used in each of the tests.]

Blank

Prepare a blank solution similarly, using 200 mL of Purified Water or Water for Injection, omitting the elastomeric components.

Elastomeric component categories, Type I and II

Elastomeric components may be classified in two types: Type I elastomeric closures meet the strictest requirements and are preferred; Type II elastomeric closures have mechanical properties suitable for special uses (e.g., multiple piercing) but cannot meet the Type I acceptance criteria for 4.2.1 Appearance, 4.2.4 Absorbance, and 4.2.5 Reducing Substances. For these tests, Type II elastomeric closures have alternative acceptance criteria that must be met. The intended final product application will determine whether a Type I or Type II elastomeric component is more appropriate.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Logic of sample solution preparation (surface area method):

A surface area of 100 cm² is used as the standardized basis rather than weight, because extractables migrate mainly from the surface, so surface area better represents the actual contact conditions. If exactly 100 cm² cannot be reached, the volume of water is adjusted by multiplying the actual area by 2 mL/cm², keeping the extraction concentration consistent.

Autoclave extraction (121°C/30 min): simulates the most severe steam sterilization conditions to ensure that potentially migratable chemicals are extracted. This is the concept of "worst-case extraction".

Formula and Calculation: Surface Area Adjustment

Standard case:
Surface area = 100 cm²
Extraction water = 200 mL (that is, 2 mL/cm²)

Adjusted case (100 cm² cannot be reached):
Assume actual surface area = 85 cm²
→ Adjusted water volume = 85 × 2 = 170 mL

Type I vs Type II differentiated testing:
Only 3 parameters have different criteria:
  • 4.2.1 Appearance (turbidity)
  • 4.2.4 Absorbance
  • 4.2.5 Reducing substances
The other 4 (color / acidity-alkalinity / volatile sulfides / ammonium)
use the same criteria for Type I and Type II

Analogy

Type I vs Type II is like grades of kitchen cutting boards: Type I is a glass board (cleanest, barely absorbs bacteria or chemicals); Type II is a wooden board (more durable and with special functional properties, but may release more substances). For high-risk food such as raw meat you would always use the glass board (Type I); but for slicing bread, the functional advantages of the wooden board (Type II) may matter more. Which one you choose depends on the end use.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is the sample solution prepared by autoclaving at 121°C rather than by soaking at room temperature? How does this affect how representative the test results are?
  2. If your rubber stopper has an irregular shape, how would you estimate its surface area?
4.2.1 Appearance (Turbidity)

Original Text

The determination of turbidity may be performed using either a visual or instrumental comparison. For a discussion of turbidimetry, see Nephelometry and Turbidimetry 〈855〉. Instrumental assessment of clarity provides a more discriminatory test that does not depend on the visual acuity of the analyst.

Reference suspensions — Prepare according to Table 1. Mix and shake before use.

Acceptance criteria:

  • Type I—Sample solution is not more opalescent than Reference suspension B (6 NTU/FTU).
  • Type II—Sample solution is not more opalescent than Reference suspension C (18 NTU/FTU).

4.2.2 Color

Fill one tube to a depth of 40 mm with Sample solution, and fill the second tube with the Color standard (3.0 mL Matching Fluid O diluted with 97.0 mL diluted hydrochloric acid). Compare the liquids in diffuse daylight, viewing vertically against a white background.

Acceptance criteria: Sample solution is not more intensely colored than the Color standard.

4.2.3 Acidity or Alkalinity

Add 0.1 mL of Bromothymol blue solution to 20 mL of Sample solution. If the solution is yellow, titrate with 0.01 N sodium hydroxide until a blue endpoint is reached. If the solution is blue, titrate with 0.01 N hydrochloric acid until a yellow endpoint is reached.

Acceptance criteria: NMT 0.3 mL of 0.01 N sodium hydroxide (to produce blue) or NMT 0.8 mL of 0.01 N hydrochloric acid (to produce yellow), or no titration required.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Turbidity / Opalescence: an indicator of the amount of suspended particles in the extract. High turbidity suggests the rubber material may release more particles or colloidal substances, increasing the risk of visible foreign matter in the drug product.

NTU (Nephelometric Turbidity Unit): the higher the value, the more turbid the solution. Type I requires ≤6 NTU and Type II allows up to 18 NTU, a threefold difference that reflects the significant difference in chemical purity between the two.

Formazin (formazin suspension): a chemical standard that is diluted in different proportions to make four comparison standards, A–D, providing a reproducible turbidity reference.

Key Notes

Asymmetric criteria in the acidity/alkalinity test: the limit for NaOH (0.3 mL) is far lower than that for HCl (0.8 mL), meaning USP is stricter on alkaline extractables. The reason is that alkaline substances (such as unreacted accelerators) are generally more irritating at the injection site than mildly acidic ones.

The color test uses "Matching Fluid O". This is the official USP color reference solution, where O stands for orangish; it is not an arbitrary choice but reflects the yellow-brown tint that a typical rubber extract may show.

Analogy

Turbidity testing is like judging whether drinking water is clean: you compare your water sample against a reference standard of known "dirtiness" (formazin suspension). The higher the NTU, the more the water looks like dishwater. Type I is "mineral water grade" and Type II is "tap water grade". Both are usable, but for different purposes.

Practice Questions

  1. If your sample solution measures 8 NTU for turbidity, which type requirement does the stopper meet? Can it be used for vials of high-risk biologics?
  2. What is the basis for judging the acidity/alkalinity test (bromothymol blue)? If the result is "green (neutral)", what is the conclusion?
4.2.4–4.2.7 Absorbance, Reducing Substances, Volatile Sulfides, Ammonium

Original Text

4.2.4 Absorbance

[Note—Perform this test within 5 h of preparing Sample solution.]

Pass Sample solution through an inert filter of 0.45-µm pore size, discarding the first few milliliters of filtrate. Measure the absorbance of the filtrate at wavelengths between 220 and 360 nm in a 1-cm cell using the Blank in a matched cell in the reference beam.

Acceptance criteria: Type I—NMT 0.2; Type II—NMT 4.0.

4.2.5 Reducing Substances

[Note—Perform this test within 4 h of preparing Sample solution.]

To 20.0 mL of Sample solution add 1 mL of diluted sulfuric acid and 20.0 mL of 0.002 M potassium permanganate. Boil for 3 min. Cool, add 1 g of potassium iodide, and titrate immediately with 0.01 M sodium thiosulfate using 0.25 mL of starch solution TS as the indicator. Perform a titration using 20.0 mL of Blank and note the difference in volume of 0.01 M sodium thiosulfate required.

Acceptance criteria: Type I—NMT 3.0 mL of 0.01 M sodium thiosulfate. Type II—NMT 7.0 mL.

4.2.6 Volatile Sulfides

Place elastomeric components with a total surface area of 20 ± 2 cm² in a 100-mL flask, and add 50 mL of a 20-g/L citric acid solution. Place a piece of lead acetate paper over the mouth of each flask. Also prepare a control solution containing 0.154 mg of sodium sulfide. Heat in an autoclave at 121 ± 2° for 30 min.

Acceptance criteria: Any black stain on the paper produced by the test solution is not more intense than that produced by the control solution.

4.2.7 Ammonium

Dilute 5 mL of Sample solution with water to 14 mL. Add 0.3 mL of Alkaline potassium tetraiodomercurate solution and close the container. Prepare Ammonium standard solution [1 ppm of ammonium (NH₄⁺)].

Acceptance criteria: After 5 min, any yellow color in the Test solution is no darker than the Ammonium standard solution [NMT 2 ppm of ammonium (NH₄⁺) in Sample solution].

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Absorbance 220–360 nm: absorbance in the UV range detects organic extractables such as aromatic compounds and conjugated systems. The Type I limit is 0.2 (very strict) and Type II allows up to 4.0 (20 times more lenient), directly reflecting the difference in organic additive content in the rubber formulation.

Reducing Substances: reducing substances are oxidized with potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) to indirectly measure extractables in the rubber such as antioxidants and vulcanization co-agents. These substances may affect the oxidative stability of the drug product.

Volatile Sulfides: detects unreacted elemental sulfur or sulfides in vulcanized rubber. These are typical extractables of conventional butyl rubber formulations and may affect the stability of some metal-chelating drugs.

Ammonium: some rubber manufacturing processes use ammonia as a process chemical, and residual NH₄⁺ may interfere with certain drug products or affect pH; a limit of 2 ppm ensures safety.

Key Notes

Time-limited tests: the absorbance test must be completed within 5 hours of preparing the sample solution, and the reducing substances test within 4 hours. This is because chemicals in the extract may oxidize or degrade over time, giving falsely low results (false negatives). The sample preparation time must be clearly recorded in the SOP.

The volatile sulfides test uses lead acetate paper. H₂S reacts with lead acetate to form black lead sulfide (PbS), and the depth of color directly reflects the sulfide concentration. This is a simple but effective semi-quantitative colorimetric test.

Practical Application

When performing supplier qualification for packaging materials at a CDMO, you need to require the supplier to provide a Certificate of Analysis covering all 7 physicochemical tests of <381>. Key point: ask the supplier to state whether Type I or Type II criteria were used, because the same rubber may be classified differently in different clients' specifications. If the CDMO purchases stoppers on behalf of a client, it must confirm that the classification is consistent with the regulatory filing (such as NDA/BLA) for the final drug product packaging.

Practice Questions

  1. A stopper has an absorbance test result of 2.5. Does it meet Type II criteria? Can it be used for an application that requires Type I?
  2. What potential effect do the antioxidants detected by the reducing substances test have on injectables that contain sulfite antioxidants?
  3. Why is the surface area used for the volatile sulfides test (20 cm²) much smaller than the 100 cm² used for the standard sample solution?
Complete Summary of <381> Physicochemical Tests

Section Summary

  • The sample solution is prepared by autoclave extraction at 121°C/30 min using 100 cm² of surface area and 200 mL of water; when the surface area falls short, the water volume is adjusted at 2 mL/cm²
  • Type I (strictest, preferred) and Type II (for special mechanical uses) have differentiated acceptance criteria for three tests: appearance, absorbance and reducing substances; the other four are the same
  • Absorbance must be completed within 5 hours and reducing substances within 4 hours; the time limits prevent oxidative degradation of the sample from causing false negatives, and the SOP must specify them clearly
  • Volatile sulfides (specific to vulcanized rubber) and ammonium (process residue) are important indicators of the cleanliness of the manufacturing process and are directly related to drug product stability and safety

USP General Chapter 〈381〉 | 4.1 Biological Reactivity, 4.3 Functionality Tests (Penetrability, Fragmentation, Self-Sealing)

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the relationship between the <87> in vitro biological reactivity tests and the <88> in vivo tests, and the selection logic for Type I/II components
  • Master the procedural requirements and acceptance criteria of the three functionality tests (penetrability, fragmentation and self-sealing capacity)
  • Be able to explain how the 21 gauge needle with a bevel angle of 12±2° relates to the stopper penetrability test
  • Understand the limitations of the "baseline information" provided by biological reactivity testing, and why product-specific suitability needs to be discussed with regulators
4.1 Biological Reactivity

Original Text

Elastomeric components (Type I and Type II) meet the requirements of Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vitro 〈87〉. If components do not meet the requirements of 〈87〉, they can be subjected to in vivo testing set forth in Biological Reactivity Tests, In Vivo 〈88〉, Systemic Injection Test and 〈88〉, Intracutaneous Test. Components that meet the requirements of 〈87〉 are not required to undergo 〈88〉 testing.

Type I and Type II closures must both conform to the requirements of either the in vitro or the in vivo biological reactivity tests.

Acceptance criteria: Test selection and results are consistent with 〈87〉 and/or 〈88〉.

Footnote 1: "Biological reactivity testing in support of elastomeric components used for final pharmaceutical product packaging/delivery systems (drugs and drug/device combination products) provides baseline information and will often not be sufficient to assess the final suitability for use expectations of regulatory authorities. Thus, it is important to work with the appropriate regulatory authority for guidance regarding a product-specific application."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

<87> In Vitro Biological Reactivity: uses cell culture methods (such as MEM Elution, Agar Diffusion and Direct Contact) to test the extract for cytotoxicity. In vitro tests are fast and inexpensive, and are the preferred method.

<88> In Vivo Biological Reactivity: when the in vitro test does not meet the requirements, in vivo tests in mice (systemic injection test) or rabbits (intracutaneous test) can be used as a supplement. If <87> is met, <88> is not required.

This logic of "in vitro first, in vivo only if it fails" is consistent with the 3Rs principle (Replace, Reduce, Refine), minimizing animal testing.

Key Notes

Footnote 1 is a very important regulatory statement: the biological reactivity tests in <381> provide only "baseline information" and are generally not sufficient to meet the final suitability requirements of regulators. This means:

  • Passing the <87>/<88> tests ≠ regulatory approval
  • In particular, for novel biologics and highly sensitive formulations, full <1663>/<1664> studies are also required
  • You must proactively consult regulators (FDA, EMA) to confirm the requirements for your specific product

Analogy

Biological reactivity testing is like a restaurant's basic ingredient hygiene inspection. Passing the health department's basic inspection (<87>/<88>) only shows that the ingredients contain no obvious toxins; it does not mean they are suitable for guests with special dietary needs (such as allergies or special medications). For specific patients (high-risk drug products), an additional in-depth assessment (<1663>/<1664>) is needed. The baseline is the minimum threshold, not the finish line.

Practical Application

A CDMO takes on an mRNA vaccine client who will use fluoropolymer-coated stoppers. Even if the supplier provides a test report showing compliance with <87>, you still need to: (1) require the supplier to provide a CoA for the specific batch; (2) assess whether the <1663> extractables study has been performed under the special conditions of the mRNA formulation (such as low-temperature storage and pH range); and (3) set out in the CMC module for FDA the complete scientific rationale for the packaging selection.

Practice Questions

  1. Why do both Type I and Type II stoppers need biological reactivity testing? Type II allows more chemical extractables, so why is its biological safety standard the same as Type I?
  2. A stopper passes the <87> cytotoxicity test, but your CDMO client's drug product is a protein antibody. Are the <87> results sufficient to support this application?
4.3 Functionality Tests

Original Text

[Note—Samples treated as described for preparation of Sample solution and air-dried should be used for the functionality tests of 4.3.1 Penetrability, 4.3.2 Fragmentation, and 4.3.3 Self-Sealing Capacity. Functionality tests are performed on closures intended to be pierced by a hypodermic needle. The 4.3.3 Self-Sealing Capacity test is required only for closures intended for multiple-dose containers. The needle specified for each test is a lubricated, long-bevel (bevel angle 12 ± 2°) hypodermic needle with an external diameter of 0.8 mm (21 gauge).]

4.3.1 Penetrability

Fill 10 suitable vials to the nominal volume with water, fit the closures to be examined, and secure with a cap. Using a new hypodermic needle as described above for each closure, pierce the closure with the needle perpendicular to the surface.

Acceptance criteria: The force for piercing is no greater than 10 N (1 kilogram-force) for each closure, determined with an accuracy of ±0.25 N (25 gram-force).

4.3.2 Fragmentation

Closures for liquid preparations: Fill 12 clean vials with water to 4 mL less than the nominal capacity. Fit the closures to be examined, secure with a cap, and allow to stand for 16 h.

Closures for dry preparations: Fit closures to be examined into 12 clean vials, and secure each with a cap.

Procedure: Using a hypodermic needle as described above fitted to a clean syringe, inject into each vial 1 mL of water while removing 1 mL of air. Repeat this procedure 4 times for each closure, piercing each time at a different site. Use a new needle for each closure. Filter the total volume of liquid in all the vials through a single filter with a nominal pore size NMT 0.5 µm. Count the rubber fragments on the surface of the filter visible to the naked eye.

Acceptance criteria: There are no more than 5 fragments visible. This limit is based on the assumption that fragments with a diameter >50 µm are visible to the naked eye. In case of doubt or dispute, the particles are examined microscopically to verify their nature and size.

4.3.3 Self-Sealing Capacity

[Note—Required only for closures intended for multiple-dose containers.]

Fill 10 suitable vials with water to the nominal volume. Fit the closures that are to be examined, and cap. Using a new hypodermic needle as described above for each closure, pierce each closure 10 times, piercing each time at a different site. Immerse the 10 vials in a solution of 0.1% methylene blue, and reduce the external pressure by 27 kPa for 10 min. Restore to atmospheric pressure, and leave the vials immersed for 30 min. Rinse the outside of the vials.

Acceptance criteria: None of the vials contain any trace of blue solution.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Purpose of the three functionality tests:

  • Penetrability: ensures that nurses/pharmacists can pierce the stopper easily; a stopper that is too tight makes handling difficult, deflects the needle, and even increases the risk of needle-stick injury. A force of ≤10 N (about 1 kg) ensures safe handling.
  • Fragmentation: every puncture can cut off rubber fragments, and fragments entering the drug solution are a serious visible/subvisible particulate contamination problem. The criterion of ≤5 fragments is the bottom line for patient protection.
  • Self-Sealing Capacity: after repeated punctures of multi-dose containers, the rubber must be able to "close automatically" to keep bacteria out. The blue color of methylene blue is a very sensitive leak indicator.

Formula and Calculation: Design Logic of the Fragmentation Test

12 vials × 5 punctures = 60 punctures in total

Each puncture at a different site
→ ensures the whole stopper surface is tested,
  rather than concentrating on one weak point

Liquid from all 12 vials is pooled and filtered (single filter)
→ maximizes fragment collection efficiency

Acceptance criterion: ≤5 fragments (visible, >50 µm)
→ if in doubt, confirm material and size under a microscope

Note: liquid formulations must be soaked for 16 hours
(simulating liquid contact during storage)

Key Notes

Significance of standardizing the needle specification: 21 gauge with a bevel angle of 12±2° are standardized test conditions that make results comparable between laboratories. The needles used clinically vary widely (18G to 27G), but 21G was selected through scientific study as the "representative worst-case" size for testing. In actual product design, if a larger-diameter needle is expected (such as 18G for viscous drug products), additional testing may be needed.

The 27 kPa negative pressure in the self-sealing test: simulates the negative pressure inside the vial when a syringe withdraws the drug solution clinically, which is the moment when external liquid (including bacteria) is most likely to be drawn in. If the rubber seal fails under negative pressure, the external methylene blue solution will be drawn into the vial, which is the simulation logic of the test.

Analogy

The self-sealing test is like checking whether a swim ring (the rubber) still holds water after being pierced many times. You pierce 10 holes with a needle, then immerse the swim ring (the vial) in colored water (methylene blue) and create negative pressure (like pulling on the opening of the ring) to see whether the colored water seeps in. If no blue is found, the rubber's self-sealing capacity is strong enough to keep the barrier intact after repeated punctures.

Practical Application

When a CDMO accepts a prefilled syringe client, the penetrability test for the plunger is usually not applicable (the plunger is not pierced by a needle), but fragments can still arise during assembly and injection. The assessment should focus on: (1) the stability of the silicone oil film on the plunger under glide force; (2) the penetration force of the needle shield; and (3) for stoppers of multi-dose vials, all three functionality tests must be performed in full.
