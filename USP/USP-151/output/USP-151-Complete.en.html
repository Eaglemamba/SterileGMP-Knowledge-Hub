Chapter Learning Objectives

  • Understand the design principle of the Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT): evaluating the pyrogen content of an injectable by monitoring the rise in rabbit body temperature after injection
  • Master the prerequisites: depyrogenation of test apparatus, calibration of temperature-recording equipment, and test animal conditions
  • Understand the staged decision logic: initial test with 3 rabbits, then retest with 5 additional rabbits (individual rise ≥0.5°C, sum of rises ≤3.3°C)
  • Become familiar with the special dose calculation rules for radioactive pharmaceuticals
  • Understand the historical relationship between USP⟨151⟩ RPT and USP⟨85⟩ BET, and the industry shift from in vivo to in vitro testing

Introduction

Original Text

The pyrogen test is designed to limit to an acceptable level the risks of febrile reaction in the patient to the administration, by injection, of the product concerned. The test involves measuring the rise in temperature of rabbits following the intravenous injection of a test solution and is designed for products that can be tolerated by the test rabbit in a dose not to exceed 10 mL/kg injected intravenously within a period of NMT 10 min.

For products that require preliminary preparation or are subject to special conditions of administration, follow the additional directions given in the individual monograph or, in the case of antibiotics or biologics, the additional directions given in the federal regulations (see Biologics ⟨1041⟩).

A validated, equivalent in vitro pyrogen or bacterial endotoxin test may be used in place of the in vivo rabbit pyrogen test, where appropriate.1

"The pyrogen test is designed to limit to an acceptable level the risks of febrile reaction in the patient to the administration, by injection, of the product concerned."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Pyrogen: Any substance that causes a febrile reaction when injected into the human body. The most common is endotoxin from the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria, but pyrogens also include non-endotoxin pyrogens (NEP) such as lipoteichoic acid, fungal components, and viral proteins.

Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT): The classic in vivo method defined in USP⟨151⟩. The principle is intuitive: the test sample is injected into the marginal ear vein of rabbits, and if it contains pyrogens, the rabbits develop a fever. The size of the temperature rise determines whether the sample passes.

Key limiting conditions:

  • Dose limit - 10 mL/kg of body weight, with the injection completed within 10 minutes
  • Substitutability - The text expressly allows a validated in vitro test (such as BET) to be used instead

Analogy

The "food taster" concept:

In ancient times, someone would taste the emperor's food first to confirm it was safe. The logic of the rabbit pyrogen test is exactly the same: before the drug is injected into a patient, a rabbit gets a "trial injection" first. If the rabbit does not develop a fever, the pyrogen content of the drug is considered acceptable.

Of course, modern pharmaceutical science has developed more refined chemical test methods (BET), just as modern food safety has evolved from "taste testing" to chemical analysis. But this "food taster" method still cannot be fully replaced in certain special situations.

Key Points

RPT detects more than endotoxin: This is the most important advantage of RPT over BET. BET (Bacterial Endotoxin Test, USP⟨85⟩) can only detect lipopolysaccharide endotoxin from Gram-negative bacteria. RPT, however, is a biological response: the rabbit's immune system produces a fever response to all kinds of pyrogens, including non-endotoxin pyrogens.

FDA position (2012 Guidance): FDA states clearly that a properly validated BET or in vitro pyrogen test may replace RPT. Footnote 1 cites this FDA guidance.

Discussion Questions

  1. If a product is known to interfere with LAL reagent (for example, certain anticoagulants), why might RPT be the only usable pyrogen test?
  2. The text says RPT is used for products that "can be tolerated by the test rabbits". What should you do if your product is toxic to rabbits?
Apparatus and Diluents

Original Text

Render the syringes, needles, and glassware free from pyrogens by heating at 250° for NLT 30 min or by any other suitable method.

Treat all diluents and solutions for washing and rinsing of devices or parenteral injection assemblies in a manner that will assure that they are sterile and pyrogen-free.

Periodically perform control pyrogen tests on representative portions of the diluents and solutions for washing or rinsing of the apparatus.

Where Sodium Chloride Injection is specified as a diluent, use Injection containing 0.9% of sodium chloride (NaCl).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Depyrogenation: 250°C × 30 minutes is the classic dry-heat depyrogenation condition in the pharmaceutical industry. Endotoxin is a very stable molecule, and ordinary sterilization temperatures (121°C autoclaving) cannot destroy it effectively. Very high dry-heat temperatures are needed to carbonize and break down the endotoxin.

Why run control pyrogen tests?

  • Even if a diluent is labeled "pyrogen-free", contamination can still be introduced during long-term storage or handling
  • Run positive/negative controls on rinse solutions periodically to ensure that the test system itself does not generate false positives or false negatives

Analogy

Cleaning of oven trays:

Imagine you are cooking a dish to test whether an ingredient has an off smell. If your pot still holds the smell of the previous dish, you cannot judge the ingredient accurately. So you must wash the pot thoroughly (even bake it at high temperature) before you start testing. 250°C/30 minutes is "baking away all the residual smell (pyrogen)".

Discussion Questions

  1. Why is the depyrogenation temperature 250°C rather than the 121°C commonly used for sterilization? Think about it from the molecular structure of endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide, LPS).
  2. The text says depyrogenation may be done by "any other suitable method". Besides dry heat, what other methods do you know that can destroy endotoxin? (Hint: chemical and radiation methods.)

Key Points

CDMO practice: In your aseptic filling facility, depyrogenation is not used only for RPT apparatus. All glass containers (vials), stoppers, and injection needles that come into direct contact with the injectable must be depyrogenated before entering the filling line.

Temperature Recording

Original Text

Use an accurate temperature-sensing device such as a clinical thermometer, or thermistor probes or similar probes that have been calibrated to assure an accuracy of ±0.1° and have been tested to determine that a maximum reading is reached in less than 5 min.

Insert the temperature-sensing probe into the rectum of the test rabbit to a depth of NLT 7.5 cm, and, after a period of time NLT that previously determined as sufficient, record the rabbit's body temperature.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Accuracy requirement of ±0.1°C: This accuracy requirement underpins the decision logic of the whole test. Because the pass/fail threshold is a body temperature rise of 0.5°C, a thermometer with an error of ±0.3°C would make the test result meaningless.

Rectal insertion depth ≥7.5 cm: This ensures the probe reaches the core body temperature zone and avoids readings disturbed by shallow tissue affected by ambient temperature.

Maximum reading reached within 5 minutes: This ensures the temperature probe responds quickly enough that a slow instrument does not miss a brief temperature peak.

Analogy

Just like taking a temperature at the hospital: When you visit a hospital, the nurse uses an ear thermometer or an oral thermometer to measure your core body temperature. The logic of RPT is exactly the same, except that rabbits are measured rectally (which is more accurate). Imagine that the nurse's thermometer had an error of 1°C: 37.5°C and 38.5°C could not be told apart, and you would have no idea whether the patient had a fever. The same reasoning applies to RPT.

Key Points

Calibration is a GMP basic: The ±0.1°C accuracy requirement for temperature probes means you must have a sound calibration procedure (Calibration SOP), including:

  • Calibration against NIST-traceable reference thermometers
  • A defined calibration interval (typically every 6-12 months)
  • Acceptance criteria for calibration deviation (the OOS investigation trigger)
  • In-use verification: confirming before each test that the readings are reasonable

Discussion Questions

  1. If a temperature probe is found to be out of calibration during a test, can the data from that test still be used? How would your deviation investigation proceed?
Test Animals

Original Text

Use healthy, mature rabbits. House the rabbits individually in an area of uniform temperature between 20° and 23° and free from disturbances likely to excite them. The temperature varies NMT ±3° from the selected temperature.

Before using a rabbit for the first time in a pyrogen test, condition it NMT 7 days before use by a sham test that includes all of the steps as directed in Procedure except injection.

Do not use a rabbit for pyrogen testing more frequently than once every 48 h, nor prior to 2 weeks following a maximum rise of its temperature of 0.6° or more while being subjected to the pyrogen test, or following its having been given a test specimen that was adjudged pyrogenic.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts

Sham test: Before the rabbits are used in a real test, run one complete "dummy" procedure, including restraint, temperature measurement, and simulated injection, but without injecting anything. The purpose is to let the rabbits become used to the whole procedure and avoid stress-induced hyperthermia caused by nervousness or fear.

Three major restrictions on use:

  • 48-hour interval - ensures the rabbit has fully recovered from the previous test
  • 2-week exclusion period - if a rabbit's temperature rises by ≥0.6°C, it may have become sensitized and needs a longer recovery period
  • Exclusion after a positive sample - a rabbit that has received a pyrogen-positive sample may develop immune memory that affects later determinations

Analogy

An athlete's fitness management:

Test rabbits are like professional athletes: they need enough rest (48 hours) between "matches" (tests). If they were "injured" last time (a large temperature rise), they need a longer recovery period (2 weeks). And before the match they do a "warm-up" (the sham test) so the athlete gets used to the venue and routine and does not perform abnormally from nervousness on match day.

Key Points

The turning point for animal welfare: These strict animal management requirements reflect a fundamental problem of RPT: it depends on live animals. Driven by the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), regulatory trends worldwide encourage a move from RPT to in vitro methods (BET or MAT).

A milestone for the European Pharmacopoeia: Starting in 2010, the European Pharmacopoeia deleted RPT in favor of BET and the Monocyte Activation Test (MAT). USP retains ⟨151⟩ but points explicitly to BET as a substitute in its footnote.

Discussion Questions

  1. Why is a sham test needed? If this step is skipped, what effect might it have on the test results? (Hint: think about the psychological stress of a rabbit being restrained and having its temperature taken for the first time.)
  2. Does your CDMO still use RPT? If so, how large a rabbit colony would you need to maintain to meet routine release testing demand? (Consider the 48-hour interval and the 2-week exclusion period.)
Procedure

Original Text

Perform the test in a separate area designated solely for pyrogen testing and under environmental conditions similar to those under which the animals are housed and free from disturbances likely to excite them. Withhold all food from the rabbits used during the period of the test. Access to water is allowed at all times, but may be restricted during the test.

If rectal temperature-measuring probes remain inserted throughout the testing period, restrain the rabbits with light-fitting neck stocks that allow the rabbits to assume a natural resting posture.

NMT 30 min prior to the injection of the test dose, determine the “control temperature” of each rabbit: this is the base for the determination of any temperature increase resulting from the injection of a test solution. In any one group of test rabbits, use only those rabbits whose control temperatures do not vary by more than 1° from each other, and do not use any rabbit with a temperature exceeding 39.8°.

Unless otherwise specified in the individual monograph, inject into an ear vein of each of three rabbits 10 mL of the test solution per kg of body weight, completing each injection within 10 min after start of administration. The test solution is either the product, constituted if necessary as directed in the labeling, or the material under test treated as directed in the individual monograph and injected in the dose specified therein.

For pyrogen testing of devices or injection assemblies, use washings or rinsings of the surfaces that come in contact with the parenterally administered material or with the injection site or internal tissues of the patient.

Assure that all test solutions are protected from contamination. Perform the injection after warming the test solution to a temperature of 37 ± 2°. Record the temperature at 30-min intervals between 1 and 3 h subsequent to the injection.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

A "fair starting line" for a scientific experiment:

Imagine a race. You need to make sure that all the runners (rabbits) are on the same starting line (baseline temperatures within 1°C of each other), that nobody starts injured (temperature ≤39.8°C), and that nobody ate a big meal before the race (food withheld). The injected sample must also be at body temperature (37°C), just as the track temperature must be neither too hot nor too cold. Then you record the runners' results (temperature readings) at fixed time points (every 30 minutes).

Key Points

Why the ear vein? The blood vessels in a rabbit's ear are superficial and clearly visible, making it the easiest site for intravenous injection. This is one of the reasons RPT uses rabbits rather than other animals.

Why a sample temperature of 37 ± 2°C matters: If an ice-cold solution (for example, 4°C) is injected, the rabbit may show a temperature response to the cold stimulus, causing a false positive. Conversely, an overly hot solution may also cause a direct local tissue reaction.

CDMO device testing: The text specifically mentions that for medical devices or injection components, the test is run on a "rinse solution" of the contact surfaces. This is particularly important when a CDMO validates a Container Closure System.

Discussion Questions

  1. Why must the baseline temperatures of rabbits in the same group differ by ≤1°C? If one rabbit has a baseline of 38.0°C and another 39.5°C, what problem would this cause for the interpretation of results?
  2. Temperature is monitored for 1-3 hours after injection, recorded every 30 minutes. How many temperature readings does this give you? Why not start recording immediately after injection?

Core Concepts

Control Temperature: The baseline temperature measured within the 30 minutes before injection. All later "temperature rises" are calculated relative to this baseline. It is the most critical data point in the whole test.

Procedure summary:

Test Interpretation and Continuation

Original Text

Consider any temperature decreases as zero rise. If no rabbit shows an individual rise in temperature of 0.5° or more above its respective control temperature, the product meets the requirements for the absence of pyrogens.

If any rabbit shows an individual temperature rise of 0.5° or more, continue the test using five other rabbits.

If NMT three of the eight rabbits show individual rises in temperature of 0.5° or more and if the sum of the eight individual maximum temperature rises does not exceed 3.3°, the material under examination meets the requirements for the absence of pyrogens.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The "acceptance sampling" logic of quality control:

It is like a factory QC sampling plan: first take 3 samples for a quick check. If all pass, the whole lot is released. If any one is questionable, expand the sampling to 8. If no more than 3 of the 8 are defective and the overall severity (the sum) is within an acceptable range, the lot can still be released. This is the statistical "Double Sampling Plan".

Key Points

The dual conditions of Stage 2: The second stage must satisfy both of the following conditions to pass:

  • No more than 3 rabbits with an individual rise ≥0.5°C (individual criterion)
  • The sum of the maximum rises of the 8 rabbits ≤3.3°C (group criterion)

This means that even if only 2 rabbits exceed 0.5°C (meeting the individual criterion), if the rises of those 2 are very large (for example, 2.0°C each, so the sum already exceeds 3.3°C), the result is still a FAIL.

Inherent variability of RPT: Different rabbits can respond very differently to the same sample lot. This is one reason RPT is gradually being replaced by BET, whose quantitative precision and reproducibility are far better than those of in vivo animal tests.

Decision Logic Examples

Stage 1 (3 rabbits):
  Rabbit A: 38.5 → 38.8  Rise = +0.3°C  (< 0.5 √)
  Rabbit B: 38.9 → 39.5  Rise = +0.6°C  (≥ 0.5 ×)
  Rabbit C: 38.7 → 38.9  Rise = +0.2°C  (< 0.5 √)
  → Rabbit B triggered ≥0.5°C, proceed to Stage 2

Stage 2 (add 5 rabbits, total 8):
  Rabbit D: +0.1  E: +0.3  F: +0.0  G: +0.7  H: +0.2

  Summary: 2/8 rabbits ≥0.5°C (B=0.6, G=0.7) → ≤3  √
  Sum of 8 rises: 0.3+0.6+0.2+0.1+0.3+0.0+0.7+0.2 = 2.4°C ≤ 3.3  √
  → PASS

Discussion Questions

  1. If 2 of the 3 rabbits in Stage 1 show a rise above 0.5°C, after 5 more rabbits are added in Stage 2, how many more can exceed the limit while the test still passes?
  2. Suppose that among 8 rabbits only 1 shows a rise above 0.5°C, but that rabbit rose 3.5°C (the other 7 all showed 0). Is the result PASS or FAIL? Why?
  3. Why does the text specify that "a temperature decrease is considered zero"? What effect would it have on the determination if negative values were allowed to count?

Core Concepts

Two-stage decision logic:

Stage 1: 3 rabbits
↓
All <0.5°C → PASS
↓ Any ≥0.5°C
Stage 2: 5 additional rabbits (8 in total)
↓
≤3 rabbits ≥0.5°C and sum for the 8 rabbits ≤3.3°C → PASS
↓ Otherwise
FAIL - the product is judged to contain pyrogens

Key numbers to remember:

Temperature decrease = zero: If a rabbit's temperature falls after injection (for example, from 39.0°C to 38.7°C), it must not be counted as −0.3°C; it must be recorded as 0°C. This prevents negative readings from "diluting" the positive responses of other rabbits.

Radioactive Pharmaceuticals

Original Text

Test Dose for Preformulated, Ready-to-Use Products Labeled with Radioactivity

Aggregated albumin and other particle-containing products: For the rabbit pyrogen test, dilute the product with Sodium Chloride Injection to NLT 100 µCi/mL, and inject a dose of 3 mL/kg of body weight into each rabbit.

Other products (half-life > 1 day): Calculate the maximum volume of the product that might be injected into a human subject. This calculation takes into account the maximum recommended radioactive dose of the product, in µCi, and the radioactive assay, in µCi/mL, of the product at its expiration date or time. Using this information, calculate the maximum volume dose per kg to a 70-kg human subject. For the rabbit pyrogen test, inject a minimum of 10 times this dose per kg of body weight into each rabbit. The total injected volume per rabbit is NLT 1 mL and NMT 10 mL of solution.

Other products (half-life < 1 day): Dosage calculations are identical to the above. These products may be released for distribution prior to completion of the rabbit pyrogen test, but such test must be initiated at NMT 36 h after release.

Test Dose for Pharmaceutical Constituents or Reagents to Be Labeled

For “cold kits” (nonradioactive reagents to be labeled with radioactive solutions such as Sodium Pertechnetate Tc 99m Injection): Assume that the entire contents of the vial will be injected into a 70-kg human subject. For the rabbit pyrogen test, inject 1⁄70 of the vial contents per kg of body weight into each rabbit. The maximum dose per rabbit is the entire contents of a single vial. The total injected volume per rabbit is NLT 1 mL and NMT 10 mL of solution.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

The conflict between "shelf life" and quality testing:

Imagine you run a sushi restaurant. Fresh tuna delivered from the fishing port stays at its best for only a few hours. If you insisted on waiting for the laboratory's bacterial culture results (at least 24 hours) before serving, the fish would long since have lost its freshness. So you are forced to "serve first, report later". This is the logic behind allowing radioactive pharmaceuticals with a half-life of <1 day to be "released first, with RPT completed within 36 hours".

Of course, this also means that if the RPT result later fails, you need to start a recall procedure, so upstream manufacturing controls and environmental monitoring must be extremely strict.

Key Points

CDMO relevance: Most aseptic filling CDMOs do not handle radioactive pharmaceuticals directly, but the "release first, test afterward" concept in this paragraph is not unusual in the pharmaceutical industry, for example the incubation period of an aseptic process simulation (APS) or the accelerated release of some biologics.

The advantage of BET for radioactive pharmaceuticals: BET gives a result in only about 1 hour, much faster than the 3+ hours of RPT. This is why most radioactive pharmaceuticals now use BET for release testing.

Discussion Questions

  1. Radioactive pharmaceuticals with a half-life of <1 day may be released before RPT is done. If the RPT result is positive (fails), the drug may already have been injected into patients. What contingency mechanisms should your quality system have?
  2. Why is the dose calculation for Cold Kits based on a 70 kg human? If the target patient population is children (average 25 kg), does this calculation still apply?

Core Concepts

Special challenges of radioactive pharmaceuticals: Radiopharmaceuticals have two unique problems:

  • Dose limit - Rabbits cannot be injected with 10 mL/kg as with ordinary drugs, because an excessive radioactive dose would harm the rabbits
  • Time sensitivity - Short half-life radiopharmaceuticals (such as Tc-99m, half-life 6 hours) must be used as soon as possible after preparation and cannot wait for the RPT result before release

RPT vs BET: The Method Transition

Background: Why RPT Still Exists

USP ⟨151⟩ remains an official chapter in the United States Pharmacopeia. However, the footnote in the Introduction clearly states:

"A validated, equivalent in vitro pyrogen or bacterial endotoxin test may be used in place of the in vivo rabbit pyrogen test, where appropriate."

— Reference: FDA Guidance for Industry, "Pyrogen and Endotoxins Testing: Questions and Answers" (June 2012)

Key milestones in the RPT-to-BET transition:

  • 1942 — USP first adopts the Rabbit Pyrogen Test
  • 1970s — Discovery of Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) reaction to endotoxin
  • 1987 — FDA approves LAL as an end-product endotoxin test for most drugs and devices
  • 2001 — USP publishes ⟨85⟩ Bacterial Endotoxins Test
  • 2005 — FDA Guidance allows BET to replace RPT with proper validation
  • 2010 — European Pharmacopoeia deletes the RPT chapter
  • 2012 — FDA Q&A Guidance further clarifies RPT replacement
  • 2020s — Recombinant Factor C (rFC) assays gain regulatory acceptance as LAL alternatives

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point: When Is RPT Still Needed?

Although BET has become the mainstream, RPT may still be needed in the following situations:

  • Products that interfere with BET - Some drugs (such as fungal-derived products containing β-glucan, high-protein formulations, and certain anticoagulants) interfere with LAL reagent, causing false positives or false negatives. Interference cannot be overcome even after trying various dilutions and inhibition/enhancement tests.
  • Detection of non-endotoxin pyrogens (NEP) - BET can only detect LPS from Gram-negative bacteria. If pyrogens of Gram-positive bacterial, fungal, or viral origin must be detected, RPT remains the only compendial method (MAT is being developed but is not yet widely adopted).
  • Legacy regulatory requirements - The regulations of some countries/regions, or the monograph of an individual product, may still expressly require RPT.

Pressure from animal welfare regulation: EU Directive 2010/63/EU expressly requires that animal testing must not be used wherever a validated alternative method exists. This has driven the transition from RPT to BET/MAT at the regulatory level.

Analogy

From carrier pigeon to telegraph to email:

RPT is like a carrier pigeon: the oldest communication method that still works. BET (LAL) is like the telegraph: faster and more reliable, but still dependent on a special resource (horseshoe crab blood). rFC (recombinant Factor C) is like email: fully synthetic and independent of any animal. The industry is moving from "telegraph" to "email", but the "carrier pigeon" is still retained in a very small number of special scenarios.

Chapter Summary

  • USP⟨151⟩ Rabbit Pyrogen Test (RPT) is the oldest compendial pyrogen test method; it determines the pyrogen content of a product by monitoring the rise in rabbit body temperature after injection
  • Depyrogenation of apparatus requires dry-heat treatment at 250°C × ≥30 min, and temperature probe accuracy must reach ±0.1°C
  • Test animal management is strictly required: sham test conditioning, a minimum interval of 48 hours, and a 2-week exclusion after a positive result
  • Two-stage decision logic: initial test with 3 rabbits → PASS if all are <0.5°C; if any is ≥0.5°C, add 5 more rabbits → PASS if ≤3 of 8 exceed the limit and the sum is ≤3.3°C
  • Radioactive pharmaceuticals have special dose calculation rules, and short half-life products may be released first with RPT completed afterward (within 36 h)
  • Method transition trend: RPT is gradually being replaced by USP⟨85⟩ BET, and EP deleted RPT in 2010. RPT now has a role only for products that interfere with BET or when non-endotoxin pyrogens must be detected
  • CDMO strategy: Use BET first for release testing, and retain RPT capability only where scientifically justified and required by regulation. Keep following the regulatory progress of newer in vitro methods such as rFC and MAT
