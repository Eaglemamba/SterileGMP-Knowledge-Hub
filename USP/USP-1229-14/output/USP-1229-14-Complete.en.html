Introduction

Original Text

The proper development and implementation of a sterilization process requires a number of important sequential steps to ensure that an appropriate sterilization process results in materials and products that are both microbiologically safe and suitable for their intended use.

Sterilization technologies that rely on heat, ionizing radiation, and chemicals often have the potential to alter the physicochemical properties of materials to which they are applied. In some cases, sterilization could either leave toxic residues on materials that were sterilized or result in impurities (i.e., leachables) being more readily released into products that contact them. Thus, it is necessary to consider sterilization not only as a means of eliminating microorganisms but also as a process that has the potential to change materials in a manner that impacts usefulness, safety, or both.

A balance between the microbial lethality of a sterilization process and other aspects of patient safety must be established. A robust sterilization process that satisfies both is the desired objective of the cycle development activities.

"A balance between the microbial lethality of a sterilization process and other aspects of patient safety must be established."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept

The dual objectives of cycle development: USP ⟨1229.14⟩ states at the outset that sterilization development must satisfy two objectives at the same time:

  • Microbiological safety - ensuring that the sterilized product achieves the required sterility assurance level (SAL)
  • Suitability for intended use - ensuring that the sterilization process does not damage product quality or leave harmful residues

These two objectives sometimes conflict: more aggressive sterilization conditions improve microbiological safety but may damage the product. The art of cycle development lies in finding the optimal balance.

Key Notes

CDMO perspective: In a CDMO environment, every new client product may require a sterilization cycle to be developed or adjusted. You cannot handle all products with a "one cycle fits all" approach. Protein drugs, small-molecule drugs, and medical devices each have different heat sensitivities. Before development, you must thoroughly understand the product's critical quality attributes (CQAs) and material properties.

Leachables: Sterilization can cause container/packaging materials to release chemicals into the product. This is especially important for prefilled syringes and plastic packaging materials, and is a core topic of E&L (Extractables & Leachables) studies.

Process Screening

Original Text

Radiation: The methods for establishment of radiation sterilization are described in detail in ISO 11137, and adherence to the most recent version of that standard is recommended.

Sterilizing Filtration: Filtration sterilization processes rely on exclusion of microorganisms and differ substantially from those that rely on lethality (see Sterilizing Filtration of Liquids ⟨1229.4⟩). Process development for filtration must consider many factors, such as adverse effects of the fluid on the filter (e.g., chemical compatibility) and of the filter on the fluid (e.g., extractables and leachables), as well as adsorption of fluid components. Physical interactions such as pressure differential, hydraulic shock, flow rate, temperature, and filtration capacity must also be considered.

Other Sterilization Methods: The first step in the development of a sterilization cycle is exposing the materials to the sterilization process and evaluating the impact that the process has on them. Initially, this determination can be a general screening process in which gross changes in appearance may be sufficient to confirm incompatibility.

Where appearance is unchanged, the material should be evaluated for changes to its physical and chemical attributes. If the results are marginal, reductions in those parameters that most impact lethality and/or material effects may be beneficial. Tests conducted over a range of process lethality that would result in microbiological destruction should be performed.

The following conditions are merely examples for consideration when evaluating materials for compatibility with the sterilization processes indicated:

  1. Moist heat — at conditions sufficient to reliably kill the expected bioburden (see ⟨1229.1⟩ and ⟨1229.2⟩)
  2. Dry heat — check for the lowest melting point of materials and process at 25° below the lowest melting temperature (see ⟨1229.8⟩)
  3. Liquids, gases, and vapors — at conditions sufficient to reliably kill the expected bioburden (see ⟨1229.6⟩, ⟨1229.7⟩, and ⟨1229.11⟩)

The selected sterilization method should be the process that has the least impact on the essential quality attributes of the material and delivers lethality sufficient to exceed the desired minimum probability of a nonsterile unit (PNSU).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Screening Principles

The 25°C safety margin for dry heat: The original text specifically states that dry heat processes should operate at least 25°C below the lowest melting point of the materials. This is a practical safety margin. For example, if a plastic component melts at 200°C, the dry heat depyrogenation process temperature should be ≤ 175°C.

First appearance of PNSU: PNSU (Probability of a Nonsterile Unit), the core metric of all sterilization validation, is mentioned here for the first time. The pharmacopeia requires a PNSU of ≤ 10−6 for terminally sterilized products, i.e., no more than one nonsterile unit per million units.

Analogy

Choosing shoes: Process screening is like "choosing shoes" for the product. You would not simply buy the most expensive pair (the strongest sterilization conditions). Instead, you first look at the foot size (product characteristics), the occasion (intended use), and the durability of the material (material compatibility), and then pick a pair that both fits well and lasts (balancing lethality and product protection).

Bioburden Evaluation

Original Text

In parallel with the process described above, an assessment of the bioburden present in or on the items to be sterilized should be conducted (see Monitoring of Bioburden ⟨1229.3⟩). The initial evaluation should include an estimation of bioburden population and resistance. A control strategy for periodic bioburden monitoring for resistance and population should be instituted regardless of the results of the initial study.

Bioburden Resistance: For the remaining sterilization processes, the execution of a boil test (100° for 10 min) can serve as a basic screen for spore-forming microorganisms. The absence of survivors in a boil test greatly simplifies the sterilization cycle development as the sterilizing conditions for non-spore-formers are less stressful on the production materials. If microorganisms are determined to survive the boil test, their resistance to the chosen sterilization process should be established. The boil test allows for estimation of a D value for a moist heat process.

Bioburden Population: If no microorganisms are recovered or only non-spore-formers are recovered, an estimation of bioburden population can be utilized to expedite selection of the appropriate process duration. As spore-forming bacteria are ubiquitous, the potential presence of spores in the bioburden should be given consideration.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: The Two Dimensions of Bioburden

Bioburden evaluation has two key dimensions, and neither can be omitted:

  • Population - How many microorganisms are present before sterilization? Expressed as N0. The higher the count, the longer the required sterilization time
  • Resistance - How resistant are these microorganisms to heat or chemicals? Expressed as the D value. The higher the resistance, the more stringent the required conditions

Formula Explained: D Value (Decimal Reduction Time)

D value = the time (in minutes) required at a specific temperature to reduce the microbial population by 90% (1 log)

Example: D₁₂₁ = 1.5 min
→ At 121°C, the microbial population falls to 1/10 of its previous level every 1.5 minutes

Starting from 10⁶ CFU:
  1.5 min → 10⁵
  3.0 min → 10⁴
  4.5 min → 10³
  6.0 min → 10²
  7.5 min → 10¹
  9.0 min → 10⁰ (1 CFU)
  → Heating must continue to reach a PNSU of 10⁻⁶

The larger the D value, the more heat-resistant the microorganism and the longer the sterilization time required.

Key Notes: The Role of the Boil Test

Boil test: Heating at 100°C for 10 minutes is a rapid screening test. Vegetative bacteria (non-spore-formers) are killed, while spore-formers survive.

Interpreting the results:

  • No survivors → the bioburden is mainly vegetative bacteria → sterilization conditions can be milder → less impact on the product
  • Survivors present → spore-formers are present → their D value must be established → sterilization conditions must be more stringent

CDMO practice: Bioburden varies greatly with the source of the drug substance. API raw materials, excipients, and packaging materials each carry different microbial risks. Re-evaluate for every new product transferred in.

Estimation of Process Duration

Original Text

The minimum duration of the sterilization dwell period can be determined once the sterilizing method has been selected and the bioburden (as determined or assumed) information has been obtained. This is accomplished by rearranging Equation 1, used to estimate the PNSU (see Sterilization of Compendial Articles ⟨1229⟩).

Equation 1 — PNSU Estimation:

log NU = −F/D + log N0

Rearrangement of the equation and solving for F results in Equation 2:

F = D (log N0 − log NU)

Where:

  • NU = assumed PNSU (NMT 1 positive unit in 106 units)
  • F = dwell time of the sterilization process at a defined condition expressed in minutes
  • D = estimated D value of the bioburden in minutes
  • N0 = estimated maximum population of the bioburden

The calculated F value represents the minimum duration of the process dwell period to reduce the assumed initial microbial population to less than the desired PNSU. Safety factors can be added to the assumed D value of the bioburden, the minimum desired PNSU, and the bioburden population estimate.

The F value is equivalent to F0 for moist heat, FH for dry heat, or process dwell time L for other sterilization processes and is typically expressed in minutes.

Depending on the F value (or process dwell time) selected, the validation approach for sterilization can be determined. The preferred, but not required, method is the use of an overkill approach (see ⟨1229⟩) where the F value allows for complete inactivation of a resistant biological indicator. Where material limitations restrict the use of lengthy sterilizing conditions, the bioburden/biological indicator or bioburden methods may be more appropriate.

Tutorial Commentary

Formula Explained: PNSU & F Value Calculation

Equation 1 - PNSU estimation:
  log N_U = −F / D + log N₀

Equation 2 - Solving for F (minimum sterilization time):
  F = D × (log N₀ − log N_U)

Meaning of the variables:
  N₀  = initial bioburden (CFU)
  N_U = target PNSU (usually = 10⁻⁶)
  D   = D value of the bioburden (min)
  F   = required sterilization time (min)

Worked Example

Assumed conditions:
  N₀ = 100 CFU (10²)
  D  = 1.5 min (D₁₂₁ value of the bioburden)
  Target PNSU = 10⁻⁶

F = D × (log N₀ − log N_U)
F = 1.5 × (log 10² − log 10⁻⁶)
F = 1.5 × (2 − (−6))
F = 1.5 × 8
F = 12 min

→ A hold time of at least 12 minutes at 121°C is required
→ This is the meaning of F₀ = 12

Safety factor: In practice, a safety factor is applied to the D value, N0, or PNSU. For example, assume the D value is twice the measured value, or assume N0 is 10 times the measured value.

Key Notes: Choosing the Validation Approach

Three validation approaches:

  • Overkill approach - The preferred option. F0 ≥ 12 min, which fully inactivates a highly resistant BI (such as G. stearothermophilus, D121 ≥ 1.5 min, 106 spores). It places the greatest demands on the materials
  • Bioburden/BI approach - Used when the product cannot withstand overkill conditions. Both the actual bioburden and the BI inactivation data must be considered
  • Bioburden approach (bioburden only) - The mildest. The cycle is designed solely on actual bioburden data. It requires more stringent bioburden monitoring

CDMO strategy: For heat-stable products (such as stainless steel instruments and glass vials), use overkill first: it is the simplest to validate, requires the least documentation, and is the most widely accepted by regulators. For heat-sensitive products (such as protein drugs), the Bioburden/BI approach is needed, together with enhanced routine bioburden monitoring.

Formal Material Evaluation

Original Text

Once the appropriate sterilization process has been chosen, the effects of that process on the materials should be reconfirmed. The requirements for this can vary from simple physical examinations for materials expected to be largely unaffected by the sterilization process, such as stainless steel or glass with steam, to comprehensive stability examinations for formulated products in their final container-closure configuration.

The criteria for these studies should be objective wherever possible. It is useful to do a literature search for materials about which there is minimal experience, and to search and discuss potential material effects with suppliers.

The preparation and sterilization of the test units for these formal studies can be conducted in a laboratory setting and must be fully documented.

When sterilizing using chemical agents, the sterilized materials should be tested for the presence of residual sterilant and its known degradation by-products. Post-sterilization cycle aeration or other treatments may be necessary to reduce these to acceptable levels.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: Chemical Sterilant Residues

EO (ethylene oxide) residues: When gas or chemical sterilization is used, residual sterilant is a major safety concern. For example, after EO sterilization, the EO residue on the product and the by-product ECH (ethylene chlorohydrin) must meet the limits in ISO 10993-7. Post-sterilization aeration is a standard procedure.

CDMO practice: When a new product is received, material compatibility studies are a key activity during the technology transfer stage. The client usually provides preliminary data, but the CDMO should re-verify it on its own equipment and under its own conditions. These studies can be performed at laboratory scale but must be fully documented, which is a basic GMP requirement.

Biological Indicator Selection

Original Text

A biological indicator should be selected that is appropriate for the chosen sterilization process and its duration, as well as the intended validation approach (see Biological Indicators for Sterilization ⟨1229.5⟩).

The chosen biological indicator provides a means to measure the lethality of the process through the selection of biological indicator population and resistance.

The sterilization process duration should not be adapted to accommodate the specifics of any biological indicator.

"The sterilization process duration should not be adapted to accommodate the specifics of any biological indicator."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: BI Selection Principles

The role of the BI: The biological indicator is the "yardstick" of sterilization validation. It is used to measure whether the process achieves the required lethality, not to decide the process conditions.

Key principle: "The process should not be adapted to the BI." This is a very clear USP position. It means:

  • First determine the sterilization conditions based on the product and the bioburden
  • Then select a suitable BI to verify the lethality of those conditions
  • If the BI cannot be completely inactivated under those conditions, choose a less resistant BI rather than extending the process time

Key Notes: Commonly Used BIs

CDMO note: The D value and spore count of a BI must be supported by a Certificate of Analysis (CoA) from the supplier. Different BI lots may have different D values, so the validation report should record the specific lot number and characteristics of the BI used.

Container and Item Mapping

Original Text

Studies should be performed to determine the location within the materials to be sterilized that is least likely to achieve sterilizing conditions. The mapping can be conducted using thermocouples, dosimeters, biological indicators, and/or chemical indicators placed within the materials.

The materials to be sterilized should be wrapped or packaged and oriented as intended for routine use of the sterilization process. The wrapping and packaging materials and methods should be controlled to ensure reproducibility.

It is essential when conducting these studies that the introduction of the measuring devices be accomplished in a manner that does not alter (positively or negatively) the penetration of the sterilant through any wrapping and packaging or through the material itself.

If the materials to be sterilized have simple geometry, with minimal internal volume, and are sterilized with other more complex items, mapping of the simpler materials can be omitted. Interpolation of volume, density, dimension, and mass may be possible to reduce the extent of studies required. Mapping studies should be documented for future reference.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concept: Identifying the Cold Spot

Purpose of container/item mapping: To find the "coldest point" (cold spot) or "most difficult-to-reach point" inside the container or item, i.e., the location where sterilization conditions are least likely to be achieved. If the cold spot meets the target, all other locations will naturally meet it too.

Choice of measurement tools:

  • Thermocouples - temperature distribution in moist heat/dry heat sterilization
  • Dosimeters - dose distribution in radiation sterilization
  • BI / CI - supplementary verification that lethality is achieved at each location

Key Notes: Measurement Must Not Alter the Result

Non-invasive principle: USP specifically emphasizes that placing measuring devices must not alter the penetration path of the sterilant. For example:

  • Piercing a sealed package to insert a thermocouple may create a shortcut for steam to enter, which makes the measurement results overly optimistic
  • Placing a large temperature probe in a vial may displace liquid and change the heat transfer characteristics

Interpolation: USP allows interpolation for items with simple geometries to reduce the scope of the study. For example, if the cold spots of 100 mL and 500 mL vials have been validated, the behavior of a 250 mL vial can reasonably be inferred. However, this inference must be scientifically justified and documented.

Load Mapping

Original Text

The initial use of a sterilization process should include confirmation that the sterilization conditions can be delivered throughout the entire load. Additional guidance on this, and the subsequent aspect of sterilization validation and process control, can be found in other chapters within the ⟨1229⟩ family of chapters.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes: CDMO Re-validation Triggers

When is re-validation needed?

  • Introduction of a new product or container size (new container mapping needed)
  • Change in loading pattern
  • Sterilizer repair or major modification
  • Abnormal bioburden trends (higher population or resistance)
  • Change in the container closure system (may affect steam penetration)
  • Periodic re-validation (usually annually)

For a CDMO, transferring in a new client product almost inevitably triggers container mapping and possibly a load mapping update.

Both must be performed. Load mapping ensures there are no "dead spots" in the sterilizer chamber: whether an item is placed on the top shelf, the bottom shelf, or in a corner, the required sterilization conditions are achieved.

Chapter Summary

  • Sterilization cycle development workflow: process screening → bioburden evaluation → process duration estimation → material evaluation → BI selection → container mapping → load mapping
  • Dual balance: microbial lethality vs product/material protection. Over-sterilization may damage product quality
  • PNSU formula: F = D × (log N0 − log NU), where F0 is used for moist heat and FH for dry heat
  • Choosing the validation approach: Overkill (preferred, simplest) → Bioburden/BI → Bioburden (mildest, most stringent monitoring)
  • BI principle: The process should not be adapted to the BI; the BI is a yardstick, not a design basis
  • Two levels of heat distribution: container mapping (cold spot within the item) + load mapping (uniformity within the chamber)
  • CDMO key point: Every new product transfer is a potential trigger for cycle development/re-validation
