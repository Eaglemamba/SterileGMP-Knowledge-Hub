Learning Objectives

  • Understand the legal status of USP <788>: a mandatory chapter (numbered <1000) and the release test for subvisible particulate matter in all injections
  • Master the definition of "particulate matter": extraneous, mobile, undissolved substances that are not gas bubbles
  • Understand the principles of the two test methods: Method 1 Light Obscuration and Method 2 Microscopic, and how they complement each other
  • Recognize the international harmonization of USP <788> with EP 2.9.19 and JP
  • Master the basic requirements for system suitability and environment suitability testing
0.1 Introduction & Scope

Original Text

This general chapter is harmonized with the corresponding texts of the European Pharmacopoeia and/or the Japanese Pharmacopoeia. These pharmacopeias have undertaken not to make any unilateral change to this harmonized chapter.

Particulate matter in injections and parenteral infusions consists of extraneous mobile undissolved particles, other than gas bubbles, unintentionally present in the solutions.

As stated in Injections and Implanted Drug Products 〈1〉, solutions for injection administered by the intramuscular or subcutaneous route must meet the requirements of Particulate Matter in Injections 〈788〉. This requirement has been indefinitely postponed for products for veterinary use.

Parenterals packaged and labeled exclusively for use as irrigating solutions are exempt from the requirements of 〈788〉. Radiopharmaceutical preparations are exempt. Parenteral products for which the labeling specifies use of a final filter prior to administration are exempt, provided that scientific data are available to justify this exemption.

"Particulate matter in injections and parenteral infusions consists of extraneous mobile undissolved particles, other than gas bubbles, unintentionally present in the solutions."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Legal status of USP <788>: This is a mandatory chapter (General Chapter number <1000). When an injection monograph references this chapter, its test methods and limits must be followed strictly. It is not a "recommended guideline" but a legally binding release test.

The four elements of the particulate matter definition:

  • Extraneous — not a component of the product itself
  • Mobile — not fixed matter adhering to the container wall
  • Undissolved — solid particles, not solutes in solution
  • Unintentionally present — excludes the active ingredient particles in suspension formulations

Harmonization: USP <788> has been harmonized with EP 2.9.19 and JP, and the three pharmacopoeias will not revise this chapter unilaterally. This means a global pharmaceutical company needs to establish only one particle test method to meet the requirements of the US, European and Japanese markets.

Analogy

Testing for impurities in drinking water:

Imagine you need to ensure the quality of bottled water. USP <788> is like the test standard for "total suspended solids (TSS)" in tap water, but ten million times stricter, because these liquids are injected directly into blood vessels or tissue.

Excluding "gas bubbles" from the definition is important, just as you would not count tiny air bubbles in water as "impurities". Solid foreign particles (glass fragments, rubber shavings, fibers) are a completely different matter: they can block capillaries or trigger immune reactions.

Key Notes

Exempt products: Three categories of products are exempt: (1) irrigating solutions, (2) radiopharmaceuticals, (3) products labeled for use with a terminal filter (supported by scientific data). In a CDMO setting, this means you need to clearly determine whether a client's product is subject to <788>. Most injections (IV, IM, SC) must comply.

IM and SC are also included: Not only intravenous (IV) products but also intramuscular (IM) and subcutaneous (SC) products must comply with <788>. This broadens the scope to cover almost all injectable dosage forms.

CDMO Industry Application

CDMO scenario: As a sterile fill-finish contract manufacturer, USP <788> is one of the QC release tests you perform most often. Every batch of injectable product must pass the particle test before shipment.

  • Client product registration — the <788> test method and acceptance criteria must be included in the registration dossier (CTD Module 3)
  • Batch release — the Certificate of Analysis (CoA) for each batch must report the particle count results
  • Process validation — PPQ batches must demonstrate that particle counts are stable and within the limits

Practice Questions

  1. Why does USP <788> need international harmonization? If the three pharmacopoeias had different particle limits, what problems would that cause for a global CDMO?
  2. A product labeled "use with a 0.22 µm terminal filter" can be exempt from <788>. As a CDMO, would you advise a client to rely on this exemption? Why or why not?
0.2 Method Selection

Original Text

For the determination of particulate matter, two procedures, Method 1 (Light Obscuration Particle Count Test) and Method 2 (Microscopic Particle Count Test), are specified hereinafter. When examining injections and parenteral infusions for subvisible particles, Method 1 is preferably applied.

However, it may be necessary to test some preparations by the Light Obscuration Particle Count Test followed by the Microscopic Particle Count Test to reach a conclusion on conformance to the requirements.

Not all parenteral preparations can be examined for subvisible particles by one or both of these methods. When Method 1 is not applicable, e.g., in the case of preparations having reduced clarity or increased viscosity, the test should be carried out according to Method 2. Emulsions, colloids, and liposomal preparations are examples. Similarly, products that produce air or gas bubbles when drawn into the sensor may also require microscopic particle count testing.

If the viscosity of the preparation to be tested is sufficiently high so as to preclude its examination by either test method, a quantitative dilution with an appropriate diluent may be made to decrease viscosity, as necessary, to allow the analysis to be performed.

The results obtained in examining a discrete unit or group of units for particulate matter cannot be extrapolated with certainty to other units that remain untested. Thus, statistically sound sampling plans must be developed if valid inferences are to be drawn from observed data to characterize the level of particulate matter in a large group of units.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Two ways of checking banknotes:

Method 1 (light obscuration) is like a banknote counting machine: you feed the notes in and the machine quickly and automatically verifies authenticity and count. But if the notes are wet, crumpled or stuck together, the machine may not read them correctly.

Method 2 (microscopic) is like a bank teller manually examining each note with a magnifying glass: slow, but it can handle any situation, and it is the final "referee method".

In most cases the counting machine is enough (fast and accurate); manual inspection is needed only in special cases.

Key Notes

Cannot extrapolate: USP specifically emphasizes that results from testing a few units cannot represent the whole batch. This is because particulate contamination is randomly distributed: one vial may be completely clean while the vial next to it contains glass fragments. A statistically sound sampling plan is therefore required.

Common CDMO challenges: In sterile fill-finish contract manufacturing, product types that commonly make Method 1 unsuitable include:

  • Liposomal formulations — the liposomes themselves are detected as "particles" by light obscuration
  • Protein formulations — some high-concentration protein solutions are opalescent
  • Suspension formulations — the API itself is undissolved particulate
  • Products containing surfactants — shaking generates large numbers of bubbles that interfere with light obscuration

Practice Questions

  1. An mRNA-LNP vaccine (lipid nanoparticle carrier) requires <788> particle testing. Which method would you choose, and why?
  2. Why does USP require "statistically sound sampling plans" rather than simply specifying "test N units per batch"? How does this relate to the nature of particulate contamination?

Key Concepts: Method Selection Decision Tree

How the two methods complement each other:

Decision logic: Test with Method 1 first. If it passes, the product complies; if it exceeds the limits, retest with Method 2. If Method 1 is not applicable (turbid, highly viscous, emulsions), go directly to Method 2.

0.3 Method 1: Light Obscuration Particle Count Test

Original Text

Apparatus

Use a suitable apparatus based on the principle of light blockage that allows for an automatic determination of the size of particles and the number of particles according to size.

The apparatus is calibrated using dispersions of spherical particles of known sizes between 10 µm and 25 µm. These standard particles are dispersed in particle-free water. Care must be taken to avoid aggregation of particles during dispersion. System suitability can be verified by using the USP Particle Count RS.

General Precautions

The test is carried out under conditions limiting particulate matter, preferably in a laminar flow cabinet.

Very carefully wash the glassware and filtration equipment used, except for the membrane filters, with a warm detergent solution, and rinse with abundant amounts of water to remove all traces of detergent. Immediately before use, rinse the equipment from top to bottom, outside and then inside, with particle-free water.

Take care not to introduce air bubbles into the preparation to be examined, especially when fractions of the preparation are being transferred to the container in which the determination is to be carried out.

Environmental Suitability Test

Determine the particulate matter in five samples of particle-free water, each of 5 mL, according to the method described below. If the number of particles of 10 µm or greater size exceeds 25 for the combined 25 mL, the precautions taken for the test are not sufficient. The preparatory steps must be repeated until the environment, glassware, and water are suitable for the test.

Sample Preparation

Mix the contents of the sample by slowly inverting the container 20 times successively. If necessary, cautiously remove the sealing closure. Clean the outer surfaces of the container opening using a jet of particle-free water, and remove the closure, avoiding any contamination of the contents. Eliminate gas bubbles by appropriate measures such as allowing to stand for 2 min or sonicating.

For large-volume parenterals, single units are tested. For small-volume parenterals less than 25 mL in volume, the contents of 10 or more units are combined in a cleaned container to obtain a volume of not less than 25 mL; the test solution may be prepared by mixing the contents of a suitable number of vials and diluting to 25 mL with particle-free water or with an appropriate particle-free solvent when particle-free water is not suitable. Small-volume parenterals having a volume of 25 mL or more may be tested individually.

Powders for parenteral use are reconstituted with particle-free water or with an appropriate particle-free solvent when particle-free water is not suitable.

Remove four portions, not less than 5 mL each, and count the number of particles equal to or greater than 10 µm and 25 µm. Disregard the result obtained for the first portion, and calculate the mean number of particles for the preparation to be examined.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Principle and Operation of Light Obscuration

Principle of light obscuration: The liquid passes through a light-beam sensor. When a particle crosses the beam it casts a "shadow" that blocks part of the light. The sensor detects the drop in light intensity, calculates the equivalent spherical diameter of the particle from the size of the shadow, and counts the particles at the same time.

Calibration standards: The instrument is calibrated with spherical standard particles of known size (usually polystyrene latex beads). The calibration range covers the two key thresholds of 10 µm and 25 µm.

USP Particle Count RS: A USP Reference Standard used to verify system suitability, confirming that the instrument counts and sizes particles correctly on the day of testing.

Key Notes: Environment Suitability Test

Environment blank limit: In 5 × 5 mL = 25 mL of particle-free water, there must be no more than 25 particles of ≥10 µm (an average of no more than 1 per mL). This is the basic threshold to ensure that your test environment, glassware and water quality do not interfere with the results.

Discard the first portion: The first 5 mL (first portion) withdrawn from the container must be discarded and not counted. The first portion flushes the tubing and sensor and carries residual particles from the system. Only the average of the remaining three portions represents the true particle level of the sample.

Calculation: Sample Preparation Logic

SVP (small-volume parenteral) sample preparation:

If the volume per container is < 25 mL:
  Combine ≥10 containers → total volume ≥ 25 mL
  Or combine several containers + dilute to 25 mL with particle-free water

If the volume per container is ≥ 25 mL:
  A single container can be tested

Test procedure:
  Withdraw 4 × (≥5 mL) = ≥20 mL
  Discard the result of portion 1
  Take the average of portions 2, 3 and 4

LVP (large-volume parenteral): Test single containers directly; no pooling is needed.

Analogy

The logic of the first cup from the tap:

Why discard the first portion? It is like turning on the tap in the morning: the first cup usually carries impurities and rust that sat overnight in the pipes, and you would not use that cup to judge the quality of the tap water. Let the water run for a few seconds to "flush" the pipes, and only the water that comes afterward represents the true water quality.

Likewise, when the first sample is drawn, the sensor and tubing of a light obscuration instrument carry out particles left from earlier, so the first portion of data is unreliable.

CDMO Industry Application: Particle Contamination Sources

Common sources of particulate contamination in a CDMO:

  • Glass — vial delamination, and glass fragments from damaged vial necks
  • Stopper — rubber particles cut from the stopper by a piercing needle (coring)
  • Stainless steel — metal particles from wear of compounding equipment or shedding from weld defects
  • Fiber — fibers released from garments and gloves during personnel operations
  • Filter membrane fragments — fragments shed when membrane integrity is compromised during sterilizing filtration
  • Crystals/precipitates — crystallization of API or excipients caused by an unstable formulation

  1. Your CDMO has just completed filling a batch of 2 mL vials (10,000 vials in total). To perform <788> Method 1, how many vials do you need to pool to prepare one test solution? If you need to test 3 independent test solutions, how many vials are needed in total?
  2. The environment suitability test fails (in 25 mL of particle-free water, ≥10 µm particles = 35, exceeding the limit of 25). Which areas would you investigate to find the cause?
0.4 Method 2: Microscopic Particle Count Test

Original Text

Apparatus

Use a suitable binocular microscope, a filter assembly for retaining particulate matter, and a membrane filter for examination.

The microscope is adjusted to 100 ± 10 magnifications and is equipped with an ocular micrometer calibrated with an objective micrometer, a mechanical stage capable of holding and traversing the entire filtration area of the membrane filter, and two suitable illuminators to provide episcopic illumination in addition to oblique illumination.

The ocular micrometer is a circular diameter graticule (see Figure 1) and consists of a large circle divided by crosshairs into quadrants, transparent and black reference circles 10 µm and 25 µm in diameter at 100 magnifications, and a linear scale graduated in 10 µm increments.

Two illuminators are required. One is an episcopic brightfield illuminator internal to the microscope, the other is an external, focusable auxiliary illuminator that can be adjusted to give reflected oblique illumination at an angle of 10°–20°.

The membrane filter is of suitable size, black or dark gray in color, nongridded or gridded, and 1.0 µm or finer in nominal pore size.

General Precautions

Determine the particulate matter of a 50-mL volume of particle-free water according to the method described below. If more than 20 particles 10 µm or larger or if more than 5 particles 25 µm or larger are present within the filtration area, the precautions taken for the test are not sufficient.

Particle Sizing

The particle sizing process with the use of the circular diameter graticule is carried out by estimating the equivalent diameter of the particle in comparison with the 10 µm and 25 µm reference circles on the graticule. The particles are not moved from their initial locations within the graticule field of view and are not superimposed on the reference circles for comparison. The inner diameter of the transparent graticule reference circles is used to size white and transparent particles, while dark particles are sized by using the outer diameter of the black opaque graticule reference circles.

In performing the Microscopic Particle Count Test, do not attempt to size or enumerate amorphous, semiliquid, or otherwise morphologically indistinct materials that have the appearance of a stain or discoloration on the membrane filter.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts: Key Elements of the Microscopic Method

The unique value of the microscopic method: Light obscuration tells you "how many particles" there are and "how large" they are, but microscopy also tells you "what the particles look like": glass fragments? Rubber shavings? Fibers? Metal particles? This is extremely important for root cause analysis.

Dual illumination system:

  • Episcopic illumination — light from above, so that opaque particles (metal, rubber) are clearly visible on the dark filter membrane
  • Oblique illumination (10°–20°) — light at a low angle from the side, so that transparent particles (glass, crystals) produce edge reflections and are detected

Purpose of the black filter membrane: A black or dark gray membrane (not the common white membrane) is used so that light-colored and transparent particles are easier to see against the dark background.

Key Notes: Particle Sizing Rules

Different measurement for transparent vs. opaque particles:

  • White/transparent particles → compare using the inner diameter of the transparent reference circle
  • Dark/opaque particles → compare using the outer diameter of the black reference circle

This rule ensures that particles of different color and transparency are sized consistently. Not following it leads to systematic measurement bias.

Material not counted: Amorphous, semiliquid material that appears as a stain or film on the membrane should not be counted as particles. It may be protein aggregates or deposits of formulation components and needs to be confirmed separately by light obscuration.

Analogy

CSI crime scene forensics:

If light obscuration (Method 1) is an "automatic fingerprint scanner" that quickly tells you how many fingerprints are at the scene, then microscopy (Method 2) is the forensic expert on the floor with a magnifying glass examining them one by one: not just counting, but judging "Is this a footprint or a handprint? A leather shoe or a sneaker?"

In particle analysis, microscopy can distinguish glass (transparent, irregular fragments), rubber (opaque, round or irregular), fibers (elongated) and metal (opaque, shiny), which is essential for finding the source of contamination.

Practice Questions

  1. While performing Method 2, your QC laboratory observes a large number of transparent, irregular, fragment-like particles on the membrane. What is the most likely source? Which steps would you ask the production department to check?
  2. Why does USP specify that "stain-like or film-like" material is not counted in the microscopic method? If such material is actually present, how should it be handled?

Key Notes: Environment Suitability Criteria for the Microscopic Method

Note that the environment blank test for the microscopic method is stricter than for light obscuration: it tests not only ≥10 µm but also ≥25 µm. This is because the microscopic method is the referee method and requires a higher assurance of environmental cleanliness.

Section Summary

  • USP <788> is a mandatory release test that applies to almost all injections (IV, IM, SC) and is harmonized with EP and JP
  • The four elements of the particle definition: extraneous, mobile, undissolved, unintentionally present
  • Method 1 light obscuration is the preferred method (automated, fast); Method 2 microscopic is the referee/supplementary method (can identify particle morphology)
  • Environment suitability testing is a prerequisite: Method 1: ≤25 particles/25 mL of water; Method 2: ≤20 particles (≥10 µm)/50 mL and ≤5 particles (≥25 µm)/50 mL
  • SVP sample pooling rule: pool ≥10 containers of <25 mL to ≥25 mL; test LVP as single containers
  • Light obscuration discards the first portion of data (flush effect) and uses the average of the remaining three portions
  • CDMO particle sources: glass, stoppers, stainless steel, fibers, filter membrane fragments, crystals/precipitates

1.1 Method 1 Evaluation: Light Obscuration Acceptance Criteria

Original Text

Evaluation

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of more than 100 mL, apply the criteria of Test 1.A.

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of less than 100 mL, apply the criteria of Test 1.B.

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of 100 mL, apply the criteria of Test 1.B.

[Note — Test 1.A is used in the Japanese Pharmacopoeia.]

If the average number of particles exceeds the limits, test the preparation by the Microscopic Particle Count Test.

Test 1.A — LVP (>100 mL)

Solutions for parenteral infusion or solutions for injection supplied in containers with a nominal content of more than 100 mL:

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed 25 per mL equal to or greater than 10 µm and does not exceed 3 per mL equal to or greater than 25 µm.

Test 1.B — SVP (≤100 mL)

Solutions for parenteral infusion or solutions for injection supplied in containers with a nominal content of less than 100 mL:

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed 6000 per container equal to or greater than 10 µm and does not exceed 600 per container equal to or greater than 25 µm.

Tutorial Commentary

Calculation: LVP vs SVP Unit Conversion

LVP example (500 mL infusion bag):
  Limit: ≤25 per mL (≥10 µm)
  Total allowed per bag: 25 × 500 = 12,500 particles (≥10 µm)
  Total allowed per bag: 3 × 500 = 1,500 particles (≥25 µm)

SVP example (5 mL vial):
  Limit: ≤6000 per container (≥10 µm)
  Converted per mL: 6000 ÷ 5 = 1,200 per mL

SVP example (50 mL vial):
  Limit: ≤6000 per container (≥10 µm)
  Converted per mL: 6000 ÷ 50 = 120 per mL

Key finding: Once the SVP "per container" limit is converted to a per mL figure, it is far higher than the LVP limit of 25 per mL. This is because an SVP delivers only a small volume, so the total particle burden the patient receives is naturally capped by the container size.

Key Point: The 100 mL Classification Rule

100 mL = SVP: USP states explicitly that a container with a nominal volume of exactly 100 mL is tested against Test 1.B (the SVP criteria), not Test 1.A (the LVP criteria). This is a common source of confusion.

Memory aid: "Only above 100 is an LVP." Only containers greater than 100 mL use Test 1.A; 100 mL and below all use Test 1.B.

What happens after an out-of-limit result: A Method 1 result above the limit does not mean the product "fails." Instead, you retest using Method 2 (microscopic). Only if Method 2 also exceeds the limit is the product finally judged non-conforming. This gives the product a chance to "appeal."

Analogy

A two-stage alcohol test:

A Method 1 exceedance is like blowing over the limit on a roadside breathalyzer: it is not the final verdict. The police take you to the station for a more accurate blood test (Method 2). Only if the blood test also exceeds the limit is it a formal violation.

In particulate testing, light obscuration can miscount air bubbles or translucent protein aggregates as solid particles, so it is scientifically sound to use the microscopic method for final confirmation.

Practice Questions

  1. A batch of 250 mL NS (normal saline) infusion bags gives a light obscuration result of 28 per mL on average for particles ≥10 µm. Does this pass, or is further testing needed? Why?
  2. Your client asks: "Why does a 5 mL vial get to have 6000 particles ≥10 µm (equivalent to 1200/mL), while a 500 mL infusion bag is allowed only 25/mL? Shouldn't the small vial be held to a stricter limit?" How do you explain it?

Core Concept: Method 1 (Light Obscuration) Acceptance Limits

Key distinction: LVPs are counted "per mL," while SVPs are counted "per container." This difference has far-reaching practical consequences; see the calculation below.

1.2 Method 2 Evaluation: Microscopic Method Acceptance Criteria

Original Text

Evaluation

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of more than 100 mL, apply the criteria of Test 2.A.

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of less than 100 mL, apply the criteria of Test 2.B.

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of 100 mL, apply the criteria of Test 2.B.

[Note — Test 2.A is used in the Japanese Pharmacopoeia.]

Test 2.A — LVP (>100 mL)

Solutions for parenteral infusion or solutions for injection supplied in containers with a nominal content of more than 100 mL:

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed 12 per mL equal to or greater than 10 µm and does not exceed 2 per mL equal to or greater than 25 µm.

Test 2.B — SVP (≤100 mL)

Solutions for parenteral infusion or solutions for injection supplied in containers with a nominal content of less than 100 mL:

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed 3000 per container equal to or greater than 10 µm and does not exceed 300 per container equal to or greater than 25 µm.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Pass mark for the first test vs the retest:

Imagine a university entrance exam with two stages: the pass mark for the first round (Method 1) is 60, and for the second round (Method 2) it is 70. Why is the second round stricter? By then, you have had the chance to rule out "interfering factors" from the first round (misreading a question, noise in the exam room, and so on), so under cleaner conditions the standard is naturally higher.

Likewise, the microscopic method eliminates the false positives of light obscuration (air bubbles, transparent protein aggregates), so it is reasonable to judge it against a stricter limit.

Practice Questions

  1. A batch of 10 mL vials gives a Method 1 result of ≥10 µm = 7200 per container (above the 6000 limit). Retested by Method 2, the result is ≥10 µm = 2800 per container. Does the batch finally pass?
  2. Why doesn't USP simply have every product use Method 2 (more accurate, stricter limits) instead of starting with Method 1? Analyze from the standpoint of efficiency and practicality.

Key Point: Method 1 vs Method 2 Limit Comparison

Why are the microscopic limits stricter? Because the microscopic method is the "referee method," used only when light obscuration exceeds the limit. Light obscuration may misread non-solid matter such as air bubbles and protein aggregates as particles (false positives), whereas in the microscopic method an operator observes directly and can exclude these false positives. The "true particle count" seen by microscopy should therefore be lower, so the limits are set more strictly.

Pharmacy Bulk Packages

For pharmacy bulk packages for parenteral use labeled "Not for Direct Infusion", proceed as directed for small-volume parenterals when the volume is 25 mL or more. Calculate the test result on a portion that is equivalent to the maximum dose given in the labeling.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Decision Flow

Method 1 light obscuration test
↓
Result ≤ limit → PASS
↓ exceeds limit
Method 2 microscopic retest
↓
Result ≤ limit → PASS
↓ exceeds limit
FAIL → deviation investigation

Note: The product is judged non-conforming only if Method 2 also exceeds the limit. If the Method 2 result is within the limit, the product still passes even though Method 1 exceeded it.

Calculation: Pharmacy Bulk Package Example

Pharmacy Bulk Package example:
  Total volume: 100 mL
  Labeled maximum dose: 10 mL

Steps:
  1. Test the whole container by Method 1 to obtain the mean particle count per mL
  2. Calculated result = mean particles per mL × 10 mL (maximum dose)
  3. Evaluate against the SVP criteria (Test 1.B)

  Example: mean 450 particles/mL (≥10 µm)
  Result = 450 × 10 = 4,500 per container (maximum-dose container)
  Decision: 4,500 ≤ 6,000 → PASS

Key Point: Handling Special Products

Dual compartment products: For example, a lyophilized product supplied with a prefilled diluent must be mixed as labeled, shaken, and fully dissolved before testing. This simulates the clinical "reconstitution" step, so that what is tested is the finished product in the state the patient would actually be injected.

Powders for parenteral use: These must be reconstituted with particle-free water (or a suitable particle-free solvent) before testing. Reconstitution itself may introduce particles (such as rubber fragments from stopper puncture), and this is part of what the test is meant to control.

  2. A batch gives a normal <788> Method 1 result (well below the limit), but <790> visual inspection shows an abnormally high reject rate (3% vs the normal 0.5%). What does this mean, and what action would you take?

CDMO Practical Application: Correlation with <790> Visual Inspection

How <788> subvisible particles and <790> visible particles complement each other:

CDMO strategy: <788> and <790> are the "double safeguard" for particulate control in injectables. <790> uses 100% visual inspection to remove individual units containing visible particles, while <788> uses sampled testing to confirm the subvisible particle level of the whole batch. Neither can be dropped.

1.4 Batch Release Decision

Key Regulatory Context

The results obtained in examining a discrete unit or group of units for particulate matter cannot be extrapolated with certainty to other units that remain untested. Thus, statistically sound sampling plans must be developed if valid inferences are to be drawn from observed data to characterize the level of particulate matter in a large group of units.

The number of test specimens must be adequate to provide a statistically sound assessment. For large-volume parenterals or for small-volume parenterals having a volume of 25 mL or more, fewer than 10 units may be tested, using an appropriate sampling plan.

"Statistically sound sampling plans must be developed if valid inferences are to be drawn from observed data to characterize the level of particulate matter in a large group of units."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Sampling Plan Design

Why sampling matters so much in particulate testing: Particulate contamination is random and non-uniform. Unlike an assay (where the API content is fairly consistent from vial to vial), the particle count in an individual unit can be far higher than in the others because of a chance event (for example, a chipped vial neck).

USP flexibility: USP does not prescribe "test N units from every batch." Instead it requires a "statistically sound sampling plan." This gives the manufacturer flexibility, but you must also have a scientific rationale to support the number of units you sample.

Commonly used sampling schemes:

  • SVP (pooled testing): pool 10 vials × 3 pooled samples = 30 vials
  • LVP (single-unit testing): typically 3–10 units
  • Based on sampling standards such as ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 or ISO 2859

Key Point: CDMO Batch Release Checklist

A complete particulate release decision for an injectable requires confirmation that:

  • USP <788> subvisible particle test passes (Method 1, or Method 1 + Method 2)
  • USP <790> 100% visual inspection for visible particles is complete, with a reject rate within the acceptable range
  • Container Closure Integrity (CCI) testing passes
  • Sterility Test (USP <71>) passes
  • Bacterial endotoxins test (BET, USP <85>) passes
  • Physicochemical tests required by other monographs pass

The <788> particulate test is an indispensable part of this multi-layer testing system.

Analogy

QC is a safety net, not a wall:

Think of injectable quality control as the safety system of a building. The <788> particulate test is one alarm, the <790> visual inspection is another, and the <71> sterility test is a third. Each alarm detects only one type of problem: particles, visible foreign matter, or microbial contamination.

No single alarm can guarantee the building is safe, but all the alarms working together form a tight safety net. This is why batch release is a comprehensive decision and not a "pass/fail" on any single test.

CDMO Practical Application: Investigation Triggers

When to start an investigation:

  • Out of specification (OOS): any parameter exceeds the USP limit → start the OOS investigation process
  • Out of trend (OOT): results are within limits but significantly above the historical trend → start a trend investigation
  • Abnormal morphology: unusual particle types seen under the microscope (such as metal particles or unidentified black particles) → investigate even if the count is acceptable
  • Batch-to-batch variation: particle counts differ markedly between batches of the same product → investigate the source of process variation

In a CDMO environment, OOT investigation is especially important: it gives early warning before an OOS occurs, leaving time to adjust the process.

  3. Your client asks you to set the internal acceptance criterion for <788> at 50% of the USP limit (that is, ≥10 µm ≤3000 per container). What are the advantages and disadvantages of this approach in a CDMO?

Section Summary

  • LVP (>100 mL) limits are expressed per mL: Method 1: ≤25 (≥10 µm), ≤3 (≥25 µm); Method 2: ≤12 (≥10 µm), ≤2 (≥25 µm)
  • SVP (≤100 mL) limits are expressed per container: Method 1: ≤6000 (≥10 µm), ≤600 (≥25 µm); Method 2: ≤3000 (≥10 µm), ≤300 (≥25 µm)
  • 100 mL is classified as an SVP (Test 1.B / 2.B applies); only >100 mL is an LVP
  • Decision flow: Method 1 passes → PASS; Method 1 exceeds → retest by Method 2; Method 2 passes → PASS; Method 2 also exceeds → FAIL
  • Microscopic limits are about 50% of the light obscuration limits, because the microscopic method eliminates false positives and counts "true particles"
  • <788> and <790> are complementary: <788> tests subvisible particles (sampled), <790> tests visible particles (100% inspection), and together they form a complete particulate control system for injectables
  • CDMO internal controls: set internal acceptance criteria stricter than the USP limits (for example 50%–70% of the USP limit) and monitor trends to prevent OOS
