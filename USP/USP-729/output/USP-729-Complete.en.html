Introduction

Original Text

INTRODUCTION

Lipid injectable emulsions for intravenous administration are sterile oil-in-water emulsions of soybean oil, used to provide an ample supply of essential fatty acids, linoleic and linolenic, dispersed with the aid of an emulsifying agent in Water for Injection. Alternatively, soybean oil can be mixed with other suitable oils (neutral triglycerides), such as safflower oil, medium-chain triglycerides (MCT) derived from coconut or palm kernel oils, olive oil, or a marine oil, such as menhaden oil.

The size of the lipid droplets is critical: because of mechanical filtration, larger-size fat globules (>5 µm) can be trapped in the lungs. The essential size characteristics of a lipid injectable emulsion for intravenous use include the mean diameter of the lipid droplets and the range of the various droplet diameters distributed around the mean diameter, expressed as the standard deviation. In particular, the amounts of fat globules comprising the large-diameter tail of the globule size distribution are especially important with respect to infusion safety.

These two regions of the globule size distribution (mean droplet size and large-diameter tail) must be controlled within specified limits.

"The size of the lipid droplets is critical: because of mechanical filtration, larger-size fat globules (>5 µm) can be trapped in the lungs."

The two methods described below are used for determination of the mean lipid droplet diameter and the distribution of large-diameter globule sizes in lipid injectable emulsions. Method I and Method II must be validated. The methods described below to assess the quality of lipid injectable emulsions are to be performed in two stages.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Lipid Injectable Emulsion: This is an intravenous oil-in-water emulsion whose main component is soybean oil, evenly dispersed in water for injection by means of emulsifiers. Clinically, it is used for patients who cannot eat by mouth or through the gut, providing essential fatty acids and a source of calories.

Large-Diameter Tail: In a particle size distribution curve, the small number of very large particles lying to the right of the mean. Although few in number, these "tail" particles are the greatest safety concern.

PFAT5: Percentage of Fat Residing in Globules Larger Than 5 µm (the volume percentage of the dispersed phase contained in globules larger than 5 µm). This is the most central quality indicator in USP <729>.

Analogy

Think of an intravenous emulsion as pumping fruit juice (fat) into the bloodstream. Normal fat globules are about 200–500 nm and pass easily through the pulmonary capillaries (about 5–8 µm in diameter). But if the emulsion contains globules larger than 5 µm, it is like mixing watermelon seeds into the juice: these "seeds" lodge in the pulmonary capillaries and cause pulmonary embolism. PFAT5 measures how many "seeds" are in the glass.

Key Notes

Why are two methods needed? This is the clever part of the design of <729>:

  • Method I (DLS/light scattering): Measures the mean droplet size. It is highly sensitive and suited to small particles in the 1–1000 nm range, but cannot accurately count or quantify large particles.
  • Method II (light obscuration): Designed specifically to count and quantify large particles >5 µm, allowing PFAT5 to be calculated directly.

The two are complementary: DLS tells you the average quality, while light obscuration tells you the safety risk.

Practical Application

For a CDMO manufacturing lipid injectable emulsions, PFAT5 is not only a release criterion but also an early warning signal of process stability. If a rising PFAT5 trend is seen during batch manufacture, it may indicate drift in homogenization process parameters, an imbalance in the emulsifier ratio, or abnormal storage temperature. The earlier it is detected, the better the loss of a whole rejected batch can be avoided.

  2. The "large-diameter tail" of a lipid injectable emulsion may be very small in number, so why does USP define PFAT5 on a volume-weighted rather than a number-weighted basis?
Method I: Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) / Classical Light Scattering

Original Text

METHOD I — MEASUREMENT OF MEAN DROPLET DIAMETER BY DYNAMIC LIGHT SCATTERING OR CLASSICAL LIGHT SCATTERING

For the determination of the mean droplet size of lipid injectable emulsions, either of two common light-scattering techniques may be employed: (1) dynamic light scattering (DLS), also known as photon correlation spectroscopy (PCS), or (2) classical light scattering [laser diffraction (LD)], based on Mie scattering theory.

The DLS, or PCS, technique is based on analyzing the rapid temporal fluctuations in the scattered light intensity that occur due to the random Brownian motion, or diffusion, of any particles, including lipid droplets, suspended in liquid. The intensity is measured at a given angle (usually 90°) by a suitable detector (e.g., photomultiplier tube) able to measure the rapidly fluctuating scattered light intensity produced by the suspended, diffusing droplets.

These scattered intensity data are typically used to calculate the intensity autocorrelation function, which is a simple decaying exponential function in time for droplets of uniform size. A distribution of droplet sizes expresses itself by the superposition, or summation, of exponential functions of different decay times, each representative of a given droplet size. The autocorrelation function can be "inverted" by means of an appropriate deconvolution algorithm in order to obtain the approximate distribution of intensity-weighted diffusion coefficients. From the latter, the distribution of small-diameter droplets is calculated, using the Stokes–Einstein equation and the rules of classical (Mie) light scattering.

By contrast, classical light scattering based on Mie theory analyzes the spatial (angular), rather than temporal, variation of the scattered light intensity by measuring the latter as a function of the scattering angle, typically over a large range of detected angles. The extent of the angular variation is significant whenever the droplet diameter is not small compared with the wavelength of the laser light (typically 633–635 nm).

Thus, light scattering, using either DLS (varying temporal fluctuations due to droplet diffusion) or classical light scattering/Mie theory (varying, average intensity versus angle), can provide acceptable results for both the mean diameter and, to a lesser degree, the standard deviation of the droplet size distribution.

For guidance regarding instruments employing classical Mie-theory light scattering, see Light Diffraction Measurement of Particle Size 〈429〉.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dynamic Light Scattering, DLS: Also known as Photon Correlation Spectroscopy (PCS). The principle is that suspended particles move randomly and continuously through Brownian motion, so the intensity of the light they scatter fluctuates rapidly over time. The smaller the particle, the faster it diffuses and the more frequent the intensity fluctuations. DLS analyzes these time-domain intensity fluctuations, calculates the particle diffusion coefficient, and converts it to particle size using the Stokes–Einstein equation.

Stokes–Einstein Equation: D = kT / (3πηd), where D is the diffusion coefficient, k is the Boltzmann constant, T is temperature, η is viscosity, and d is particle diameter. DLS measures D and calculates d.

Formula and Calculation

Stokes–Einstein Equation:
  D = kT / (3πηd)

Where:
  D = diffusion coefficient
  k = Boltzmann constant = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K
  T = absolute temperature (K)
  η = dynamic viscosity of solvent
  d = hydrodynamic diameter

DLS measures D → calculates d
MDD must be < 500 nm (0.5 µm) per USP <729>

Analogy

DLS is like watching floating balls in a pond with a flashlight in the dark. Small balls (small particles) are pushed quickly by the water (thermal motion), so the light flickers at a high frequency; large balls move slowly and flicker at a low frequency. By analyzing how fast the light flickers, you can work out the size of the balls. This is the basic logic of DLS. Classical light scattering (laser diffraction) instead looks at the pattern of light intensity reflected by the balls at different angles, much as stones of different sizes dropped into water produce different ripple patterns.

Key Notes

Limitations of DLS (why Method II is needed):

  • DLS is an "intensity-weighted" method. Large particles scatter far more light than small ones (in proportion to the sixth power of diameter), which masks the presence of a small number of large particles.
  • DLS is mainly applicable in the 1–1000 nm range and provides only semi-quantitative information on large particles >5 µm.
  • DLS does not count particles and therefore cannot directly calculate PFAT5.
  • USP states explicitly: "DLS is only a sizing method and provides only semi-quantitative information on the relative droplet size distribution."

Practice Questions

  1. DLS and laser diffraction (LD) are both light scattering techniques, but their principles are completely different: the former analyzes time-domain fluctuations, the latter angle-domain intensity. Why does USP allow both to be used for Method I? How do their results differ?
  2. DLS measures an intensity-weighted mean size rather than a number-weighted mean size. What risk does this imply for the assessment of PFAT5?

Section Summary

  • Large fat globules >5 µm in lipid injectable emulsions are a potential risk for pulmonary embolism, and PFAT5 is the key indicator of this risk
  • USP <729> uses a two-stage approach: Method I (DLS/light scattering) measures the mean droplet size, and Method II (light obscuration) quantifies large particles and calculates PFAT5
  • DLS is based on time-domain fluctuations of light intensity caused by Brownian motion and converts them to particle size using the Stokes–Einstein equation; its limitation is that it cannot count particles or accurately quantify large particles
  • Both methods must be validated, and system suitability is a prerequisite for every analysis

Method I: Apparatus, Standard Preparation & Procedure

Original Text

Apparatus

A suitable DLS/PCS instrument with or without the capability of automatic sample dilution is controlled by validated software and is used to perform the measurement, with the scattering angle typically set at 90°. The intensity-weighted results (mean diameter and standard deviation) are reported, provided it is clearly stated which values are given and that the necessary parameter values required for all requisite calculations are also given.

Water

Pass distilled water through a filter of 0.2-µm pore size, and degas by sonication, or use Sterile Water for Injection stored in a glass container.

Standard Preparation

To a pre-established volume of Water add an appropriate amount of concentrated suspension, containing National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)-traceable polystyrene latex standard particles or other suitable nanospheres. Gently mix the fluids to achieve a homogeneous suspension. This should be performed separately for three different size standards of approximately 100, 250, and 400 nm (triplicate analyses per size), and the corresponding results of intensity-weighted mean diameter and standard deviation should coincide with the expected values within acceptable errors.

Test Preparation

To a pre-established volume of Water add an appropriate volume of sample from the lipid injectable emulsion. Gently mix the fluids to achieve a homogeneous suspension. The concentration of lipid injectable emulsion in the final sample must be optimized for the DLS/PCS instrument used. Testing should be done in triplicate or, as appropriate, based on method validation data.

System Suitability

Using the Standard Preparation, measure the intensity-weighted mean particle diameter and the corresponding standard deviation. The system is suitable once the sample temperature has reached equilibration and the results have stabilized and triplicate mean droplet diameter measurements are obtained. The coefficient of variation (CV) should not exceed 15% of the NIST-traceable mean droplet diameter. A larger CV value indicates that the latex microspheres are not suitable as a standard because they either inherently lack uniformity or have become agglomerated to an unacceptable extent.

Procedure and Interpretation

Allow the sample to equilibrate to a preset controlled temperature close to ambient (between 20° and 25°). Set the instrument scattering angle to 90°, and carry out the measurements. As long as the chi-square (χ²) goodness-of-fit parameter remains acceptably low (per instrument specifications), the results for the Test preparation are acceptable. Excessive values of the χ² parameter suggest that the droplet distribution is not normal and may indicate an unstable emulsion.

"The intensity-weighted mean droplet diameter (MDD) for lipid injectable emulsions must be less than 500 nm or 0.5 µm, irrespective of the concentration of the dispersed lipid phase."

Submicron results from the DLS method and classical (Mie) light scattering method may vary due to the different physical principles of the mechanism of testing and the different mathematical procedures used to analyze the underlying data.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

NIST-Traceable Standard: Polystyrene latex spheres certified by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), with a known and traceable particle size. USP requires standards of three sizes, 100, 250 and 400 nm, covering the normal size range of fat emulsions, to ensure the instrument calibration is verified before analysis.

Intensity-Weighted Mean Diameter: The size reported by DLS is a mean weighted by scattered light intensity, not by number. Because large particles scatter far more light than small ones, the intensity-weighted mean is usually larger than the number-weighted mean, so users must be clear about which weighting is being reported.

Chi-Square (χ²) Goodness-of-Fit: The DLS instrument uses this to assess how well the measured data match the assumed particle size distribution model (usually a lognormal distribution). A high χ² indicates an abnormal size distribution and may be a sign of emulsion instability or coalescence.

Key Notes

MDD < 500 nm is a hard criterion, independent of oil concentration: Whether the emulsion contains 10%, 20% or 30% fat, the mean droplet diameter must be below 500 nm. This is the single core acceptance criterion of USP <729> Method I.

System suitability of CV ≤ 15%: This 15% limit is looser than for typical analytical methods because DLS and LD (laser diffraction) calculate the mean differently, a point USP explains specifically in a note. If the CV is exceeded, sample analysis must not proceed; the standard must be replaced and the situation re-evaluated.

Formula and Calculation

Method I Acceptance Criterion:
  MDD (Mean Droplet Diameter) < 500 nm (0.5 µm)
  — Irrespective of lipid concentration —

System Suitability:
  CV of triplicate MDD measurements ≤ 15%
  CV = (Standard Deviation / Mean) × 100%

Temperature Control:
  Sample equilibration: 20°C – 25°C
  (per USP 〈659〉 ambient definition)

Analogy

System suitability is like checking that the brakes work before driving each day. You would not test the brakes by stomping on them on the open road; you first confirm at low speed in the parking lot that the system works, and only then dare to take the highway. DLS system suitability (using NIST standards to confirm the instrument reads accurately) is this "brake test before hitting the road", and only after it passes can real samples be analyzed.

Practice Questions

  1. If a batch of fat emulsion has an MDD of 480 nm but a clearly elevated χ² value, how should you, as quality head, decide? Is MDD < 500 nm alone sufficient for release?
  2. Why is the system suitability requirement of CV ≤ 15% so much looser than the CV ≤ 2% of typical analytical methods? What does this say about the limitations of DLS as a quantitative tool?
Method II: Light Obscuration (LE/SPOS)

Original Text

METHOD II — MEASUREMENT OF LARGE GLOBULE CONTENT BY LIGHT OBSCURATION OR EXTINCTION METHOD

For determination of the extent of the large-diameter droplet tail (>5 µm) of lipid injectable emulsions, a light obscuration (LO) or light extinction (LE) method that employs a single-particle (globule) optical sizing (SPOS) technique is used.

DLS methodology is effective only for characterizing smaller particle sizes in the nominal size range of about 1–1000 nm; therefore, DLS is complementary to LE and is particularly effective in sizing droplets smaller than 500 nm. Importantly, DLS is only a sizing method and provides only semi-quantitative information on the relative droplet size distribution. DLS does not count particles or droplets; thus, it is mainly a qualitative method. In contrast, LE both sizes (1.3–400 µm) and counts fat globules providing "reliable" count values at a size threshold of 5 µm.

During application of the LE/SPOS technique, passage of a droplet through a thin optical sensing zone results in effective blockage of a portion of the incident light beam, causing a momentary decrease in the light intensity reaching the "extinction" detector. The magnitude of this decrease in the signal is related to the cross-sectional area of the droplet, i.e., to the square of the droplet diameter. The larger the droplet diameter, the larger the height (voltage) of the signal pulse produced.

As long as the fat globule concentration is below the "coincidence limit" of the sensor, only one globule at most will pass through the sensing zone at any given time, allowing it to be counted and accurately sized (with less than 1% particle or globule coincidence events).

The resulting single pulse heights are converted to droplet diameters using a standard calibration curve previously constructed from NIST-traceable monosized polystyrene microspheres of known diameters. For additional guidance in the use of the light obscuration methodology, see Particulate Matter in Injections 〈788〉.

Apparatus

A suitable light obscuration instrument with or without the capability of automatic sample dilution and controlled by a personal computer (PC) is used for the measurement. The number- and volume-weighted particle size distribution data are reported, provided that it is clearly stated which values are given and that the necessary parameter values required for all necessary calculations are also given.

Standard Preparation (Method II)

The sizing and counting accuracy of the light obscuration instrument should be obtained using two different size standards of approximately 5 and 10 µm (triplicate analyses per size). Set the instrument threshold of detection at 1.8 µm, extended to an upper limit of 50 µm. The corresponding results for the mean diameter should coincide with the expected values, within 10% of the relative standard deviation and 90%–110% size accuracy. The number of particle counts obtained per milliliter should also agree within ±10% with the concentration values certified in the documentation provided with each NIST-traceable size standard.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Light Obscuration / Light Extinction (LE): When a particle passes through a narrow light beam, it blocks part of the light, causing an instantaneous drop in the light intensity received by the detector. The voltage pulse height is proportional to the particle cross-sectional area (that is, the square of the diameter). This is the same principle as the technique used for particulate monitoring of injectables in <788>.

Single-Particle Optical Sizing (SPOS): Only one particle at a time is allowed through the sensing zone, so that each pulse signal corresponds to one particle, enabling accurate counting and sizing. This is what allows the LE method to calculate PFAT5 reliably: it is a true "counting" method.

Coincidence Limit: When the particle concentration is too high, two particles may pass through the sensing zone at the same time and be miscounted as one large particle, causing "coincidence error". The sample must be diluted appropriately so that the concentration stays below this limit (coincidence events <1%).

Analogy

LE/SPOS is like the electronic toll collection (ETC) system at a toll station: each vehicle (each particle) passes through the sensing zone in turn, and the system records each vehicle's "size" (pulse height) and counts it. If two motorcycles arrive at the same time, the system may mistake them for one sedan; this is "coincidence error". The purpose of diluting the sample is therefore not only to make it measurable, but to make sure the particles pass through one by one in a queue so that counting is accurate.

Key Notes

LE standards of 5 µm and 10 µm (not the 100/250/400 nm used for DLS): This makes clear that the two methods cover very different size ranges. Method I verifies instrument accuracy at the nanometer scale (<500 nm), and Method II verifies instrument accuracy at the micrometer scale (>5 µm).

Detection range setting: lower limit 1.8 µm, upper limit 50 µm: The 1.8 µm lower limit is below the 5 µm target threshold so that particles near 5 µm are also reliably detected; the 50 µm upper limit covers the largest fat globule size that could be clinically harmful.

Counting accuracy requirement of ±10%: This is consistent with the accuracy requirement of <788> for particle counting in injectables, and is the standard acceptance criterion for light obscuration in counting applications.

Practical Application

When a CDMO performs <729> Method II, developing the dilution scheme is one of the most critical steps of method validation. Different fat concentrations (10%, 20%, 30% TPN emulsions) need different dilution factors to get below the coincidence limit. For routine release testing, the dilution scheme must be standardized and documented in an SOP, and the method validation report must include dilution linearity study data.

Method II: Procedure, Interpretation & PFAT5 Acceptance Criteria

Original Text

System Suitability (Method II)

Perform prior to the test procedure, using the Standard preparation of a 5- and 10-µm NIST-traceable particle. Measure in triplicate the number-weighted particle diameter and the counts/mL of the standard. The system is suitable when the triplicate mean number-weighted particle diameter measurements are within 10% of the target value, both in terms of repeatability (CV) and closeness to the certified size on the label of the NIST-traceable standard.

Procedure and Interpretation

Set the instrument threshold of detection at 1.8 µm, extended to an upper size limit of 50 µm, and vary the concentration and/or data collection times such that there is at least a factor of two in the difference of the total number of globules that measure >5 µm between at least two sample runs. In any case, the number of globules that measure >5 µm should be large enough so that it represents an adequate number of globules that are statistically representative of the large-diameter tail population of the native emulsion.

"The volume-weighted, large-diameter fat globule limits of the dispersed phase, expressed as the percentage of fat residing in globules larger than 5 µm (PFAT5) for a given lipid injectable emulsion, must not exceed 0.05%."

Most commonly used instruments provide calculation in software (HIAC Royco, PSS AccuSizer, Klotz). However, the general equation applied is based on the sum of the number of droplets per milliliter of sample per channel multiplied by the volume of a sphere of the geometric mean of each channel:

PFAT5 = Σ(#/mL/channel) × (π D³/6) for D > 5 µm

Key Concepts

What does PFAT5 ≤ 0.05% mean? In intuitive numbers: in 100 mL of fat emulsion, the total volume of all fat globules larger than 5 µm in diameter must not exceed 0.05 mL (50 µL). This limit was established on the basis of intravenous infusion safety research by ASHP/ASPEN (the American Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition and related bodies) and has been formally adopted by USP.

Volume-Weighted vs. Number-Weighted: PFAT5 is calculated on a volume-weighted basis because the mechanism of lung injury is directly related to the volume of the large fat globules (that is, the actual amount of fat), not just their number. One 20 µm globule has the volume of 64 globules of 5 µm (volume ∝ diameter³), so number weighting would seriously underestimate the risk.

Key Notes

The requirement that the >5 µm globule counts differ by no more than a factor of two between at least two samplings: This is a statistical adequacy requirement, ensuring that enough large particles are counted for the PFAT5 result to be statistically representative. If too few samplings are made, the result may lack reliability because of insufficient counts, even if every individual result meets ≤ 0.05%.

PVC/DEHP container prohibition: In a footnote, USP specifically warns that PVC tubing containing DEHP (di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate) has been shown to destroy the emulsion stability of fat emulsions. Type I glass containers must be used when preparing laboratory samples, to avoid false positives (falsely high PFAT5) caused by DEHP.

Practice Questions

  1. A fat emulsion batch has PFAT5 = 0.04% (within the criterion), but one very large 30 µm fat globule is found. Can the USP <729> calculation capture this kind of anomaly? Should this batch be released?
  2. Why does the system suitability requirement for Method II use 5 µm and 10 µm standards, rather than smaller standards (100–400 nm) as in Method I? What does this reflect about the technical differences between the two methods?
  3. A CDMO plans to use the same HIAC Royco instrument for both USP <788> (particulate matter in injections) and USP <729> (PFAT5 of fat emulsions). Is this technically feasible? What method transfer points need attention?

Practical Application

In CDMO manufacture of TPN (total parenteral nutrition) or lipid nanoparticles (LNP), the PFAT5 monitoring strategy should be integrated into process validation and continued process verification (CPV). A trending chart can detect process drift early, before PFAT5 exceeds the ≤ 0.05% criterion, giving CAPA more lead time. LNP vaccine manufacture (such as mRNA lipid nanoparticles) starts with a much smaller particle size (about 80–120 nm), but uncontrolled emulsification can also produce oversized aggregates, so the monitoring principles of <729> remain a useful reference.

Section Summary

  • Method I (DLS) acceptance criteria: MDD < 500 nm; system suitability CV ≤ 15%; χ² value within the instrument specification
  • Method II (LE/SPOS) acceptance criterion: PFAT5 ≤ 0.05% (volume-weighted; proportion of the total dispersed phase in globules >5 µm)
  • PFAT5 is calculated on a volume-weighted basis (∝ Di³) because this reflects the clinical safety risk of large particles more accurately than number weighting
  • Both methods must pass system suitability verification with NIST-traceable standards before samples can be analyzed
  • Cross-references: <429> (LD), <788> (particulate matter in injections); avoid PVC/DEHP containers in sample preparation
