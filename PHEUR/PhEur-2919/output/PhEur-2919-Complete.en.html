Overview, Applicability & Method 1 — Light Obscuration Particle Count Test

European Pharmacopoeia 11.0, Chapter 2.9.19 | Pharmacopoeial Harmonisation Chapter (ICH) | Pages 382–383

Original Text — English

Introduction & Scope

Particulate contamination of injections and infusions consists of mobile, undissolved particles, other than gas bubbles, that are unintentionally present. Particulate contamination may originate from various sources and is to be minimised, independent of its type. The level of particulate contamination in parenteral preparations must be controlled.

For the determination of particulate contamination, 2 procedures, Method 1 (light obscuration particle count test) and Method 2 (microscopic particle count test), are specified hereinafter. When examining injections and infusions for sub-visible particles, Method 1 is preferably applied. However, it may be necessary to test some preparations by the light obscuration particle count test followed by the microscopic particle count test to reach a conclusion on conformance to the requirements.

Not all parenteral preparations can be examined for sub-visible particles by one or both of these methods. When Method 1 is not applicable, e.g. in case of preparations having reduced clarity or increased viscosity, the test is carried out according to Method 2. Emulsions, colloids, and liposomal preparations are examples. Similarly, products that produce air or gas bubbles when drawn into the sensor may also require specific precautions during sample preparation and/or microscopic particle count testing. If the viscosity of the preparation to be tested is sufficiently high so as to preclude its examination by either test method, a quantitative dilution with an appropriate particle-free diluent may be made to decrease viscosity, as necessary, to allow the analysis to be performed.

The results obtained in examining a discrete unit or group of units for particulate contamination cannot be extrapolated with certainty to other units that remain untested. Thus, statistically sound sampling plans must be developed if valid inferences are to be drawn from observed data to characterise the level of particulate contamination in a large group of units.

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Introduction and Scope

Core Concepts

Sub-visible particles: mobile, undissolved particles with a size of 1–100 µm that cannot be seen directly by the naked eye but can be detected with optical instruments. Sources of particulate contamination in injections and infusions include crystallization of the active substance, glass delamination from the container, rubber stopper fragments, filter debris and airborne particles.

Choosing Between the Two Methods
  • Method 1 (light obscuration, LO): the preferred method; the instrument counts automatically and is efficient, but it requires a clear, low-viscosity liquid.
  • Method 2 (microscopic evaluation): the alternative method, used when Method 1 cannot be performed: turbid liquids (emulsions, colloids, liposomes), high-viscosity preparations and products that generate air bubbles.
  • Using both methods: if the Method 1 result exceeds the limit, Method 2 can be used for further confirmation (the two methods have different limits; see Section 2).
Statement on Sampling Representativeness

The chapter states explicitly that the particulate test result for one batch cannot be extrapolated to untested units. This reflects a basic statistical principle: particles are not distributed uniformly within a batch, so a sound statistical sampling plan is needed before valid conclusions can be drawn about the batch.

Original Text — English

Method 1Light Obscuration — Apparatus & Calibration

Use a suitable apparatus based on the principle of light blockage which allows an automatic determination of the size of particles and the number of particles according to size.

The apparatus is calibrated using suitable certified reference materials consisting of dispersions of spherical particles of known sizes between 10 µm and 25 µm. These standard particles are dispersed in particle-free water R. Care must be taken to avoid aggregation of particles during dispersion.

General Precautions

The test is carried out under conditions limiting particulate contamination, preferably in a laminar-flow cabinet.

Very carefully wash the glassware and filtration equipment used, except for the membrane filters, with a warm detergent solution and rinse with abundant amounts of water to remove all traces of detergent. Immediately before use, rinse the equipment from top to bottom, outside and then inside, with particle-free water R.

Take care not to introduce air bubbles into the preparation to be examined, especially when fractions of the preparation are being transferred to the container in which the determination is to be carried out.

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Method 1Light Obscuration: Instrument Principle and Calibration

Principle of the Light Obscuration Instrument

The core of a light obscuration (LO) instrument is a narrow flow channel through which the liquid passes, crossed by a narrow beam of light (usually a laser). When a particle passes through, it blocks part of the light, and the sensor detects the momentary drop in light intensity. From this the equivalent circular diameter (ECD) of the particle is derived and the particle is counted. Advantages: fast, automatable and highly reproducible. Disadvantages: it cannot distinguish particle shape, and the assumption of a spherical particle may overestimate the size of non-spherical particles.

Calibration Parameters
  • Calibrate with spherical particle reference standards of known size (10–25 µm).
  • The standards are dispersed in particle-free water R.
  • Avoid particle aggregation during dispersion; otherwise particle size will be misjudged.
Practical Points for CDMOs
  • LAF environment: particulate testing must be performed in a laminar air flow cabinet or an equivalent clean environment; otherwise particles in the surroundings will contaminate the sample.
  • Cleaning of glassware: wash repeatedly with a warm detergent solution and rinse thoroughly with purified water; immediately before use, rinse again with particle-free water, from the outside to the inside. Inadequate cleaning is one of the most common causes of a high particulate background.
  • Control of air bubbles: air bubbles are counted as particles by the LO instrument and lead to false positives. Handle the sample gently when withdrawing portions.

Environmental Suitability Check (Method 1 Blank)

In order to check that the environment is suitable for the test, that the glassware is properly cleaned and that the water to be used is particle-free, the following test is carried out: determine the particulate contamination of 5 samples of particle-free water R, each of 5 mL, according to the method described below.

If the number of particles of 10 µm or greater size exceeds 25 for the combined 25 mL, the precautions taken for the test are not sufficient. The preparatory steps must be repeated until the environment, glassware and water are suitable for the test.

Purpose of the Blank Check

This step is equivalent to a system suitability test (SST): it confirms that the test environment, the glassware and the water used for testing do not introduce background particles that interfere with the test. If the blank fails, the test data are invalid, and the environment and glassware must be cleaned again before retesting.

Comparison: Method 2 Blank Limit (Section 2)

For the Method 2 (microscopic evaluation) blank check, 50 mL of particle-free water is filtered and the particles are counted on the membrane. The limits are: ≥10 µm ≤ 20 particles and ≥25 µm ≤ 5 particles (over the filtration area). The two methods use different acceptance criteria because their principles differ.

Method 1 — Test Procedure

Mix the contents of the sample by slowly inverting the container 20 times successively. If necessary, cautiously remove the sealing closure. Clean the outer surfaces of the container opening using a jet of particle-free water R and remove the closure, avoiding any contamination of the contents. Eliminate gas bubbles by appropriate measures such as allowing the sample to stand for 2 min or sonicating.

For large-volume parenterals (LVP), single units are tested. For small-volume parenterals (SVP) less than 25 mL in volume, the contents of 10 or more units are combined in a cleaned container to obtain a volume of not less than 25 mL; where justified and authorised, the test solution may be prepared by mixing the contents of a suitable number of vials and diluting to 25 mL with particle-free water R or with an appropriate solvent without contamination of particles when particle-free water R is not suitable. Small-volume parenterals having a volume of 25 mL or more may be tested individually.

Powders for parenteral administration are reconstituted with particle-free water R or with an appropriate solvent without contamination of particles when particle-free water R is not suitable.

The number of test specimens must be adequate to provide a statistically sound assessment. For large-volume parenterals or for small-volume parenterals having a volume of 25 mL or more, fewer than 10 units may be tested, based on an appropriate sampling plan.

Remove 4 portions, each of not less than 5 mL, and count the number of particles equal to or greater than 10 µm and 25 µm. Disregard the result obtained for the first portion, and calculate the mean number of particles for the preparation to be examined.

Alternative Method (for Biological Preparations)

This method is intended to improve the applicability of the test to biological preparations. However, it can be used for any type of preparation.

Clean the outer surfaces of the container(s) using a jet of particle-free water R, avoiding any contamination of the contents. Samples are tested under in-use conditions, as directed in the instructions for use (e.g. expelled syringe contents).

For parenteral preparations that have a sufficient volume (i.e. a volume large enough to permit testing), testing of individual units is often preferred.

If the volume is not sufficient, take a suitable number of units, mix each one carefully and thoroughly and then combine the contents in a separate container to obtain the volume required for a single test, depending on the instrument capability and the properties of the sample.

Powders for parenteral administration are reconstituted with particle-free water R or with an appropriate particle-free solvent when particle-free water R is not suitable. Eliminate gas bubbles by appropriate measures such as allowing the sample to stand, applying a gentle vacuum or sonicating. Sonication of preparations containing proteins is not recommended.

Remove 4 approximately equal portions, typically 5 mL each, and count the number of particles equal to or greater than 10 µm and 25 µm. Disregard the result obtained for the first portion, and calculate the mean number of particles for the preparation to be examined. Volumes smaller than 5 mL can also be tested, provided that this amount is appropriately justified. In general, for parenteral preparations that do not have a sufficient volume (e.g. less than 25 mL), performing the test using a volume of 1 mL to 5 mL may be acceptable if permitted by the instrument.

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Method 1: Detailed Procedure

Sample Mixing Steps
  • Invert slowly 20 times: this suspends the particles evenly and avoids sampling bias caused by settling. Note that the inversion must be "slow", not vigorous shaking, which can generate air bubbles or break up particles.
  • Stand for 2 minutes or use ultrasound: to remove air bubbles. Air bubbles are the greatest source of interference in the LO method.
  • Rinse the container opening before opening it: to prevent contaminants on the outer surface from falling in.
Sampling rules for LVP vs SVP

LVP (>100 mL): test each container individually
SVP (<25 mL): pool ≥10 containers, total volume ≥25 mL
SVP (25 mL ≤ volume < 100 mL): may be tested individually

Note: powders for injection: reconstitute with particle-free water or a suitable solvent before testing
Four Portions, Discarding the First

In LO testing, four portions (each ≥5 mL) are withdrawn, but the data from the first portion are discarded. This is because the first portion may be affected by incomplete flushing of the tubing, which gives a high background. The mean of the remaining three portions is used as the final result, which improves accuracy.

Alternative Method (Suitable for Biologics)

The standard method requires mixing and sampling after opening the container. The alternative method instead simulates in-use conditions; for example, the contents of a prefilled syringe are expelled directly for testing, which better reflects the actual particulate exposure at the time of administration. For protein-containing biologics, ultrasound is not recommended for removing air bubbles, because it may cause protein aggregation or degradation.

Points to Note When Testing Biologics at a CDMO
  • Protein products (such as monoclonal antibodies, mAbs) usually use the alternative method: sampling under simulated in-use conditions.
  • If the volume is insufficient (for example, a small single-dose syringe), 1–5 mL may be used for testing, provided that the instrument supports it and there is a sound justification.
  • Emulsions and lipid preparations cannot be tested by the LO method (they obscure the light path) and require Method 2 (microscopic evaluation) or another special method.

Method 1: Evaluation & Acceptance Criteria

Evaluation (Method 1)

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of more than 100 mL, apply the criteria of test 1.A.

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of less than 100 mL, apply the criteria of test 1.B.

♦For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of 100 mL, apply the criteria of test 1.B.♦

If the average number of particles exceeds the limits, test the preparation by the microscopic particle count test (Method 2).

Test 1.A — LVP (>100 mL)

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed:

  • 25 per millilitre equal to or greater than 10 µm
  • 3 per millilitre equal to or greater than 25 µm

Test 1.B — SVP (<100 mL)

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed:

  • 6 000 per container equal to or greater than 10 µm
  • 600 per container equal to or greater than 25 µm

Method 1 Acceptance Criteria (with Comparison to Method 2)

Design Logic of the Limits: A 2:1 Ratio

The Method 1 limits are twice those of Method 2. This is a feature of the ICH-harmonized chapter and reflects the different physical principles of the two methods: the LO method has an optical magnification effect on particles (non-spherical particles may be overestimated), whereas the microscopic method relies on manual visual counting, which is more difficult to perform but gives more conservative counts. The 2:1 design makes the two methods functionally equivalent.

The ♦ Difference for 100 mL Containers

Ph.Eur. explicitly specifies that containers of exactly 100 mL are tested using Test 1.B (the SVP criteria). This is a difference between Ph.Eur. and ICH (marked with ♦) and must be kept in mind when writing validation protocols.

Method 1 Exceeds the Limit → Confirm with Method 2

If the Method 1 count exceeds the limit, the product is not immediately judged non-compliant; the result must first be confirmed by Method 2 (microscopic evaluation). Because the Method 2 limit is stricter (only half that of Method 1), this step in effect "gives a chance of recourse": false positives from the LO method (such as air bubbles and protein aggregates) can sometimes be identified and excluded under the microscope.

Original Text — English

Method 2Microscopic — Apparatus

Use a suitable binocular microscope, filter assembly for retaining particulate contamination and membrane filter for examination.

The microscope is equipped with an ocular micrometer calibrated with an objective micrometer, a mechanical stage capable of holding and traversing the entire filtration area of the membrane filter, 2 suitable illuminators to provide episcopic illumination in addition to oblique illumination, and is adjusted to 100 ± 10 magnifications.

The ocular micrometer is a circular diameter graticule (see Figure 2.9.19.-1.) and consists of a large circle divided by crosshairs into quadrants, transparent and black reference circles 10 µm and 25 µm in diameter at 100 magnifications, and a linear scale graduated in 10 µm increments. It is calibrated using a stage micrometer that is certified by either a domestic or an international standard institution. A relative error of the linear scale of the graticule within ± 2 per cent is acceptable. The large circle is designated the graticule field of view (GFOV).

2 illuminators are required. One is an episcopic brightfield illuminator internal to the microscope, the other is an external, focusable auxiliary illuminator adjustable to give reflected oblique illumination at an angle of 10–20°.

Filter Assembly

The filter assembly for retaining particulate contamination consists of a filter holder made of glass or other suitable material, and is equipped with a vacuum source and a suitable membrane filter.

The membrane filter is of suitable size, black or dark grey in colour, non-gridded or gridded, and 1.0 µm or finer in nominal pore size.

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Method 2Microscopic Evaluation: Instrument Requirements

Binocular Microscope Specifications
  • Magnification: 100 ± 10× (that is, 90–110× is acceptable).
  • Ocular micrometer: a built-in circular diameter graticule with transparent circles and black reference circles of 10 µm and 25 µm, used to measure white/transparent particles and dark particles respectively.
  • Mechanical stage: allows the whole filter area to be scanned horizontally so that no area is missed.
  • Relative error ≤ ±2%: the graticule must be calibrated periodically against a certified stage micrometer.
Principle of the Dual Light Source
  • Internal episcopic brightfield illumination: light from above makes particles appear dark against a white background, which suits viewing particle outlines.
  • External oblique illumination (10–20°): light from the side at a low angle makes particles cast shadows, adding depth and contrast, which is especially helpful for identifying translucent particles. Using the two light sources together reduces counting errors.
Filter Membrane Specifications
  • Colour: black or dark grey, so that white and transparent particles are easier to see against the dark background (optimum contrast).
  • Pore size: ≤1.0 µm, to ensure that all target particles ≥10 µm are fully retained on the membrane surface and do not pass through.
  • Gridded/non-gridded: a gridded membrane helps locate the scanning position and avoids double counting or missed areas.

General Precautions & Environmental Suitability (Method 2)

The test is carried out under conditions limiting particulate contamination, preferably in a laminar-flow cabinet.

Very carefully wash the glassware and filter assembly used, except for the membrane filter, with a warm detergent solution and rinse with abundant amounts of water to remove all traces of detergent. Immediately before use, rinse both sides of the membrane filter and the equipment from top to bottom, outside and then inside, with particle-free water R.

In order to check that the environment is suitable for the test, that the glassware and the membrane filter are properly cleaned and that the water to be used is particle-free, the following test is carried out: determine the particulate contamination of a 50 mL volume of particle-free water R according to the method described below. If more than 20 particles 10 µm or larger in size or if more than 5 particles 25 µm or larger in size are present within the filtration area, the precautions taken for the test are not sufficient. The preparatory steps must be repeated until the environment, glassware, membrane filter and water are suitable for the test.

Method 2 — Test Procedure

Mix the contents of the samples by slowly inverting the container 20 times successively. If necessary, cautiously remove the sealing closure. Clean the outer surfaces of the container opening using a jet of particle-free water R and remove the closure, avoiding any contamination of the contents.

For large-volume parenterals, single units are tested. For small-volume parenterals less than 25 mL in volume, the contents of 10 or more units are combined in a cleaned container; where justified and authorised, the test solution may be prepared by mixing the contents of a suitable number of vials and diluting to 25 mL with particle-free water R or with an appropriate solvent without contamination of particles when particle-free water R is not suitable. Small-volume parenterals having a volume of 25 mL or more may be tested individually.

Powders for parenteral administration are constituted with particle-free water R or with an appropriate solvent without contamination of particles when particle-free water R is not suitable.

The number of test specimens must be adequate to provide a statistically sound assessment. For large-volume parenterals or for small-volume parenterals having a volume of 25 mL or more, fewer than 10 units may be tested, based on an appropriate sampling plan.

Wet the inside of the filter holder fitted with the membrane filter with several millilitres of particle-free water R. Transfer to the filtration funnel the total volume of a solution pool or of a single unit, and apply vacuum. If needed, add stepwise a portion of the solution until the entire volume is filtered. After the last addition of solution, begin rinsing the inner walls of the filter holder by using a jet of particle-free water R. Maintain the vacuum until the surface of the membrane filter is free from liquid. Place the filter in a Petri dish and allow the filter to air-dry with the cover slightly ajar. After the filter has been dried, place the Petri dish on the stage of the microscope, scan the entire membrane filter under the reflected light from the illuminating device, and count the number of particles that are equal to or greater than 10 µm and the number of particles that are equal to or greater than 25 µm. Alternatively, partial filter count and determination of the total filter count by calculation is allowed. Calculate the mean number of particles for the preparation to be examined.

Blank Check and Method 2 Procedure

Method 2 blank limit (50 mL of particle-free water, filtered and counted)
Particles ≥10 µm: ≤ 20 (over the whole filter area)
Particles ≥25 µm: ≤ 5 (over the whole filter area)

Compare the Method 1 blank: 5 × 5 mL = 25 mL of water, total ≥10 µm ≤ 25
The blank check frameworks of the two methods differ and are not interchangeable.
Key Steps of the Method 2 Procedure
  1. Pre-wetting the membrane: before filtration, wet the membrane surface with a small amount of particle-free water to prevent uneven deposition of particles.
  2. Vacuum filtration: transfer all of the sample liquid into the filter funnel and apply vacuum; add the sample in portions until it has been completely filtered.
  3. Rinsing the filter wall: after the last portion has been added, rinse the inner wall of the filter with particle-free water to make sure that residual particles are washed onto the membrane.
  4. Drying: place the membrane in a Petri dish with the lid slightly ajar to let moisture escape, and air-dry it. Do not heat, to avoid melting or displacing particles.
  5. Full scan: scan the whole membrane under the microscope and count all particles ≥10 µm and ≥25 µm.
Partial Membrane Counting Option

If the sample volume is large or the particle density is too high (for example, a sample with a high particle concentration), counting only part of the membrane area is allowed, and the total count is then calculated proportionally (total filter count by calculation). The proportion of the membrane area scanned must be recorded.

Particle Sizing with Circular Diameter Graticule

The particle sizing process with the use of the circular diameter graticule is carried out by transforming mentally the image of each particle into a circle and then comparing it to the 10 µm and 25 µm graticule reference circles. Thereby the particles are not moved from their initial locations within the graticule field of view and are not superimposed on the reference circles for comparison. The inner diameter of the transparent graticule reference circles is used to size white and transparent particles, while dark particles are sized by using the outer diameter of the black opaque graticule reference circles.

In performing the microscopic particle count test do not attempt to size or enumerate amorphous, semi-liquid, or otherwise morphologically indistinct materials that have the appearance of a stain or discolouration on the membrane filter. These materials show little or no surface relief and present a gelatinous or film-like appearance. In such cases the interpretation of enumeration may be aided by testing a sample of the solution by the light obscuration particle count test.

Alternative Method (for Biological Preparations)

This method is intended to improve the applicability of the test to biological preparations. However, it can be used for any type of preparation.

Samples are tested under in-use conditions, as directed in the instructions for use (e.g. expelled syringe contents). For parenteral preparations that have a sufficient volume, testing of individual units is often preferred. Continue as described under Method 2 above, starting from "Wet the inside...".

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Determining Particle Size with the Circular Diameter Graticule

Principles for Measuring Particles (the Three "Don'ts")
  • Do not move the particle: leave the particle in place, mentally "convert" it into a circle by eye, and then compare it with the graticule.
  • Do not overlap the comparison: do not superimpose the particle image on the reference circle; compare them side by side in the same field of view.
  • Do not count material of indefinite shape: amorphous, semi-liquid or film-like material and stained patches are not included in the particle count.
Differences in Graticule Use for Transparent vs Dark Particles
Why Material of Indefinite Shape Is Not Counted

Film-like material (such as films of protein aggregates and flakes of silicone oil) appears "stained" or "gel-like" on the membrane, and its boundaries are hard to define; forcing a count would cause very large inter-operator variability. Ph.Eur. explicitly excludes such material but recommends using Method 1 (LO) as supporting confirmation, because LO is sensitive to particle size and can detect the total particle count even for particles of unusual morphology.

The Alternative Method (Biologics) Is the Same as for Method 1

The alternative method for Method 2 follows the same logic as that for Method 1: sample under "in-use conditions" and then complete the standard Method 2 filtration and counting procedure (starting from "Wet the inside..."). Protein preparations likewise avoid ultrasound.

Method 2 — Evaluation & Complete Acceptance Criteria Summary

Evaluation (Method 2)

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of more than 100 mL, apply the criteria of test 2.A.

For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of less than 100 mL, apply the criteria of test 2.B.

♦For preparations supplied in containers with a nominal volume of 100 mL, apply the criteria of test 2.B.♦

Test 2.A — LVP (>100 mL)

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed:

  • 12 per millilitre equal to or greater than 10 µm
  • 2 per millilitre equal to or greater than 25 µm

Test 2.B — SVP (<100 mL)

The preparation complies with the test if the average number of particles present in the units tested does not exceed:

  • 3 000 per container equal to or greater than 10 µm
  • 300 per container equal to or greater than 25 µm

Summary Table of Acceptance Criteria (with Method Selection Decision)

Decision Flow: How to Choose the Method
  1. Try Method 1 (LO) first: suitable for clear, low-viscosity liquids.
  2. Method 1 exceeds the limit → use Method 2 (microscopic evaluation) to confirm. The Method 2 limit is stricter, but it can identify false positives (such as protein films and traces of air bubbles).
  3. Method 1 not applicable (turbid liquids, emulsions, high viscosity) → use Method 2 directly.
  4. Neither method applicable (viscosity too high) → perform a quantitative dilution before testing, or use another validated method.
Comparison of Ph.Eur. 2.9.19 and USP <788>
  • Acceptance criteria are identical (a result of ICH harmonization): LVP ≤25/mL (≥10µm), SVP ≤6000/container (≥10µm) and the other limits are the same for both methods.
  • Differences: the Ph.Eur. ♦ symbol explicitly specifies that 100 mL containers follow Test B; USP <788> is accompanied by USP <787> (biotherapeutic proteins), which relaxes the counting requirements for protein aggregates.
  • In validating products for export to Europe, citing both Ph.Eur. 2.9.19 and USP <788> allows the same set of test data to be used, with mutual regulatory recognition.
Release Testing Strategy for CDMOs
  • Routine release: use Method 1 (LO), which is fast and suited to batch release testing.
  • OOS investigation: if the LO result exceeds the limit, first rule out air bubbles (prepare the sample again), then perform a confirmatory count by Method 2 and identify the particle morphology (glass fragments? rubber particles? fibres?).
  • Biologics (mAb/ADC/proteins): use the alternative method (in-use conditions) by preference and avoid ultrasound; consider micro-flow imaging (MFI) as an additional information tool (not an official method; for scientific understanding only).
