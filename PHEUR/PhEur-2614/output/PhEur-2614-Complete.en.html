Section 1: Introduction, Reagents, Endotoxin Limit & MVD

p1-p2

European Pharmacopoeia 11.0 — Chapter 2.6.14: Bacterial Endotoxins | p1–p2

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the biological principle of the Bacterial Endotoxins Test (BET): the clotting reaction between horseshoe crab lysate reagent and the endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria
  • Master the classification of the 6 test methods (A-F): the technical characteristics of gel-clot and photometric quantitative methods, and which method is the referee method
  • Be familiar with the reagents required: LAL reagent (amoebocyte lysate), Standard Endotoxin BRP, and the specification of water for BET
  • Be able to calculate the MVD (Maximum Valid Dilution) correctly and understand its central role in test design
Introduction & Methods Overview

Original Text

The test for bacterial endotoxins (BET) is used to detect or quantify endotoxins from gram-negative bacteria using amoebocyte lysate from the horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus or Tachypleus tridentatus). There are 3 techniques for this test:

  • the gel-clot technique, which is based on gel formation;
  • the turbidimetric technique, based on the development of turbidity after cleavage of an endogenous substrate;
  • the chromogenic technique, based on the development of colour after cleavage of a synthetic peptide-chromogen complex.

The following 6 methods are described in the present chapter:

Proceed by any of the 6 methods for the test. In the event of doubt or dispute, the final decision is made based upon method A unless otherwise indicated in the monograph.

The test is carried out in a manner that avoids endotoxin contamination.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why the horseshoe crab?

The blood of the horseshoe crab (pale blue, containing copper rather than iron) is extremely sensitive to the endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria. On contact with endotoxin, an enzyme cascade inside the amoebocytes (amoeba-like blood cells) triggers clotting. This is an innate immune mechanism that evolved over 400 million years.

LAL vs TAL：

  • Limulus polyphemus (American horseshoe crab) → LAL (Limulus Amebocyte Lysate)
  • Tachypleus tridentatus (Chinese/tri-spine horseshoe crab) → TAL (Tachypleus Amebocyte Lysate)

Both are accepted by Ph. Eur.; they differ only in geographic source and supply chain.

Method A as the referee method: Any method may be used for routine testing, but in case of dispute Method A (gel-clot limit test) prevails, because Method A was the first method established and its regulatory interpretation is the clearest.

Analogy

The lysate reagent works like a "natural alarm system": the endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria (LPS, lipopolysaccharide) is the "signal molecule" that triggers the alarm. Once contact occurs, the clotting reaction (gel formation) is the alarm sounding. The modern photometric quantitative methods (C/D/E/F) are like digitizing the alarm signal, so that it can be quantified rather than being just "yes/no".

Key Notes

Beta-glucan (β-glucan) interference: Conventional LAL reagent also reacts with certain β-glucans (such as fungal cell wall components and some excipients in intravenous infusions), causing false positives. Solution: use "G factor-free LAL" (lysate reagent with factor G removed), which detects endotoxin specifically and is not affected by β-glucans. This is particularly important when testing products containing β-glucans (such as certain biologic excipients).

1. Apparatus

Original Text

Depyrogenate all glassware and other heat-stable apparatus in a hot-air oven using a validated process. A commonly used minimum time and temperature is 30 min at 250 °C. If employing plastic apparatus, such as microplates and pipette tips for automatic pipetters, use apparatus shown to be free of detectable endotoxin and which does not interfere in the test.

NOTE: in this chapter, the term 'tube' includes all types of receptacles, for example microplate wells.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Why depyrogenation is necessary: If the test apparatus itself contains endotoxin, it interferes with the test result (false positives). Dry heat for glassware (250°C × 30 min) reliably destroys endotoxin (3-log reduction per USP/Ph. Eur. standards).

Plastic consumables: Microplates and pipette tips cannot withstand 250°C, so certified products validated as "endotoxin-free" must be purchased (for example pyrogen-free pipettes, usually stated on the CoA as <0.005 EU/mL).

Key Notes

Regulatory basis for dry-heat depyrogenation: 250°C × 30 min is the "minimum time-temperature" recognized by both Ph. Eur. and USP, corresponding to Fh ≥ 30 min (equivalent to ≥ 3-log reduction of endotoxin). Any other time-temperature combination must be validated. In FDA inspections, the depyrogenation cycle validation is an essential document for a hot air tunnel or oven.

2. Reagents & Test Solutions

Original Text

(1) Amoebocyte lysate
Amoebocyte lysate is a lyophilised product obtained from amoebocyte lysate from the horseshoe crab (Limulus polyphemus or Tachypleus tridentatus). This reagent refers only to a product manufactured in accordance with the regulations of the competent authority.

NOTE: amoebocyte lysate reacts with some β-glucans in addition to endotoxins. Amoebocyte lysate preparations which do not react with glucans are available (G factor-free preparations).

(2) Lysate solution
Dissolve amoebocyte lysate in water for BET or in a buffer, as recommended by the lysate manufacturer, by gentle stirring. Store the reconstituted lysate, refrigerated or frozen, as indicated by the manufacturer.

(3) Water for BET (water for bacterial endotoxins test)
Water for injections R or water produced by other procedures that shows no reaction with the lysate employed at the detection limit of the reagent.

3. Preparation of the Standard Endotoxin Stock Solution
The standard endotoxin stock solution is prepared from an endotoxin reference standard that has been calibrated against the International Standard, for example endotoxin standard BRP.
Endotoxin is expressed in International Units (IU). The equivalence in IU of the International Standard is stated by the World Health Organization.
NOTE: one International Unit (IU) of endotoxin is equal to one Endotoxin Unit (EU).

4. Preparation of Standard Endotoxin Solutions
After vigorously mixing the standard endotoxin stock solution, prepare appropriate serial dilutions using water for BET. Use the solutions as soon as possible to avoid loss of activity by adsorption.

5. Preparation of Test Solutions
Prepare the test solutions by dissolving or diluting active substances or medicinal products using water for BET. Adjust the pH of the test solution so that the pH of the mixture of the lysate and test solution falls within the pH range specified by the lysate manufacturer, usually 6.0 to 8.0. Buffers must be validated to be free of detectable endotoxin and interfering factors.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Reagent hierarchy (3 key reagents):

  • LAL reagent (amoebocyte lysate): A freeze-dried product, reconstituted with water for BET and stored according to the manufacturer's instructions (usually -20°C or 2–8°C).
  • Standard Endotoxin BRP: Calibrated by the European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & HealthCare (EDQM), 1 IU = 1 EU; used for the positive control and sensitivity confirmation in every test.
  • Water for BET: WFI quality, which must be shown to give no reaction at the detection limit of the reagent. It is the "blank background" of the whole test system.

IU vs EU: Ph. Eur. uses IU (International Unit) while USP traditionally uses EU (Endotoxin Unit). The two are equivalent (1 IU = 1 EU); only the name differs.

Key Notes

Adsorption loss when diluting standard endotoxin: Endotoxin molecules are negatively charged and readily adsorb onto glassware or plastic surfaces. After dilution, "the more dilute, the easier to lose", therefore:

  • Use the dilution "as soon as possible" after preparation
  • Mix "vigorously" before each dilution step
  • Use certified pyrogen-free, low-adsorption pipettes

Importance of pH 6.0–8.0: The enzyme activity of the LAL reaction is pH-sensitive. A pH that is too low or too high inhibits the reaction and leads to false negatives. Products containing acidic buffers (for example certain vitamin C injections, pH ≈ 5.5) must have their pH adjusted before BET.

Practical Application

When a CDMO QC laboratory establishes a BET method for a new product, "validation of the water for BET" is the first step: the intended WFI batch is tested to confirm that it gives no reaction at the lowest detection limit of the LAL. This result must be recorded and retained for audits, and must be reconfirmed after changing to a new WFI batch or a new water system.

6. Maximum Valid Dilution (MVD)

Original Text

The Maximum Valid Dilution (MVD) is the maximum allowable dilution of a sample at which the endotoxin limit can be determined. Determine the MVD using the following formula:

MVD = (Endotoxin limit × Concentration of test solution) / λ

Endotoxin limit: the endotoxin limit for active substances administered parenterally, defined on the basis of dose, is equal to:

Endotoxin limit (K) = K / M
  • K = threshold pyrogenic dose of endotoxin per kilogram of body mass
  • M = maximum recommended bolus dose of product per kilogram of body mass

When the product is to be injected at frequent intervals or infused continuously, M is the maximum total dose administered in a single hour period.

The endotoxin limit for active substances administered parenterally is specified in units such as IU/mL, IU/mg, IU/Unit of biological activity, etc., in monographs.

Concentration of test solution:

  • mg/mL if the endotoxin limit is specified by mass (IU/mg)
  • Units/mL if the endotoxin limit is specified by unit of biological activity (IU/Unit)
  • mL/mL if the endotoxin limit is specified by volume (IU/mL)

λ = the labelled lysate sensitivity in the gel-clot technique (IU/mL) or the lowest concentration used in the standard curve of the turbidimetric or chromogenic techniques.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Meaning of the MVD: The MVD is the "safety boundary" of test design. Dilution can remove interfering factors, but if the sample is diluted too far it becomes impossible to judge whether it meets the endotoxin limit. The MVD tells you that, provided this dilution factor is not exceeded, the result is meaningful.

The three key variables in the formula:

  • Endotoxin limit: Determined by the pharmacopoeial monograph or the K/M formula; a fixed value
  • Concentration: The actual concentration of the solution being tested (mg/mL, mL/mL, etc.)
  • λ (Lambda): The sensitivity of the LAL reagent, as labeled by the manufacturer; the higher the sensitivity (the smaller the λ), the larger the MVD

K/M formula (calculation of the limit):

  • K = 5 IU/kg (threshold pyrogenic dose for intravenous administration, as specified by Ph. Eur.)
  • M = maximum bolus dose in a single administration (mg/kg or mL/kg); for continuous infusion, take the maximum total dose in 1 hour
  • Limit = K/M, expressed in IU/mg or IU/mL

MVD Calculation Examples

[Scenario 1: Small-volume injection (aminoglycosides)]
  Product: Gentamicin injection, 20 mg/mL
  Maximum dose: 5 mg/kg/hour (IV infusion)
  K = 5 IU/kg
  M = 5 mg/kg → Endotoxin limit = K/M = 1 IU/mg
  Concentration = 20 mg/mL
  λ = 0.25 IU/mL (LAL reagent sensitivity)

  MVD = (1 IU/mg × 20 mg/mL) / 0.25 IU/mL = 80

  → The sample can be diluted up to 80-fold for testing

[Scenario 2: Effect of sensitivity on the MVD]
  Same product, using LAL with λ = 0.125 IU/mL:
  MVD = (1 × 20) / 0.125 = 160
  → More sensitive LAL → larger MVD → easier to overcome interference

[Origin of K = 5 IU/kg]
  Derived from the Rabbit Pyrogen Test,
  where 0.2 EU/kg is the minimum pyrogenic dose,
  multiplied by 25 (safety factor) = 5 IU/kg
  This value is adopted consistently by ICH, Ph. Eur. and USP

Key Notes

The MVD is an upper limit, not the target dilution: Many people misunderstand this as "dilute by the MVD", but the correct approach is to "find, within the MVD, the lowest dilution that eliminates the interference and gives a valid result". The more the sample is diluted, the lower the sensitivity and the easier it is to miss low levels of endotoxin.

Calculating M for "infusion products": For continuous infusion or high-frequency dosing (for example every 4 hours), M is taken as the "total dose in 1 hour", to avoid underestimating the dose given, which would lead to an overly lenient endotoxin limit.

Practice Questions

  1. For a 100 mL bag of 0.9% NaCl injection with a maximum infusion rate of 500 mL/hour, K = 5 IU/kg, body weight 70 kg and λ = 0.25 IU/mL, calculate the endotoxin limit and the MVD.
  2. Why must "water for BET" give "no reaction at the detection limit" of the LAL reagent? If the water for BET itself contains trace endotoxin, how does that affect the test result?
  3. The pharmacopoeial monograph for an active substance for injection specifies an endotoxin limit of 0.5 IU/mg. If the LAL sensitivity λ = 0.25 IU/mL and the test concentration is 2 mg/mL, calculate the MVD. If the test for interfering factors fails, what methods can be tried to eliminate the interference?

Section Summary

  • BET uses horseshoe crab lysate reagent (LAL/TAL) to detect the endotoxin of Gram-negative bacteria; of the 6 methods, Method A (gel-clot limit test) is the referee method.
  • β-glucan interference requires G factor-free LAL; standard endotoxin 1 IU = 1 EU, calibrated against the WHO International Standard.
  • Depyrogenation (250°C × 30 min) is the standard treatment for glassware; water for BET must be shown to give no reaction at the detection limit of the reagent; pH must be maintained at 6.0–8.0.
  • MVD = (Endotoxin limit × Concentration) / λ, the upper limit of the dilution design; the endotoxin limit is calculated from K/M (K = 5 IU/kg, M = maximum dose); the higher the sensitivity (the smaller the λ), the larger the MVD.

7. Gel-Clot Technique & Lysate Sensitivity Confirmation

Original Text

The gel-clot technique allows detection or quantification of endotoxins and is based on clotting of the lysate in the presence of endotoxins. The minimum concentration of endotoxins required to cause the lysate to clot under standard conditions is the labelled lysate sensitivity.

1. PREPARATORY TESTING
(i) Confirmation of the labelled lysate sensitivity

Confirm in 4 replicates the labelled sensitivity λ (expressed in IU/mL) of the lysate solution prior to use in the test. Confirmation is required when a new lot of lysate is used or when test conditions change.

Prepare standard solutions of at least 4 concentrations equivalent to 2λ, λ, 0.5λ and 0.25λ by diluting the standard endotoxin stock solution with water for BET.

Mix a volume of the lysate solution with an equal volume of 1 of the standard solutions (such as 0.1 mL aliquots) in each tube. Incubate the reaction mixture for a constant period according to the lysate manufacturer's recommendations (usually at 37 ± 1 °C for 60 ± 2 min), avoiding vibration.

Test the integrity of the gel: take each tube directly from the incubator and invert it through approximately 180° in one smooth motion. If a firm gel has formed that remains in place upon inversion, record the result as positive. A result is negative if an intact gel is not formed.

The test is considered valid when the lowest concentration of the standard solutions shows a negative result in all replicate tests.

The end-point is the lowest concentration in the series of decreasing concentrations of standard endotoxin that clots the lysate.

Determine the geometric mean end-point concentration:
Geometric mean = antilog(Σf / f) where Σf = sum of log₁₀ end-point concentrations, f = number of replicates.

The geometric mean end-point concentration is the measured sensitivity of the lysate solution (IU/mL). If this is not less than 0.5λ and not more than 2λ, the labelled sensitivity is confirmed.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Mechanism of gel formation:

Endotoxin (LPS) → activates Factor C in the LAL → activates Factor B → activates the clotting enzyme → cleavage of coagulogen → formation of insoluble coagulin gel.

Statistical logic of sensitivity confirmation:

  • 4 dilutions (2λ / λ / 0.5λ / 0.25λ) × 4 replicates = 16 tubes
  • Calculate the geometric mean end-point concentration for each series of 4 tubes
  • Acceptance criterion: the geometric mean lies between 0.5λ and 2λ (that is, within ±1 two-fold dilution step of the labeled sensitivity)

Analogy

Sensitivity confirmation is like "calibrating" a thermometer before use. You know the kit states λ = 0.25 IU/mL, but each lot of reagent may differ slightly. Running a sensitivity confirmation is like using ice water (0°C) and boiling water (100°C) to confirm that the thermometer is still within the correct range.

Reading the "180° tube inversion" is very intuitive: if the gel is solid like jelly, it is positive; if no gel has formed or the contents flow, it is negative.

Key Notes

Strictly avoid vibration: Any vibration during incubation (a passing trolley, centrifuge vibration) can break a gel that has already formed and cause a false negative. The BET area is usually placed in a vibration-free location or fitted with an anti-vibration table.

When to confirm sensitivity: It must be repeated for every new lot of LAL reagent. QC staff often overlook this requirement: changing the LAL lot is a significant "change in conditions".

Geometric mean vs arithmetic mean: The geometric mean is used because the dilution series is logarithmic (two-fold steps): the arithmetic mean in log space equals the geometric mean in original space, and better reflects the center point of the dilution series.

Geometric Mean Calculation

Sensitivity confirmation calculation example (λ = 0.25 IU/mL):

End-points of the 4 dilution series:
  Series 1: 0.25 IU/mL (= λ, lowest positive concentration)
  Series 2: 0.125 IU/mL (= 0.5λ)
  Series 3: 0.25 IU/mL
  Series 4: 0.25 IU/mL

Σ log₁₀ = log(0.25) + log(0.125) + log(0.25) + log(0.25)
         = (-0.602) + (-0.903) + (-0.602) + (-0.602)
         = -2.709
f = 4
Mean = -2.709 / 4 = -0.677
Geometric mean = 10^(-0.677) = 0.210 IU/mL

Conclusion: 0.5λ = 0.125 ≤ 0.210 ≤ 2λ = 0.50 ✓
→ Sensitivity confirmation passed
7.1 Test for Interfering Factors (Table 2.6.14-1)

Original Text

(ii) Test for interfering factors

Prepare solutions A, B, C and D as shown in Table 2.6.14.-1, and use the test solutions at a dilution less than the MVD, not containing any detectable endotoxins.

Table 2.6.14.-1

Legend:

  • Solution A = test solution, free of detectable endotoxins
  • Solution B = test for interference (test solution + known endotoxin)
  • Solution C = positive control (water for BET + known endotoxin)
  • Solution D = negative control (water for BET alone)

The test is considered valid when all replicates of solutions A and D show no reaction and the result of solution C confirms the labelled lysate sensitivity.

If the sensitivity of the lysate determined with solution B is not less than 0.5λ and not greater than 2λ, the test solution does not contain interfering factors.

If the preparation interferes at a dilution less than the MVD, repeat the test using a greater dilution (not exceeding MVD), or use a more sensitive lysate. Interference may also be overcome by filtration, neutralisation, dialysis or heat treatment.

To verify treatment effectiveness, repeat the test with endotoxin-spiked treated solution — confirming elimination without loss of endotoxins.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Design logic of the four solutions (ABCD framework):

  • A (test product): Confirms that the test product itself contains no detectable endotoxin (baseline)
  • B (test product + known endotoxin): The core solution: a known amount of endotoxin is added in the product matrix to test whether it is detected correctly, so as to judge whether there is interference
  • C (water for BET + known endotoxin): The control: confirms that the sensitivity of the reagent itself is normal (eliminates a variable)
  • D (water for BET, no endotoxin): The negative control: confirms that neither the water for BET nor the reagent gives any background signal

Intent of the acceptance criterion: If the geometric mean of B is within 0.5λ–2λ, the product matrix does not distort the LAL reaction (it neither inhibits nor enhances beyond the permitted range).

Key Notes

The informal "50%–200% recovery" description: A result for B between 0.5λ and 2λ is equivalent to saying that "50%–200% of the added endotoxin was recovered". Below 50% (inhibition) → the product contains components that inhibit the LAL reaction; above 200% (enhancement) → the product or its diluent itself contains substances that enhance the reaction (such as certain detergents).

Priority order of strategies to eliminate interference (CDMO practice):

  1. Further dilution (the simplest, as long as the MVD is not exceeded)
  2. Switch to a more sensitive LAL (smaller λ → larger MVD → more dilution possible)
  3. Filtration, neutralization, dialysis, heat treatment (it must be validated that the treatment itself does not cause loss of endotoxin)

Practical Application

Biologics with a high protein content (such as antibody injections) commonly show interference. Proteins may interfere with BET through the following mechanisms:

  • Protein binds endotoxin (LPS-binding protein) → masks the endotoxin → false negative
  • Protein precipitates at the bottom of the tube → obscures the reading of gel formation

Solutions: commonly a 1:10 dilution plus pH adjustment, or switching to rFC (recombinant Factor C) reagent, which is more tolerant of protein matrices and is not affected by β-glucan interference. The rFC method is also covered by Ph. Eur. chapter 2.6.32 (but chapter 2.6.14 itself does not include rFC).

7.2 Method A: Limit Test (Table 2.6.14-2)

Original Text

2. LIMIT TEST (METHOD A)

(i) Procedure
Prepare solutions A, B, C and D as shown in Table 2.6.14.-2.

Prepare solution A and solution B (positive product control) using a dilution not greater than the MVD. Solutions B and C (positive controls) contain the standard endotoxin at a concentration corresponding to twice the labelled lysate sensitivity (2λ). Solution D (negative control) consists of water for BET.

(ii) Interpretation

The test is considered valid when both replicates of solutions B and C are positive and those of D are negative.

  • When a negative result is found for both replicates of solution A → the preparation complies.
  • When a positive result is found for both replicates of solution A → the preparation does not comply.
  • When a discordant result (one positive, one negative) is found for solution A → repeat the test.

In the repeat test:

  • Both A replicates negative → complies
  • One or both A replicates positive → does not comply

If the preparation does not comply at a dilution less than the MVD, the test may be repeated using a greater dilution (not exceeding MVD).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The nature of Method A: a qualitative (pass/fail) test

Method A only answers the question "Does the endotoxin in the product exceed the limit?", judged by whether a gel appears when the sample is diluted to exactly λ (the concentration obtained by converting limit/MVD).

Simplified 4-tube design: Compared with the test for interfering factors, Method A is leaner:

  • A×2: test product (the main subject of the pass/fail decision)
  • B×2: test product spiked with 2λ endotoxin (confirms that the LAL still reacts in the test matrix)
  • C×2: water for BET spiked with 2λ (pure reagent control)
  • D×2: water for BET (negative control)

Key Notes

Handling of "discordant" results: If one A tube is positive and the other negative, the test must be repeated. This rule prevents a single test error from affecting the decision. In the repeat test, "any positive tube" means non-compliance: the criterion for the repeat is stricter because there was already doubt in the first test.

Why does the positive control (B) use 2λ rather than λ? Using 2λ (twice the sensitivity) ensures a "definite positive" result in the positive control, so that even if reagent activity fluctuates slightly it will not be wrongly read as negative.

Common CDMO pitfall: In batch release records, a BET result reported as "Negative (≤ endotoxin limit)" means a pass, but FDA investigators often ask for: the raw LAL test records (lot number / sensitivity / dilution calculation) and the validation records of the B/C/D tubes. Retaining only the "final conclusion" is not enough.

Analogy

Method A is like a pregnancy test: only two results, "positive/negative", quick and easy to read. Although simple, it carries the most credibility in regulatory disputes (referee status), like a court that trusts a unanimous jury verdict more than a complex statistical analysis.

Practice Questions

  1. In a BET of a batch of intravenous solution, the A tubes give "one positive, one negative", B/C are both positive and D is negative. What is the next step? What is the acceptance criterion for the repeat test?
  2. If both B tubes are negative, is the test valid? What problem does this indicate? How should it be handled?
  3. The results of both Method A and Method B can be used for release, so why is Method A the referee method?
7.3 Method B: Quantitative Test (Table 2.6.14-3)

Original Text

3. QUANTITATIVE TEST (METHOD B)

(i) Procedure
The test quantifies bacterial endotoxins in the test solution by titration to an end-point. Prepare solutions A, B, C and D as shown in Table 2.6.14.-3.

(ii) Calculation and interpretation

The test is considered valid when the following 3 conditions are met:

  1. both replicates of solution D (negative control) are negative
  2. both replicates of solution B (positive product control) are positive
  3. the geometric mean end-point concentration of solution C is in the range of 0.5λ to 2λ

To determine the endotoxin concentration of solution A, calculate the end-point concentration for each replicate by multiplying each end-point dilution factor by λ.

The endotoxin concentration in the test solution is the end-point concentration of the replicates. If the test is conducted with a diluted test solution, calculate the concentration in the original solution by multiplying the result by the dilution factor.

If none of the dilutions of the test solution is positive → report as less than λ (or less than the lowest dilution factor × λ).

If all dilutions are positive → report as equal to or greater than the largest dilution factor × λ.

The preparation meets the requirements if the endotoxin concentration in both replicates is less than that specified in the monograph.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The fundamental difference between Method B and Method A:

  • Method A: only asks "Does it exceed the limit?" → a single dilution factor (≤ MVD) → one pass/fail result
  • Method B: asks "How much endotoxin is there?" → serial dilution (1→2→4→8) → find the "critical dilution (end-point)" → calculate the concentration

Principle of end-point titration:

Dilution series: 1-fold, 2-fold, 4-fold, 8-fold. Find the "last dilution at which a gel can still form" (the end-point). End-point concentration = end-point dilution factor × λ.

Method B End-point Calculation

Calculation example:
  λ = 0.25 IU/mL
  The test solution is first diluted 10-fold before Method B

  4 tubes of A (dilution factors 1, 2, 4, 8):
    1-fold → positive (gel)
    2-fold → positive
    4-fold → negative  ← end-point lies after the 3rd tube
    8-fold → negative

  End-point = dilution factor of the last positive tube = 2
  End-point concentration = 2 × λ = 2 × 0.25 = 0.5 IU/mL

  Endotoxin content in the original solution = 0.5 × 10 (dilution factor) = 5 IU/mL

If "even the 1-fold is negative" (all negative):
  Report < λ = < 0.25 IU/mL (below the detection limit)

If "the 8-fold is still positive" (all positive):
  Report ≥ 8 × λ = ≥ 2 IU/mL (beyond the dilution range, result is truncated)

Key Notes

Meaning of the 3 validity conditions:

  • (a) D tubes negative → rules out background contamination
  • (b) B tubes positive → confirms that the LAL can still react in the matrix (same concept as the B tubes in the test for interfering factors)
  • (c) Geometric mean of C within 0.5λ–2λ → reagent sensitivity is normal

Use of Method B: Commonly used for "tracing the source of endotoxin" (root cause investigation) or for "establishing endotoxin content data for an active substance", rather than routine batch release (Method A is simpler and faster).

Practical Application

A CDMO usually applies Method A (pass/fail) to finished products, but uses Method B (quantitative) in supplier evaluation of active substances (API) or in deviation investigations, to determine:

  • The range of batch-to-batch variation in endotoxin content of the raw material
  • How much endotoxin is removed at each process step (validation of ultrafiltration / chromatography steps)
  • In a deviation investigation, the source and level of endotoxin in a suspect batch

Section Summary

  • Sensitivity confirmation (for every new LAL lot): 4 concentrations (2λ/λ/0.5λ/0.25λ) × 4 replicates, geometric mean within 0.5λ–2λ, 37±1°C, 60±2 min, avoid vibration.
  • Test for interfering factors (ABCD four-solution framework): the geometric mean of B (test product + known endotoxin) must be within 0.5λ–2λ; interference is eliminated in this order: dilution → switch to a more sensitive reagent → special treatment (must be validated not to lose endotoxin).
  • Method A (limit test): 4-tube design (A×2/B×2/C×2/D×2), pass/fail decision; a discordant result requires a repeat test, and after the repeat any positive means non-compliance.
  • Method B (quantitative test): serial dilution to find the end-point, end-point conc. = end-point dilution factor × λ, 3 validity conditions (D negative / B positive / C within 0.5λ–2λ); Method A remains the referee method.

  • Be familiar with the 3 validity conditions of the test and be able to interpret the final result correctly (mean < limit = pass)
8. Photometric Quantitative Techniques

Original Text

8. PHOTOMETRIC QUANTITATIVE TECHNIQUES (METHODS C, D, E AND F)

1. TURBIDIMETRIC TECHNIQUE (METHODS C AND F)

This technique is a photometric test to measure the increase in turbidity. Based on the test principle employed, this technique may be classified as:

  • The end-point-turbidimetric test (Method F): based on the quantitative relationship between the endotoxin concentration and the turbidity (absorbance or transmission) of the reaction mixture at the end of an incubation period.
  • The kinetic-turbidimetric test (Method C): measures either the time (onset time) needed for the reaction mixture to reach a predetermined absorbance or transmission, or the rate of turbidity development.

The test is carried out at the incubation temperature recommended by the lysate manufacturer (usually 37 ± 1 °C).

2. CHROMOGENIC TECHNIQUE (METHODS D AND E)

This technique is used to measure the chromophore released from a suitable chromogenic peptide by the reaction of endotoxins with the lysate.

  • The end-point-chromogenic test (Method E): based on the quantitative relationship between the endotoxin concentration and the quantity of chromophore released at the end of an incubation period.
  • The kinetic-chromogenic test (Method D): measures either the time (onset time) needed for the reaction mixture to reach a predetermined absorbance, or the rate of colour development.

The test is carried out at the incubation temperature recommended by the lysate manufacturer (usually 37 ± 1 °C).

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

End-point vs kinetic: Like two ways of measuring a runner's speed:

  • End-point: wait 60 minutes and see how far the runner has gone (measure distance)
  • Kinetic: calculate how many seconds it takes to run 50 meters (measure time), or calculate the speed

Advantages of kinetic methods: results are available earlier (no need to wait until the end of incubation), and they are more sensitive for low-concentration samples (because differences in reaction time are easier to detect than differences in end-point concentration).

Key Notes

Why are photometric techniques increasingly common?

  • Fully automated (automated microplate reader) → high throughput and better data integrity
  • Strong quantitative capability → endotoxin trends can be monitored, not just pass/fail
  • Kinetic methods of Methods C/D: a wide standard curve range (can cover 2–3 log₁₀), suitable for samples with large concentration variation
  • End-point methods of Methods E/F: simpler to perform, but a narrow standard curve range (usually 1 log₁₀)

Key Concepts

Classification matrix of the 6 methods:

Comparison of signal mechanisms:

  • Turbidimetric (C/F): coagulogen clots → the solution becomes turbid → absorbance ↑
  • Chromogenic (D/E): the clotting enzyme in the LAL cleaves the pNA chromogen (p-nitroaniline) → releases a yellow dye → absorbance at 405 nm ↑
8.3 Preparatory Testing for Photometric Techniques (Table 2.6.14-4)

Original Text

3. PREPARATORY TESTING

Preparatory tests are conducted to show that the criteria for the standard curve are satisfied and that the test solution does not interfere with the test. Validation of the test method is required when any changes are made to the experimental conditions.

(i) Assurance of criteria for the standard curve

Using the standard endotoxin solution, prepare at least 3 endotoxin concentrations within the range indicated by the lysate manufacturer to generate the standard curve. Perform the test using at least 3 replicates of each standard endotoxin solution.

If the desired range is greater than 2 log₁₀ in the kinetic methods, additional standards must be included to bracket each log₁₀ increase in the range.

The absolute value of the correlation coefficient |r| must be greater than or equal to 0.980, for the range of endotoxin concentrations set up.

(ii) Test for interfering factors

Select an endotoxin concentration at or near the middle of the standard curve.

Prepare solutions A, B, C and D as shown in Table 2.6.14.-4.

The test is considered valid when:

  • |r| of standard curve from solution C ≥ 0.980
  • Solution D ≤ blank value limit or < endotoxin detection limit

Calculate the mean recovery of the added endotoxin by subtracting the mean concentration in solution A from that in solution B.

The test solution is considered free of interfering factors if the measured concentration of endotoxin added to the test solution is within 50–200 per cent of the known added endotoxin concentration (after subtraction of background from solution A).

When the endotoxin recovery is out of the specified range, the test solution is considered to contain interfering factors. Repeat the test using a greater dilution (not exceeding MVD), or use validated treatment (filtration, neutralisation, dialysis or heat treatment).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

The requirement for additional points "per log₁₀" in kinetic methods (C/D): If the standard curve range exceeds 2 log₁₀ (for example 0.005–5 IU/mL = 3 log₁₀), the density of standard points must be increased for every additional log₁₀. This ensures that linearity is verified in every concentration interval of a wide-range curve, and extrapolation from a few points is not allowed.

Spiking design at the "middle of the standard curve": Spiking at the middle point of the curve reflects the true interference situation better than spiking at the highest or lowest point, and avoids edge effects distorting the interpretation.

Practical Application

When a CDMO uses a fully automated BET analyzer (such as the Charles River Endosafe-nexgen-MCS), the instrument has built-in standard curve management and |r| calculation, and the system determines validity automatically. This does not relieve QC staff of the responsibility to understand the principles: during FDA audits they are often asked "Why is the |r| this value? How do you ensure that the standard curve is still valid?"

Key Concepts

The standard curve requirement of |r| ≥ 0.980:

|r| is the absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient and reflects the degree of linear correlation between endotoxin concentration and photometric signal.

  • |r| = 1.000 → perfect linearity
  • |r| ≥ 0.980 → minimum permitted linearity (corresponds to R² ≥ 0.960)
  • |r| < 0.980 → the test is invalid and the standard curve must be re-established

Correspondence between interference testing for photometric and gel-clot techniques:

Essentially identical, only expressed differently. The gel-clot technique uses the "geometric mean sensitivity range", the photometric technique uses "percentage recovery".

8.4 Test Procedure & Interpretation of Photometric Techniques

Original Text

4. TEST

(i) Procedure
Follow the procedure described in 3. Preparatory testing, (ii) Test for interfering factors.

(ii) Calculation
Calculate the endotoxin concentration of each replicate of solution A using the standard curve generated by the positive control solution C.

The test is considered valid when the following 3 requirements are met:

  1. the results obtained with solution C comply with the requirements for validation defined under 3. Preparatory testing, (i) — Assurance of criteria for the standard curve (|r| ≥ 0.980);
  2. the endotoxin recovery, calculated from the endotoxin concentration found in solution B after subtracting the endotoxin concentration found in solution A, is within the range of 50–200 per cent;
  3. the result obtained with solution D (negative control) does not exceed the limit of the blank value required, or it is less than the endotoxin detection limit of the lysate reagent employed.

(iii) Interpretation
The preparation being examined complies with the test if the mean endotoxin concentration of the replicates of solution A, after correction for dilution and concentration, is less than the endotoxin limit for the product.

Guidelines on the test for bacterial endotoxins are given in general chapter 5.1.10.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The 3 validity conditions of the photometric test:

  1. C (standard curve) |r| ≥ 0.980: Confirms that the standard curve of this test is valid: "is the measuring scale itself accurate?"
  2. B (positive control) 50–200% recovery: Confirms that the test matrix does not interfere with the LAL reaction: "is the scale still accurate when used on this sample?"
  3. D (negative control) ≤ blank limit: Confirms that the background is not contaminated: "is the test environment clean?"

Correction for dilution and concentration: The photometric technique reads the endotoxin concentration of the diluted sample directly. It must be multiplied by the dilution factor (DF) to return to the concentration of the original sample, and then compared with the pharmacopoeial limit.

Final Result Calculation

Final result calculation flow for photometric techniques:

1. Read the endotoxin concentration of Solution A from the standard curve
   (calculate each of the two replicates and take the mean)
   Example: Solution A reading = 0.12 IU/mL (after 10-fold dilution)

2. Correct for the dilution factor
   Original sample concentration = 0.12 × 10 = 1.2 IU/mL

3. If the limit is expressed in IU/mg, convert
   Sample concentration = 20 mg/mL
   Reported value = 1.2 IU/mL ÷ 20 mg/mL = 0.06 IU/mg

4. Compare with the limit
   Limit = 0.5 IU/mg
   0.06 IU/mg < 0.5 IU/mg → PASS ✓

Recovery calculation (validity confirmation):
  Solution A reading: 0.12 IU/mL
  Solution B reading: 0.38 IU/mL (spiked with 0.25 IU/mL, mid-curve point)
  Recovery = (0.38 - 0.12) / 0.25 × 100% = 104% ✓
  104% is within the 50–200% range → no interference

Key Notes

Complementary roles of photometric and gel-clot techniques (basis for selection in a CDMO):

  • Batch release (routine): Either may be used. Choose a photometric technique (Methods C/D/E/F) where a high degree of automation is required; choose the gel-clot technique (Method A) where resources are limited or batch numbers are low.
  • Regulatory dispute: Method A is the referee method; if a photometric result is disputed, it must be confirmed with Method A.
  • Biologics (proteins / antibodies): Photometric techniques usually require more detailed interference testing; for some products the rFC method (Ph. Eur. 2.6.32) is more suitable.
  • Water system monitoring (WFI / purified water): Photometric techniques (automated) are suited to high-frequency monitoring.

Practical Application

Common clauses on the BET method in a CDMO's Quality Agreement with its client:

  • "In case of any dispute, the decision is made according to Method A": citing the referee clause of Ph. Eur. 2.6.14
  • "Records of method suitability testing are retained by the client, and must be repeated whenever the test conditions are changed"
  • "Photometric test records must include: the |r| value of the standard curve, the recovery of Solution B, and the blank value of D"

These three items have been cited many times in FDA 483s and Warning Letters as observations of incomplete BET records.

Section Summary

  • Photometric techniques are divided into turbidimetric (C/F) and chromogenic (D/E); kinetic methods (C/D) measure the "onset time", end-point methods (E/F) measure the "signal value after incubation"; kinetic methods have a wider range but need more standard points.
  • The standard curve requires |r| ≥ 0.980, at least 3 concentrations × 3 replicates; for kinetic methods exceeding 2 log₁₀, points must be added for each log₁₀.
  • The test for interfering factors uses "50–200% recovery" as the criterion, equivalent to "geometric mean within 0.5λ–2λ" for the gel-clot technique.
  • The test is valid only if all 3 validity conditions (|r| ≥ 0.980 / recovery 50–200% / D ≤ blank) are met; the result is judged as "mean of solution A (corrected for dilution) < limit → pass".
