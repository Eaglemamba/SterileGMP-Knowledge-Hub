General Introduction

Sterility Definition, SAL, Kinetics, Preference Hierarchy

Sterility Definition and SAL

Original Text

Sterility is the absence of viable micro-organisms. The test for sterility (2.6.1) cannot be used as the sole means of assuring sterility since the result is a function of the size of the sample tested, and it is unlikely that all units in a batch will be tested. The probability of any unit being non-sterile is expressed as the Sterility Assurance Level (SAL).

Sterility of any one item in a population cannot be guaranteed individually. The chosen sterilisation procedure must be studied and validated before use.

The SAL is defined as the probability of a single viable micro-organism occurring on an item after sterilisation. A commonly used SAL for sterile products is 10⁻⁶, meaning that there is less than a 1 in 1,000,000 chance that any item is non-sterile after sterilisation.

Inactivation of micro-organisms by physical or chemical sterilisation processes generally follows an exponential law. This means that when a population of micro-organisms is exposed to a sterilising agent, there is always a statistical probability that some micro-organisms may survive.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: SAL ≤10⁻⁶

SAL (sterility assurance level) expresses the probability of a viable microorganism being present in a sterilised product. SAL ≤10⁻⁶ means that the probability of a viable microorganism being present is no more than 1 in 1 million sterilised units.

Sterility Assurance Level (SAL) expresses the probability that a viable microorganism remains on a sterilised item. SAL ≤10⁻⁶ = no more than 1 non-sterile unit per 1,000,000 sterilised items.

Regulatory Critical Limit

SAL ≤ 10⁻⁶

Universal requirement for all terminal sterilisation methods

Exponential Inactivation Kinetics

Microbial inactivation follows an exponential law. This means that:

  • The probability of microbial survival is (theoretically) never zero; it can only approach zero
  • The higher the initial bioburden, the longer the exposure needed to reach the same SAL
  • SAL 10⁻⁶ is established from statistical models and validation data, not from test results on each individual product

Microbial inactivation follows an exponential law — survival probability never reaches exactly zero. Higher bioburden requires longer exposure to achieve the same SAL. SAL 10⁻⁶ is a statistical construct validated through process studies.

Analogy

It is like shining a light into a dark room: the darkness fades quickly once the light is on, but it never reaches "absolute zero". Each halving of the darkness corresponds to an exponential reduction in microorganisms. SAL is the standard that quantifies "how dark it still is".

Like shining light into a dark room: darkness diminishes exponentially with each doubling of light intensity but never reaches absolute zero. SAL quantifies "how dark" the remaining probability of contamination is.

Sterilisation Preference Hierarchy

Original Text

The method of preparation of sterile products shall be chosen according to the following preference:

  1. Terminal sterilisation in the final container by steam, dry heat, or ionising radiation, wherever possible. This enables parametric release.
  2. Aseptic assembly or filtration through a bacterial-retentive filter, when terminal sterilisation is not possible.
  3. Additional treatment (e.g. heating) in the final container where possible, when sterile filtration and/or aseptic processing must be used.

Parametric release — Release of a sterilised product based on process compliance data rather than end-product sterility testing — may be used when a fully validated terminal sterilisation method is applied, subject to competent authority approval.

Critical parameters affecting sterilisation efficacy: nature of the process, processing conditions (time, temperature, moisture), pre-sterilisation microbial contamination (bioburden), and product formulation (water activity Aw, pH, antimicrobial compounds, nutrients).

Tutorial Commentary

Regulatory Logic of the Hierarchy

Ph.Eur. 5.1.1 explicitly establishes a principle of "terminal sterilisation first", for the following reasons:

  • Terminal sterilisation: sterilisation is applied after the product has been packaged and sealed, when human intervention is finished, so the contamination risk is lowest. Parametric release can be applied, using process data in place of the end-product sterility test.
  • Aseptic assembly: every human intervention is a potential contamination event. Parametric release is not possible, and every batch must pass the sterility test (2.6.1).

The hierarchy prioritises terminal sterilisation because human intervention ends before sterilisation occurs — dramatically reducing contamination risk. Aseptic assembly requires every human intervention to be controlled and every batch to pass sterility testing.

Formulation Factors

  • Water activity (Aw): affects the efficacy of steam and dry heat sterilisation; products with low Aw (such as oily products) are more heat-resistant, but steam sterilisation is less effective for them
  • pH: affects the heat resistance of microorganisms; acidic or alkaline conditions can enhance heat sterilisation
  • Bacteriostatic components: preservatives (such as benzyl alcohol) can provide additional sterility assurance and reduce the required F₀
  • Bioburden: the lower the initial contamination level, the shorter the exposure needed to reach SAL 10⁻⁶

Parametric Release

Definition: release based on process data (time, temperature, F₀) from a fully validated sterilisation process, rather than on end-product sterility test results.

Prerequisite: applicable only to fully validated terminal sterilisation (steam, dry heat, radiation) and requires approval by the Competent Authority.

Parametric Release: Release based on real-time process monitoring data (F₀, temperature, pressure) rather than post-production sterility testing. Requires fully validated terminal sterilisation + regulatory authority approval.

Steam Sterilisation

Principle, Reference Cycle, F₀, F_bio Formula, Equipment, Routine Control

Principle

Original Text

Steam sterilisation is based on the transfer of heat by condensation of saturated steam on the surfaces of the items being sterilised. The hydrating effect of the condensate adds to the sterilising effect. Items must be fully penetrated by saturated steam, free of air and non-condensable gases.

For closed containers: the chamber acts as a steam jacket; sterile conditions must be achieved inside the closed containers — both the product itself and the headspace.

Equipment: Autoclaves — pressure vessels designed to admit or generate steam continuously, remove condensate, maintain controlled pressure and temperature. At least 1 independent thermal probe at the slowest-to-heat position or slowest-to-heat closed container.

Air removal methods:

  • Gravity displacement: items designed to allow natural air removal; no inaccessible air pockets permitted.
  • Vacuum cycles + steam pulses: ensure items not affected by evacuation.
  • Pressure-sensitive products: steam-air mixtures balance pressure inside closed containers.
  • Porous loads/hollow bodies: verified by physical/chemical and biological indicators (5.1.2).

Tutorial Commentary

Heat Transfer by Steam Condensation

When saturated steam contacts a cold surface it condenses, releasing latent heat of condensation of about 2257 kJ/kg (at 100°C), more than 5 times that of hot air. This is why steam transfers heat to the product more efficiently than dry heat.

Saturated steam releases its latent heat of condensation (~2257 kJ/kg at 100°C) upon contact with cooler surfaces — far more efficient than dry heat convection. This is why steam sterilisation is preferred when products can tolerate moisture.

Why Air Removal is Critical

If air (a non-condensable gas) remains in the autoclave chamber, it will:

  • Form "cold spots": the air layer blocks contact between steam and the surface of the load
  • Give normal pressure gauge readings while the temperature may be below the requirement (because air occupies chamber space)
  • Invalidate the sterilisation cycle and possibly lead to insufficient F₀

Residual air creates cold spots — air layers prevent steam contact with item surfaces. Pressure may appear normal but temperature at the cold spot may be below spec. This is why air removal validation (e.g. Bowie-Dick test for porous loads) is mandatory.

Closed Container Sterilisation

For sealed containers (such as glass ampoules and vials), steam cannot enter the container directly, so it is necessary to:

  • Bring the container contents to sterilisation temperature by heat conduction
  • Confirm that the required F₀ is achieved at the slowest-to-heat position
  • Use a steam-air mixture or superheated water spray to balance the pressure inside and outside the containers and prevent container breakage
Reference Cycle and F₀ Concept

Original Text

The reference sterilisation cycle is 121°C for 15 minutes in saturated steam at the coldest position. Alternative time/temperature combinations may be adopted with adequate validation.

Minimum acceptable temperature: 110°C

Minimum F₀ at the slowest-to-heat position: not less than 8 minutes

The F₀ concept (general chapter 5.1.5): F₀ is the calculated sterilisation effectiveness in equivalent minutes at a reference temperature of 121°C with a z-value of 10°C.

F_bio calculation:

F_bio = D₁₂₁ × (log₁₀ N₀ − log₁₀ N)
  • D₁₂₁ = D-value of biological indicator at 121°C (minutes to achieve 1-log reduction)
  • N₀ = initial viable count of biological indicator spores
  • N = number of survivors after exposure

F_phys (physical F₀) is correlated with F_bio. F_bio is determined at the most-difficult-to-sterilise position to define parameters that achieve SAL ≤10⁻⁶.

Tutorial Commentary

Steam Sterilisation Critical Limits

F₀ ≥ 8 min (regulatory minimum) Minimum temperature 110°C Reference cycle: 121°C / 15 min

F_bio Formula Explained

F_bio is the biologically equivalent sterilisation time based on a biological indicator (BI). The elements of the formula:

  • D₁₂₁: the time (in minutes) at 121°C required to reduce the spore count by 1 log (90%). The D₁₂₁ of a standard BI is typically 1.5–2.5 min.
  • log₁₀ N₀ − log₁₀ N: the log reduction in spore count from the initial value, i.e. the "effective number of log kills" of the sterilisation cycle.
  • To reach SAL 10⁻⁶ with an initial spore count N₀ = 10⁶, N = 1 survivor is required, so the log reduction = 6.
Example:
  D₁₂₁ = 2.0 min
  N₀ = 10⁶ (initial spore count)
  Target SAL: 10⁻⁶ → log reduction = 12
  F_bio = 2.0 × (6 − (−6)) = 2.0 × 12 = 24 min
  → at least 24 min of equivalent exposure is required

Industry F₀ Practice

Regulatory floor: F₀ ≥8 min (Ph.Eur., at the slowest-to-heat position)

Industry convention: most processes are designed for F₀ ≥15–25 min (121°C)

Two validation strategies:

  • Overkill approach: F₀ ≥12 min (for the most heat-resistant BI), independent of bioburden data; conservative but simple
  • Bioburden-based approach: calculates the required F₀ from the actual bioburden level, allowing a shorter exposure time but requiring strict bioburden monitoring
Routine Control

Routine Monitoring Requirements for Steam Sterilisation

  • Record the chamber pressure and the temperature profile at the coldest position (Pressure and Temperature Profile at Coldest Position) for every cycle
  • Record the sterilisation time (exposure time)
  • Calculate and record the F₀ value (where possible)
  • Chemical indicators to confirm steam penetration
  • Biological indicators (5.1.2) for periodic verification (not every cycle)
  • Closed containers: confirm that the temperature at the slowest-to-heat container position meets the requirement

Dry Heat Sterilisation

Principle, Reference Conditions, Depyrogenation, Equipment, Routine Control

Principle and Equipment

Original Text

Principle: Terminal sterilisation by transfer of heat via convection, radiation, or direct transfer (conduction).

Equipment: Oven with forced air circulation (for batch processing), or tunnel (continuous depyrogenation of glass vials). Temperature-sensing elements at the coolest part of the loaded steriliser.

Reference sterilisation conditions: 160°C for at least 2 hours. Other time/temperature combinations are allowed if adequately validated. SAL ≤10⁻⁶ required.

Validation uses temperature mapping + biological indicator studies (5.1.2) to demonstrate uniform heat distribution and adequate microbial kill.

Depyrogenation: Dry heat at >220°C (validated time) is frequently used for depyrogenation of glassware and other heat-stable materials.

Validation criterion for depyrogenation: 3 log₁₀ reduction in heat-resistant endotoxin. Biological indicators are not required for depyrogenation validation — the endotoxin challenge test is the measure.

Routine control: Temperature profiles at the coldest position. Time and temperature recorded for each cycle.

Tutorial Commentary

Dry Heat vs Steam: When to Choose Dry Heat?

  • Products that cannot tolerate steam and moisture: oily products, powders, glassware, metal instruments
  • Where depyrogenation is needed: glass containers for injectables (vials, ampoules) require depyrogenation before filling
  • Poor heat transfer: dry heat is far less efficient than steam at killing microorganisms, so a higher temperature (160°C vs 121°C) or a longer time is needed

Dry Heat Critical Limits

Sterilisation: 160°C / ≥2 h Depyrogenation: >220°C (validated time) Depyrogenation validation: 3 log₁₀ endotoxin reduction

Depyrogenation: Special Notes

Pyrogens are mainly bacterial endotoxins, the lipopolysaccharides (LPS) of the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria, which are strongly pyrogenic.

3 log₁₀ reduction: the acceptance criterion is a reduction of at least 1000-fold (3 log) in a known quantity of heat-resistant endotoxin challenge.

No biological indicator is used: depyrogenation validation does not use a BI; instead it uses an endotoxin standard of a defined concentration as the challenge, and the residual endotoxin after the process is quantified by the LAL or rFC method.

Note: the depyrogenation conditions (>220°C) are far above the sterilisation conditions (160°C), so sterilisation is in effect achieved at the same time, but the target of the process is the removal of endotoxin.

Depyrogenation Tunnel

Modern aseptic pharmaceutical plants make extensive use of continuous tunnel ovens (depyrogenation tunnels): glass vials enter at one end, are depyrogenated in the high-temperature zone (>300°C), and leave at the other end in a sterile state and enter the Grade A filling area.

The challenge in tunnel validation: ensure that the combination of conveyor speed and heating zone temperature still achieves a 3 log₁₀ endotoxin reduction under worst-case conditions.

Ionising Radiation Sterilisation

Three Types, 25 kGy, Dosimetry, Routine Control

Original Text

Principle: Exposure to ionising radiation. Three types:

  • Gamma rays — from cobalt-60 (Co-60) isotopic source; deep penetration; batch or continuous mode
  • Electron beam (e-beam) — from electron accelerator; faster but lower penetration than gamma
  • X-rays — from electron bombardment of a suitable target; intermediate penetration
  • Low-energy electrons: surface sterilisation of materials entering isolators

Uses: Terminal sterilisation of finished dosage forms, microbial inactivation of tissues/cells, sterilisation of materials/containers for aseptic processing.

Reference absorbed dose: 25 kGy. Other doses may be used if adequately validated to achieve SAL ≤10⁻⁶.

Biological indicators may be required for tissue/cell products or products with potential to prevent spore inactivation.

Routine control: Dosimetry system traceable to national standards monitors sterilisation dose during process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Value for Ionising Radiation

25 kGy (reference dose)

The actual dose may be adjusted based on bioburden validation (for example 15 kGy or 35 kGy), but complete validation data demonstrating SAL ≤10⁻⁶ are required

The Importance of Dosimetry

Unlike steam sterilisation, where temperature and pressure allow real-time monitoring of sterilisation efficacy, radiation sterilisation cannot be monitored this way. Dosimetry is the only means of process control:

  • Place dosimeters at the position of the product that is hardest to penetrate
  • The dosimetry system must be traceable to national standards
  • Confirm that the minimum dose is ≥ the dose required by validation, and that the maximum dose does not exceed the upper tolerance limit of the product

Why Doesn't Radiation Sterilisation Rely on BIs?

Steam sterilisation uses BIs to confirm heat penetration; radiation sterilisation uses dosimeters (physical measurement) to measure directly the radiation energy actually received by the product, which is more direct and more reliable than a BI. In radiation sterilisation, BIs are used mainly for special products (tissue/cell products) or as an additional validation tool.

Gas / Vapour Phase Sterilisation

Two Agent Classes, Three Phases, Critical Parameters, Residuals

Original Text

Principle: Gas sterilisation of surfaces — used for primary packaging materials, equipment, and some pharmaceuticals. Gas and moisture must penetrate into material. Followed by aeration to eliminate gas residues below toxic concentration thresholds.

Two categories of gaseous sterilising agents:

  1. Alkylating agents: Highly reactive; interact with amino, sulfhydryl, hydroxyl groups in proteins and purine bases in nucleic acids.
    Principal example: Ethylene oxide (EtO) — alkylating agent associated with cytotoxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic effects.
  2. Oxidising agents: Highly reactive, toxic.
    Current examples: Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and peracetic acid (PAA).

Process development and validation — 3 phases:

  1. (Pre)conditioning: Temperature and humidity equilibration of load
  2. Sterilisation: Exposure to gas at defined concentration, temperature, humidity, and time
  3. Aeration: Removal of gas residues to below toxic concentration thresholds

Critical parameters: Gas concentration, temperature, humidity, exposure time, load configuration/characteristics, product and packaging characteristics.

Any change in parameters must be investigated. Biological indicators must be at least as resistant as the microbiological contaminants of the product. Relevant cycle parameters + BI results recorded.

Tutorial Commentary

EtO Safety Alert

Ph.Eur. 5.1.1 explicitly identifies EtO as an alkylating agent that is cytotoxic, carcinogenic and mutagenic. Therefore:

  • Adequate aeration is essential, to ensure that residual EtO and its by-products (ethylene chlorohydrin, ethylene glycol) are below toxicity thresholds
  • Residual levels must meet the limits of relevant standards such as ISO 10993-7
  • EU environmental regulations place strict limits on EtO emissions, which has driven the wider adoption of hydrogen peroxide vapour (VHP)

Use of VHP (Vaporised Hydrogen Peroxide) in Aseptic Manufacturing

VHP (Vaporised Hydrogen Peroxide) has become one of the most important gas sterilisation technologies in modern aseptic pharmaceutical plants. Its main uses are:

  • Biodecontamination of isolators
  • Decontamination of RABS (restricted access barrier systems)
  • Surface decontamination of sterile packaging materials transferred into Grade A areas
  • In-situ decontamination of equipment surfaces

Advantages of VHP: it decomposes into only water and oxygen and is environmentally friendly; cycle times are short; and material compatibility is broad.

Common Misconception

  • Wrong: EtO sterilisation is the same as terminal sterilisation
  • Correct: EtO is a gas sterilisation method used mainly for surface sterilisation; it is not the "sterilisation in the final container" preferred by Ph.Eur. EtO cannot be used for the terminal sterilisation of liquid products.

Membrane Filtration

Removal Mechanism, 0.22 µm, Integrity Tests, Sterilising Filter, 7 Routine Parameters

Original Text

Principle: Used for reduction of viable and non-viable particles in gases and fluid products not amenable to sterilisation by heat or irradiation. Mechanism: removal (not inactivation) of microorganisms — by combination of sieving and surface interaction.

Equipment: Flat stock (discs) in holders, or cartridges. Pore size ratings based on correlation between microbial retention and diffusion characteristics or bubble-point measurement.

Integrity test procedures:

  • Diffusive flow measurement (Forward Flow)
  • Bubble-point determination
  • Water-intrusion testing

Chemical and physical compatibility of membranes with product demonstrated in development.

Filtration process: Product passes through microporous membrane with nominal pore size ≤0.22 µm. Pre-sterilisation microbial contamination determined for each batch.

Multiple filters: Where multiple bioburden-reduction filters are used, the filter closest to the filling point in the final container = the sterilising filter.

Filtration should be carried out as close as possible to the filling point to minimise contamination risk after filtration.

Routine control parameters (7 items):

  1. Pre-sterilisation microbial contamination (Bioburden)
  2. Pre-filtration integrity test results
  3. Duration of filtration
  4. Volume filtered
  5. Differential pressure across filter
  6. Post-filtration integrity test results
  7. Sterilisation of membrane filters (off-line or in-line steam)

Tutorial Commentary

What "Removal" Rather Than "Inactivation" Means for Filtration

The sterilising mechanism of membrane filtration is physical removal, not the "inactivation" achieved by steam, dry heat or radiation. This means that:

  • The microorganisms are still viable; they are merely retained on the upstream face of the membrane
  • Once the membrane integrity is compromised (for example by a pinhole), microorganisms can pass straight through
  • Pre- and post-filtration integrity tests are the only way to confirm that "this filtration was actually effective"
  • The filtered product must not come into contact with a non-sterile environment again (it must be handled immediately under Grade A conditions)

Key Specifications of Membrane Filtration

Nominal pore size ≤ 0.22 µm

This pore size is set on the basis of validated retention of the smallest known bacterium (Brevundimonas diminuta)

The 7 Routine Monitoring Points for Filtration Sterilisation

  1. Pre-filtration bioburden: confirm the microbial contamination level before filtration for each batch (affects the SAL calculation)
  2. Pre-filtration integrity test: confirm the filter is intact (a prerequisite for effective filtration)
  3. Filtration duration: prolonged filtration may lead to membrane fouling or a risk of microbial grow-through
  4. Filtration volume: a large volume may exceed the capacity of the filter and affect retention efficiency
  5. Differential pressure: an abnormal pressure rise may indicate membrane blockage or damage
  6. Post-filtration integrity test: the most critical confirmation step, confirming that the filtration of this batch was effective
  7. Filter sterilisation (in-line or off-line steam): records of the sterilisation treatment before use

Aseptic Assembly

Definition, Scope, Critical Factors, Media Fill, Sterility Testing of Each Batch

Original Text

Principle: Maintain sterility of a product assembled from components each sterilised by one of the above methods. Uses conditions and facilities designed to prevent microbial contamination.

Aseptic processing includes:

  • Aseptic filling into container/closure systems
  • Freeze-drying under aseptic conditions (aseptic lyophilisation)
  • Aseptic blending of formulations followed by aseptic filling
  • Aseptic packaging

Development and validation — Critical factors:

  • Environment (Grade A filling zone, Grade B background)
  • Personnel (gowning, training, behaviour)
  • Critical surfaces (all surfaces contacting product/container)
  • Container/closure sterilisation and transfer procedures
  • Maximum holding period before filling (established by hold-time studies)

Process validation: Includes appropriate checks + regular process simulation tests using microbial growth media (media fill tests), incubated and examined for contamination.

Each batch of aseptically processed product tested for sterility (2.6.1).

Tutorial Commentary

Aseptic Assembly vs Terminal Sterilisation: The Core Risk Difference

The essence of aseptic assembly is to "maintain" an existing sterile state, whereas terminal sterilisation "creates" a sterile state. This fundamental difference means that:

  • Every human intervention is a contamination risk event: personnel are the greatest source of contamination, and every action in the Grade A area is a potential risk
  • Parametric release is not possible: every batch must pass the Ph.Eur. 2.6.1 sterility test
  • Media fill is the core of validation and monitoring

Media Fill (Process Simulation Test) in Detail

Purpose: to simulate the complete filling operation using microbial growth medium (usually tryptic soy broth, TSB) in place of the actual product, in order to verify that the process does not introduce microbial contamination under normal and challenging conditions.

Acceptance criteria (based on PIC/S Annex 1, 2022):

  • Units filled <5000: no contamination (0 contaminated units)
  • Units filled 5000–10000: 1 contaminated unit requires investigation; 2 require a repeat
  • Units filled >10000: 1 contaminated unit requires investigation; 2 require a repeat

Frequency: typically 3 runs for initial validation; thereafter at least once every six months per shift/product line.

Contamination Control Technologies in Modern Aseptic Assembly

  • Isolator: completely separates personnel from the sealed enclosure, greatly reducing the risk of contamination by personnel; requires VHP biodecontamination validation
  • RABS (restricted access barrier system): physical barrier plus positive pressure protection, giving better separation of personnel than a conventional laminar flow workstation
  • Continuous environmental monitoring: online particle counters plus microbiological samplers detect abnormalities in real time
  • Automated filling robots: reduce the number of human interventions and the probability of contamination

GMP Context

Terminal Sterilisation Preference, Parametric Release, F₀, Cross-references, Audit Readiness

Why Terminal Sterilisation is Preferred

Regulatory Position

"Terminal sterilisation gives the greatest assurance of sterility. If a product can be terminally sterilised, it should be. Aseptic processing should only be used when terminal sterilisation is not possible."
— PIC/S Annex 1 (2022) core principle

Reasons why terminal sterilisation is preferred:

  • Human intervention is finished: the product is sterilised in its sealed final container and does not need to be opened afterwards, so the risk of contamination by personnel is zero
  • Parametric release is possible: physical process data such as F₀, temperature and pressure confirm sterilisation efficacy in real time, without waiting for the end-product sterility test result (14 days)
  • Consistent regulatory preference: FDA, EMA, PIC/S and WHO all express this preference explicitly in their guidance
  • Lower overall process risk: environmental monitoring requirements are relatively simple, and the complex environmental controls of Grade A/B are not needed

Parametric Release Practice

Three Prerequisites for Parametric Release

  1. A fully validated terminal sterilisation method: steam (usually by F₀ calculation), dry heat (time-temperature profile) or radiation (dosimetry)
  2. Competent Authority approval: a formal application must be made to and approved by the EMA, FDA or national regulatory authority, as part of the marketing authorisation (MA) of the medicinal product
  3. A robust real-time monitoring system: the process data of every cycle must be recorded automatically, calculated and compared with predefined acceptance criteria
F₀ Decision Framework

Comparison of F₀ Strategies

Key F₀ Values

≥8 min: Ph.Eur. regulatory minimum ≥15–25 min: industry convention target ≥12 min: minimum requirement for the overkill approach (for a standard BI)

Practical Considerations

F₀ Decision Process in CDMO Process Development

  1. Assess the thermal stability of the product: degradation kinetics studies (Arrhenius model) confirm the maximum heat exposure the product can tolerate
  2. Select the validation strategy: for heat-resistant products, overkill is preferred; for heat-sensitive products, consider the bioburden-based approach
  3. Design the worst case: the largest container, the fullest fill volume and the coldest position serve as the validation basis
  4. Confirm the monitoring plan: ensure the calibration status of the F₀ calculation system for every cycle
  5. Establish bioburden limits: even when the overkill approach is used, an alert limit still needs to be set

An Intuitive Understanding of F₀

F₀ is an "equivalent time": however complex the actual temperature profile, F₀ converts the whole process into "the equivalent number of minutes of exposure to saturated steam at 121°C".

The F₀ value "discounts" the sterilising effect at every temperature point into equivalent minutes at 121°C, and is the common currency for assessing the effectiveness of a steam sterilisation cycle.

Key Cross-References
Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

Common Audit Findings / Common Learning Misunderstandings

  • [Mistake 1] Treating filtration sterilisation as "terminal sterilisation": membrane filtration is not terminal sterilisation; after filtration the product must still be handled under aseptic conditions, and every batch must pass the sterility test (2.6.1).
  • [Mistake 2] Relying solely on the end-product sterility test as the basis for release: Ph.Eur. 5.1.1 states clearly that sterility assurance comes from validation of the whole process, not from a single end-product test.
  • [Mistake 3] Confusing EtO (gas) sterilisation with sterilisation in the final container: EtO is a surface gas sterilisation method used for packaging materials and equipment and is not suitable for the terminal sterilisation of liquid products.
  • [Mistake 4] Ignoring the post-filtration integrity test: the pre-filtration integrity test confirms that the "starting point is acceptable"; the post-filtration test confirms that "the filtration of this batch was effective"; neither can be omitted.
  • [Mistake 5] Believing that F₀ ≥8 min is an adequate industrial target: 8 min is the minimum value specified by Ph.Eur.; most mature processes are designed for 15–25 min to provide an adequate safety margin.
  • [Mistake 6] Using biological indicators for depyrogenation validation: dry heat depyrogenation validation uses a heat-resistant endotoxin challenge, not a BI.
