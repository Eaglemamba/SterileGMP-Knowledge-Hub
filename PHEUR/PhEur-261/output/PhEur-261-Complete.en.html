Section 1: Overview, Culture Media & Growth Promotion Test

p1-p2

European Pharmacopoeia 11.0 — Chapter 2.6.1: Sterility | p1–p2

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the scope of the sterility test and how its results should be correctly interpreted
  • Master the composition, preparation and storage conditions of Fluid Thioglycollate Medium (FTM) and Soya-bean Casein Digest Medium (SCDM)
  • Understand media suitability testing: the sterility test and the Growth Promotion Test
  • Be able to identify the six standard test microorganisms in Table 2.6.1-1, their strain numbers, and the logic for assigning them to FTM/SCDM
2.6.1 Overview

Original Text

The test is applied to substances, preparations or articles which, according to the Pharmacopoeia, are required to be sterile. However, a satisfactory result only indicates that no contaminating micro-organism has been found in the sample examined in the conditions of the test.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Sterility test: Under the pharmacopoeia, any product required to be "sterile" (injectables, ophthalmic preparations, surgical sutures, etc.) must pass this test.

Important limitation: A "pass" result only means that no microbial contamination was found under the sampling conditions of this test; it does not mean the whole batch is absolutely sterile. This is the statistical nature of a destructive end-product test.

Key Notes

The pharmacopoeia states explicitly that a satisfactory result only indicates that no contamination was found under the conditions of the test. This sentence is the key to understanding the requirement: sterility assurance comes from validation of the whole process, not from the end-product sterility test alone.

This is also why ICH Q9 and PIC/S Annex 1 place more weight on "sterilization validation + aseptic process validation" than on finished-product sterility testing.

Analogy

It is like a restaurant taking one spoonful from a pot of soup to taste (the sterility test). A good spoonful does not prove the whole pot is fine. Real quality assurance comes from kitchen hygiene management (process control), not from tasting alone.

Precautions Against Microbial Contamination

Original Text

The test for sterility is carried out under aseptic conditions. In order to achieve such conditions, the test environment has to be adapted to the way in which the sterility test is performed. The precautions taken to avoid contamination are such that they do not affect any micro-organisms which are to be revealed in the test. The working conditions in which the tests are performed are monitored regularly by appropriate sampling of the working area and by carrying out appropriate controls.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Aseptic conditions: The sterility test must be performed under aseptic conditions, usually in a grade A (ISO Class 5) isolator or RABS, to avoid false positives.

The key tension: The precautions taken to prevent contamination must not affect the test's ability to detect microorganisms. The two goals, "prevent contamination" and "allow target organisms to grow", have to be balanced.

Key Notes

"Regular monitoring of the working area" means that environmental monitoring (EM) is a supporting requirement of the sterility test. If the EM records show an abnormality, this can be grounds for declaring the test invalid (see the Interpretation section of this chapter).

Practical Application

When a CDMO performs sterility tests on a client's product, the QC laboratory needs documents as evidence: HEPA integrity tests for the isolator/RABS, EM trend data, and training records on aseptic technique for operators. These are the supporting documents most often checked in audits.

Culture Media and Incubation Temperatures

Original Text

Media for the test may be prepared as described below, or equivalent commercial media may be used provided that they comply with the growth promotion test.

The following culture media have been found to be suitable for the test for sterility. Fluid thioglycollate medium is primarily intended for the culture of anaerobic bacteria; however, it will also detect aerobic bacteria. Soya-bean casein digest medium is suitable for the culture of both fungi and aerobic bacteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Division of roles between the two media:

  • FTM (Fluid Thioglycollate Medium): Mainly detects anaerobes, but can also detect aerobes. Incubation temperature 30–35°C.
  • SCDM (Soya-bean Casein Digest Medium): Detects aerobes and fungi. Incubation temperature 20–25°C.

Commercially prepared media are acceptable, provided they pass the growth promotion test.

Analogy

FTM is like an "oxygen-poor room": only anaerobes can thrive in it, but aerobes do not die off completely either. SCDM is like a "normal office environment": aerobes and fungi can both multiply easily. Each room has its own job, and together they cover all possible sources of contamination.

Fluid Thioglycollate Medium: Composition

Original Text

Fluid thioglycollate medium

pH after sterilisation: 7.1 ± 0.2

Mix the L-cystine, agar, sodium chloride, glucose, water-soluble yeast extract and pancreatic digest of casein with the water R and heat until solution is effected. Dissolve the sodium thioglycollate or thioglycollic acid in the solution and, if necessary, add 1 M sodium hydroxide so that, after sterilisation, the solution will have a pH of 7.1 ± 0.2.

Sterilise using a validated process. Store at a temperature between 2 °C and 25 °C in a sterile, airtight container.

If more than the upper one-third of the medium has acquired a pink colour, the medium may be restored once by heating the containers in a water-bath or in free-flowing steam until the pink colour disappears and cooling quickly.

Incubation temperature: 30–35 °C

For products containing a mercurial preservative that cannot be tested by the membrane-filtration method, fluid thioglycollate medium incubated at 20–25 °C may be used instead of soya-bean casein digest medium provided that it has been validated.

An alternative thioglycollate medium (without agar and resazurin) may be used where prescribed or justified and authorised. Incubate at 30–35 °C under anaerobic conditions.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Analysis of key ingredients:

  • Sodium thioglycollate: A reducing agent that consumes dissolved oxygen and creates an anaerobic environment so that anaerobes can grow.
  • Agar: Added in a small amount (0.75 g/L) to increase the viscosity of the medium and limit oxygen diffusion; it does not form a solid medium.
  • Resazurin: A redox indicator. Colorless or pale pink when no oxygen is present; it turns pink in the presence of oxygen. If the pink color extends beyond the upper one third, the medium has been over-oxidized.
  • L-Cystine: A sulfur-containing amino acid that helps maintain the reducing environment.

Key Notes

Practical meaning of the resazurin color: If more than the upper one third of the FTM turns pink, the medium may be restored once by heating. The rule that it can be restored "only once" is very important: in FDA inspections, restoring the same batch of medium more than once is recorded as an observation.

Products containing mercurial preservatives: Products containing thiomersal cannot be tested by membrane filtration. FTM incubated at 20–25°C is used in place of SCDM, and the method must be validated.

Analogy

Resazurin works like an "oxygen warning light" for the medium: light off (colorless) means the reducing environment is good; light on (pink) means oxygen has got in and the medium may no longer be fit for use. It is like the temperature indicator on a refrigerator: the color tells you whether quality is still on track.

pH Control

Target pH (after sterilization): 7.1 ± 0.2
Adjustment: add 1 M NaOH to adjust the pre-sterilization pH,
         because the pH changes slightly during sterilization
         and must be compensated for in advance.

Laboratory points to note:
- Record the post-sterilization pH of every medium batch
- pH outside the range → the batch of medium is rejected
  and must not be used for product sterility tests
Soya-bean Casein Digest Medium: Composition

Original Text

Soya-bean casein digest medium

pH after sterilisation: 7.3 ± 0.2

Dissolve the solids in water R, warming slightly to effect solution. Cool the solution to room temperature. Add 1 M sodium hydroxide, if necessary, so that after sterilisation the solution will have a pH of 7.3 ± 0.2. Filter, if necessary, to clarify, distribute into suitable vessels and sterilise using a validated process.

Store at a temperature between 2 °C and 25 °C in a sterile well-closed container, unless it is intended for immediate use.

Incubation temperature: 20–25 °C

The media used comply with the following tests, carried out before or in parallel with the test on the product to be examined.

Sterility. Incubate portions of the media for 14 days. No growth of micro-organisms occurs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

FTM vs SCDM comparison:

  • SCDM contains no reducing agent and has a slightly higher pH (7.3 vs 7.1); it is used for aerobic conditions.
  • SCDM contains more nitrogen sources than FTM (pancreatic digest of casein, 17 g), which favors fungal growth.
  • Dipotassium hydrogen phosphate provides buffering capacity and stabilizes the pH of the medium.
  • The lower incubation temperature (20–25°C) favors the growth of fungi.

Key Notes

Confirmation of medium sterility (Media Sterility): Before use, a portion of each batch of medium must be incubated for 14 days and observed to confirm that no microbial growth occurs. This is the minimum quality control requirement for media, and no commercial medium is exempt.

If the medium sterility test fails → the whole batch is rejected, and the results of related product tests must also be re-evaluated.

Practical Application

CDMO QC laboratories usually buy commercially prepared media directly (from suppliers such as Sigma-Aldrich or BD), because in-house preparation is time-consuming and risky. Even so, a Growth Promotion Test must still be performed after purchase, and the COA and batch records must be retained for audit review.

Growth Promotion Test of Aerobes, Anaerobes and Fungi

Original Text

Growth promotion test of aerobes, anaerobes and fungi. Test each batch of ready-prepared medium and each batch of medium prepared either from dehydrated medium or from ingredients. Suitable strains of micro-organisms are indicated in Table 2.6.1.-1.

Inoculate portions of fluid thioglycollate medium with a small number (not more than 100 CFU) of the following micro-organisms, using a separate portion of medium for each species: Clostridium sporogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus.

Inoculate portions of soya-bean casein digest medium with a small number (not more than 100 CFU) of the following micro-organisms: Aspergillus brasiliensis, Bacillus subtilis, Candida albicans. Incubate for not more than 3 days in the case of bacteria and not more than 5 days in the case of fungi.

Table 2.6.1.-1. Strains of the test micro-organisms suitable for use in the growth promotion test and the method suitability test.

Seed lot culture maintenance techniques (seed-lot systems) are used so that the viable micro-organisms used for inoculation are not more than 5 passages removed from the original master seed-lot.

The media are suitable if a clearly visible growth of the micro-organisms occurs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Logic for assigning the six standard strains:

  • FTM（30–35°C）：
    • Clostridium sporogenes (representative anaerobe)
    • Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Gram-negative aerobe)
    • Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive aerobe)
  • SCDM（20–25°C）：
    • Aspergillus brasiliensis (representative mold)
    • Bacillus subtilis (spore-forming bacterium)
    • Candida albicans (representative yeast)

100 CFU limit: An inoculum of "not more than 100 CFU" ensures a low-inoculum condition that simulates real contamination, and prevents an excessive inoculum from masking poor medium performance.

Key Notes

Passage limit (no more than 5 passages): Repeated subculturing can cause genotypic or phenotypic drift, which affects the validity of the strain as a positive control. A seed-lot system (master seed lot/working seed lot) is the standard way to ensure strain stability, and is required by both USP and Ph. Eur.

Acceptance criterion: Only "clearly visible growth" counts as a pass; faint or weak growth is not accepted.

Analogy

The growth promotion test is like a restaurant testing its stoves before opening each day: it is not actually cooking for customers, but lighting the burner first to confirm the stove works. These standard strains are the "calibration dishes" that confirm the medium, the "stove", really does support microbial growth.

Timing Reference

Growth promotion test incubation time:
  - Bacteria: ≤ 3 days
  - Fungi: ≤ 5 days

Control (positive control):
  - Performed at the same time as the test
  - Same batch of medium, without the product being tested

Acceptance:
  ✓ Clearly visible growth → medium acceptable
  ✗ No visible growth → medium not acceptable, do not use

Practice Questions

  1. Why is Clostridium sporogenes assigned to FTM rather than SCDM? What biological characteristics make it the representative anaerobe?
  2. If the Growth Promotion Test of a batch of commercial medium shows "unclear" growth of Aspergillus brasiliensis, what would your next step be?
  3. Why is the number of passages limited to 5? What could happen beyond 5 passages?

Section Summary

  • A satisfactory sterility test result only means that "no contamination was found"; it does not mean the batch is absolutely sterile. True sterility assurance comes from process validation.
  • FTM (mainly anaerobes, 30–35°C) and SCDM (aerobes + fungi, 20–25°C) have separate roles, and the six standard strains are assigned according to the characteristics of each medium.
  • Before use, each batch of medium must pass: (1) a sterility test (no growth in 14 days); (2) a growth promotion test (clearly visible growth of each standard strain, bacteria within 3 days / fungi within 5 days).
  • Standard strains must not be subcultured more than 5 times (seed-lot system), to keep the strain characteristics stable.

Method Suitability Test

Original Text

Carry out a test as described below under Test for sterility of the product to be examined using exactly the same methods except for the following modifications.

Membrane filtration. After transferring the contents of the container or containers to be tested to the membrane add an inoculum of a small number of viable micro-organisms (not more than 100 CFU) to the final portion of sterile diluent used to rinse the filter.

Direct inoculation. After transferring the content of the container or containers to be tested to the culture medium add an inoculum of a small number of viable micro-organisms (not more than 100 CFU) to the medium.

In both cases use the same micro-organisms as those described above under Growth promotion test of aerobes, anaerobes and fungi. Perform a growth promotion test as a positive control. Incubate all the containers containing medium for not more than 5 days.

If clearly visible growth of micro-organisms is obtained after the incubation, visually comparable to that in the control vessel without product, either the product possesses no antimicrobial activity under the conditions of the test or such activity has been satisfactorily eliminated. The test for sterility may then be carried out without further modification.

If clearly visible growth is not obtained in the presence of the product to be tested, the product possesses antimicrobial activity that has not been satisfactorily eliminated. Modify the conditions in order to eliminate the antimicrobial activity and repeat the method suitability test.

This method suitability test is performed:

  1. when the test for sterility has to be carried out on a new product;
  2. whenever there is a change in the experimental conditions of the test.

The method suitability test may be performed simultaneously with the test for sterility of the product to be examined.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Purpose of the method suitability test: To confirm that the product under test does not itself inhibit microbial growth, so that a "false negative" does not lead to a wrong pass of the sterility test.

Core logic: Under normal sterility test conditions, a known number of microorganisms (≤100 CFU) is deliberately added:

  • If the standard organisms grow normally → the product has no antimicrobial activity and the method is valid
  • If the standard organisms fail to grow → the product has antimicrobial activity and the method must be adjusted (e.g. add a neutralizer, dilute, or increase the number of rinses)

Key Notes

When it must be done (memorize this):

  • (a) When a sterility test is performed for the first time on a new product
  • (b) Whenever there is any change in the test conditions (a change of medium batch is not a "change of conditions", but a change of membrane type is)

Note: The method suitability test can be performed at the same time as the sterility test itself. This matters when the production schedule is tight, because you do not have to wait for the method to be confirmed before running the actual test.

Practical Application

Injectables containing antibiotics (e.g. vancomycin) or ophthalmic solutions containing preservatives (e.g. benzalkonium chloride) usually need special handling:

  • Membrane filtration: Increase the number of rinses (but not beyond 5 × 100 mL) or add a neutralizer (e.g. polysorbate 80)
  • Direct inoculation: Add a suitable amount of neutralizer (e.g. lecithin + polysorbate)

When a CDMO takes on a new client's product, the method suitability test records are an essential deliverable under the Quality Agreement.

Analogy

The method suitability test is like speaking into a microphone to check that your voice comes out before the event starts. If the standard organisms disappear once you add the product, the microphone has failed, so how can you trust the results of the tests that follow?

Membrane Filtration

Original Text

The test may be carried out using the technique of membrane filtration or by direct inoculation of the culture media with the product to be examined. The technique of membrane filtration is used whenever the nature of the product permits, that is, for filterable aqueous preparations, for alcoholic or oily preparations and for preparations miscible with or soluble in aqueous or oily solvents provided these solvents do not have an antimicrobial effect in the conditions of the test.

Use membrane filters having a nominal pore size not greater than 0.45 μm whose effectiveness to retain micro-organisms has been established. Cellulose nitrate filters, for example, are used for aqueous, oily and weakly alcoholic solutions and cellulose acetate filters, for example, for strongly alcoholic solutions.

The filtration apparatus and membrane are sterilised by appropriate means. The apparatus is designed so that the solution to be examined can be introduced and filtered under aseptic conditions.

Aqueous solutions. If appropriate, transfer a small quantity of a suitable, sterile diluent such as a 1 g/L neutral solution of meat or casein peptone pH 7.1 ± 0.2 onto the membrane and filter. Transfer the contents of the container(s) to the membrane(s). Filter immediately. If the product has antimicrobial properties, wash the membrane not less than 3 times by filtering through it each time the volume of the chosen sterile diluent. Do not exceed a washing cycle of 5 times 100 mL per filter.

Transfer the whole membrane to the culture medium or cut it aseptically into 2 equal parts and transfer one half to each of 2 suitable media. Incubate the media for not less than 14 days.

Soluble solids. Use for each medium not less than the quantity prescribed in Table 2.6.1.-2. Dissolve in a suitable solvent and proceed as for aqueous solutions.

Oils and oily solutions. Oils and oily solutions of sufficiently low viscosity may be filtered without dilution through a dry membrane. Viscous oils may be diluted with isopropyl myristate (shown not to have antimicrobial activity). Wash the membrane at least 3 times by filtering about 100 mL of a suitable sterile solution containing a suitable emulsifying agent, for example polysorbate 80 at a concentration of 10 g/L. Incubate at the same temperatures and times.

Ointments and creams. Dilute to 1 per cent in isopropyl myristate, heating if necessary to not more than 40 °C (in exceptional cases, not more than 44 °C). Filter as rapidly as possible and proceed as described for oils.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Priority of membrane filtration: Whenever the nature of the product allows it (i.e. it can be filtered), membrane filtration should be chosen first. It concentrates a large sample volume on the membrane for higher detection sensitivity, and the rinsing step can also remove antimicrobial components of the product.

0.45 μm membrane filter: The standard pore size, which has been shown to retain bacteria. It is generally also effective for fungal spores, but this needs to be specifically confirmed.

Choice of membrane material:

  • Cellulose nitrate → aqueous, oily and weakly alcoholic solutions
  • Cellulose acetate → strongly alcoholic solutions (e.g. those containing 70% ethanol or more)

Key Notes

Meaning of the maximum rinse of 5 × 100 mL: Even if the method suitability test shows that this rinse volume cannot completely eliminate the antimicrobial activity, the rinse volume may not be increased further. A different approach must be used instead (e.g. add a neutralizer or switch to direct inoculation). This limit prevents a falsely "sterile" result caused by excessive dilution.

Incubation for 14 days (not less): Products such as injectables and ophthalmic preparations must be incubated for at least 14 days as specified by the pharmacopoeia. This ensures that slow-growing microorganisms (such as certain anaerobes) have enough time to be detected.

Special attention for oily preparations: Isopropyl myristate itself has no antimicrobial activity (to be verified), and polysorbate 80 (10 g/L) is used for emulsification so that the oil phase is evenly dispersed and passes through the membrane.

Practical Application

For CDMO injectable manufacturing, the QC laboratory is usually equipped with a closed membrane filtration sterility test system, such as Millipore Steritest or Sartorius Microsart. The advantages of such closed systems are:

  • Lower risk of contamination during handling (fewer false positives)
  • Can be operated safely outside the isolator
  • Complete record chain, which makes audits easier
Direct Inoculation of Culture Medium

Original Text

Transfer the quantity of the preparation to be examined prescribed in Table 2.6.1.-2 directly into the culture medium so that the volume of the product is not more than 10 per cent of the volume of the medium, unless otherwise prescribed.

If the product to be examined has antimicrobial activity, carry out the test after neutralising this with a suitable neutralising substance or by dilution in a sufficient quantity of culture medium. When it is necessary to use a large volume of the product it may be preferable to use a concentrated culture medium.

Oily liquids. Use media to which have been added a suitable emulsifying agent at a concentration shown to be appropriate in the method suitability test, for example polysorbate 80 at a concentration of 10 g/L.

Ointments and creams. Prepare by diluting to about 1 in 10 by emulsifying with the chosen emulsifying agent. Transfer the diluted product to a medium not containing an emulsifying agent.

Incubate the inoculated media for not less than 14 days. Observe the cultures several times during the incubation period. Shake cultures containing oily products gently each day. However when fluid thioglycollate medium is used for the detection of anaerobic micro-organisms keep shaking or mixing to a minimum in order to maintain anaerobic conditions.

Catgut and other surgical sutures for veterinary use. Use for each medium not less than the quantities in Table 2.6.1.-2. Open the sealed package using aseptic precautions and remove 3 sections of the strand for each culture medium. Carry out the test on 3 sections, each 30 cm long, cut off from the beginning, the centre and the end of the strand.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

When direct inoculation applies: When the product cannot be filtered (e.g. highly viscous semi-solids, or suspensions containing particles that cannot be filtered), direct inoculation must be used.

Logic of the 10% volume limit: If too much product is added, the medium's own "buffering capacity" (the dilution of inhibitory substances by the medium volume) can no longer work. The 10% limit ensures that the bulk of the medium is not excessively altered by the product.

Concentrated medium: When a large amount of product must be added (e.g. the whole content of a large-volume injection), 2× or 5× concentrated medium can be used so that the final mixture reaches normal concentration.

Key Notes

Special handling of FTM for anaerobes: Oily products need gentle shaking every day (so that the oil does not separate into a layer and block contact with the organisms), but when FTM is used to detect anaerobes, shaking should be kept to a minimum, because shaking introduces oxygen and destroys the anaerobic environment, so that Clostridium cannot grow (false negative).

This conflict calls for a specific decision: if the product is oily and FTM is also needed to detect anaerobes, the SOP should state clearly how the mixing is to be done.

Analogy

Direct inoculation is like bringing the "suspect" (the product) straight into the "interrogation room" (the medium). The 10% limit makes sure the conditions of the "interrogation room" are not wrecked by the suspect's own characteristics (antimicrobial activity). If the suspect is too strong, he must first be "disarmed" (with a neutralizer) before coming in.

Practice Questions

  1. Why is membrane filtration regarded as the "method of choice"? When must you switch to direct inoculation?
  2. For an ophthalmic solution containing 0.02% benzalkonium chloride as preservative, which method would you choose? How would you deal with the antimicrobial activity?
  3. Why should FTM "not be shaken" when it is used to detect anaerobes? What conflict does this create between the product (oily, in FTM) and the objective (anaerobe detection)?
Table 2.6.1-2: Minimum Quantity to be Used for Each Medium

Original Text

Table 2.6.1.-2. Minimum quantity to be used for each medium

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Common injectable scenarios (key points to remember):

  • Small-volume injections (1–40 mL, e.g. a 2 mL ampoule) → half the contents of the container, but not less than 1 mL per medium
  • Medium-volume injections (40–100 mL, e.g. a 50 mL infusion bottle) → a fixed 20 mL per medium
  • Large-volume injections (>100 mL, e.g. a 250 mL infusion bag) → 10% of the contents, but not less than 20 mL
  • Antibiotic liquids → a fixed 1 mL per medium (the quantity is small because the antimicrobial activity is harder to neutralize)

Key Notes

These quantities are the "minimum per medium", not the total for the whole test. If membrane filtration is used with two media, FTM + SCDM, each medium must meet the minimum quantity above.

"Unless otherwise justified and authorised" allows a deviation in special cases (e.g. insufficient sample quantity), but a written justification and authorization are required. During FDA/EMA inspections, such deviation records are often spot-checked for completeness.

Quick Reference

Worked example:
  Product: 10 mL injection (1 container)
  Test: membrane filtration, one membrane each for FTM and SCDM

  Quantity per medium:
    Half of 10 mL = 5 mL ≥ 1 mL ✓
    → 5 mL for each medium
    → 10 mL needed in total (= 1 whole container)

  If each container holds only 1 mL:
    Half of 1 mL = 0.5 mL < 1 mL
    → use the whole container (1 mL) for each medium
    → 2 containers needed (1 for FTM + 1 for SCDM)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Corresponding lot sizes for injectables (the most common CDMO scenarios):

  • Lot size ≤ 100 containers → 10% or 4 containers, whichever is greater
    Example: lot of 50 → 5 vs 4 → test 5 containers
  • Lot size 101–500 containers → a fixed 10 containers
  • Lot size > 500 containers → 2% or 20 containers, whichever is less
    Example: lot of 1,000 → 20 vs 20 → test 20 containers
    Example: lot of 2,000 → 40 vs 20 → test 20 containers

Special case for large-volume parenterals (LVP): For lots of >500 containers, at most 10 containers are needed (rather than 20), because the quantity taken from each container is already sufficient.

Key Notes

"When the lot size is unknown, use the maximum": At a CDMO, if the client does not provide lot size information, or if a batch has partial returns or is split into sub-lots, base the calculation on the largest possible lot size to avoid under-sampling.

The contents of one container are sufficient to inoculate both media: If one 10 mL injection can supply a sufficient quantity for both FTM and SCDM, the "number" in Table 2.6.1-3 refers to the total number of containers needed for both media together, not the number needed for each medium.

Observation and Interpretation of Results

Original Text

At intervals during the incubation period and at its conclusion, examine the media for macroscopic evidence of microbial growth. If the material being tested renders the medium turbid so that the presence or absence of microbial growth cannot be readily determined by visual examination, 14 days after the beginning of incubation transfer portions (each not less than 1 mL) of the medium to fresh vessels of the same medium and then incubate the original and transfer vessels for not less than 4 days.

If no evidence of microbial growth is found, the product to be examined complies with the test for sterility.

If evidence of microbial growth is found the product does not comply with the test for sterility, unless it can be clearly demonstrated that the test was invalid for causes unrelated to the product. The test may be considered invalid only if one or more of the following conditions are fulfilled:

  1. the data of the microbiological monitoring of the sterility testing facility show a fault;
  2. a review of the testing procedure used during the test in question reveals a fault;
  3. microbial growth is found in the negative controls;
  4. after determination of the identity of the micro-organisms isolated from the test, the growth of this species or these species may be ascribed unequivocally to faults with respect to the material and/or the technique used in conducting the sterility test procedure.

If the test is declared to be invalid it is repeated with the same number of units as in the original test.

If no evidence of microbial growth is found in the repeat test the product examined complies with the test for sterility. If microbial growth is found in the repeat test the product examined does not comply with the test for sterility.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The four statutory conditions for an invalid test:

  • (a) Abnormal environmental monitoring: EM shows that the cleanliness of the testing facility is unacceptable (e.g. microbial counts in the isolator exceed the limit)
  • (b) Error in the operating procedure: A retrospective review finds a deviation in the operating steps (e.g. the rinsing steps were not performed according to the SOP)
  • (c) Growth in the negative control: Microbial growth in the blank control medium directly negates the validity of the test
  • (d) Identification confirms handling contamination: The organism identified in the test can be clearly traced to the operator's hands or the test equipment, and not to the product

Key Notes

"Invalid" does not mean "pass": An invalid test only allows the test to be repeated; it does not mean the product is automatically released. If growth is found again in the repeat test (same number of units), the product is confirmed as not complying with the requirement for sterility.

Special handling when "the product makes the medium turbid": If the product itself (e.g. a fat emulsion or protein preparation) makes the medium appear cloudy, then after 14 days transfer ≥1 mL to fresh medium and observe for a further 4 days, adding a subculture step.

Inspection focus: FDA/EMA auditors pay close attention to investigation reports for invalid tests: is the rationale sufficient, was the investigation timely, and is there a CAPA? Every "invalid" result should have a complete deviation investigation record.

Practical Application

Standard handling when the sterility test of a lyophilized injectable batch at a CDMO shows an initial positive:

  1. Notify QA immediately and open a deviation investigation
  2. Collect EM data, operator training records and the environmental conditions of the day
  3. Identify the microorganism in the positive medium (16S rRNA sequencing or MALDI-TOF)
  4. Assess whether any of the four invalidity conditions above is met
  5. If invalidity is confirmed → repeat the test (with client notification)
  6. If invalidity cannot be confirmed → do not release the batch, and start a market assessment (if marketed batches exist)

Analogy

The four invalidity conditions are like a football match's video review (VAR): the decision can be overturned and the match replayed only when there is clear evidence that the referee (the test environment) or a player (the operator) made an error. You cannot ask for a replay just because you dislike the result; you need grounds.

Section Summary

  • The method suitability test is a mandatory prerequisite for a new product or a change in conditions, confirming that the standard organisms can grow normally under the test conditions (the product has no un-neutralized antimicrobial activity).
  • Membrane filtration (method of choice) vs direct inoculation (for products that cannot be filtered): membrane filtration allows a maximum rinse of 5 × 100 mL and incubation for ≥14 days; in direct inoculation, the product volume is ≤10% of the medium volume.
  • Table 2.6.1-2 (minimum quantity for each medium) and Table 2.6.1-3 (minimum number of containers to be tested) are the quantitative requirements most often cross-checked in regulatory inspections; the tiered calculation rules for injectables must be memorized.
  • A test is invalid only if one of the four statutory conditions is met, and after an invalid test the result of the repeat test is the final basis for judgement. Every invalid test needs a complete deviation investigation record.
