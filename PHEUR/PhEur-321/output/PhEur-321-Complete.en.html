Original Text — English

Glass Containers — Definitions & Classification

Glass containers for pharmaceutical use are glass articles intended to come into direct contact with pharmaceutical preparations.

Colourless glass is highly transparent in the visible spectrum. Coloured glass is obtained by the addition of small amounts of metal oxides, chosen according to the desired spectral absorbance.

Neutral glass is a borosilicate glass containing significant amounts of boric oxide, aluminium oxide, alkali metal oxides and/or alkaline earth oxides in the glass network. Due to its composition, neutral glass has a high hydrolytic resistance and a high thermal shock resistance.

Soda-lime-silica glass is a silica glass containing alkali metal oxides, mainly sodium oxide, and alkaline earth oxides, mainly calcium oxide, in the glass network. Due to its composition, soda-lime-silica glass has only a moderate hydrolytic resistance.

The hydrolytic stability of glass containers is expressed by the resistance to the release of soluble mineral substances into water under the prescribed conditions of contact. The hydrolytic resistance is evaluated by titrating released alkali reacting ions. According to their hydrolytic resistance, glass containers are classified as follows:

  • Type I glass containers: neutral glass, with a high hydrolytic resistance due to the chemical composition of the glass itself;
  • Type II glass containers: usually of soda-lime-silica glass with a high hydrolytic resistance resulting from suitable treatment of the inner surface;
  • Type III glass containers: usually of soda-lime-silica glass with only moderate hydrolytic resistance.

The Two Basic Glass Materials

How Hydrolytic Resistance Is Measured

"Hydrolytic resistance" is not an abstract concept. Ph.Eur. quantifies it by titration: water is brought into contact with the glass, and the alkali ions released are measured (back-titrated with 0.01 M or 0.02 M hydrochloric acid). The result is expressed in mL HCl/100 mL solution (Test A) or mL HCl/g glass (Test B). The lower the value, the better the hydrolytic resistance.

Original Text — English

Suitability for Pharmaceutical Preparations

Type I glass containers are suitable for most preparations whether or not for parenteral administration.

Type II glass containers are suitable for most acidic and neutral, aqueous preparations whether or not for parenteral administration.

Type III glass containers are in general suitable for non-aqueous preparations for parenteral administration, for powders for parenteral administration (except for freeze-dried preparations) and for preparations not for parenteral administration.

Glass containers with a hydrolytic resistance higher than that recommended above for a particular type of preparation may generally also be used.

The container chosen for a given preparation shall be such that the glass material does not release substances in quantities sufficient to affect the stability of the preparation or to present a risk of toxicity. In justified cases, further detailed information may be necessary to assess the impact on chronic use and for vulnerable patient groups.

Preparations for parenteral administration are normally presented in colourless glass, but coloured glass may be used for substances known to be light-sensitive. It is recommended that all glass containers for liquid preparations and for powders for parenteral administration permit the visual inspection of the contents.

The inner surface of glass containers may be specially treated to improve hydrolytic resistance, to confer water-repellancy, etc. Except for type I glass containers, glass containers for pharmaceutical preparations are not to be re-used. Containers for human blood and blood components must not be re-used.

Recommended Preparations for Each Type

Selection Decision Matrix
The "Higher Type May Also Be Used" Principle

Think of it as "upward compatibility" in a material specification: Type I has higher hydrolytic resistance than Type II and Type III, so if Type II is the recommended minimum, using Type I is also compliant. The reverse does not hold: Type III cannot replace Type II where Type II should be used.

Key Regulatory Requirements for CDMOs
  • No reuse: apart from Type I, glass containers of all types must not be reused for pharmaceutical production; containers for transfusion bags/blood products must never be reused.
  • Light-sensitive products: coloured glass (brown/amber) must be used and must meet the spectral transmission limits (Test C, see Section 3).
  • Visual inspectability: containers for liquid injections and powders for injection should allow visual inspection for visible particles (that is, they need sufficient transparency).
  • Internal surface treatment: the high hydrolytic resistance of Type II depends on an internal surface treatment (sulfur treatment). If this surface is damaged (for example by excessive high-temperature sterilization), the resistance falls.

Production — Delamination Risk

When glass containers for pharmaceutical use are manufactured under stressed conditions (e.g. temperature-time profile) and/or are placed in contact with particularly aggressive pharmaceutical preparations, they may undergo delamination, i.e. the separation of the inner glass surface into thin layers called lamellae or flakes.

Glass delamination may be the result of a chemical attack that occurs according to well-known glass corrosion mechanisms, such as dissolution by hydrolysis and ion exchange (leaching) as a function of the pH. The process of interaction between the glass surface and the pharmaceutical preparation requires incubation time, and flaking may only become visible a number of months after filling.

Several risk factors are known to increase the propensity of a glass to delaminate. The chemical composition of the pharmaceutical preparation, the presence of buffers like citrate or phosphate, which are known to corrode glass, and the ionic strength of the liquid medium may all strongly favour delamination. The manufacturing process of the container, chemical treatments of the inner surface, and terminal sterilisation and processing at the pharmaceutical filling lines are other important risk factors.

It is recommended that the user of the container assesses the compatibility of the glass container and the pharmaceutical preparation on a case-by-case basis, considering for example the dosage form, properties of the formulation and glass quality.

The propensity to delamination of glass containers from different sources can be assessed and ranked by exposing the container to accelerated degradation testing, carried out at specified temperatures for a short time and using the solutions associated with the actual pharmaceutical preparation as extractants. The presence of particles in the extraction solution, the occurrence of phase separation on the inner surface, and the steep increase of silica concentration in the extraction solution are all indicators of a potential propensity for delamination.

Production Requirements: Glass Delamination

Glass Delamination: A Major Safety Risk

Delamination is the flaking of the glass inner surface into thin lamellae/flakes. It is one of the major sources of visible and subvisible particles in injectables. In 2011 the FDA issued a number of drug recalls caused by glass delamination, which drew strong attention from industry.

High-Risk Factors for Delamination
  • Buffers: citrate and phosphate buffers are highly corrosive to glass and markedly increase the risk of delamination.
  • High ionic strength: high-salt formulations accelerate attack on the glass surface.
  • Terminal sterilization: high-temperature (121°C) steam sterilization promotes glass-solution reactions and increases the risk of delamination.
  • Glass tube manufacturing process: the temperature-time profile during tube forming affects the structure of the inner surface.
  • Delayed onset: delamination usually becomes visible to the naked eye only months after filling, so it may not be detected at IPC inspection.
Methods for Assessing Delamination Propensity
  • Accelerated degradation testing: short exposure at a defined temperature, using the actual drug product solution as the extraction medium, to detect: appearance of particles, phase separation of the inner surface, and a sharp rise in silicon concentration.
  • Formal compatibility stability testing: accelerated testing is only a (predictive) screening tool. A full stability study under normal conditions is ultimately needed to confirm the compatibility of the glass with the drug solution.
CDMO Actions
  • Conduct a glass compatibility study when selecting containers for new products, especially for formulations with pH >7 or containing phosphate/citrate buffers.
  • Terminally sterilized products: assess the risk of glass delamination and consider giving preference to Type I (highest resistance).
  • Refer to the compatibility assessment frameworks in USP <1660> (Evaluation of the Inner Surface Durability of Glass Containers) and PDA TR87.

Tests Overview & Hydrolytic Resistance

Glass containers for pharmaceutical use comply with the relevant test or tests for hydrolytic resistance. Tests for hydrolytic resistance are carried out to define the type of glass (I, II or III) and to control its hydrolytic resistance. In addition, containers for aqueous parenteral preparations are tested for arsenic release and coloured glass containers are tested for spectral transmission.

Test Overview and Selection of Hydrolytic Resistance Tests

The Three Hydrolytic Resistance Tests and Their Uses
Logic of the Test A + B (or A + C) Combination

Test A distinguishes "high hydrolytic resistance (Type I or II)" from "moderate hydrolytic resistance (Type III)", but cannot tell Type I from Type II, because their Test A results can be similar (both high). Test B (composition of the glass itself) or Test C (checking after etching whether the inner surface is damaged) is needed to separate them further: if the result after etching (Test C) rises substantially, a surface treatment is present and the container is Type II; if it is unchanged, the container is Type I.

Additional Mandatory Tests
  • Arsenic test: applies to Type I and II glass containers for aqueous injections. Limit: ≤ 0.1 ppm As (hydride generation AAS method, 193.7 nm).
  • Spectral transmission: applies to coloured glass containers. UV-Vis measurement over the 290–450 nm range (see Table 3.2.1-5 in Section 3).
Glassware Pretreatment Requirements

The flasks and beakers used for the tests must have been used before, or have been autoclaved at 121°C for ≥1 hour. The purpose is to saturate the leachables of the glassware beforehand so that they do not interfere with the sample titration (high blank values). This is a pretreatment control for accurate titration testing.

Determination of the Filling Volume

The filling volume is the volume of water to be introduced into the container for the purpose of the test. For vials and bottles the filling volume is 90 per cent of the brimful capacity. For ampoules it is the volume up to the height of the shoulder.

Vials and Bottles

Select, at random, 6 containers (or 3 if their capacity exceeds 100 mL). Weigh empty containers (±0.1 g). Fill with water R to the brimful line. Weigh filled containers. Calculate mean brimful capacity and multiply by 0.9. This volume (to 1 decimal place) is the filling volume.

Precision: expressed to 2 decimal places for containers ≤ 30 mL nominal; to 1 decimal place for containers > 30 mL nominal.

Ampoules

Place at least 6 dry ampoules on a flat surface and fill with water R from a burette until the water reaches point A, where the body declines to the shoulder. Read capacities (to 2 decimal places) and calculate the mean. Expressed to 1 decimal place, this is the filling volume. May also be determined by weighing.

Syringes and Cartridges

Select 6 syringes or cartridges. Close the small opening (needle/Luer cone for syringes; mouth for cartridges) using an inert material (e.g. a tip cap). Determine mean brimful volume as per vials and bottles and multiply by 0.9.

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Determination of Filling Volume

Definition and Calculation of Filling Volume

The filling volume is the volume of water introduced in the test, representing "the volume of liquid in actual use". It is specified as follows:

Vials/bottles: Filling volume = brimful capacity × 0.9
Ampoules: Filling volume = volume filled up to the "start of the shoulder (point A)"
Syringes/cartridges: Filling volume = brimful capacity × 0.9

Why 90% rather than 100%: to simulate the filling state in actual use (containers are normally not filled to overflowing), and to ensure the liquid does not overflow during autoclaving.

Why Filling Volume Matters

The filling volume directly determines the volume of test solution for Test A (Table 3.2.1-2, see Section 2) and the number of titrations required. The larger the filling volume, the larger the contact area and the more alkali ions are extracted, but the dilution volume is also larger. Determining the filling volume correctly is a prerequisite for reproducible test results.

The "Shoulder" of an Ampoule

The filling volume of an ampoule is specified specifically up to the "shoulder", the point where the glass body begins to taper (point A shown in Figure 3.2.1-1). This is because the neck of an ampoule is narrow, and filling to the neck would interfere with the test procedure; the shoulder is the position that best represents the actual fill volume of the drug solution.

Statistical Meaning of Sampling Six Containers

Six containers are selected (three for large-volume containers of ≥100 mL) and the mean filling volume is calculated, to account for the volume uniformity within a batch of containers. A single container may not be representative because of manufacturing variation; the mean of six reflects the actual filling volume of the batch more accurately.

Cleaning

Remove any debris or dust. Shortly before the test, fill each container to the brim with water R and allow to stand for 20 ± 5 min. Empty the containers, carefully rinse twice with water R and once with water R1 and allow to drain. Closed ampoules are not rinsed before testing.

Test A: Hydrolytic Resistance of the Inner Surfaces of Containers (Water Attack Test)

What Does Test A Measure?

Test A simulates the reaction between the drug solution and the glass inner surface: "water R1" (highly purified water) is used to extract leachable alkali ions from the inner surface of the glass under autoclave conditions at 121°C, and the extract is back-titrated with hydrochloric acid to quantify the "amount of alkali released". The lower the value, the better the hydrolytic resistance.

Selection of Test Solution Volume (Table 3.2.1-2)
Water R vs Water R1

Water R: general-purpose test water (conductivity ≤ 2 µS/cm). Used for the initial cleaning of containers.
Water R1: highly purified water (conductivity ≤ 1 µS/cm), near-neutral pH, free of interfering dissolved ions. Used as the extraction solution, for the final rinse and as the blank, so that solutes in the water do not affect the titration result.

Special Notes on Ampoules

Closed ampoules are not pre-cleaned, because a sealed ampoule is already free of dust contamination and pre-cleaning could alter the state of the inner surface. Before testing, the ampoule may be pre-warmed in a water bath at about 40°C for ~2 minutes to prevent the liquid from spraying out when it is opened because of the pressure difference between inside and outside.

Test A — Autoclaving Process

Reference Thermal Cycle

The autoclave is run in such a way that the temperature in the containers follows this thermal cycle:

  • Temperature raised from room temperature to 100 °C within 20–30 min;
  • Temperature maintained at 100 ± 1 °C for 10 ± 1 min;
  • Temperature raised from 100 °C to 121 °C within 20–22 min;
  • Temperature maintained at 121 ± 1 °C for 60 ± 1 min;
  • Temperature cooled to 100 °C within 40–44 min.

Autoclave Calibration

Before being used for the first time, the autoclave and the temperature measuring system are calibrated. NOTE: significant differences may be observed between the temperature measured in the autoclave chamber and inside the containers. The calibrated thermocouple is inserted into one of the containers through a hole in the closure.

For manually operated autoclaves, leave the vent cock open; heat at a regular rate so that steam issues vigorously from the vent cock after 20–30 min and maintain vigorous steam for a further 10 min. When cooling down, vent to prevent the formation of a vacuum. Do not open the autoclave before the water in the containers has reached 95 °C. Remove hot samples and cool cautiously to room temperature within 30 min.

Routine Autoclave Runs

Use autoclave settings established during calibration. Container sets of different capacity can be tested during the same run. Keep the glass load very similar to the calibration load. The calibrated thermocouple is no longer necessary once calibration is validated.

Test A: Autoclaving Procedure

The Reference Thermal Cycle Explained
Heat to 100°C: 20–30 min
Hold at 100 ± 1°C: 10 ± 1 min (to vent non-condensable gases)
Heat to 121°C: 20–22 min
Hold at 121 ± 1°C: 60 ± 1 min (extraction hold time)
Cool to 100°C: 40–44 min
Safe opening temperature: ≤ 95°C

This thermal cycle is designed to ensure that the temperature inside the container reaches 121°C, not merely the temperature of the autoclave chamber. There can be a significant temperature difference between the containers and the chamber, so a thermocouple must be placed inside a container for calibration.

Purpose of the 10-Minute Hold at 100°C

In the initial phase of a steam sterilizer, the non-condensable gases (mainly air) in the chamber must first be removed. If the air is not removed, steam cannot contact the containers directly, and the temperature inside the containers will be lower than specified. The purpose of the 10-minute hold at 100°C is a steam purge: the non-condensable gases are removed completely before the pressure and temperature are raised. This is also why, with a manual autoclave, the vent cock is opened to let steam escape continuously for 20–30 minutes.

Importance of Calibrating the Temperature "Inside the Container"

Ph.Eur. specifically emphasizes that there may be a significant difference between the autoclave chamber temperature reading and the water temperature inside the containers. When establishing this test method, the CDMO must first complete instrument calibration: a thermocouple is inserted inside actual containers to confirm that the chamber setting brings the container contents to 121 ± 1°C, and the calibrated setting is then recorded as the routine operating parameter.

Test vs Product Sterilization: Same Equipment, Different Purposes

The autoclaving in Ph.Eur. 3.2.1 is a "chemical extraction test": the aim is to let water react fully with the glass inner surface and extract alkali ions. Terminal sterilization of a drug product, by contrast, aims to kill microorganisms. Both use the same temperature (121°C), but the test does not need a biological indicator (BI); it only requires strict control of the thermal cycle profile to keep the extraction conditions consistent.

Carry out the titration within 1 h of removal of the containers from the autoclave. Combine the liquids obtained from the containers and mix. Test B — Hydrolytic Resistance of Glass Grains (Glass Grains Test)

Check that the articles as received have been annealed to a commercially acceptable quality. The test may be performed on the canes used for the manufacture of tubing glass containers or on the containers.

Equipment

  • Mortar, pestle and hammer (tempered magnetic steel) or a ball mill (agate/zirconia/stainless steel, 250 mL volume, 2×40 mm or 3×30 mm balls);
  • 3 stainless steel square-mesh sieves: (a) no. 710, (b) no. 425, (c) no. 300;
  • Permanent magnet, metal foil, hot-air oven (140 ± 5 °C), balance (±0.005 g), desiccator, ultrasonic bath.

Method — Crushing & Sieving

Rinse containers with water R, dry. Wrap ≥3 glass articles in clean paper and crush to produce 2 samples of ~100 g. Crush pieces in mortar (30–40 g at a time, one heavy hammer strike) and sieve through set (a)–(c). Collect grains that pass through sieve (b) and are retained on sieve (c) — target particle size range 300–425 µm. Continue until ~10 g of glass on sieve (a) (reject this portion and the portion passing through (c)).

Remove iron particles with magnet. Clean grains: 3 × 30 mL acetone R washes + ultrasonic bath (1 min × 2), then dry at 140 °C for 20 min, cool in desiccator. Weigh 10.00 g of cleaned dried grains into 2 separate conical flasks.

Autoclaving & Titration

Add 50 mL of water R1 into each flask. Use the autoclaving procedure as in Test A, but maintain 121 ± 1 °C for only 30 ± 1 min (not 60 min). Add 0.05 mL methyl red solution R. Titrate blank with 0.02 M HCl, then titrate test solutions to match blank colour. Subtract blank from test titration. Express as mL of 0.02 M HCl per gram of sample.

1 mL of 0.02 M HCl is equivalent to 620 µg of sodium oxide.

Repeat the test if the highest and lowest observed values differ by more than 20 per cent.

Limits

  • Type I: not more than 0.1 mL of 0.02 M HCl per gram of glass
  • Type II and III: not more than 0.85 mL of 0.02 M HCl per gram of glass

Test B: Hydrolytic Resistance of Glass Grains

Fundamental Difference Between Test B and Test A

Test A tests the "treated inner surface" of the container (including the surface treatment layer of Type II); Test B tests the hydrolytic resistance of the bulk glass itself: once the glass is crushed, fresh fracture surfaces are exposed and the effect of any surface treatment is eliminated. Test B therefore reflects only the glass material. Type I (borosilicate) is inherently hydrolytically resistant, whereas Type II, although its inner surface is resistant, is similar in material to Type III, so its Test B value is much higher.

Key Steps in Grain Preparation Explained
1Sieve to a grain size of 300–425 µm (passing the 425 µm sieve, retained on the 300 µm sieve). A standardized grain size ensures the same surface area from batch to batch, so results are comparable.
2Remove iron with a magnet. Steel tools can shed iron particles during crushing, and these interfere with extraction (iron may react by oxidation in an alkaline environment), so they must be removed.
3Ultrasonic cleaning in acetone. This removes organic contaminants (skin oils, dust) from the grain surface, ensuring that only dissolution from the glass itself is measured.
4Dry at 140°C for 20 min. This removes residual acetone and adsorbed moisture, ensuring that the 10.00 g is weighed accurately (moisture affects the weight).
Key Test B Parameters Compared with Test A

Test solution: 50 mL water R1 (fixed)
Autoclave hold: 30 min (60 min for Test A)
Titration acid: 0.02 M HCl (Test A uses 0.01 M HCl)
Result unit: mL/g glass (Test A: mL/100 mL test solution)
Conversion factor: 1 mL 0.02 M HCl ≡ 620 µg Na₂O

Type I limit: ≤ 0.1 mL/g
Type II/III limit: ≤ 0.85 mL/g
Test B Distinguishes Type I from Type II/III

The design of the Test B limits is critical: the Type I limit (≤0.1 mL/g) is far stricter than that of Type II/III (≤0.85 mL/g), with no overlap between them. This is because the chemical composition of Type I (borosilicate) is inherently hydrolytically resistant, whereas Type II, although it performs well in the inner-surface test (Test A), is made of soda-lime glass like Type III, so its Test B result is close to that of Type III. If a container gives Test B <0.1 mL/g, the material is judged to be Type I; if Test B is 0.1–0.85 mL/g, the material is Type II or III (to be distinguished further by Test A or C).

The 20% Reproducibility Rule

Test B uses two independent samples (10.00 g each). If the difference between the two results exceeds 20% of the higher value, the test must be repeated. This reflects the variability of grain-size uniformity and surface area in the crushing process: sufficient reproducibility is needed before the result can be trusted.

Test C — To Determine Whether Containers Have Been Surface-Treated (Etching Test)

If there are uncertainties whether a container has been surface-treated, and/or to distinguish between type I and type II glass containers, test C is used in addition to test A. Alternatively, tests A and B may be used. Test C may be carried out either on unused samples or on samples previously used in test A.

Vials and Bottles

Rinse the containers twice with water R, fill to the brimful point with a mixture of 1 volume of hydrofluoric acid R and 9 volumes of hydrochloric acid R and allow to stand for 10 min. Empty the containers and rinse carefully 5 times with water R. Immediately before the test, rinse once again with water R. Submit the containers thus prepared to the same autoclaving and determination procedure as described in Test A.

If the results are considerably higher than those obtained from the original surfaces (by about a factor of 5 to 10), the samples have been surface-treated.

Ampoules, Cartridges and Syringes

NOTE: ampoules, cartridges and syringes made from glass tubing are not normally subjected to internal surface treatment because their high chemical resistance is dependent upon the chemical composition of the glass as a material. Apply the test method as described above. If the ampoules are not surface-treated, the new values are slightly lower than those obtained in previous tests.

Test C: Etching Test (Identifying Surface Treatment)

Design Principle of Test C

The high hydrolytic resistance of Type II glass comes from an "inner surface treatment layer" (usually a sulfur treatment, forming a thin SiO₂-enriched layer). Test C first etches with an HF/HCl acid mixture (1:9 ratio) for 10 minutes to destroy this surface treatment layer, and then repeats Test A. If the Test A result rises substantially after etching (5–10 times), the original high hydrolytic resistance depended on the surface treatment, so the container is Type II. If the Test A result changes little after etching, the resistance comes from the composition of the glass itself, so the container is Type I.

Interpretation Logic of Table 3.2.1-4
Special Notes on Ampoule/Syringe Tubing

Ampoules, syringes and cartridges are usually made from glass tubing and are normally not given an inner surface treatment, because their high chemical resistance comes from the composition of the glass tubing itself (Type I material). After etching in Test C, the result should therefore be slightly lower than the original Test A (part of the surface is etched away, slightly reducing the contact area). A large increase would be an abnormal situation requiring further investigation.

Practical Applications of Test C
  • Supplier audit: when a new batch of glass vials is received and the supplier CoA states Type II but there are doubts, Test C can be run to confirm whether a surface treatment is really present.
  • Investigation of surface treatment failure: if abnormal delamination or pH shift is found in stability testing, Test C can be used to assess whether the surface treatment layer is intact.
  • Reuse of test samples: containers already used for Test A may be used for Test C, saving sample quantity, which is especially useful for small-volume, scarce containers.

Arsenic Test

The test applies to glass containers for aqueous parenteral preparations.

Method: Hydride generation atomic absorption spectrometry (2.2.23, Method I).

Test solution: Use the extraction solution obtained from containers of types I and II, after autoclaving at 121 °C for 1 h as described under Test A. Transfer 10.0 mL to a 100 mL volumetric flask. Add 10 mL of hydrochloric acid R and 5 mL of a 200 g/L solution of potassium iodide R. Heat on a water-bath at 80 °C for 20 min, allow to cool and dilute to 100.0 mL with water R.

Reference solutions: arsenic standard solution (1 ppm As) R. Apply same acid/KI treatment. Concentration range: typically 0.005–0.015 ppm of As.

Wavelength: 193.7 nm. Atomisation device: air-acetylene flame. Source: hollow-cathode lamp.

Limit: maximum 0.1 ppm of As.

Educational Commentary — Traditional Chinese

Arsenic Test

Why Is an Arsenic Test Needed?

During high-temperature melting, soda-lime silicate glass may incorporate trace arsenic as a fining agent: arsenic oxide (As₂O₃) accelerates the removal of gas bubbles from the glass at high temperature. Although modern manufacturing has largely reduced this use, a potential risk remains. Injectables enter the blood circulation directly, and the toxicity of arsenic (a metalloid and carcinogen) must be strictly controlled.

Summary of Arsenic Test Parameters

Applicable to: glass containers for aqueous injections (Type I and II)
Source of extract: the autoclaved extract from Test A (121°C × 1 h)
Analytical method: hydride generation atomic absorption spectrometry (HG-AAS)
Wavelength: 193.7 nm
Reference solution range: 0.005–0.015 ppm As
Limit: ≤ 0.1 ppm As
Principle of Hydride Generation AAS

Standard AAS has limited sensitivity for arsenic. In "hydride generation", KI first reduces As(V) to As(III), which is then reacted with sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) to form arsine gas (AsH₃); this is introduced into a quartz tube in the AAS for atomization and detection. The method improves detection sensitivity by more than 1000-fold, to the ppb (µg/L) level, so trace arsenic release can be detected effectively.

Reuse of the Extract by Design

The test solution for the arsenic test comes directly from the Test A extract: a single autoclave extraction can be used both for the alkali titration (hydrolytic resistance) and for arsenic analysis (10.0 mL is taken). This saves time and containers and also ensures that the extraction conditions for the two tests are consistent.

Spectral Transmission for Coloured Glass Containers

Equipment: A UV-Vis spectrophotometer equipped with a photodiode detector or photomultiplier tube coupled with an integrating sphere.

Preparation of Specimen

Break the glass container or cut it with a circular saw fitted with a wet abrasive wheel. Select sections representative of the wall thickness. If the specimen is too small, mask the uncovered portion with opaque paper or tape. Wash, dry and wipe with a lens tissue. Mount with wax, avoiding fingerprints.

Method

Place the specimen with its cylindrical axis parallel to the slit and the light beam perpendicular to the surface (minimise reflection losses). Measure the transmission of the specimen with reference to air in the spectral region of 290–450 nm, continuously or at intervals of 20 nm.

Limits

For preparations not for parenteral administration: spectral transmission does not exceed 10 per cent at any wavelength in 290–450 nm, irrespective of type and capacity.

For preparations for parenteral administration: see Table 3.2.1.-5.

Spectral Transmission of Coloured Glass Containers

Purpose of the Spectral Transmission Test

Coloured glass (usually brown/amber) is used to protect light-sensitive drugs (such as certain vitamins and peptide drugs). The spectral transmission test confirms the light-blocking effect of the glass over 290–450 nm (UV to near-visible range): the lower the transmission, the better the light protection. 290 nm is the typical onset wavelength of drug photodegradation, and 450 nm is the boundary of the near-visible range.

Design Logic of the Table 3.2.1-5 Limits
  • The limits for flame-sealed containers are more lenient than for containers with closures: the closure (stopper/cap) itself may allow a small amount of light through, so the container glass itself must compensate with stricter light protection.
  • The larger the volume, the stricter the limit (lower transmission): large-volume containers (such as 50 mL) have thinner walls, and the glass is not thick enough to provide sufficient light blocking per square centimetre, so deeper colouring is needed to meet the limit. Small-volume containers have relatively thicker walls and are allowed a slightly higher transmission.
Key Points of Sample Preparation

The test glass pieces must be "representative of the wall thickness", because cross-sections of different thickness have different light transmission. An integrating sphere is used to collect the light scattered by the glass, so that true transmission (not just direct light) is measured. Fingerprints (grease) absorb light in the UV region, so the piece is wiped with lens tissue before measurement.

Points for CDMOs to Note
  • When purchasing glass vials, require the supplier to provide a batch spectral transmission CoA corresponding to the relevant Table 3.2.1-5 limits.
  • If the product label covers too much of the glass surface, visual inspection may be affected; the design must leave a sufficient label-free area for visual inspection of visible particles (refer to the requirements of Ph.Eur. 2.9.20).
  • Injectables exported to the EU market require the glass supplier to provide test reports conforming to Ph.Eur. 3.2.1, including confirmation of the Type classification and spectral transmission data.

Annex — Test for Surface Hydrolytic Resistance by Flame Spectrometry

The surface hydrolytic resistance of glass of types I and II may be determined by analysis of the leaching solution by flame spectrometry. A number of elements that, when present as oxides in glass, contribute to the alkalinity of the solution, are determined and used to express an alkali equivalent.

The spectrometric method has the advantage of allowing the use of a much smaller sample of extract so that it can be applied to small individual containers. This enables an evaluation of the uniformity of the containers in a given batch where this is critical.

The results of this measurement are not equivalent to those of titrimetry and the 2 methods cannot be considered interchangeable. The titrimetric method is the reference method of the Pharmacopoeia; the spectrometric method may be used in justified and authorised cases.

Elements determined: sodium oxide (Na₂O), potassium oxide (K₂O), calcium oxide (CaO). Spectrochemical buffer: caesium chloride solution (80 g/L CsCl in 0.01 M HCl). Concentrations reported as alkali equivalent in µg/mL.

Appendix: Flame Spectrometry (Alternative Method) and Chapter Summary

Titration vs Flame Spectrometry
The Role of the Cs Buffer (Spectrochemical Buffer)

In flame spectrometry, measurement of potassium (K) and calcium (Ca) is susceptible to "ionization interference" from other elements (such as Na). Adding a large amount of Cs⁺ (caesium ions) saturates the ionization in the flame, so that K and Ca remain in the atomic state (are not ionized) and can be measured accurately. This is the standard practice of using a "releasing agent" in flame AAS/AES.

Ph.Eur. 3.2.1: Summary of the Complete Test Pathway
Ph.Eur. 3.2.1 vs USP <660> Comparison
  • Same classification system: Type I (borosilicate), Type II (surface-treated soda-lime glass) and Type III (ordinary soda-lime glass) are defined consistently in Ph.Eur. and USP.
  • Differences in test methods: Ph.Eur. Test A uses titration with 0.01 M HCl; the Surface Glass Test in USP <660> likewise uses 0.02 M H₂SO₄, with slightly different limits. The spectral transmission tests of the two are similar, but the limit tables differ slightly.
  • Additional tests: USP <660> also includes a definition of Type IV (general-purpose glass, not for injectables), for which Ph.Eur. has no explicit type.
  • Products exported to the global market must comply with both Ph.Eur. 3.2.1 and USP <660>; it is recommended to confirm the requirements of both pharmacopoeias at the same time during container validation.
