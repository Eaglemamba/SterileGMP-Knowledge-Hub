Definition & Materials

Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 | Scope, Components, and Material Requirements

Definition

Original Text

Sterile single-use plastic syringes are medical devices intended for immediate use for the administration of injectable preparations. They are supplied sterile and bacterial endotoxin-free and are not to be re-sterilised or re-used.

They consist of a syringe barrel and a piston that may have an elastomer sealing ring; they may be fitted with a needle that may be non-detachable. Each syringe is presented with individual protection for maintaining sterility.

The barrel is sufficiently transparent to permit dosages to be read without difficulty and allow air bubbles and foreign particles to be discerned.

The plastics and elastomer materials comply with appropriate specifications or requirements of the competent authority. The most commonly used materials are polypropylene and polyethylene.

The syringes comply with current standards regarding dimensions and performance.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Single-use: Re-sterilisation and re-use are expressly prohibited. This is not only a manufacturing standard but a core patient-safety requirement: re-use can lead to cross-contamination from residual drug, degraded mechanical performance, and loss of sterility assurance.

Medical device: Under the EU MDR 2017/745 framework, syringes are classified as Class IIa medical devices, so they must meet CE marking requirements and have MDR technical documentation. Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 provides the pharmacopoeial quality requirements for them.

Material selection: Polypropylene (PP) is the most common, with good chemical inertness, transparency, and autoclavability. Some prefilled syringes use cyclic olefin copolymer (COC/COP) for better barrier properties.

GMP Key Point

"Individual protection for maintaining sterility" means that every syringe must have its own primary packaging; once the pack is opened, the sterile state is considered ended. This is the basic requirement behind container closure integrity (CCI) testing.

Analogy

The transparency requirement for a syringe is like that for a glass vial: you must be able to see the dose graduations, air bubbles, and any foreign matter clearly. This is not just a convenience; it is the basis of final 100% visual inspection. A syringe you cannot see through is equivalent to non-conforming packaging.

Material Requirements

Original Text

Silicone oil (3.1.8) may be applied to the internal wall of the barrel, in which case there remains no excess capable of contaminating the contents at the time of use.

Inks, glues and adhesives for marking do not migrate across the walls.

The plastics and elastomer materials comply with appropriate specifications or requirements of the competent authority.

Tutorial Commentary

Dual Role of Silicone Oil

Silicone oil (PDMS, polydimethylsiloxane) is the essential lubricant on the inner barrel wall: it lowers the plunger glide force and ensures a uniform injection force. But it is also a critical quality attribute (CQA):

  • Issue: Silicone oil droplets can trigger aggregation of protein drugs (such as monoclonal antibodies, mAb), forming visible or subvisible particles.
  • Limit: Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 specifies ≤0.25 mg/cm² of inner surface area.
  • Audit focus: Process control records for the amount of silicone oil applied, and batch-to-batch consistency.

Ink Migration

"Ink must not migrate through the barrel wall." This requirement applies to the printed dose scale markings. In an audit, you need to provide ink migration test data or a supplier declaration of conformity.

Practical Application

For prefilled syringe (PFS) manufacturers, the siliconisation process is a critical process control point. Excess silicone oil increases the particle risk; too little causes excessive glide force and affects patient safety. A design space for the amount of silicone oil must be established.

Solution S Preparation

Reference Solution for Chemical Tests

Original Text

Solution S. Prepare solution avoiding contamination by foreign particles.

Using sufficient syringes to produce 50 mL of solution, fill the syringes to their nominal volume with water R and maintain at 37°C for 24 h.

Combine contents in a suitable borosilicate-glass container.

Key Parameters

  • Volume target: 50 mL
  • Leaching solvent: Water R (purified water)
  • Temperature: 37°C (body temperature simulation)
  • Duration: 24 hours
  • Container: Borosilicate glass
  • Fill volume: Nominal volume of each syringe

Tutorial Commentary

Scientific Logic Behind the Design of Solution S

Why 37°C / 24 h? This simulates the worst case for contact between the drug solution and the plastic before storage or use. 37°C is roughly body temperature and represents the highest likely use temperature.

Why Water R? Water is a polar solvent and extracts water-soluble extractables. Purified water is used instead of water for injections because this is a material performance test, not a final product release test.

Borosilicate glass container: It is chemically inert and does not introduce additional extractables or interfere with subsequent tests.

Analogy

Solution S is like a "sweat test for the syringe": soak the syringe in body-temperature water for a full day, collect everything that leaches out, and then analyse whether those substances are safe. It is a simplified version of an extractables study and is suitable for routine pharmacopoeial release testing.

GMP Operating Points

Foreign particle contamination must be specifically prevented when preparing Solution S, which is especially important inside a cleanroom or laminar air flow (LAF) cabinet. Contaminated Solution S will cause false failures in the appearance and transparency tests.

Chemical Tests

Appearance, Acidity/Alkalinity, Absorbance, Reducing Substances

Appearance of Solution

Original Text

Appearance of solution. Solution S is clear (2.2.1) and colourless (2.2.2, Method II) and practically free from foreign solid particles.

Tutorial Commentary

Interpretation of the Criteria

Clear (2.2.1): Refer to the European Pharmacopoeia clarity standard (Reference suspension I or better), comparing visually in natural light using colourimetric tubes.

Colourless (2.2.2, Method II): Compared with the BY7 reference solution (yellow standard); the solution must show no visible colour.

Practically free from foreign solid particles: Besides particles visible to the naked eye, the background level of subvisible particles is also considered.

Acceptance Criteria

Solution S must be:

  • Clear
  • Colourless
  • Practically free from foreign solid particles
Acidity or Alkalinity

Original Text

Acidity or alkalinity. To 20 mL of solution S add 0.1 mL of bromothymol blue solution R1.

Not more than 0.3 mL of 0.01 M sodium hydroxide or 0.01 M hydrochloric acid is required to change the colour of the indicator.

Limit

Titrant volume: ≤ 0.3 mL of 0.01 M NaOH or 0.01 M HCl

Indicator: Bromothymol blue R1

Tutorial Commentary

Principle of the Test

Bromothymol blue (BTB): An indicator that changes from yellow at pH 6.0 to blue at pH 7.6. This test evaluates whether the syringe material causes a significant pH shift in the extract.

0.3 mL × 0.01 M = 0.003 meq of acid or base. This is a very strict limit, meaning the effect of the material on pH must be minimal.

GMP Significance

A pH shift can affect the stability of an injectable, especially a protein drug (biologic). Polypropylene itself is inert, but additives (antioxidants, stabilisers) can leach out and affect pH. This test confirms that the material is manufactured to specification.

Absorbance

Original Text

Absorbance (2.2.25). Maximum 0.40, determined between wavelengths of 220 nm and 360 nm on solution S.

Limit

Absorbance: Maximum 0.40

Wavelength range: 220–360 nm (UV)

Reference: Ph.Eur. 2.2.25 (Absorption Spectrophotometry, UV and Visible)

Tutorial Commentary

Significance of the UV Absorbance Test

220–360 nm is the ultraviolet range: Most organic compounds (including plastic additives, antioxidants, and light stabilisers) absorb in this band. High absorbance indicates the presence of organic leachables in the extract.

A maximum absorbance of 0.40 is a relatively generous limit that allows a reasonable degree of leaching from the material. The point is to pass the test, not to chase the lowest value.

Relationship with E&L Studies

This test is a routine release test. For high-risk drug products (such as protein injectables and high-concentration drugs), a full extractables / leachables (E&L) study is also needed, with broader chemical characterisation according to ISO 10993 or USP <661>.

Reducing Substances

Original Text

Reducing substances. To 20.0 mL solution S add 1 mL dilute sulfuric acid R and 20.0 mL of 0.002 M potassium permanganate. Boil under reflux 3 min, cool.

Add 1 g potassium iodide R, titrate with 0.01 M sodium thiosulfate, starch indicator. Blank titration on 20.0 mL water R.

Limit

Difference between sample and blank titrations: ≤ 3.0 mL

Titrant: 0.01 M sodium thiosulfate

Tutorial Commentary

Principle of the Reducing Substances Test

Back-titration with KMnO₄:

  1. KMnO₄ (a strong oxidant) reacts with reducing substances in the extract, and the KMnO₄ is consumed
  2. The remaining KMnO₄ oxidises KI to I₂
  3. The I₂ is titrated with sodium thiosulfate (starch as indicator)
  4. Difference between sample and blank = amount of KMnO₄ consumed by the reducing substances

GMP Significance

Reducing substances come mainly from plastic antioxidants (such as BHT and the Irganox series). When these leach into the drug solution, they may affect the stability of the active ingredient (API) or pose a toxicological risk for injectable use.

Analogy

This test is like a "food freshness check": a strong oxidant "burns off" everything that can spoil, and the amount consumed tells you how many "spoilage agents" (reducing leachables) the material released. A difference of ≤3.0 mL means the material is clean enough.

Ethylene Oxide Testing

Head-space GC-MS Method (2.2.28 + 2.2.43)

Key Limit — EtO Residuals

If ethylene oxide sterilisation is stated on the label:

EtO Residual Limit: ≤ 10 ppm (μg/g)

Reference standard: ISO 10993-7 (Biological evaluation — Ethylene oxide sterilisation residuals)

Sample Preparation

Original Text

Crush sample into fine particles. Weigh 0.10–3.00 g depending on expected EtO residue, into a 20 mL injection vial.

Low-EtO-releasing materials (e.g. COC/COPs, polyurethane, silicone): pre-thermal extraction at 120°C for ≥15 h.

Reference solutions a–h: dilutions from 1000 μg/mL EtO stock down to 25 μg/mL.

Reference solution j includes acetaldehyde (co-extractant) for system suitability.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Considerations in Sample Preparation

Grinding to fine particles: This increases surface area and ensures that EtO is fully released from the matrix into the headspace. The smaller the particle size, the higher the extraction efficiency.

Sample amount 0.10–3.00 g: This depends on the expected EtO concentration. Use a small amount for high-residue samples (to avoid exceeding the detection range) and a larger amount for low-residue samples (to increase sensitivity).

Thermal extraction for low-release materials (120°C, ≥15 h): In dense materials such as COC/COP, EtO diffuses slowly, so high-temperature pre-extraction is needed to ensure complete release of EtO into the headspace; otherwise the residue will be underestimated.

Role of Acetaldehyde

Reference solution j contains acetaldehyde for the system suitability test: the EtO and acetaldehyde peaks on the GC must achieve a resolution ≥1.5, confirming that the chromatographic system can separate the two compounds and avoiding interference with quantitation.

Chromatographic Conditions

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Advantages of SIM Mode

Selected Ion Monitoring (SIM):

Monitoring only specific m/z values greatly improves sensitivity and selectivity:

  • m/z = 44: The molecular ion of EtO, used for quantitation
  • m/z = 29: The CHO⁺ fragment ion, used for qualification (confirmation)
  • m/z = 15: The CH₃⁺ fragment, providing further confirmation of EtO identity

The ratios of the three ions (ion ratio) must fall within the permitted range to prevent false positives.

Analogy

SIM mode is like "DNA fingerprinting": you do not only look at the outline (m/z = 44) but also confirm that the other DNA markers (m/z = 29, 15) all match, so you can be sure it is "genuine" EtO and not interference from another compound of similar molecular weight.

System Suitability

Original Text

  • Resolution (Rs) ≥ 1.5 between EtO and acetaldehyde peaks in reference solution j
  • Signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) ≥ 10 for EtO in reference solution h (lowest calibration standard, 25 μg/mL)

Tutorial Commentary

Logic of the System Suitability Test

Rs ≥ 1.5: Ensures baseline separation of EtO from acetaldehyde, ruling out interference from acetaldehyde in EtO quantitation. If the resolution is insufficient, the EtO peak area may be overestimated.

S/N ≥ 10 at lowest standard: Confirms that the method is sensitive enough to detect EtO at concentrations near the limit (10 ppm). S/N ≥ 10 is the standard criterion for the limit of quantitation (LOQ).

Audit Preparation

An auditor may ask for:

  • SST (system suitability test) records, including the calculated Rs and S/N values
  • Calibration curve and linear range (r² value)
  • Method validation report (accuracy, precision, linearity, detection limit, quantitation limit)
  • Analyst training records
EtO Testing Decision Tree

When is EtO Testing Required?

Testing Required
  • The label declares EtO sterilisation
  • Label states "sterilised using ethylene oxide"
Testing Not Required
  • Other sterilisation methods are used (radiation, steam, filtration)
  • Non-EtO sterilisation methods used

Silicone Oil Testing

Extraction Method & Acceptance Limit (Ph.Eur. 3.1.8)

Key Limit — Silicone Oil

Silicone Oil Limit: ≤ 0.25 mg per cm² internal surface area

Confirmed by IR spectrophotometry (2.2.24) vs silicone oil CRS

Internal Surface Area Calculation

Original Text

Calculate internal surface area using 2V·π·h expression.

Take sufficient syringes for 100–200 cm² internal surface area.

Calculation Formula

Internal Surface Area (cm²) = 2 × V × π × h

Where:
V = Volume (mL)
h = Height/length of barrel (cm)
π = pi (3.14159)

For a cylindrical syringe barrel:
A = 2πr × L (lateral surface of cylinder)
  = πd × L
  = (V/L) × 2π × L = 2πr L

Tutorial Commentary

Why calculate by "area" rather than by "weight" or "volume"?

Silicone oil is applied uniformly on the inner barrel wall surface, so the amount used is essentially a surface coverage. It is expressed in mg/cm²:

  • It allows standardised comparison across syringes of different capacities (1 mL, 5 mL, 10 mL, 20 mL)
  • It corresponds directly to the spray amount in the siliconisation process
  • It can be converted to the total silicone oil per syringe (mg/syringe = mg/cm² × A)

Analogy

It is like calculating paint usage: whether you are painting a large room or a small one, you standardise as "litres of paint per square metre" rather than "total litres used for the whole house". The concept of silicone oil surface coverage is exactly the same.

Extraction Method

Original Text

Aspirate methylene chloride R equal to half nominal volume, make up with air, invert 10 times. Expel extracts, repeat, evaporate to dryness on water-bath, dry at 100–105°C for 1 h.

Confirm by IR spectrophotometry (2.2.24) vs silicone oil CRS.

Procedure Summary

  1. Calculate target internal surface area (100–200 cm²)
  2. Aspirate DCM (half nominal volume per syringe)
  3. Add air, invert 10× to extract silicone
  4. Expel extracts into collection vessel
  5. Repeat extraction step
  6. Evaporate DCM on water bath to dryness
  7. Dry residue at 100–105°C for 1 h
  8. Weigh residue → calculate mg/cm²
  9. Confirm identity by IR vs. silicone oil CRS

Tutorial Commentary

Scientific Logic of Methylene Chloride (DCM) Extraction

Methylene Chloride (DCM, CH₂Cl₂): Silicone oil (polydimethylsiloxane, PDMS) is highly soluble in non-polar organic solvents, making DCM an ideal extraction solvent.

Inverting 10 times: Ensures that the DCM contacts the whole inner barrel wall thoroughly, improving extraction efficiency.

Two extractions: First extraction removes majority of silicone; second extraction confirms complete removal (recovery check).

Importance of IR Confirmation

Weighing the evaporation residue gives only the "total amount". IR spectroscopy confirms that the residue is indeed silicone oil (PDMS characteristic peaks: Si-O-Si stretching ~1000–1100 cm⁻¹, Si-CH₃ bending ~1260 cm⁻¹), ruling out other non-silicone residues that would interfere with the weighing.

Special Considerations for Prefilled Syringes (PFS)

The particular challenge of silicone oil in PFS: silicone droplets may:

  • Cause aggregation of protein drugs (mAb, fusion protein)
  • Appear in USP <787>/<788> subvisible particle testing
  • Trigger FDA OOS/OOT investigations

Solution: Baked-on silicone or cross-linked silicone coating can reduce silicone migration.

Biological Tests

Transparency, Sterility, Bacterial Endotoxins, Pyrogens

Transparency

Original Text

Transparency. Fill a syringe with water R (blank), fill another with 10-fold dilution of primary opalescent suspension (2.2.1). Use suspension stored at 20±2°C for 24 h.

Compare with naked eye in diffuse light against dark background. Opalescence of suspension must be detectable vs blank.

Acceptance Criteria

The syringe barrel must be transparent enough that, in diffuse light against a dark background, the naked eye can distinguish:

  • Dosage markings
  • Air bubbles
  • Foreign particles
  • The primary opalescent suspension (diluted 10 times) is visible

Tutorial Commentary

Design Logic of the Transparency Test

A primary opalescent suspension diluted 10 times is used as the positive control: if the opalescence of this suspension cannot be distinguished through the syringe in diffuse light, the material is not transparent enough to meet visual inspection needs.

Diffuse light + dark background: Simulates the visual check conditions before clinical use, ensuring that healthcare staff can spot abnormalities in an ordinary clinical setting.

Link to Visual Inspection (100% VI)

Syringe transparency directly affects the feasibility of 100% visual inspection. If opaque or translucent materials are used (such as some coloured PP), an alternative CCI testing approach must be provided and agreed with the competent authority.

Sterility (2.6.1)

Original Text

Sterility (2.6.1):

Case A — Sterile internally and externally: Pass 50 mL buffered sodium chloride-peptone pH 7.0 (2.6.12) through the set; membrane filtration method. Or direct inoculation method.

Case B — Syringes stated sterile: Open package aseptically, withdraw syringe, separate components, place each in culture medium to cover completely. Use both recommended media.

Case C — Sterile internally only: Use 50 mL inoculation medium per syringe. Flush 5 times by withdrawing plunger to fullest extent.

Acceptance Criteria

No microbial growth detected after incubation

Reference: Ph.Eur. 2.6.1 — Sterility

Tutorial Commentary

Membrane Filtration vs Direct Inoculation

Membrane filtration (preferred): Suitable for large-volume samples; more liquid can be filtered, giving higher statistical sensitivity. It must be confirmed that membrane filtration does not affect microbial survival (bacteriostasis/fungistasis test).

Direct inoculation: Suitable where membrane filtration is not possible (such as highly viscous solutions and oily products).

Bacterial Endotoxins (2.6.14)

Original Text

Bacterial endotoxins (2.6.14). Less than 0.5 IU/mL, determined on 10 syringes filled with 40 mL water for BET (unless otherwise justified and authorised).

Key Limit

Bacterial Endotoxins: < 0.5 IU/mL

Test volume: 40 mL water for BET (Bacterial Endotoxin Testing)

Sample size: 10 syringes

Method: LAL test (2.6.14)

Tutorial Commentary

Basis for Setting the Endotoxin Limit

0.5 IU/mL: This limit is based on the worst-case use condition of the syringe as a drug-contact container. For intravenous drug products, the endotoxin limit is usually calculated with the K/M formula:

Limit = K / M
K = 5 EU/kg/h (IV route)
M = Maximum dose rate (mL/kg/h)

Choice of LAL (Limulus Amebocyte Lysate) Test Method

Ph.Eur. 2.6.14 allows three LAL methods:

  • Gel-clot: Semi-quantitative, the most traditional
  • Turbidimetric: Quantitative, high throughput
  • Chromogenic: Quantitative, with precise optical readout

Modern CDMOs mostly use chromogenic or recombinant Factor C (rFC) methods to avoid the problem of horseshoe crab lysate supply shortages.

Pyrogens (2.6.8)

Original Text

Pyrogens (2.6.8). Connect together 5 sets and pass at ≤10 mL/min, 250 mL sterile pyrogen-free 9 g/L sodium chloride R. Collect aseptically in pyrogen-free container. Inject 10 mL/kg rabbit mass.

Note: This test applies to complete administration sets / infusion sets (multi-component assemblies). For individual syringes, bacterial endotoxin testing (2.6.14) is the primary method.

Tutorial Commentary

The 3Rs Principle and Regulatory Trends

Following ICH Q4B Annex 14 and the direction of European Pharmacopoeia updates, BET (bacterial endotoxins test) has been established as the preferred method, and the pyrogen test is used only when BET cannot be applied. Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 is designed on this principle.

Labelling

EtO Sterilisation Declaration & ISO 15223 Symbols

Original Text

Labelling. The label states, where applicable, that the syringe/set has been sterilised using ethylene oxide.

Conditions That Trigger the Labelling Requirement

A declaration on the label is required only when the syringe is sterilised with ethylene oxide (EtO). Other sterilisation methods (such as gamma irradiation, electron beam (E-beam), and steam) have their own ISO symbols, but this chapter prescribes the declaration obligation for EtO only.

Tutorial Commentary

GMP Significance of EtO Sterilisation Labelling

For syringes declared as EtO sterilised, the pharmaceutical manufacturer must at incoming inspection:

  • Perform EtO residue testing (or require the supplier CoA to include EtO test results)
  • Confirm that the aeration / degassing time has been completed and EtO is ≤10 ppm
  • Track the EtO sterilisation batch records (sterilisation cycle records)

The Label Is a Commitment

Declaring "EtO sterilised" on the label is like a food label saying "contains peanuts": once declared, you must be able to provide the corresponding test data and process control records to support it. If the declaration does not match reality, it is a major GMP deficiency.

GMP Context & Audit Readiness

Sterile Fill-Finish Operations

Why Single-Use Matters in Aseptic Fill-Finish

Technical Background

In aseptic filling operations, the syringe, as a primary packaging component, is in direct contact with the drug solution, and its quality directly affects:

  • Sterility assurance: The sterility of the syringe is the prerequisite for the sterility of the final product
  • Compatibility: Long-term compatibility of the material with the drug solution, preventing leachables from affecting drug stability
  • Functionality: The uniformity of glide force directly affects the accuracy of automated filling equipment
  • Container closure integrity (CCI): The needle seal and plunger seal of prefilled syringes require 100% verification

"Not to be re-used" is not only a pharmacopoeial requirement but also one of the core principles of FDA cGMP 21 CFR 211 and EU GMP Annex 1.

Audit Focus Areas

CDMO Incoming Inspection Audit Checklist

  • Supplier qualification records (Qualified Supplier List, QSL)
  • Certificate of Analysis (CoA) covering the full set of Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 tests
  • Declaration of Conformity (DoC) per EU MDR 2017/745
  • Material change control tracking records
  • EtO residue test results (if applicable)
  • Silicone oil content test results
  • Endotoxin test results (<0.5 IU/mL)
  • Sterility test results (Ph.Eur. 2.6.1)
  • Sterilisation method and batch records
  • Dimensional compliance to ISO 11040/11608
EtO Residual Monitoring & Release Testing

Regulatory Framework

Practical Guidance

EtO Release Testing Workflow

  1. The supplier completes EtO residue testing before shipment (end-of-aeration testing)
  2. The CoA includes the GC-MS test report (batch number, test date, results)
  3. After receipt, the pharmaceutical manufacturer takes acceptance samples and confirms using the Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 method
  4. If the supplier can provide full-batch testing (100% testing), skip-lot testing may be considered
  5. Abnormal results trigger an OOS investigation and a return procedure (deviation / NCR)
Silicone Oil as a CQA for Prefilled Syringes

Scientific Background

Effects of Silicone Oil on Protein Drugs

The interaction between silicone oil (PDMS) and protein drugs (biologics) has been widely studied. The main risks are:

  • Protein adsorption: The silicone oil-water interface can adsorb proteins, altering protein higher-order structure (HOS) and triggering aggregation
  • Subvisible particles: Silicone oil droplets can act as nucleation sites for protein aggregation
  • Immunogenicity: Protein-silicone oil particle aggregates may trigger an immune response

Control Strategy

Key Points for Controlling Silicone Oil as a CQA

  • Specification setting: Per Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 ≤0.25 mg/cm², plus a tighter internal specification (such as ≤0.15 mg/cm²)
  • Process control: Silicone oil spray weight + curing conditions (baked-on)
  • Batch-to-batch consistency: Establish an SPC (statistical process control) chart for the silicone oil amount
  • Compatibility study: Long-term stability of silicone oil + drug solution (6–24 months)
  • Particle monitoring: USP <787> / <788> subvisible particles (<10 μm, <25 μm)

Final Audit Readiness Tips

  • Documentation traceability: The CoA for each batch of syringes must be retained for at least 1 year after the product expiry date (per EU GMP Annex 16 and 21 CFR 211.68)
  • Supplier audits: Conduct an on-site audit of syringe suppliers at least once every 3 years, with the audit focusing on sterilisation process validation reports and EtO aeration records
  • Change control: Any change in material, supplier, or process (including silicone oil brand or siliconisation process) must go through the CMC change notification process, and compatibility with Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 must be re-evaluated
  • Trend analysis: Establish SPC tracking of batch-to-batch trends in silicone oil content, EtO residue, endotoxin, and so on, to prevent problems before they occur
  • Knowledge management: Make sure all QC analysts are familiar with the Ph.Eur. 3.3.8 methods, especially the GC-MS SST requirements and the silicone oil surface area calculation
