Foreword — The Development Background of ISO 13485:2016

Original Text

Foreword

ISO (the International Organization for Standardization) is a worldwide federation of national standards bodies (ISO member bodies). The work of preparing International Standards is normally carried out through ISO technical committees.

The committee responsible for this document is Technical Committee ISO/TC 210, Quality management and corresponding general aspects for medical devices.

This third edition of ISO 13485 cancels and replaces the second edition (ISO 13485:2003) and ISO/TR 14969:2004, which have been technically revised. It also incorporates the Technical Corrigendum ISO 13485:2003/Cor.1:2009. A summary of the changes incorporated into this edition compared with the previous edition is given in Annex A.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: The Responsibilities of ISO/TC 210

ISO/TC 210 (Technical Committee 210) is responsible for international standards on "quality management and corresponding general aspects for medical devices". Unlike TC 84, which deals with injection devices, TC 210 focuses on the quality management system architecture of the medical device industry as a whole.

Main changes in the third edition (2016):

  • It incorporates the content of ISO/TR 14969:2004 (the implementation guidance document)
  • It strengthens the requirements for a risk-based approach (the risk-based approach appears in several clauses)
  • It more explicitly requires the organization to document its own role under the applicable regulatory requirements (manufacturer / authorized representative / importer / distributor)
  • It adds requirements for the control of outsourced processes (4.1.5), including written quality agreements

Key Point: 2003 Edition vs. 2016 Edition

If a customer's QMS certification is based on the 2003 edition, note the following differences on upgrading:

  • The 2016 edition adds 4.1.6: validation of QMS software (electronic batch records, ERP systems, etc. must be validated)
  • The 2016 edition explicitly requires the Medical Device File (4.2.3) as a stand-alone documentation requirement (the 2003 edition had no such explicit clause)
  • 5.6.2 adds "new or revised regulatory requirements" as a mandatory input to management review
  • ISO/TR 14969 (the implementation guidance) has been withdrawn, and its intent has been integrated into the main text
Introduction 0.1 — General: The Design Intent of the Standard

Original Text

0.1 General

This International Standard specifies requirements for a quality management system that can be used by an organization involved in one or more stages of the life-cycle of a medical device, including design and development, production, storage and distribution, installation, servicing and final decommissioning and disposal of medical devices, and design and development, or provision of associated activities (e.g. technical support).

The requirements in this International Standard can also be used by suppliers or other external parties providing product (e.g. raw materials, components, subassemblies, medical devices, sterilization services, calibration services, distribution services, maintenance services) to such organizations.

Several jurisdictions have regulatory requirements for the application of quality management systems by organizations with a variety of roles in the supply chain for medical devices. Consequently, this International Standard expects that the organization:

  • identifies its role(s) under applicable regulatory requirements;
  • identifies the regulatory requirements that apply to its activities under these roles;
  • incorporates these applicable regulatory requirements within its quality management system.

The adoption of a quality management system is a strategic decision of an organization. The design and implementation of an organization's quality management system is influenced by:

  • organizational environment, changes in that environment;
  • organization's varying needs and particular objectives;
  • product the organization provides and processes employed;
  • organization's size and organizational structure;
  • regulatory requirements applicable to the organization's activities.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: The Scope of ISO 13485 Is Much Broader Than You Think

The standard explicitly states that it applies to organizations involved in "one or more stages of the life cycle", which means:

  • Design and development (R&D companies)
  • Production (manufacturers, CDMOs)
  • Storage and distribution (3PL / warehousing providers)
  • Installation and servicing (equipment suppliers)
  • Suppliers of raw materials / components / sterilization services (second-tier supply chain)

For a CDMO, ISO 13485 certification is both a customer requirement (supply chain audit) and a passport to high-end markets (EU CE marking/MDR, US FDA QSR).

Analogy: Why Identifying Your Role Matters

The standard requires the organization to "identify its role under the applicable regulatory requirements". It is like applying for a food license: you must first determine whether you are a "food manufacturer", a "food repackager" or a "contract producer". Each role carries different regulatory obligations.

A CDMO may play several roles at once across different customer relationships:

  • CMO (Contract Manufacturing Organization) → Manufacturer role
  • Sterilization service provider → outsourced service provider role
  • Component supplier → supplier role

Each role corresponds to different QMS documentation and regulatory obligations, and they must be clearly distinguished in the QMS scope statement.

Introduction 0.2–0.5 — Clarification of Concepts, Process Approach and Relationship with ISO 9001

Original Text

0.2 Clarification of concepts

In this International Standard, the following terms or phrases are used in the context described below.

  • When a requirement is qualified by the phrase "as appropriate", it is deemed to be appropriate unless the organization can justify otherwise.
  • When the term "risk" is used, the application pertains to safety or performance requirements of the medical device or meeting applicable regulatory requirements.
  • When a requirement is required to be "documented", it is also required to be established, implemented and maintained.
  • When the term "product" is used, it can also mean "service".
  • When the term "regulatory requirements" is used, it encompasses requirements contained in any law applicable to the user of this International Standard (e.g. statutes, regulations, ordinances or directives).

Verbal forms used in this Standard:

  • "shall" indicates a requirement
  • "should" indicates a recommendation
  • "may" indicates a permission
  • "can" indicates a possibility or a capability

0.3 Process approach

This International Standard is based on a process approach to quality management. Any activity that receives input and converts it to output can be considered as a process. The "process approach" refers to the application of a system of processes within an organization, together with the identification and interactions of these processes, and their management to produce the desired outcome.

0.4 Relationship with ISO 9001

While this is a stand-alone standard, it is based on ISO 9001:2008. This International Standard includes some particular requirements for organizations involved in the life-cycle of medical devices and excludes some of the requirements of ISO 9001 that are not appropriate as regulatory requirements. Because of these exclusions, organizations whose quality management systems conform to this International Standard cannot claim conformity to ISO 9001 unless their quality management system meets all the requirements of ISO 9001.

0.5 Compatibility with other management systems

This International Standard does not include requirements specific to other management systems, such as those particular to environmental management, occupational health and safety management, or financial management. However, it enables an organization to align or integrate its own quality management system with related management system requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: The Three Meanings of "documented"

In ISO 13485, "documented" is a heavyweight word, and auditors are particularly sensitive to it. The standard states explicitly:

documented = established + implemented + maintained

In practice: if a clause requires a "documented procedure", the auditor does not just want to see an SOP. He or she will also ask:

  • Has the procedure been formally approved (established)?
  • Do employees actually follow the procedure (implemented)?
  • Is the document reviewed and updated periodically (maintained)?

All three conditions must be met; none can be omitted.

Analogy: The Practical Meaning of "shall" vs. "should"

In the language of ISO standards, this distinction is critical:

"shall" (requirement): like "must" in a legal text, failing to comply in an audit is a nonconformity.

"should" (recommendation): like "ought to" in a legal text, non-compliance is possible, but a reasonable justification is needed.

ISO 13485 has a much higher density of "shall" than ISO 9001, which reflects the mandatory regulatory nature of the medical device industry. An engineer who can clearly distinguish shall from should comes across as more professional in front of auditors.

Practice Questions

  1. A customer asks a CDMO to provide ISO 13485 certification. A colleague says, "We have ISO 9001 certification, so that should count as ISO 13485 compliance too." According to clause 0.4, is this statement correct? Why?
  2. A procedure in the QMS documentation uses the wording "should review annually". If an auditor finds that it has not been reviewed for three years, does this constitute a nonconformity?
  3. A CDMO has taken on an outsourced sterilization business. According to clause 0.1, which roles under the regulations does this CDMO need to identify for itself?

Concept Analysis: Key Differences Between ISO 13485 and ISO 9001

Key point: Conforming to ISO 13485 does not mean conforming to ISO 9001, and vice versa. They are parallel, independent standards.

Clause 1: Scope — Applicability and Exclusions

Original Text

1 Scope

This International Standard specifies requirements for a quality management system where an organization needs to demonstrate its ability to provide medical devices and related services that consistently meet customer and applicable regulatory requirements.

Such organizations can be involved in one or more stages of the life-cycle, including design and development, production, storage and distribution, installation, or servicing of a medical device and design and development or provision of associated activities (e.g. technical support). This International Standard can also be used by suppliers or external parties that provide product, including quality management system-related services to such organizations.

Requirements of this International Standard are applicable to organizations regardless of their size and regardless of their type except where explicitly stated.

The processes required by this International Standard that are applicable to the organization, but are not performed by the organization, are the responsibility of the organization and are accounted for in the organization's quality management system by monitoring, maintaining, and controlling the processes.

"If applicable regulatory requirements permit exclusions of design and development controls, this can be used as a justification for their exclusion from the quality management system."

If any requirement in Clauses 6, 7 or 8 is not applicable due to the activities undertaken by the organization or the nature of the medical device, the organization does not need to include such a requirement in its quality management system. For any clause that is determined to be not applicable, the organization records the justification as described in 4.2.2.

2 Normative References

The following documents are normatively referenced and are indispensable for its application:

ISO 9000:2015 — Quality management systems — Fundamentals and vocabulary

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: The Core Purpose of the Scope Statement

The Scope of ISO 13485 contains a key phrase: the organization needs to "demonstrate its ability to provide… that consistently meet customer and applicable regulatory requirements".

Note these three keywords:

  • demonstrate: it is not enough for the QMS to "be done"; it must be proven with documents and records
  • consistently: not compliance now and then, but repeatable, predictable compliance
  • applicable regulatory: different markets have different regulations, and you need to identify which ones apply

Key Point: The Design and Development Exclusion

ISO 13485 allows design and development controls (Clause 7.3) to be excluded under certain conditions, which is very important for a pure manufacturing CDMO:

  • Conditions for exclusion: the applicable regulatory requirements permit the exclusion, and the organization does not carry out design and development activities
  • Record requirement: the justification for the exclusion must be documented in the quality manual (4.2.2)
  • CDMO practice: if a CDMO is responsible only for production/filling and the customer owns the design, it can reasonably exclude 7.3 from the QMS scope, but the decision must be supported by written documentation

An auditor will not necessarily require you to do design and development, but will always ask you to explain why it is excluded.

Analogy: Who Is Responsible for Outsourced Processes

The standard says an outsourced process is not performed by the organization but remains the organization's responsibility. It is like a developer subcontracting the plumbing and electrical work: after awarding the contract you cannot walk away, and the developer (the party that outsourced) is still responsible for the finished building.

In the CDMO context: if a CDMO outsources sterilization to a third-party sterilizer, that sterilization process must be brought into the CDMO's QMS monitoring framework (4.1.5 requires a written quality agreement), and the auditor will look at how the CDMO monitors the outsourced sterilizer.

Clause 3: Terms and Definitions (3.1–3.10) — Core Terms (Part 1)

Original Text

3 Terms and Definitions

For the purposes of this document, the terms and definitions given in ISO 9000:2015 and the following apply.

3.1 advisory notice

Notice issued by the organization, subsequent to delivery of the medical device, to provide supplementary information or to advise on action to be taken in the: use of a medical device, modification of a medical device, return of the medical device to the organization that supplied it, or destruction of a medical device.

Note 1: Issuance of an advisory notice can be required to comply with applicable regulatory requirements.

3.2 authorized representative

Natural or legal person established within a country or jurisdiction who has received a written mandate from the manufacturer to act on his behalf for specified tasks with regard to the latter's obligations under that country or jurisdiction's legislation.

[SOURCE: GHTF/SG1/N055:2009]

3.3 clinical evaluation

Assessment and analysis of clinical data pertaining to a medical device to verify the clinical safety and performance of the device when used as intended by the manufacturer.

[SOURCE: GHTF/SG5/N4:2010]

3.4 complaint

Written, electronic or oral communication that alleges deficiencies related to the identity, quality, durability, reliability, usability, safety or performance of a medical device that has been released from the organization's control or related to a service that affects the performance of such medical devices.

Note 1: This definition of "complaint" differs from the definition given in ISO 9000:2015.

3.5 distributor

Natural or legal person in the supply chain who, on his own behalf, furthers the availability of a medical device to the end user.

Note 1: More than one distributor may be involved in the supply chain. Note 2: Persons involved in storage and transport on behalf of the manufacturer, importer or distributor, are not distributors under this definition.

3.6 implantable medical device

Medical device which can only be removed by medical or surgical intervention and which is intended to be totally or partially introduced into the human body or a natural orifice, or replace an epithelial surface or the surface of the eye, and remain after the procedure for at least 30 days.

Note 1: This definition includes active implantable medical device.

3.7 importer

Natural or legal person in the supply chain who is the first in a supply chain to make a medical device, manufactured in another country or jurisdiction, available in the country or jurisdiction where it is to be marketed.

[SOURCE: GHTF/SG1/N055:2009]

3.8 labelling

Label, instructions for use, and any other information that is related to identification, technical description, intended purpose and proper use of the medical device, but excluding shipping documents.

[SOURCE: GHTF/SG1/N70:2011]

3.9 life-cycle

All phases in the life of a medical device, from the initial conception to final decommissioning and disposal.

[SOURCE: ISO 14971:2007, 2.7]

3.10 manufacturer

Natural or legal person with responsibility for design and/or manufacture of a medical device with the intention of making the medical device available for use, under his name; whether or not such a medical device is designed and/or manufactured by that person himself or on his behalf by another person(s).

Note 1: Has ultimate legal responsibility for ensuring compliance with all applicable regulatory requirements. Note 5: Any person who changes the intended use of, or modifies, a medical device without acting on behalf of the original manufacturer and makes it available under his own name, should be considered the manufacturer.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: Advisory Notice (3.1) — More Than a Recall Notice

An Advisory Notice is a broader concept than a "recall" and covers four types of action:

  • Supplementary information on use (modification of the IFU)
  • Modification of the device (e.g., a firmware update)
  • Return of the device (effectively a recall)
  • Destruction of the device

In post-market surveillance, the Advisory Notice procedure is triggered when the result of a CAPA investigation requires notifying devices already on the market. The FDA's Field Safety Corrective Action (FSCA) and the EU MDR's Field Safety Notice (FSN) both fall essentially into this category.

Concept Analysis: Authorized Representative (3.2) — The Regulatory Role of the EU AR

The EU MDR requires a non-EU manufacturer to appoint an EU authorized representative (EU AR), which is a direct implementation of ISO 13485 term 3.2.

For a CDMO: if the customer is a US company (non-EU) and wants to sell in the EU a device made by the CDMO, the customer (the manufacturer) must have an EU AR. The CDMO does not usually take on this role, but the identity and mandate documents of the EU AR should be confirmed in the technology transfer documentation.

Key Point: The Legal Responsibility Logic of Manufacturer (3.10)

The definition of "Manufacturer" has 7 Notes, and the core concept is:

Manufacturer = the legally responsible person who makes a device available under their own name, regardless of who actually manufactures it.

This is very important for a CDMO:

  • CDMO manufactures for a customer → the customer is the Manufacturer and the CDMO is a supplier
  • CDMO sells the contract-manufactured product under its own brand → the CDMO becomes the Manufacturer and bears full regulatory responsibility
  • Note 5 warns: if a CDMO changes the intended use of a device and sells it under its own name → it automatically becomes the Manufacturer

Analogy: Distributor (3.5) vs. a Logistics Company

Note 2 specifically states: a person who provides storage and transport on behalf of a manufacturer or importer is not a Distributor.

Analogy: the logistics company that delivers for a convenience store chain is not the "distributor"; the chain is. The logistics company only carries out the transport task, while the chain sells under its own name.

In the medical device supply chain, this distinction affects the scope of the QMS: a pure logistics provider does not need to meet the Distributor requirements of ISO 13485, but must comply with the relevant storage/transport regulations.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: The Core Distinguishing Principle of Medical Device (3.11)

The most important phrase in the definition: "does not achieve its primary intended action by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means". This is the legal dividing line between a device and a medicinal product.

This is exactly where the challenge of combination products lies:

  • Prefilled syringe (PFS) + drug → primary action is achieved by the drug (pharmacological) → usually classified as drug-led
  • Drug-eluting stent → stent (device) + drug → the primary mode of action must be determined → classification may be as a device
  • Blood glucose meter → provides information through physical measurement → medical device

For a CDMO, a combination product brought by a customer needs its regulatory classification confirmed first, before the applicable regulatory requirements can be determined.

Concept Analysis: Medical Device Family (3.12) and Its Link to the MDF

In 4.2.3, ISO 13485 requires the establishment and maintenance of one or more "Medical Device Files (MDF)" for each medical device type or medical device family.

The definition of "Medical Device Family" directly affects the number of MDFs:

  • If a CDMO makes syringes in 5 sizes for the same customer (same basic design, same intended use), the 5 can be grouped into one family, so a single MDF may be shared
  • If the design or intended use differs (e.g., a syringe vs. an infusion set), separate MDFs are required

Concept Analysis: Risk (3.17) — A Major Difference from ISO 9001

ISO 13485 adopts the ISO 14971 definition of risk (the combination of the probability of occurrence of harm × the severity of that harm), rather than the general risk thinking of ISO 9001.

This difference in a single term reflects the essentially different nature of the two standards:

  • "Risk" in ISO 9001: business risk, process risk, opportunity management
  • "Risk" in ISO 13485: patient safety, device performance, regulatory compliance

When writing an FMEA or risk assessment, a CDMO must use the ISO 14971 framework (severity × probability × detectability), not the simplified risk matrix of ISO 9001.

Key Point: What the Sterile Barrier System (3.19) Means for Sterile Manufacturing

The definition is taken from ISO 11607-1, the dedicated standard for "packaging for terminally sterilized medical devices".

A Sterile Barrier System (SBS) is the "minimum package that prevents ingress of microorganisms and allows aseptic presentation of the contents at the point of use". For a sterile CDMO:

  • The primary packaging of a PFS (a syringe with closure) = SBS
  • SBS integrity testing (package integrity validation) falls within the scope of the 7.5.7 requirements
  • SBS design must comply with the ISO 11607 series, which is not specified within ISO 13485, but 7.5.7 of ISO 13485 requires the related records

Practice Questions

  1. A CDMO makes three prefilled syringes at the same time (1 mL, 2 mL, 5 mL) with different sizes but the same design principle and intended use. According to the definition of medical device family in 3.12, can these three products share one Medical Device File? What factors need to be considered?
  2. The definition of Post-Market Surveillance (3.14) requires "systematically collecting and analyzing post-market experience". Within a CDMO's QMS, which data sources can serve as PMS inputs? How are responsibilities divided between the CDMO and the brand-owner customer?
  3. A colleague says: "The risk assessment in our ERP system uses the ISO 9001 risk matrix, so an ISO 13485 auditor should accept it too." Based on the definition in 3.17 and the overall architecture of ISO 13485, is this judgment a problem?

Section Summary

  • ISO 13485 is an independent standard, not a subset of ISO 9001: it deliberately omits the "continual improvement" requirement and adds medical device-specific requirements (MDF, advisory notice, sterile devices, etc.). Conforming to ISO 13485 does not mean conforming to ISO 9001.
  • Language conventions are critical: "shall" = mandatory requirement; "documented" = established + implemented + maintained as one package; "as appropriate" = applies by default unless you can justify otherwise.
  • The standard applies across the entire supply chain: from design and development to service provision, including CDMOs, outsourced sterilizers and component suppliers; each party needs to identify its regulatory role and integrate it into the QMS.
  • 20 core terms: pay particular attention to Manufacturer (the legally responsible person), Advisory Notice (the broadest definition of post-market action), Risk (the ISO 14971 framework, not general business risk) and Sterile Barrier System (a core concept of the ISO 11607 system).
  • Design and development exclusion: a pure manufacturing CDMO may exclude Clause 7.3 where justified, but the justification must be documented in the Quality Manual and accepted by the regulator.

Clause 4.1 — General Requirements for the Quality Management System

Original Text

4 Quality management system

4.1 General requirements

4.1.1

The organization shall document a quality management system and maintain its effectiveness in accordance with the requirements of this International Standard and applicable regulatory requirements.

The organization shall establish, implement and maintain any requirement, procedure, activity or arrangement required to be documented by this International Standard or applicable regulatory requirements.

The organization shall document the role(s) undertaken by the organization under the applicable regulatory requirements.

NOTE: Roles can include manufacturer, authorized representative, importer or distributor.

4.1.2

The organization shall:

  • determine the processes needed for the QMS and their application throughout the organization taking into account the roles undertaken by the organization;
  • apply a risk based approach to the control of the appropriate processes needed for the QMS;
  • determine the sequence and interaction of these processes.
4.1.3

For each QMS process, the organization shall:

  • determine criteria and methods to ensure operation and control are effective;
  • ensure the availability of resources and information;
  • implement actions necessary to achieve planned results;
  • monitor, measure as appropriate, and analyse these processes;
  • establish and maintain records needed to demonstrate conformance (see 4.2.5).
4.1.4

Changes to QMS processes shall be:

  • evaluated for their impact on the quality management system;
  • evaluated for their impact on the medical devices produced;
  • controlled in accordance with the requirements of this International Standard and applicable regulatory requirements.
4.1.5

When the organization chooses to outsource any process that affects product conformity to requirements, it shall monitor and ensure control over such processes. The organization shall retain responsibility of conformity to this International Standard and to customer and applicable regulatory requirements for outsourced processes. The controls shall be proportionate to the risk involved and the ability of the external party to meet the requirements in accordance with 7.4. The controls shall include written quality agreements.

4.1.6

The organization shall document procedures for the validation of the application of computer software used in the quality management system. Such software applications shall be validated prior to initial use and, as appropriate, after changes to such software or its application. The specific approach and activities associated with software validation and revalidation shall be proportionate to the risk associated with the use of the software.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: The Three-Layer Logic of 4.1

The structure of Clause 4.1 is:

  • 4.1.1: the basic obligation to establish a QMS + identifying the organization's role
  • 4.1.2–4.1.3: process identification and control methods (the PDCA cycle framework)
  • 4.1.4: change control (any change to QMS processes must be evaluated for its impact)
  • 4.1.5: control of outsourced processes (responsibility cannot be outsourced)
  • 4.1.6: validation of QMS software (an important requirement added in the 2016 edition)

For a CDMO serving multiple national markets, the 4.1.1 requirement to "document the role(s) undertaken by the organization under the applicable regulatory requirements" means that the QMS scope statement for each customer/market must clearly list the applicable regulations (FDA 21 CFR Part 820, EU MDR, PMDA, etc.).

Key Point: 4.1.5 Outsourcing Control — The Most Easily Overlooked Requirement

The core logic of 4.1.5: you can outsource the performance, but you cannot outsource the responsibility.

The questions auditors ask most often about outsourcing control:

  • Is there a Written Quality Agreement?
  • Does the quality agreement specify performance indicators and audit rights?
  • Are the controls proportionate to the risk (higher-risk outsourcing = stricter controls)?
  • Has the outsourced provider been qualified in accordance with 7.4 (purchasing)?

Common failure: after outsourcing sterilization, the CDMO relies only on the sterilizer's CoC (Certificate of Compliance) and does not independently review the sterilization validation data → this is a Major NC against 4.1.5.

Concept Analysis: 4.1.6 Validation of QMS Software — A New Requirement in the 2016 Edition

This is one of the most important additions in the 2016 edition compared with the 2003 edition. The scope covers:

  • Electronic batch record systems (eBMR/EBR)
  • LIMS (laboratory information management systems)
  • Quality modules in ERP (SAP QM, etc.)
  • Statistical analysis software (Minitab, JMP, etc.) used for QC release decisions
  • Document management systems (DMS/EDMS)

The scope of validation must be proportionate to the risk of the software's use; systems that affect release decisions require the most rigorous IQ/OQ/PQ validation.

Analogy: Risk-Based Process Control

4.1.2 requires a "risk-based approach" to be applied to QMS processes. It is like safety management at an airline:

The pre-flight engine check (a high-risk process) requires the strictest verification procedure, while adjusting a seat back (a low-risk process) does not need the same level of control.

In a CDMO's QMS this means: process controls for aseptic filling (which directly affects patient safety) are far stricter than those for expense reimbursement, and documentation requirements, monitoring frequency and personnel qualification requirements all increase with the risk level.

Clause 4.2 — Documentation Requirements: The Complete Structure from Manual to Records

Original Text

4.2 Documentation requirements

4.2.1 General

The QMS documentation shall include:

  • documented statements of a quality policy and quality objectives;
  • a quality manual;
  • documented procedures and records required by this International Standard;
  • documents, including records, determined by the organization to be necessary to ensure the effective planning, operation, and control of its processes;
  • other documentation specified by applicable regulatory requirements.

4.2.2 Quality manual

The organization shall document a quality manual that includes:

  • the scope of the quality management system, including details of and justification for any exclusion or non-application;
  • the documented procedures for the quality management system, or reference to them;
  • a description of the interaction between the processes of the quality management system.

The quality manual shall outline the structure of the documentation used in the quality management system.

4.2.3 Medical device file

For each medical device type or medical device family, the organization shall establish and maintain one or more files either containing or referencing documents generated to demonstrate conformity to the requirements of this International Standard and compliance with applicable regulatory requirements.

The content of the file(s) shall include, but is not limited to:

  • general description of the medical device, intended use/purpose, and labelling, including any instructions for use;
  • specifications for product;
  • specifications or procedures for manufacturing, packaging, storage, handling and distribution;
  • procedures for measuring and monitoring;
  • as appropriate, requirements for installation;
  • as appropriate, procedures for servicing.

4.2.4 Control of documents

Documents required by the QMS shall be controlled. A documented procedure shall define the controls needed to:

  • a) review and approve documents for adequacy prior to issue;
  • b) review, update as necessary and re-approve documents;
  • c) ensure that the current revision status of and changes to documents are identified;
  • d) ensure that relevant versions of applicable documents are available at points of use;
  • e) ensure that documents remain legible and readily identifiable;
  • f) ensure that documents of external origin are identified and their distribution controlled;
  • g) prevent deterioration or loss of documents;
  • h) prevent the unintended use of obsolete documents and apply suitable identification to them.

The organization shall define the period for which at least one copy of obsolete documents shall be retained. This period shall ensure that documents to which medical devices have been manufactured and tested are available for at least the lifetime of the medical device, but not less than the retention period of any resulting record, or as specified by applicable regulatory requirements.

4.2.5 Control of records

Records shall be maintained to provide evidence of conformity to requirements and of the effective operation of the QMS. The organization shall document procedures to define controls needed for identification, storage, security and integrity, retrieval, retention time and disposition of records.

The organization shall define and implement methods for protecting confidential health information contained in records in accordance with applicable regulatory requirements.

Records shall remain legible, readily identifiable and retrievable. Changes to a record shall remain identifiable.

The organization shall retain records for at least the lifetime of the medical device as defined by the organization, or as specified by applicable regulatory requirements, but not less than two years from the medical device release by the organization.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept Analysis: Medical Device File (4.2.3) — The Core Innovation of ISO 13485

The MDF is the most distinctive requirement of ISO 13485 relative to ISO 9001. It is not a single document but a collection of documents:

  • Device description + intended use + labeling (including IFU)
  • Product specifications (materials, dimensions, performance)
  • Procedures for manufacturing, packaging, storage and distribution
  • Measuring and monitoring procedures
  • Installation requirements (if applicable)
  • Servicing procedures (if applicable)

The MDF concept corresponds closely to the FDA QSR Device History Record (DHR) + Device Master Record (DMR) system and to the Technical Documentation of the EU MDR.

What it means for a CDMO: a CDMO usually does not own the MDF (the customer does), but it needs to contribute manufacturing-related documents to the MDF (item c) and ensure that its own production procedures map to the requirements of the customer's MDF.

Key Point: The 8 Controls for Control of Documents — Audit Hot Spots

4.2.4 lists 8 requirements (a–h) for control of documents, and each one is an audit observation point:

  • a) Approval before issue: a new SOP version used without approval → Nonconformity
  • b) Review and re-approval: an SOP not reviewed beyond its defined cycle since the last revision → Observation
  • c) Identification of the current version: a document lacking a version identifier or revision history → Nonconformity
  • d) Availability at points of use: an obsolete SOP version used on the shop floor → Major NC
  • e) Legible and readily identifiable: a work instruction with illegible writing → Observation
  • f) Control of external documents: a customer specification not entered in the document list → Nonconformity
  • g) Prevention of deterioration or loss: electronic documents with no backup mechanism → Observation
  • h) Prevention of unintended use of obsolete documents: an old SOP still on the shop floor without an "obsolete" mark → Major NC

Concept Analysis: The Logic for Calculating Record Retention Periods

The record retention requirement in 4.2.5 is the longest of three conditions:

  1. The lifetime of the device as defined by the organization
  2. At least 2 years from the date of release of the device
  3. The retention period required by applicable regulations

Practical references:

  • FDA 21 CFR 820.180: retain records for at least 2 years or the expected life of the device, whichever is longer
  • EU MDR: records for implantable devices must be kept for at least 15 years
  • General disposable devices (PFS/syringes): typically 5–10 years

The CDMO must confirm the retention period with the customer in the quality agreement so that all parties are aligned.

Analogy: The Essential Difference between Documents and Records

ISO 13485 clearly distinguishes "documents" from "records":

Documents: prescriptive documents that tell you "how it should be done" (SOPs, specifications, manuals); they can be revised and updated.

Records: objective evidence that proves "what has been done" (batch records, test reports); they cannot be modified, only corrected (with an audit trail).

Analogy: a recipe (document) can be revised, but the record of what dish you actually cooked yesterday (record) cannot be changed after the fact. What auditors care about most is whether the records truthfully reflect what actually happened.

Concept Analysis: The "Evidence" Requirement of Management Commitment (5.1)

  • If an FDA 483 observation requires improvement of a specific process, the related improvement targets should be included in the quality objectives

5.4.2(b) requires that "the integrity of the QMS is maintained when changes to the QMS are planned and implemented". It is like a hospital renovating an operating room: surgeries cannot stop during construction, so interim measures are needed to ensure the continuity of aseptic operations.

In a CDMO setting: when the organization relocates its site, replaces its ERP system or adds a new product line, it needs to plan in advance which QMS processes will be affected and how to ensure that quality control is not interrupted during the transition. This planning must be documented.

Concept Analysis: 5.5.1 Responsibility Matrix — The Regulatory Requirement for "Independence"

5.5.1 specifically requires "independence and authority" for personnel who affect quality. This has a specific meaning in the medical device industry:

  • QA/QC personnel must not report directly to the production head (to prevent commercial pressure from influencing quality decisions)
  • The QP (Qualified Person) responsible for the final release decision must have independent authority to refuse release
  • Internal auditors must not audit processes for which they are themselves responsible

Documentation requirements: an Org Chart + Job Descriptions + a Responsibility (RACI) Matrix are needed, and the three must be consistent.

Concept Analysis: Special Responsibilities of the Management Representative (5.5.2)

The Management Representative in 5.5.2 carries a specific requirement in ISO 13485 that has been dropped in ISO 9001:2015: this position must be "a member of management" (not just an ordinary employee).

Key responsibility c) requires the management representative to ensure "the promotion of awareness of applicable regulatory requirements and QMS requirements throughout the organization", which means:

  • When the FDA or the EU updates its regulations, the management representative is responsible for ensuring that the organization is aware of them
  • The regulatory training program should be overseen by the management representative
  • Auditors may ask to interview the management representative to confirm their grasp of the applicable regulations

Key Point: 5.6.2 Management Review Inputs — Medical Device-Specific Items

Compared with ISO 9001, ISO 13485 adds medical device-specific items to the management review inputs:

  • b) Complaint handling: a statistical summary is required, including complaint rate trends and analysis of serious complaint cases
  • c) Reporting to regulatory authorities: statistics on MDR (Medical Device Reporting) and adverse event reporting
  • l) New or revised applicable regulatory requirements: Top Management must be informed of regulatory changes and their impact on the QMS

Common failure: management review records lacking any discussion of "complaint trends" and "regulatory updates" → Major NC in FDA/notified body audits.

Analogy: The Essence of Management Review (5.6)

Management review is like a company's "annual board-level health check". It is not meant to deal with individual problems (that is the job of CAPA), but to assess at the system level whether the whole QMS is still suitable, adequate and effective.

The output requirements of 5.6.3 cover four types of decision: QMS improvement, product improvement, response to regulatory changes, and resource needs. Note: the wording "continual improvement" does not appear; there is only "improvement needed to maintain the suitability, adequacy and effectiveness", a subtle but important difference from the language of ISO 9001.

If the conclusion of the management review is "everything is fine, no changes needed" every year, auditors will question whether the review is really being carried out seriously.

6.3 Infrastructure: Important Details for a CDMO

Clause 7.3.6–7.3.10: Verification, Validation, Transfer, Change Control & D&D File

Original Text

7.3.6 Design and Development Verification

Verification shall be performed to ensure that the design and development outputs have met the design and development input requirements.

The organization shall document verification plans that include methods, acceptance criteria and, as appropriate, statistical techniques with rationale for sample size.

If the intended use requires that the medical device be connected to, or have an interface with, other medical device(s), verification shall include confirmation that the design outputs meet design inputs when so connected or interfaced.

Validation shall be performed to ensure that the resulting product is capable of meeting the requirements for the specified application or intended use.

The organization shall document validation plans that include methods, acceptance criteria and, as appropriate, statistical techniques with rationale for sample size.

Design validation shall be conducted on representative product. Representative product includes initial production units, batches or their equivalents. The rationale for the choice of product used for validation shall be recorded.

As part of design and development validation, the organization shall perform clinical evaluations or performance evaluations of the medical device in accordance with applicable regulatory requirements.

Validation shall be completed prior to release for use of the product to the customer.

The organization shall document procedures for transfer of design and development outputs to manufacturing. These procedures shall ensure that design and development outputs are verified as suitable for manufacturing before becoming final production specifications and that production capability can meet product requirements.

Results and conclusions of the transfer shall be recorded.

7.3.9 Control of Design and Development Changes

The organization shall document procedures to control design and development changes. The organization shall determine the significance of the change to function, performance, usability, safety and applicable regulatory requirements.

Before implementation, changes shall be: reviewed; verified; validated, as appropriate; approved.

The review of design and development changes shall include evaluation of the effect of the changes on constituent parts and product in process or already delivered, inputs or outputs of risk management and product realization processes.

The organization shall maintain a design and development file for each medical device type or medical device family. This file shall include or reference records generated to demonstrate conformity to the requirements for design and development and records for design and development changes.

Tutorial Commentary

7.3.8 Design Transfer: The Most Directly Relevant Clause for a CDMO

For a CDMO, 7.3.8 is the most directly relevant clause in all of 7.3:

  • Design transfer procedure: the CDMO must have a written procedure defining how to receive the customer's design outputs (specifications, drawings, BOM) and convert them into the CDMO's production specifications
  • Manufacturability verification: before they become final production specifications, the CDMO must confirm that the design outputs can be "reproducibly manufactured" at its facility; this is the precursor step to Process Qualification (PQ)
  • Confirmation of production capability: equipment, personnel and environment must be able to meet product requirements, corresponding to the concept of equipment IQ/OQ/PQ

Practical document: the Technology Transfer Report is the record output of 7.3.8.

7.3.9 Control of Design and Development Changes: Impact Assessment on Product "In Process or Already Delivered"

ISO 13485 design change control has one more key requirement than ISO 9001: the change must be evaluated for its effect on constituent parts and product already in process or already delivered.

This means:

  • Customer changes the design → the CDMO must assess whether existing inventory, work in process and shipped product are affected
  • If shipped product is affected and safety may be endangered → a Field Safety Corrective Action (FSCA) may be triggered
  • This assessment must include re-review of the risk management outputs (i.e., the ISO 14971 process)

Concept Analysis: 7.3.10 Design and Development File (DHF) — The ISO Counterpart of the FDA Design History File

7.3.10 requires a Design and Development File to be maintained for each medical device type (or family), which is equivalent to the Design History File (DHF) in FDA 21 CFR Part 820.

The DHF/D&D File must include or reference:

  • D&D Plan (output of 7.3.2)
  • Design input records (7.3.3)
  • Design output records (7.3.4)
  • Design review records (7.3.5)
  • Verification records (7.3.6)
  • Validation records (7.3.7)
  • Design transfer records (7.3.8)
  • Design change records (7.3.9)

The DHF is the most important design control document in an audit: it links all the activities of 7.3 together and forms a complete history of design control.

Clause 7.4: Purchasing

Original Text

7.4.1 Purchasing Process

The organization shall document procedures to ensure that purchased product conforms to specified purchasing information.

The organization shall establish criteria for the evaluation and selection of suppliers. The criteria shall be:

  • based on the supplier's ability to provide product that meets the organization's requirements;
  • based on the performance of the supplier;
  • based on the effect of the purchased product on the quality of the medical device;
  • proportionate to the risk associated with the medical device.

The organization shall plan the monitoring and re-evaluation of suppliers. Supplier performance in meeting requirements for the purchased product shall be monitored. The results of the monitoring shall provide an input into the supplier re-evaluation process.

Non-fulfilment of purchasing requirements shall be addressed with the supplier proportionate to the risk associated with the purchased product and compliance with applicable regulatory requirements.

Records of the results of evaluation, selection, monitoring and re-evaluation of supplier capability or performance and any necessary actions shall be maintained.

7.4.2 Purchasing Information

Purchasing information shall describe or reference the product to be purchased, including as appropriate:

  • product specifications;
  • requirements for product acceptance, procedures, processes and equipment;
  • requirements for qualification of supplier personnel;
  • quality management system requirements.

The organization shall ensure the adequacy of specified purchasing requirements prior to their communication to the supplier.

Purchasing information shall include, as applicable, a written agreement that the supplier notify the organization of changes in the purchased product prior to implementation of any changes that affect the ability of the purchased product to meet specified purchase requirements.

7.4.3 Verification of Purchased Product

The organization shall establish and implement the inspection or other activities necessary for ensuring that purchased product meets specified purchasing requirements. The extent of verification activities shall be based on the supplier evaluation results and proportionate to the risks associated with the purchased product.

When the organization becomes aware of any changes to the purchased product, the organization shall determine whether these changes affect the product realization process or the medical device.

When the organization or its customer intends to perform verification at the supplier's premises, the organization shall state the intended verification activities and method of product release in the purchasing information. Records of the verification shall be maintained.

Tutorial Commentary

The "change notification agreement" in 7.4.2: a key safeguard in supplier management

7.4.2 requires purchasing documents to include a written agreement on supplier change notification (Change Notification Agreement), which in practice is usually implemented as a Quality Technical Agreement (QTA).

The QTA should clearly specify:

  • Which types of change require advance notification (raw materials, processes, facilities, personnel, test methods, etc.)
  • The notification window (usually 30–90 days in advance)
  • The CDMO's/customer's evaluation and approval process
  • The consequences of unnotified changes (rejection, CAPA requirements)

If a supplier fails to notify a change as agreed and the product is nonconforming as a result, 7.4.2 provides the regulatory basis for the CDMO to hold the supplier accountable.

Analogy: the risk-proportionate logic of incoming inspection under 7.4.3

The intensity of incoming inspection should be proportionate to risk, like airport security: business travelers (low risk) use the fast lane; first-time travelers (unknown risk) go through standard screening; flagged travelers (known risk) receive enhanced checks. For a critical supplier with good performance, the frequency of incoming sampling inspection can reasonably be reduced; for a new supplier or one with a history of problems, every lot should be inspected more strictly. This is exactly the risk-proportionate approach that ISO 13485 7.4.3 authorizes.

Clause 7.5.1–7.5.5: Production Control & Sterile Medical Device Requirements

Original Text

7.5.1 Control of Production and Service Provision

Production and service provision shall be planned, carried out, monitored and controlled to ensure that product conforms to specification. As appropriate, production controls shall include but are not limited to:

  • documentation of procedures and methods for the control of production;

  • implementation of monitoring and measurement of process parameters and product characteristics;
  • availability and use of monitoring and measuring equipment;

  • product is cleaned by the organization prior to sterilization or its use;

  • product cannot be cleaned prior to sterilization or its use, and its cleanliness is of significance in use;
  • product is supplied to be used non-sterile, and its cleanliness is of significance in use;

If installation is performed by an external party, the organization shall provide documented requirements for medical device installation and verification of installation.

If servicing of the medical device is a specified requirement, the organization shall document servicing procedures, reference materials, and reference measurements. The organization shall analyse records of servicing activities:

  • to determine if the information is to be handled as a complaint;
  • as appropriate, for input to the improvement process.

The organization shall maintain records of the sterilization process parameters used for each sterilization batch. Sterilization records shall be traceable to each production batch of medical devices.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: the regulatory definition of the batch record under 7.5.1

7.5.1 requires a record for each medical device or batch of medical devices that provides:

  • Traceability (to the extent defined in 7.5.9)
  • Identification of the quantity manufactured
  • Identification of the quantity approved for distribution
  • Verification and approval status

This is the regulatory basis for the Device History Record (DHR); FDA 21 CFR 820.184 has an identical requirement. The DHR is the complete record of production history, including all manufacturing, testing, and release records related to the batch.

7.5.5 Particular requirements for sterile medical devices: the most central clause for a CDMO

Although the wording of 7.5.5 is brief, it is the most critical clause for a sterile CDMO:

  • Sterilization records for each batch: the process parameters of every sterilization batch (temperature, time, pressure, F₀ value, etc.) must be retained
  • Two-way traceability: sterilization records must be traceable to the corresponding production batches of medical devices (from the sterilization record, find which product batches were sterilized; from the product batch, find the corresponding sterilization record)
  • Link to 7.5.7: the recorded process parameters must be compared against the validated process parameter ranges, which is the regulatory basis for sterilization release

For a CDMO using EO (ethylene oxide) or steam sterilization: the batch record must be linked to the approved process parameter ranges validated under ISO 11135 (EO) or ISO 17665 (steam/moist heat).

Analogy: the triggers for product cleanliness requirements under 7.5.2

The five triggers in 7.5.2 cover all situations in which cleanliness is meaningful. Imagine a surgical instrument CDMO: (a) a device is cleaned by the CDMO after manufacture and then sterilized; (b) the customer supplies uncleaned parts that the CDMO cleans before sterilization; (c) a part cannot be cleaned, but its level of particulate contamination affects final use (such as some precision optical components); (d) the cleanliness of a non-sterile device still has clinical significance (such as dental instruments); (e) cutting fluids are used in processing and must be removed before the finished product. Each situation requires documented cleanliness requirements.

Clause 7.5.6–7.5.7: Validation of Special Processes & Sterilization

Original Text

Validation shall demonstrate the ability of these processes to achieve planned results consistently. The organization shall document procedures for validation of processes, including:

  • defined criteria for review and approval of the processes;
  • equipment qualification and qualification of personnel;
  • use of specific methods, procedures and acceptance criteria;
  • as appropriate, statistical techniques with rationale for sample sizes;
  • requirements for records;
  • revalidation, including criteria for revalidation;
  • approval of changes to the processes.

The organization shall document procedures for the validation of the application of computer software used in production and service provision. Such software applications shall be validated prior to initial use and, as appropriate, after changes. The specific approach and activities associated with software validation and revalidation shall be proportionate to the risk associated with the use of the software.

7.5.7 Particular Requirements for Validation of Processes for Sterilization and Sterile Barrier Systems

The organization shall document procedures for the validation of processes for sterilization and sterile barrier systems.

Processes for sterilization and sterile barrier systems shall be validated prior to implementation and following product or process changes, as appropriate.

Records of the results and, conclusion of validation and necessary actions from the validation shall be maintained.

NOTE: Further information can be found in ISO 11607-1 and ISO 11607-2.

Tutorial Commentary

Revalidation triggers for validation of sterilization and sterile barrier systems under 7.5.7

7.5.7 requires validation "prior to implementation" and "following changes to the product or process, where appropriate". Based on this requirement, industry defines revalidation triggers:

  • Product changes: change in packaging size/material, change in product density/load, change in the initial bioburden baseline
  • Process changes: modification of sterilization cycle parameters, change in load configuration, after major equipment repair
  • Equipment changes: replacement of the sterilizer, replacement of major chamber components
  • Facility changes: relocation of the sterilization area, changes in storage conditions that affect sterile barrier integrity
  • Periodic revalidation: usually once a year, to confirm the process remains in a validated state

Validation of sterile barrier systems (ISO 11607-2) is required alongside sterilization process validation, which means seal integrity validation of the packaging materials also falls within the scope of 7.5.7.

7.5.6 Validation of process computer software: an underestimated requirement

The last two paragraphs of 7.5.6 set out explicit validation requirements for "computer software used in production and service provision":

  • Must be validated before initial use
  • Revalidated where appropriate after changes to the software or its application
  • The scope of validation is proportionate to risk

Scope covers: filling machine PLC/SCADA systems, environmental monitoring software (EM systems), inspection equipment software (particle counters, TOC analyzers), and modules of ERP/MES that affect batch records. These are not "IT system" issues but direct GMP compliance requirements.

Compliance requirements for UDI (Unique Device Identification) under 7.5.8

7.5.8 states that "a system of Unique Device Identification shall be established where required by regulation". The main current regulatory requirements are:

  • FDA 21 CFR Part 830: mandatory, starting with Class III and extending down to Class I

For a CDMO, "customer property" typically includes:

  • Primary packaging components supplied by the customer (such as prefilled syringes purchased by the customer)

  • The customer's proprietary know-how (such as formulations and process parameters)
  • Equipment or tooling supplied by the customer

Analogy: why 7.5.11 extends preservation requirements to "constituent parts"

7.5.11 specifically provides that preservation requirements apply to the "constituent parts" of a medical device, not only the finished product. This is like making a precision mechanical watch: not only the finished watch needs moisture-proof storage, but also the gears, springs, and crystal glass waiting for assembly must be kept under appropriate conditions. For a sterile filling CDMO, this means that sterile primary packaging components awaiting filling, filled but unsterilized product, and sterilized but not yet finally packaged product each have their own preservation requirements, which must be documented and monitored.

Clause 7.6: Control of Monitoring and Measuring Equipment

Original Text

7.6 Control of Monitoring and Measuring Equipment

The organization shall determine the monitoring and measurement to be undertaken and the monitoring and measuring equipment needed to provide evidence of conformity of product to determined requirements.

The organization shall document procedures to ensure that monitoring and measurement can be carried out in a manner consistent with the monitoring and measurement requirements.

As necessary to ensure valid results, measuring equipment shall:

  • be calibrated or verified, or both, at specified intervals, or prior to use, against measurement standards traceable to international or national measurement standards: when no such standards exist, the basis used for calibration or verification shall be recorded;
  • be adjusted or re-adjusted as necessary: such adjustments or re-adjustments shall be recorded;
  • have identification in order to determine its calibration status;
  • be safeguarded from adjustments that would invalidate the measurement result;
  • be protected from damage and deterioration during handling, maintenance and storage.

The organization shall perform calibration or verification in accordance with documented procedures.

In addition, the organization shall assess and record the validity of the previous measuring results when the equipment is found not to conform to requirements. The organization shall take appropriate action in regard to the equipment and any product affected.

Records of the results of calibration and verification shall be maintained.

The organization shall document procedures for the validation of the application of computer software used for the monitoring and measurement of requirements. Such software applications shall be validated prior to initial use and, as appropriate, after changes. Records of the results and conclusion of validation and necessary actions from the validation shall be maintained.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: the obligation to assess previous results when calibration fails

The requirement of greatest regulatory impact in 7.6 is: when measuring equipment is found not to conform to requirements, the validity of the previous measuring results must be assessed and recorded.

This means that if the most recent calibration of a fill-weight balance shows it was out of tolerance, the CDMO must:

  • Determine the point in time of the last acceptable calibration
  • Assess all batches in which the equipment was used between the two calibrations
  • Determine whether the measurement results of the affected batches are still reliable
  • If the measurement results are invalid → this may trigger a batch investigation, additional testing, or even a recall assessment

This is the regulatory basis for handling "Equipment Out-of-Tolerance" events in GMP, which must be documented through a CAPA or deviation investigation.

Analogy: calibration traceability and the assessment of "suspect batches"

Imagine a factory that uses a scale every day to weigh food. If one day the scale is found to be under-weighing by 5% (outside tolerance), the questions must be asked: "When was this scale last calibrated and found acceptable? Between that acceptable calibration and the discovery of the failure, how many batches of product were released using this scale?" The weight records of those products now come into question. Handling a measuring equipment failure for medical devices is exactly the same, and the consequences are more serious because measurement results bear directly on patient safety. The "assessment of previous results" required by 7.6 is exactly what ensures the quality system can detect and correct this kind of escape.

Section Summary

  • Tiered supplier management under 7.4.1 has a regulatory basis: evaluation criteria "proportionate to risk" explicitly authorize tiered management; the supplier change notification obligation in the QTA (7.4.2) is a key safeguard against unreported changes harming product quality
  • 7.5.5 is the core obligation for a sterile CDMO: sterilization records for each batch must be retained and be traceable in both directions to the corresponding medical device production batches, which is the direct regulatory basis for sterilization batch release
  • The scope of special process validation under 7.5.6 is broad: it covers sterilization, aseptic filling, welding, sealing, and other processes whose output cannot be fully verified; process computer software also falls within this requirement
  • Sterilization validation under 7.5.7 has clear revalidation triggers: any relevant change to product, process, equipment, or facility may trigger revalidation, and the CDMO's change control procedure must include a step for assessing the impact on sterilization
  • The traceability requirements for implantable devices under 7.5.9 are far stronger than for general products: records of environmental conditions, distributors, and recipients must all be retained; UDI has become a mandatory requirement in major markets
  • Assessing previous results after a calibration failure under 7.6 is a high-risk obligation: equipment found out of tolerance must trigger an investigation of the affected batches; monitoring software (LIMS, EM systems) must be validated before use and after changes

Original Text

8.1 General

The organization shall plan and implement the monitoring, measurement, analysis and improvement processes needed to:

  • a) demonstrate conformity of product
  • b) ensure conformity of the quality management system
  • c) maintain the effectiveness of the quality management system

This shall include determination of applicable methods, including statistical techniques and the extent of their use. Records shall be maintained.

8.2.1 Feedback

As one of the measurements of the performance of the quality management system, the organization shall gather and monitor information relating to whether the organization has met customer requirements.

The organization shall utilize feedback information as an input to monitoring and maintaining the product requirements and to the risk management process.

Feedback shall come from production and post-production activities. Examples include:

  • Customer complaints
  • Field service records
  • Regulatory authority queries
  • Servicing reports
"The organization shall utilize feedback information as an input to monitoring and maintaining the product requirements and to the risk management process."

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: the three purposes of 8.1 vs. the four purposes of ISO 9001

8.1 of ISO 13485:2016 lists three purposes and deliberately omits the fourth purpose of ISO 9001: "continual improvement".

This is not an oversight but a design choice. ISO 13485 uses the wording "maintain the effectiveness" rather than "continual improvement".

Practical meaning for a CDMO:

  • ISO 13485 does not require getting better every year; it only requires that the system operate effectively and comply with regulations
  • This is consistent with the logic of FDA QSR 21 CFR Part 820: the emphasis is on control, not on constant refinement
  • In QMS applications under EU MDR/IVDR, auditors will check this distinction; do not commit to "continual improvement" in QMS documents unless you have quantitative indicators to support it

Analogy: the 8.2.1 feedback system is an "early warning radar"

A feedback system is not just a passive collector of complaints; it is a sensor that actively detects market signals.

Think of it as a factory's environmental monitoring: you do not wait for an incident to learn of a problem, but continuously detect temperature, humidity, and microbiological data. The feedback system of 8.2.1 does the same for devices on the market.

A key requirement added in the 2016 version: feedback must be fed into the risk management process. This links directly to ISO 14971, forming a closed loop of post-market surveillance → risk reassessment → design/process update.

Practical considerations: designing a feedback system at a CDMO

A CDMO usually does not deal directly with end users but still needs to establish a feedback system. Information can be collected from the following channels:

  • Summaries of market complaints forwarded by the customer (the brand owner/manufacturer)
  • The CDMO's own stability data on retained quality samples (post-release stability)
  • Quality issues raised in customer service meetings (Quarterly Business Review)
  • Regulatory authority inquiries about the customer's product (the CDMO should require the customer to notify it of relevant inquiries)
  • Rework/scrap records for the service batches the CDMO has run
Clause 8.2.2: Complaint Handling

Original Text

8.2.2 Complaint Handling

The organization shall document a written procedure for timely complaint handling in accordance with applicable regulatory requirements. This procedure shall include at minimum:

  1. Receiving and recording information
  2. Evaluating information to determine if feedback constitutes a complaint
  3. Investigating complaints
  4. Determining the need to report information to regulatory authorities
  5. Handling of complaint-related product
  6. Determining the need to initiate corrections or corrective actions

A complaint means any written, electronic, or oral communication that alleges deficiencies related to the identity, quality, durability, reliability, safety, effectiveness, or performance of a device after it has been released for distribution.

If any complaint is not investigated, the justification shall be maintained. Records of complaints shall be maintained for each complaint received.

Complaint records shall include:

  • Product identification, lot, and revision level
  • Name and address of complainant
  • Nature of complaint
  • Date complaint received
  • If investigation was conducted: date, results, and conclusions
  • Any corrective action taken

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: the broad definition of a "complaint" - Any Communication

The ISO 13485 definition of complaint is very broad: any written, electronic, or oral communication that alleges deficiencies related to the device in any of the following respects:

  • identity, quality, durability
  • reliability, safety, effectiveness
  • performance

Note the word "alleges": the complaint does not need to be proven true; as long as someone alleges a deficiency, it constitutes a complaint. Even if the investigation finally finds user error, it must still be recorded as a complaint and taken through the investigation process.

The near-miss challenge: does a user report that "something almost went wrong" constitute a complaint? The ISO 13485 answer: if the communication "alleges" a safety or performance deficiency in the device, it may constitute a complaint even if no incident actually occurred.

Practical considerations: complaint handling structure at a CDMO

A CDMO is a contract manufacturer and is usually not the legal manufacturer in the regulatory sense. A complaint handling procedure must still be established, because:

  • When the customer forwards market complaints to the CDMO, a process for receiving and evaluating them is needed
  • For complaints it receives, the CDMO must determine whether they relate to its own manufacturing activities and, if so, must investigate
  • The CDMO's investigation results must be fed back to the customer and incorporated into the customer's overall complaint records
  • If the CDMO also holds a CE mark or 510(k) (that is, it is the legal manufacturer), it directly bears the reporting obligation

The boundary of a CDMO's vigilance responsibility

Key distinction:

Original Text

The organization shall monitor and measure the characteristics of the product to verify that product requirements are met. This shall be carried out at appropriate stages during product realization.

Tutorial Commentary

8.2.4 Internal audit: the meaning of "without undue delay"

ISO 13485 uses "without undue delay" in 8.2.4, which is stricter than the "within agreed timeframe" of ISO 9001.

"Undue delay" means that even if no time limit was agreed in advance, action cannot be postponed indefinitely on the grounds of "waiting for the plan" or "insufficient resources". This wording directly mirrors a common problem in FDA 483 observations: CAPAs opened but not completed for months.

Recommendations for the audit procedure:

  • Every audit finding should have an initial response within 30 days (it need not be complete, but there must be a plan)
  • The audit report should include a "planned completion date", confirmed by signature of the responsible department head
  • Records of follow-up verification are an explicit ISO 13485 requirement, not an option

The record requirement of 8.2.6: who authorized release

ISO 13485 explicitly requires that release records document the identity of the person authorizing release. This is not merely a matter of signatures but a traceability requirement:

  • If the product is later found nonconforming, an inspection can trace which QC/QA person authorized the release
  • FDA 21 CFR 820.80 has the same requirement and requires records at the individual level (non-generic)
  • The review of technical documentation for the QMS under EU MDR Annex IX also includes verification of this item

A requirement added in the 2016 version: the record must also identify the test equipment used. This ensures that equipment calibration records can be linked to release decisions.

Clause 8.3: Control of Nonconforming Product

Original Text

8.3.1 General

The organization shall ensure that product which does not conform to product requirements is identified and controlled to prevent its unintended use or delivery. A documented procedure shall define controls and related responsibilities and authorities for identifying, segregating, evaluating, and disposing of nonconforming product.

Records of the nature of nonconformities, any subsequent action taken, and concessions obtained shall be maintained.

8.3.2 Actions in Response to Nonconforming Product Detected Before Delivery

The organization shall deal with nonconforming product by one or more of the following:

  • a) taking action to eliminate the detected nonconformity
  • b) authorizing use, release or acceptance under concession (by relevant authority and, where applicable, the customer)
  • c) taking action to preclude its original intended use or application

If product is regraded or accepted under concession, records shall include the justification and the authorization (including the name of the person granting the concession). Regulatory requirement constraints on concessions shall be met.

8.3.3 Actions in Response to Nonconforming Product Detected After Delivery

When nonconforming product is detected after delivery, the organization shall take action appropriate to the effects of the nonconformity. Actions include:

  • Notifying customers (and, where applicable, regulatory authorities)
  • Issuing advisory notices (field safety corrective actions)
  • Conducting a risk analysis to determine the scope and urgency of action
  • Initiating recalls or field corrections

Records of actions taken, advisory notices issued, and notification to regulatory authorities shall be maintained.

8.3.4 Rework

The organization shall perform rework in accordance with documented procedures that account for regulatory requirements. Before the decision is made to rework product, the organization shall determine potential adverse effects of rework on the product, using a risk management process.

Reworked product shall be re-inspected or re-tested in accordance with documented procedures. Records of rework, including re-inspection and re-testing, shall be maintained.

Tutorial Commentary

8.3.3 Nonconforming product after delivery = the legal framework for field action

8.3.3 is the clause with the most serious legal consequences in the whole of Clause 8. Once triggered, it may involve:

  • Advisory Notice: notifies customers and users of a potential risk and recommends precautionary measures, not necessarily a recall
  • Field Safety Corrective Action (FSCA): an EU MDR term covering recall, correction, and warning
  • Recall: withdrawal of the product from the market, initiated in the US by the FDA or voluntarily by the company

Risk analysis determines the scope of the action: which batches are affected? Which regions? Is the severity such that mandatory withdrawal from use is needed? The CDMO must quickly provide batch records and process data here to support the customer's decision.

8.3.4 Rework: not just "fix it and it's fine"

ISO 13485 is far stricter on rework than ISO 9001:

General industry thinking: "quality is wrong → repair → ship again"

ISO 13485 requires: "quality is wrong → first do a risk assessment (what new problems could the rework cause?) → write a rework procedure → perform the rework → re-verify (re-inspect + re-test) → record all steps"

Typical risk: reworking aseptically filled product (refiltering/refilling) affects the sterility assurance level; reworking an injection device may change material compatibility. An ISO 14971 risk assessment before rework is therefore mandatory.

Practical considerations: minimum content of a CDMO nonconforming product SOP

  • Triggers for identification and segregation (not automatic classification; a human assessment is needed to determine whether the definition of "nonconforming product" is met)
  • Authorization levels for the three disposition paths before delivery (elimination, concession, scrap)
  • Concession record format (authorizer, rationale, confirmation of regulatory limits)
  • Post-delivery notification process (customer notification time limit + decision matrix for notifying regulatory authorities)
  • Triggers for the rework decision and a pre-rework risk assessment template
  • List of tests for re-verification after rework (≥ the original release tests)

Original Text

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: 8.4 is an "integrating output", not a "standalone activity"

The six input sources of 8.4 (a-f) are not new additional work; all of them are already required elsewhere in Clause 8:

  • a) Feedback → 8.2.1
  • b) Product conformance → 8.2.6
  • c) Process trends → 8.2.5
  • d) Suppliers → 7.4
  • e) Audits → 8.2.4
  • f) Service reports → 7.5.4

Analogy: 8.4 is the QMS "health check report"

Think of 8.4 as a company's annual health check: you look not at each individual indicator but at the overall trend and the relationships among indicators.

Example: a rising complaint rate + increasing supplier NCRs + a declining process capability index for a particular process: three isolated data points, taken together, may point to the same root cause (for example, a change in the quality of a critical raw material).

This is exactly what 8.4 requires: systematic analysis, not responding to cases one by one. Only after management review (5.6) receives the output of 8.4 can it make truly effective resource allocation decisions.

Before each management review, QA should prepare a data analysis package covering the following:

  • Number of complaints and classification trend chart (last 12 months)
  • CAPA opened / completed / overdue rate
  • Internal audit finding classification and correction status
  • Quality indicators of major suppliers (incoming acceptance rate, number of NCRs)
  • Process capability index trends (Cpk by critical parameter)
  • Product release rate and batch rejection trend
  • Number of regulatory reports (if applicable)
  • Summary of post-market surveillance signals (if applicable)
Clause 8.5: Improvement, Corrective Action and Preventive Action

Original Text

8.5.1 General

The organization shall identify and implement changes needed to ensure and maintain the effectiveness of the quality management system and any resulting changes to the medical device or processes.

Note: "Maintain effectiveness" is distinct from ISO 9001's "continual improvement" — the standard does not require continuous improvement cycles, only that QMS effectiveness is sustained.

8.5.2 Corrective Action

The organization shall take corrective action to eliminate the causes of nonconformities in order to prevent recurrence. The organization shall document a procedure that defines requirements for:

  1. Reviewing nonconformities (including complaints)
  2. Determining the causes of nonconformities
  3. Evaluating the need for corrective action to ensure nonconformities do not recur
  4. Planning and implementing necessary corrective action, including updating documentation
  5. Verifying that corrective action does not adversely affect the ability to meet applicable regulatory requirements or the safety and performance of the medical device
  6. Reviewing the effectiveness of corrective action taken

Corrective action shall be appropriate to the effects of the nonconformities encountered. Records of corrective actions shall be maintained.

Corrective action shall be taken without undue delay. Verification of effectiveness is required.

8.5.3 Preventive Action

The organization shall determine action to eliminate causes of potential nonconformities in order to prevent their occurrence. Preventive actions shall be appropriate to the effects of the potential problems. A documented procedure shall define requirements for:

  1. Determining potential nonconformities and their causes
  2. Evaluating the need for action to prevent the occurrence of nonconformities
  3. Implementing appropriate action and updating documentation
  4. Verifying that preventive action does not adversely affect the ability to meet regulatory requirements or safety/performance
  5. Reviewing the effectiveness of preventive action taken

Records of preventive actions shall be maintained.

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: the seven-step ISO 13485 CAPA process

Integrating the requirements of 8.5.2, the complete logical chain of ISO 13485 CAPA can be laid out:

① Determination
   Identify the nonconformity and evaluate whether a CAPA needs to be opened

② Investigation
   Collect information on the event and confirm the scope

③ Root Cause Analysis
   Use tools such as 5-Why and Fishbone

④ Evaluation of Need for Action
   If the root cause will not recur, this can be documented and closed

⑤ Implementation
   Including document updates, training, equipment modifications, etc.

⑥ Effectiveness Review
   After the action is implemented, confirm the problem has not recurred

⑦ Adverse Effect Check
   Confirm that the CA does not affect safety or regulatory compliance

Key differences between ISO 13485 CAPA and ISO 9001 CAPA

ISO 13485 is stricter than ISO 9001 in two places:

1. Adverse Effect Check: ISO 13485 requires that every CA/PA be verified to ensure that "the action itself does not adversely affect the safety of the product or regulatory requirements". This is a new requirement in the 2016 version, with no corresponding clause in ISO 9001.

Example: a CA requires replacing a critical seal on an aseptic filling machine; after the replacement, a container closure integrity test must be repeated. This revalidation step is part of the "adverse effect check".

2. Recycling of ineffective CAPAs: if the effectiveness review shows the action was ineffective, the CAPA process must be re-entered (it cannot simply be "closed with a note"). This is the most common CAPA deficiency in FDA 483 observations: measures taken but effectiveness not verified, or verified and found ineffective but not re-opened.

CA vs. PA: retrospective vs. forward-looking

Corrective Action:
  Trigger          → a nonconformity that has already occurred
  Objective        → eliminate the root cause and prevent recurrence
  Data sources     → complaints, internal audit findings, deviations

Preventive Action:
  Trigger          → a potential risk (not yet occurred)
  Objective        → eliminate the cause before the problem appears
  Data sources     → trend analysis, risk assessment, process capability monitoring

Key distinction:
  CA is for "a problem that already exists"
  PA is for "a warning signal that has been seen"

Common misuse: writing a CA as a PA
  Example: a complaint has already occurred → a "preventive action" is opened
  Correct: a CA should be opened (because the problem has occurred)
  PA is a truly forward-looking activity

Practical considerations: CAPA system design from the FDA/EU inspection perspective

In FDA Form 483 observations and EU MDR notified body technical reviews, the CAPA system is the most frequently cited area. Key inspection questions:

  • Is the CAPA opened in a timely manner (within a defined timeframe after event detection)?
  • Is the root cause analysis documented with a methodology (5-Why, Fishbone, etc.)?
  • Were the success criteria for the effectiveness review defined before the action was implemented?
  • Has the adverse effect check of the action been documented?
  • Are ineffective CAPAs re-opened, rather than just recorded as "not effective" and closed?
  • Are CAPA trends fed into management review and the 8.4 data analysis?

Tutorial Commentary

Concept: the core philosophical shift of the 2016 version

Compared with the 2003 version, the 2016 version reflects three major changes in the medical device regulatory landscape:

1. Stronger device specificity: 4.2.3 MDF, 6.4.2 contamination control, 7.5.7 sterile barrier systems. These clauses have no counterpart at all in ISO 9001 and are requirements specific to medical device manufacturing. The 2003 version still relied heavily on the ISO 9001 framework; the 2016 version began to build independent device-specific requirements.

2. A more complete post-market closed loop: the three-way link of 8.2.1 (feedback) → 8.2.2 (complaints) → 8.2.3 (reporting) was scattered or missing in the 2003 version. The 2016 version makes the systematic requirements for post-market surveillance explicit, in line with the direction of EU MDR and FDA QMSR.

3. Clearer software and digital requirements: the explicit requirement for software validation in 4.1.6 (CSV/CSA) reflects the markedly increased reliance of the pharmaceutical and device industries on IT systems.

4.2.3 Medical Device File (MDF): one of the most important new clauses in the 2016 version

The MDF concept comes from the "Technical File / Technical Documentation" of the EU MDD/MDR and was first included in the main text of ISO 13485 in the 2016 version. The main contents of the MDF:

  • General description of the device
  • Intended use and purpose
  • Labeling and IFU (instructions for use)
  • Design specifications
  • Manufacturing specifications
  • Safety and performance requirements
  • Summary of risk management records
  • Summary of verification and validation records (V&V records)

The MDF is a superset of the Design History File (DHF): the DHF records the design process, while the MDF records the entire life cycle of the device. Implication for a CDMO: if the CDMO undertakes some design activities, it needs to contribute to the customer's MDF.

6.4.2 Contamination control: filling a major gap in the 2003 version

The 2003 version was vague on contamination control for sterile devices and relied on references to other standards (such as ISO 14644). The 2016 version explicitly requires:

  • For sterile devices at risk of microbial or particulate contamination, a documented contamination control plan must be established
  • The plan must cover personnel (gowning, behavior), equipment (cleaning/sterilization), environment (monitoring), and processes (material transfer in and out)

For an aseptic filling CDMO: 6.4.2 corresponds directly to the requirements of ISO 14644-1/2 (cleanroom classification) and EU GMP Annex 1 (sterile manufacturing), and is a key checkpoint in technical audits.

Why did ISO 13485 retain 8.5.3 Preventive Action?

ISO 9001:2015 removed the standalone Preventive Action clause and integrated it into the broad framework of "risk-based thinking" (6.1). ISO 13485:2016 chose to retain 8.5.3, for these reasons:

  • Medical device regulations (FDA QSR 21 CFR 820.100, EU MDR Annex IX) explicitly require "preventive action" as a separate element of the QMS
  • Auditors (notified bodies, FDA investigators) are still accustomed to reviewing records within the "CAPA" framework (including PA)
  • The concept of "risk" in ISO 13485 is deliberately narrowed; removing the standalone PA clause could leave a gap in the identification of potential problems

Practical tip: an organization certified to both ISO 9001 and ISO 13485 should state clearly in its CAPA procedure how it meets the requirements of both standards. Usually one CAPA procedure can cover both, but cross-references are needed within the procedure.

Analogy: ISO 13485 is the management standard of a "specialist hospital"

ISO 9001 suits all industries, like a "general business management textbook".

ISO 13485 is tailored for the medical device industry, like a "specialist hospital management manual": it assumes that if your product fails, it may harm or kill a patient. So it does not require "getting better every year" (continual improvement) but "always safe and reliable" (maintaining effectiveness).

This difference explains why the documentation requirements of ISO 13485 are more specific (more "documented procedure" than "documented information"), and why the effectiveness review of CA/PA is mandatory: "we have improved, but did not verify it" is unacceptable in the medical device field.

The strategic significance of ISO 13485 certification for a CDMO

ISO 13485 certification is the "ticket of entry" for a medical device CDMO, but the following dimensions determine the CDMO's competitive position:

  • Certification scope: "all medical devices" or "specific categories"? The broader the scope, the more attractive to customers
  • Life cycle stages covered: manufacturing only, or including design services? A CDMO that includes design services can help customers set up a 7.3 design control system
  • Integration with EU MDR/FDA QMSR: ISO 13485 is a sufficient condition for the EU MDR QMS, and the FDA QMSR (2024 updated version) aligns directly with ISO 13485:2016, so for devices registered with the US FDA, ISO 13485 certification has direct regulatory value
  • Maturity of the CAPA system: when customers (brand owners) select a CDMO, the completeness and timeliness of its CAPA system are important evaluation items
