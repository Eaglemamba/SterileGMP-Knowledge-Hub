Part 1 — Glass Cylinders for Dental Local Anaesthetic Cartridges (ISO 11040-1:2015)

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11040 specifies the design, dimensions, materials, performance and test methods for glass cylinders for dental local anaesthetic cartridges with a volume of 1.7 ml, 1.8 ml and 2.2 ml intended for single use only.

It applies to primary packs used in direct contact with a drug. The potency, purity, stability and safety of a drug during its manufacture and storage can be strongly affected by the nature and performance of the primary pack.

3 Dimensions and Designation

3.1 Dimensions: Glass cylinders shall have dimensions per Figure 1 including a truncated cone at the neck. The neck shall have:

  • Height: (5 ± 0.2) mm
  • Neck opening: (3.15 ± 0.15) mm over a depth of 5 mm
  • Total length: (62.4 ± 0.4) mm for 1.7/1.8 ml; (75.0 ± 0.4) mm for 2.2 ml

3.2 Designation: Cylinders are designated by reference to ISO 11040-1 plus colour of the glass. Example: Glass cylinders for dental local anaesthetic cartridges ISO 11040-1 – cl (clear glass).

4 Requirements

4.1 Material: Colourless (cl) glass of hydrolytic resistance grain class ISO 720 HGA 1. Any change in chemical composition shall be notified to the user at least nine months in advance.

4.2 Performance Requirements:

  • 4.2.1 Sealing surface at open end shall be flat and free from ripples
  • 4.2.2 No seeds or bubbles that interfere with visual inspection of contents
  • 4.2.3 No open air lines on inside surface that can lead to leakage
  • 4.2.4 Hydrolytic resistance per ISO 4802-1 or ISO 4802-2, class HC 1
  • 4.2.5 If annealed: maximum residual stress ≤ 40 nm/mm optical retardation

5 Marking

Outer packaging shall be marked with: size; quantity; designation; manufacturer name/symbol; lot number or date of production.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dental Local Anaesthetic Cartridge: A small glass cartridge prefilled with a local anaesthetic (such as lidocaine), which the dentist injects directly into the patient's oral tissue using a dedicated dental syringe. ISO 11040-1 specifies the requirements for these glass cylinders.

Hydrolytic Resistance HGA 1: When glass contacts a drug solution, alkaline ions may leach from the glass surface and affect the pH and purity of the product. HGA 1 is the highest hydrolytic resistance grade, meaning the glass releases only minimal extractable amounts, so the solution is not contaminated.

Primary Packaging Material: The container that comes into direct contact with the drug product and is the first line of defence for product safety. ISO stresses its direct impact on the potency, purity and stability of the drug.

Analogy

A dental cartridge is like a precision "mini ampoule". Think of it as a single-use container pre-filled with a specific dose of anaesthetic: the dentist simply loads it into a dedicated syringe and delivers the dose accurately, much like a pre-portioned seasoning sachet that is convenient to use and gives an accurate amount.

Glass hydrolytic resistance is like the difference between a stainless steel pot and an aluminium pot: stainless steel (HGA 1 glass) hardly reacts with food, whereas a lower-grade aluminium pot may leach metal ions that affect food quality.

Key Notes

The 9-month advance notification principle: Any change to the glass chemical composition must be notified to users 9 months in advance. This reflects the fact that compatibility studies for primary packaging need sufficient time: the drug manufacturer has to retest the interaction between the material and the product, and this time window protects patient safety.

Residual stress limit (40 nm/mm): Glass that has not been adequately annealed contains internal stress and may suddenly break during injection or sterilisation, creating a risk of personal injury.

  2. What risks do seeds/bubbles in the glass cylinder pose in clinical use?
  3. When a supplier notifies a change in glass chemical composition, which assessments should the CDMO's customer start immediately?
Part 2 — Plunger Stoppers for Dental Local Anaesthetic Cartridges (ISO 11040-2:2011)

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11040 specifies the shape, dimensions, material, performance requirements and labelling of plunger stoppers for dental local anaesthetic cartridges intended for single use only.

3 Classification

Plunger stoppers shall be classified as follows:

  • Type A: plunger stoppers without cavities
  • Type B: plunger stoppers with one cavity
  • Type C: plunger stoppers with two cavities

4 Shape and Dimensions

Nominal inner diameter of glass cylinder: 6.85 ± 0.15 mm

4.2 Spacers to prevent adhesion shall not exceed 0.3 mm height.

6 Material

Elastomeric material shall meet Clause 7 requirements. The material shall withstand two sterilization cycles at (121 ± 2)°C for 30 min without impairment. For irradiation sterilization, suitability shall be evaluated.

7 Requirements

7.2.1 Hardness: Nominal value ± 5 Shore A (ISO 7619-1) or ± 5 IRHD (ISO 48).

7.2.2 Freedom from leakage: Cartridges shall be free from leakage at plunger when tested per Annex A (30 N force for 1 min on 10 water-filled cartridges).

7.2.3 Sliding characteristics: Influenced by all container closure system components and siliconization.

7.2.4 Resistance to ageing: Must maintain performance throughout entire shelf life of medicinal product.

7.3 Chemical requirements: Per ISO 8871-1 (extractables in aqueous autoclavates).

7.4 Biological requirements: Per ISO 8871-4.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Plunger stopper types (Type A/B/C): The purpose of the cavity is to adjust the compressibility and sealing performance of the plunger stopper. Type A has no cavity, giving the strongest seal but higher sliding resistance; Type C has two cavities and gives a lower break-loose force, which suits applications requiring precise control of injection force.

Spacers: Tiny protrusions no more than 0.3 mm high that prevent plunger stoppers from sticking together when stacked. Though a seemingly minor design feature, they are essential to the efficiency of automated feeding on filling lines.

Shore A hardness: The hardness standard for elastomeric materials, which directly affects sealing and sliding force. Higher hardness means greater sliding resistance and a harder injection for the patient; lower hardness weakens sealing and may lead to leakage. The ±5 tolerance controls the consistency of the system.

Analogy

A plunger stopper is like the "piston seal ring" of the syringe. Think of the piston rings in a car engine: they must fit tightly against the cylinder wall (sealing) yet still slide smoothly (low friction). Types A/B/C are like piston rings of different hardness, and the cavity design is like cutting a groove into the ring so that it compresses more easily and has lower break-loose resistance.

Key Notes

cGMP requirements: ISO 11040-2 explicitly states that elastomeric primary packaging components must be manufactured according to cGMP principles (see ISO 15378). This means an elastomer supplier is not merely an industrial rubber goods manufacturer but a GMP-regulated manufacturer of pharmaceutical packaging materials.

The "original test batch" principle: The supplier must ensure that every delivery is consistent with the type sample that was originally tested and approved by the end user. In the pharmaceutical supply chain this is the concept of a formula lock.

Systemic nature of sliding characteristics: The standard states clearly that sliding characteristics are affected by all components of the container closure system, including the degree of siliconisation, which underlines the need to evaluate the system as a whole rather than the components individually.

Practical Application

When assessing plunger stopper suppliers for dental local anaesthetic cartridges, the CDMO should require: (1) the type sample approval; (2) ISO 8871-1 and ISO 8871-4 test reports; (3) validation data showing that performance is retained after autoclaving; (4) ageing stability studies. If the customer asks for radiation sterilisation instead of steam sterilisation, the suitability of the elastomer material must be reassessed.

Practice Questions

  1. Why must the break-loose force of a plunger stopper be tested in the complete container closure system rather than on the plunger stopper alone?
  2. If an elastomer supplier plans to change the rubber formulation, which assessment steps does ISO 11040-2 require?
Part 3 — Seals for Dental Local Anaesthetic Cartridges (ISO 11040-3:2012)

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11040 specifies the shape, dimensions, material, performance requirements and labelling of seals for dental local anaesthetic cartridges intended for single use only.

3 Classification

  • Type A: seals with a mono-layer disc
  • Type B: seals with a double-layer disc

4 Shape and Dimensions

4.3 The diameter of the rubber discs shall provide sufficient press-fit in the aluminium cap.

6 Material

6.1 Cap: General requirements per ISO 8872 (aluminium caps); cap shall be anodized or lacquered.

6.2 Discs: From originally tested and approved elastomeric formulation. Must withstand two sterilization cycles at (121 ± 2)°C for 30 min.

7 Requirements

7.2.1 Hardness: Nominal value ± 5 Shore A or ± 5 IRHD.

7.2.2 Fragmentation: Per ISO 8871-5:2005, using needle outer diameter 0.4 mm per ISO 7885.

7.2.3 Freedom from leakage: No leakage at seal per Annex A (30 ± 1 N force for 1 min).

7.2.4 Resistance to ageing: Seals maintain performance throughout product shelf life.

7.3 Chemical: ISO 8871-1 applies to disc material.

7.4 Biological: ISO 8871-4 applies; toxicity tests to disc only.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Seal structure: The seal of a dental cartridge consists of an aluminium cap plus a rubber disc. The aluminium cap provides mechanical strength and tamper protection; the rubber disc provides the seal and the self-resealing ability after needle puncture.

Single-layer/double-layer disc design (Type A/B): The Type A single-layer disc is simple in structure and has a lower puncture force; the Type B double-layer disc gives a better seal and better resistance to needle fragmentation. When a needle punctures the disc it may carry rubber particles into the drug solution, which is a serious contamination risk.

Fragmentation test: Simulates the generation of fragments when a needle pierces the rubber disc. In accordance with ISO 7885, a needle with an outer diameter of 0.4 mm is used to assess the amount of rubber fragments released, which bears directly on injection safety.

Analogy

A seal is like the cork of a wine bottle: it must seal the bottle neck (preventing evaporation and external contamination), yet allow the corkscrew (the needle) to pass through without dropping too many crumbs into the wine (fragmentation contamination control). The double-layer Type B design is like the composite corks used for premium wines, giving a double sealing safeguard.

Key Notes

Anodising/coating requirement for the aluminium cap: Aluminium itself may react with certain drugs, so anodising or a suitable coating gives the cap a chemically inert protective layer. When selecting a seal supplier, the surface treatment specification must be confirmed.

Combined testing of seal and plunger stopper: Annex A of ISO 11040-3 explains that the leakage tests for the seal and the plunger stopper can be performed together (ISO 11040-2 Annex A). In practical validation work, it is recommended to test the complete container closure system in combination rather than testing individual components only.

Scope of biological toxicity testing: Toxicity tests apply only to the rubber disc and not to the aluminium cap, because the cap does not directly contact the interior of the drug product. This reflects the scientific basis of the ISO standard: the test scope is determined by actual contact relationships.

Formula

Seal crimping requirement:
  Rubber disc diameter > aluminium cap inner diameter (d1 = 7.5 mm)
  → Ensures sufficient press-fit force

Leakage test conditions (Annex A):
  Applied force: (30 ± 1) N
  Time: 1 min
  Sample size: 10 water-filled cartridges
  Acceptance criterion: 0 leaks

Practice Questions

  1. When the supplier of a dental cartridge seal plans to change the elastomer formulation of the rubber disc, which requalification tests do you, as the drug manufacturer, need to carry out?
  2. What patient safety risks can result from a failed fragmentation test? How can this risk be reduced at the design stage?
  3. Why must the aluminium cap of the seal be anodised or coated? What would be the consequence of omitting this step?

Section Summary

  • ISO 11040-1/2/3 cover the three core components of dental local anaesthetic cartridges: the glass cylinder, the plunger stopper and the seal, which together form the container closure system
  • The glass material must reach the highest hydrolytic resistance grade (HGA 1 / HC 1), and elastomeric materials must meet the requirements of the ISO 8871 series to ensure drug product safety
  • Any material change to a primary packaging component must be notified to users in advance (glass: 9 months) and followed by a compatibility reassessment, which is an important part of supply chain management
  • All components must be manufactured according to cGMP principles (ISO 15378), which reflects the role of primary packaging materials as the first barrier protecting the integrity of the drug product

Part 4.1–4.3 — General Requirements: Quality Systems, Testing, Documentation

Original Text

4 General Requirements

4.1 Quality systems: Activities within this part of ISO 11040 shall be carried out within a formal quality system. NOTE: ISO 15378 contains requirements for a suitable quality management system for primary packaging materials for medicinal products.

4.2 Testing:

  • Gauge repeatability and reproducibility (GR&R) ≤ 20% of tolerance range for non-destructive tests; ≤ 30% for destructive tests, covering ±2 standard deviations.
  • Sampling plans shall be based on statistically valid rationale (e.g. ISO 2859-1, ISO 3951).
  • Testing at ambient laboratory conditions unless agreed otherwise.

4.3 Documentation:

  • All compliance demonstrations shall be documented.
  • Documentation retained considering regulatory requirements, expiration date, and traceability.
  • Electronic records, signatures, and handwritten signatures on electronic records shall be reliable.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Gauge Repeatability and Reproducibility (GR&R): An index that expresses measurement system error as a percentage of the tolerance range. GR&R ≤ 20% means the uncertainty of the measuring tool does not exceed 20% of the tolerance, ensuring that measurement results truly reflect product quality. This is a core concept of MSA (measurement system analysis).

ISO 15378: A quality management system standard designed specifically for primary packaging materials for medicinal products; it is a version of ISO 9001 with GMP requirements added. This means a glass barrel supplier needs not only ISO 9001 certification but, ideally, compliance with ISO 15378.

Key Notes

The practical meaning of GR&R: If the outer diameter tolerance of a glass barrel is ±0.1 mm (a range of 0.2 mm), the GR&R of the measuring instrument must be ≤ 0.04 mm (20% × 0.2). An excessive GR&R means you cannot distinguish "true product variation" from "measurement error", leading to wrongful release or wrongful rejection.

Reliability of electronic records: This directly echoes the spirit of 21 CFR Part 11, stressing the trustworthiness of electronic records and electronic signatures. It is essential for CDMOs that use ERP/MES systems in a paperless manufacturing environment.

Practice Questions

  1. If your glass barrel supplier measures the barrel outer diameter with a caliper that has a GR&R of 35%, does this meet the requirements of ISO 11040-4? What action should be taken?
  2. Why does destructive testing allow a higher GR&R (30% vs 20%)?
Part 4 Clause 5 — Syringe Barrel: Dimensions and Performance

Original Text

5 Syringe Barrel

5.1 Design including dimensions: Dimensions per Figure 1 and Table 1. Head design agreed between manufacturer and customer. Luer and Luer lock design per ISO 594-1, ISO 594-2, and ISO 80369-1.

5.2 Functional testing of Luer connection: Performance demonstrated using plastic female reference connectors (not steel) compliant with ISO 594-1, as glass tip has a "wavy" surface from forming process.

5.3 Material: Colourless (cl) or amber (br) glass, hydrolytic resistance grain class HGA 1 per ISO 720:1985. Also covered by Ph. Eur. 3.2.1 (Type I glass), USP <660>, JP 7.01.

5.4 Performance requirements:

  • 5.4.1 Hydrolytic resistance: HC 1 per ISO 4802-1 or ISO 4802-2
  • 5.4.2 Annealing quality: Residual stress ≤ 40 nm/mm optical retardation
  • 5.4.3 Inner surface lubrication: If lubricated, per Clause 6.5.1.2
  • 5.4.4 Flange breakage resistance: Limits per manufacturer-customer agreement; tested per Annex C.1
  • 5.4.5 Luer cone breakage resistance: Limits per agreement; tested per Annex C.2

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Luer Cone System (Luer Cone / Luer Lock): A standard connection system with a 6% taper that allows the syringe barrel to be connected to needles, IV infusion systems and other devices. A Luer (slip) fitting is held by friction; a Luer Lock has a threaded locking mechanism and is more secure. ISO 80369-7 is the latest international standard and is gradually replacing the older ISO 594 series.

Type I glass: The highest grade of pharmaceutical glass, with the strongest hydrolytic resistance (HGA 1). It is suitable for almost all injectables, especially drugs that are pH-sensitive or prone to reacting with glass. Type II glass is suitable only for aqueous solutions, and Type III only for solid dosage forms. Prefilled syringes must use Type I glass.

Amber Glass: Made by adding iron oxide and iron sulphide, it blocks light in the 290-450 nm wavelength range and protects light-sensitive drugs (such as certain biologics and vitamins). Clear glass is suitable for products where the appearance of the solution must be inspected directly.

Analogy

The Luer cone of a glass syringe barrel is like a standardised power socket: the globally uniform 6% taper ensures that needles from different manufacturers connect reliably to the barrel without an adapter. However, glass forming is not as precise as injection moulding and the surface has "waviness", so functionality must be assessed with plastic (not metal) gauges, like testing with a flexible connector rather than a rigid standard piece.

Key Notes

Flange Breakage Resistance: The finger flange of a glass syringe barrel is a key part for gripping and handling, and it bears even greater mechanical stress in auto-injectors. ISO 11040-4 requires a flange breakage test, but the specific limit is agreed between manufacturer and customer. This means that when a CDMO introduces a new drug packaging, this critical mechanical performance attribute must be clearly defined.

Inner Surface Siliconization: A trace amount of silicone oil is applied to the inner glass surface to reduce the sliding resistance of the plunger stopper. Too little silicone makes the plunger stopper hard to move (break-loose force too high); too much silicone may allow the oil to migrate into the drug product and must comply with the pharmacopoeial limits for silicone oil.

Formula

Flange breakage resistance test (Annex C.1):
  Apply a bending moment to the flange until it breaks
  Record the breaking force (N)
  Use as a baseline for production monitoring and design verification

Luer cone functional tests (using plastic reference connectors):
  Liquid leakage test
  Separation force test (needle pull-off force)
  Unscrewing torque test (Luer lock)
  → Do not use metal reference connectors (glass surface characteristics are incompatible)

Part 3: Part 5: Plunger Stoppers

Part 5

ISO 11040-5:2012 | Third Edition

Learning Objectives

  • Master the dimensional specifications and classification system of plunger stoppers for injectables (PSL snap-lid / PST threaded)
  • Understand the physical, chemical and biological requirements for plunger stopper materials (elastomers) and their link to the ISO 8871 series
  • Understand the functional role of the plunger stopper in a prefilled syringe system: sealing, sliding and plunger rod attachment
  • Recognise the importance of ageing resistance testing for shelf-life management

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11040 specifies the shape, dimensions, material, performance requirements and labelling of plunger stoppers for glass barrels (single-chamber design) for injection preparations in accordance with ISO 11040-4.

Plunger stoppers specified in this part of ISO 11040 are intended for single use only. This part of ISO 11040 is not applicable to barrier-coated plunger stoppers.

3 Classification

  • Type PSL: plunger stoppers with snap lid (snap-lid type)
  • Type PST: plunger stoppers with thread (threaded type)

4 Shape and Dimensions (Key entries)

4.2 General tolerances per Class M3, ISO 3302-1.

4.3 Spacers to prevent adhesion, height ≤ 0.3 mm.

4.4 Sprues (if present) shall not protrude beyond surface of plunger stopper.

4.5 Thread compatible with plunger rod; plunger stopper shall not detach from rod under normal use (e.g. aspiration).

5 Designation

EXAMPLE 1: Plunger stopper with snap lip, 0.5 ml: Plunger ISO 11040-5 - PSL - 0.5

EXAMPLE 2: Threaded plunger stopper, 1 ml long: Plunger ISO 11040-5 - PST - 1 - lg

6 Material

Elastomeric material shall meet requirements in Clause 7. Made from originally tested and approved elastomeric formulation by end user. Shall withstand two sterilization cycles at (121 ± 2)°C for 30 min. If other sterilization methods (e.g. irradiation), suitability shall be evaluated.

7 Requirements

7.2.1 Hardness: Nominal ± 5 Shore A (ISO 7619-1) or ± 5 IRHD (ISO 48).

7.2.2 Resistance to ageing: Maximum time from manufacture to pharmaceutical use agreed between manufacturer and user. Plunger stoppers shall maintain performance characteristics throughout entire shelf life, tested as part of stability tests by user.

7.3 Chemical requirements: Aqueous extractables shall not exceed limits for elastomeric parts per ISO 8871-1.

7.4 Biological requirements: Per ISO 8871-4.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

PSL vs PST design differences:

  • PSL (snap-lid): The top of the plunger stopper has a snap lid, which connects to the plunger rod by a snap fit. It is simple to operate and widely used for manual injection.
  • PST (threaded): The top of the plunger stopper has a thread (16 threads/25.4 mm for 1 ml), which screws onto a threaded plunger rod. The connection is firmer, and suits auto-injectors where displacement must be precisely controlled, as well as the injection of viscous formulations (such as certain monoclonal antibodies) that need greater force.

Barrier-Coated Plunger Stoppers: ISO 11040-5 explicitly does not apply to barrier-coated plunger stoppers (such as FluroTec® PTFE-coated or Teflon® FEP-coated). These coated stoppers are used for silicone-oil-sensitive drugs (such as certain protein drugs) and need additional validation requirements that fall outside the scope of this standard.

ISO 8871 series: The core performance standards for elastomeric parts for pharmaceutical use: Part 1 (extractables), Part 4 (biological requirements), Part 5 (functional requirements). By referencing ISO 8871-1 and 8871-4, ISO 11040-5 ensures that plunger stoppers meet the basic chemical and biocompatibility requirements for drug product safety.

Analogy

The plunger stopper is like the "heart valve" of the syringe: when pushed forward (injection) it must seal the drug solution perfectly without leaking, and it must also slide with the right force, neither too tight (painful injection for the patient) nor too loose (the solution bypasses the stopper and leaks). The PSL snap type is like the cap of a click-action ball-point pen, and the PST threaded type is like a tightened screw: the connection methods differ, and so do the reliability and use cases.

Key Notes

The importance of ageing resistance: Rubber elastomers age during storage (oxidation, changes in crosslink density), leading to increased hardness and reduced sealing. ISO 11040-5 requires the plunger stopper to retain its performance throughout the shelf life of the drug product, which means ageing tests for the plunger stopper (such as accelerated stability studies) must be included in the pre-marketing validation plan.

The Formula Lock principle: As in Part 2, the plunger stopper must be manufactured with the "elastomer formulation approved in the original test batch". Any formulation change may affect the extractables profile, hardness and functionality, and requires revalidation.

Plunger rod retention (clause 4.5 for the PST threaded type): A threaded plunger stopper must not detach from the plunger rod during normal use, including aspiration. This is especially important when a prefilled syringe is used for drug reconstitution (such as reconstituting lyophilised powder).

Plunger stopper dimensions and barrel fit: The plunger stopper dimension (d1 > barrel inner diameter d2) ensures an interference fit, which generates the sealing force; but a d1 that is too large causes excessive sliding force and affects the feel of the injection. Dimensional design is a delicate balance between sealing and operability.

Formula

Plunger stopper hardness tolerance:
  Nominal hardness ± 5 Shore A (ISO 7619-1)
  or nominal hardness ± 5 IRHD (ISO 48)

Plunger stopper interference (example: 1 ml long):
  Barrel inner diameter: 6.35 ±0.1 mm
  Plunger stopper diameter: 7.65–7.85 mm
  Interference ≈ 7.65–7.85 - 6.25~6.45 mm
          ≈ 1.2–1.6 mm (compression)

  ↓ More compression = stronger sealing force, but greater sliding resistance

Extractables limits reference (ISO 8871-1):
  Extractables in the aqueous autoclaved solution must
  comply with the pharmacopoeial limits for colour, pH, clarity,
  non-volatile residue, metal ions, etc.

Practical Application

When selecting plunger stoppers for prefilled syringes for an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC), the CDMO needs to consider in particular: (1) whether to use a barrier-coated plunger stopper (if the ADC is sensitive to silicone oil); (2) extractables studies: for a highly toxic ADC drug, the risk assessment threshold for extractables is stricter; (3) matching plunger stopper hardness to ADC viscosity: a high-viscosity ADC needs a lower plunger stopper hardness to ensure injectability. Note that barrier-coated plunger stoppers are outside the scope of ISO 11040-5 and need additional standards to support them.

Practice Questions

  1. A monoclonal antibody product requires the plunger stopper of the prefilled syringe to remain sealed after 24 months of storage under refrigerated conditions of -20°C to 2-8°C. What ageing validation data should the plunger stopper supplier provide?
  2. If the CDMO finds that the incoming plunger stopper hardness test result is nominal +6 Shore A, which does not meet the ±5 Shore A requirement of ISO 11040-5, what measures should be taken?
  3. Why must the silicone oil compatibility of the plunger stopper be specially evaluated for formulations containing Tween 80 (polysorbate)?

Section Summary

  • ISO 11040-5 defines two designs of plunger stoppers for injectables, PSL (snap-lid) and PST (threaded); the standard does not apply to barrier-coated plunger stoppers
  • The elastomer material of the plunger stopper must meet the requirements of ISO 8871-1 (chemical) and ISO 8871-4 (biological) and withstand two steam sterilisation cycles (121°C, 30 min)
  • Ageing resistance is a key requirement in plunger stopper supplier management and must cover the entire shelf life of the drug product
  • The "Formula Lock" principle ensures that the compatibility studies between the plunger stopper and the drug product remain valid, and any elastomer formulation change requires reassessment

Part 6 — Scope, Terms, General Requirements

Original Text

1 Scope

This document specifies materials, dimensions, quality, and performance requirements, as well as test methods for polymer barrels and sterilized subassembled syringes ready for filling, intended for single use only.

Prefilled syringes can be produced on dedicated and specifically designed processing equipment such as inline moulding and filling. This document does not apply but can be used also for such dedicated prefilled syringes.

3.1–3.8 Key Terms and Definitions

  • Customer: Business entity purchasing syringe barrels or subassembled syringes for further processing/filling
  • Manufacturer: Business entity manufacturing the polymer barrels or sterilized subassembled syringes
  • Staked needle syringe: Syringe with a needle integrated into the barrel (by insert moulding, gluing, or other bonding)
  • Sterilized subassembled syringe ready for filling: Subassembly pre-treated including injection moulding, needle assembly, inner surface lubrication, closure sealing, packaging (per ISO 11040-7), and sterilization
  • Sterile barrier system: Minimum package preventing ingress of microorganisms; for tub-and-nest: formed by tub and sealing lid

4 General Requirements

4.1 Quality systems: Per formal quality system; ISO 15378 (primary packaging QMS) and ISO 13485 (medical device design control) both referenced.

4.2 Testing: GR&R ≤ 20% (non-destructive) or ≤ 30% (destructive); statistically valid sampling plans (ISO 2859-1, ISO 3951).

4.3 Documentation: All compliance documented; retention period considering regulatory requirements, expiration date, traceability. Electronic records shall be reliable.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The rise of plastic syringe barrels: ISO 11040-6 was fully revised in 2019 and added requirements for the "subassembled sterile syringe", reflecting the rapid growth of plastic syringe barrels (especially cyclic olefin copolymer COP and cyclic olefin polymer COC) in the high-end drug packaging market. These materials are supplied by manufacturers such as DAIKYO/West, SCHOTT and Gerresheimer, and are widely used for monoclonal antibodies and cell and gene therapy products.

The introduction of ISO 13485: Compared with ISO 11040-4 (which refers only to ISO 15378), Part 6 additionally refers to ISO 13485 (quality management systems for medical devices). This reflects the fact that plastic syringes are increasingly used in combination products, which must meet both drug and medical device regulatory requirements.

Staked Needle via Insert Moulding: A plastic syringe can have the needle embedded directly into the barrel during injection moulding, with no adhesive needed. This eliminates the risk of adhesive extractables but requires more complex manufacturing process control.

Analogy

A glass syringe barrel is the traditional "ceramic teacup": long established and excellent in chemical inertness, but fragile and heavy. A plastic syringe barrel is the modern "vacuum-insulated stainless steel flask": light, drop-resistant and mass-producible, but the stability of the material under different temperatures and pressures must be considered (analogous to the oxygen transmission rate and vapour barrier of the polymer). Which container to choose depends on the nature of the beverage (drug product) and the usage scenario (route of administration, storage conditions).

Key Notes

"Inline Moulding and Filling": The standard states explicitly that it does not apply to this type of process, but it may be used as a guiding reference. This type of process (such as the plastic syringe version of blow-fill-seal) requires everything from raw material pellets to filling of the finished product to be completed on the same line, which eliminates the contamination risk of transport and storage but requires a dedicated process validation framework.

Dual standard references (ISO 15378 + ISO 13485): For a CDMO this means that when assessing a plastic syringe barrel supplier, besides confirming GMP compliance for drug products (ISO 15378), it must also assess compliance with medical device design control requirements (ISO 13485), especially in relation to design freeze and design change management.

Part 6 — Dimensions and Material Requirements

Original Text

5.1 Design including dimensions (Key sizes)

NOTE: 50 ml barrel length range is required for particular applications such as pumps and injectors.

6.2 Material Requirements

6.2.1 General properties to consider: microbial barrier, biocompatibility, physical/chemical properties, sterilization compatibility, sterility maintenance, shelf-life, fill-finish functionality, closure system robustness during transport. Material shall exhibit appropriate oxygen and water vapour permeability performance.

6.2.2 Duty of notification concerning modifications to polymers: Change control and notification procedures shall be in place between polymer transformer (syringe manufacturer) and pharmaceutical company. Particular attention to supplier raw material change notifications.

6.3 Physical requirements:

  • 6.3.1 Sterilization: Shall be compatible with selected sterilization process (EO, gamma, electron beam, X-ray, moist heat)
  • 6.3.2 Clarity and transparency: Appropriate for content inspection
  • 6.3.3 Particulate contamination: Manufacture processes to reduce risk; sub-visible limits similar to Part 4
  • 6.3.4 Lubricants: Inner surface lubricants per pharmacopoeia requirements

6.4 Chemical requirements: Per applicable pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur., USP, JP) for plastics containers for parenteral preparations.

6.5 Endotoxins and biological requirements: Endotoxin limit <0.25 EU/ml; three-log endotoxin reduction validation; per ISO 10993-1 for biocompatibility.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The main polymer materials for plastic syringe barrels:

  • COP (cyclic olefin polymer): Very low oxygen and water vapour transmission rates, good optical clarity and low extractables. Suitable for oxygen-sensitive biologics (such as insulin and monoclonal antibodies) and products requiring long-term refrigerated storage
  • COC (cyclic olefin copolymer): Similar performance to COP at a slightly lower cost. At present COP (such as ZEON's Zeonex®) and COC (such as Topas® COC) coexist in the market
  • PP (polypropylene): Can be autoclaved and is low in cost, but its clarity and barrier properties are inferior to COP/COC; mainly used for non-injection applications

Special applications of the 50 ml and 100 ml sizes: These large-volume plastic syringes are widely used for parenteral nutrition (TPN), infusion pumps for high-viscosity drugs and special injection devices. The wider size range around 50 ml also reflects design flexibility for different application scenarios (pump infusion vs. manual injection).

Key Notes

Obligation to notify polymer changes (Clause 6.2.2): This is an important new clause in ISO 11040-6. The manufacturer of the polymer raw material (such as plastic pellets) must notify the downstream syringe manufacturer of any change to its formulation or production process through an effective change control procedure, and the syringe manufacturer in turn notifies the drug manufacturer. This multi-tier supply chain notification chain is especially important for a CDMO, because a polymer change may affect the extractables profile and drug compatibility.

Compatibility with multiple sterilisation methods: Plastic syringe barrels support EO, gamma, electron beam and X-ray irradiation as well as autoclaving, a major advantage over glass syringe barrels. The effect of each sterilisation method on COP/COC materials needs to be validated, especially the yellowing and loss of mechanical properties that radiation sterilisation may cause.

Clarity and transparency (Clause 6.3.2): The transparency of plastic syringe barrels is usually lower than that of glass, and haze may appear, especially after repeated sterilisation. This affects the ability to perform visual inspection of the drug product, so a transparency standard needs to be established at incoming inspection of packaging materials to ensure that visible particles can be effectively detected.

When assessing plastic syringe barrel suppliers, the CDMO should focus on: (1) the source of the polymer material and the filing status of its DMF (drug master file); (2) the coverage and test conditions of the extractables and leachables (E&L) study reports; (3) sterilisation validation (especially the effect of gamma irradiation on clarity and extractables); (4) the notification mechanism for polymer raw material changes; (5) ISO 15378 and ISO 13485 certification status. For plastic syringe barrels used for biologics, it is recommended to require the supplier to provide "simulated extraction" study data specific to the target biologic.

  • Oxygen transmission rate, water vapour transmission rate, extractables profile and transparency of the polymer material are the key considerations in material selection; COP/COC are currently the first choice for high-end biologics

Part 5: Part 7: Packaging Systems

Part 7

ISO 11040-7:2015 | First Edition

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the structure of the tub-and-nest packaging system and the function of each component
  • Master the definition of the sterile barrier system and its role in maintaining syringe sterility
  • Learn the specific requirements for each packaging component (nest, tub, insert, lid, protective bag)
  • Recognize why standardized nest and tub dimensions matter for compatibility with CDMO filling equipment

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11040 specifies the packaging system used to deliver sterilized subassembled syringes ready for filling in tubs and nests. Downstream processes after filling are not within scope.

3 Terms and Definitions

  • Insert liner: Foil to cover the filled nest
  • Nest: Plastic plate with a defined hole pattern for the suspension of the syringe bodies
  • Tub: Plastic container to accommodate the filled nest
  • Sealing lid: Microbial barrier material for sealing the tub
  • Protective bag: Plastic bag or sealing around the tub
  • Sterile barrier system: Minimum package preventing ingress of microorganisms; for tub-and-nest: formed by tub and sealing lid
  • Packaging system: Combination of sterile barrier system and protective packaging

4.1 General Requirements

4.1.1 The packaging system shall have acceptable microbiological and particulate levels to support introduction into aseptic filling environment. Requirements agreed by manufacturer and customer.

4.1.2 Materials, sterile barrier system, and packaging system enabling sterilization and maintaining sterility shall comply with ISO 11607-1. The protective bag, at minimum, protects tub from external contaminants; ideally also maintains sterility over shelf-life.

4.2 Nest Requirements

4.2.1 Information to be provided: external dimensions, deflection, holes for syringes, centering/lifting openings, free space for lifting tool engagement. Customer and manufacturer agree on dimensions and tolerances.

4.2.2 Design shall facilitate insertion and removal of sterilized subassembled syringes (e.g. Luer lock adapter syringes) by adding bevels or other means.

4.2.3 Maximum acceptable nest deflection agreed by manufacturer and customer; determined per Annex B.

4.3 Tub Requirements

4.3.2 Tub shall allow sealing of lid. Tub flange shall be free of sharp edges to protect packaging integrity.

4.3.3 If sterilization indicators applied to tubs, per ISO 11138 and ISO 11140.

4.4 Insert Liner

Shall release minimum particles. Where appropriate, permeable for sterilization agent (e.g. non-woven polyolefin). Can consist of several layers to shield glass against electron beam during decontamination. Layers shall be connected (e.g. by sealing). Shape and dimensions shall comply with tub.

4.5 Sealing Lid

Shall be sealable to tub and completely peelable from tub while minimizing particle release. Seal properties (strength, width) and integrity tested per validated test method (per ISO 11607-1). Shall be permeable for sterilization agent. Material selection shall consider decontamination processes (e.g. electron beam) prior to transfer into aseptic filling area.

4.6 Protective Bag

4.6.1 Permanently sealed; shall enable selected sterilization method. Seal properties tested per validated method (ISO 11607-1).

4.6.2 Can be single or double bag. For single bag: bag dimensions, sealing joint width/position/type, material, orientation of tub inside bag. For double bag: additional inner bag folding information required.

6 Marking of the Tub

Marked as agreed, can include: manufacturer name; article description; quantity of syringes; warning "do not use if packaging is damaged"; date of manufacture; manufacturer batch number; serial number. Machine-readable codes (barcodes, 2D codes) recommended. Symbols per ISO 7000 or ISO 15223-1.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Tub-and-Nest System: The standardized packaging format for modern prefilled syringes. The syringes are inserted into the holes of a plastic nest and held in suspension, the nest is placed in a plastic tub, and the tub is heat-sealed with a permeable lid to form the "sterile barrier system". The whole sealed tub is then placed in a protective bag, sterilized with EO and delivered. At filling, the customer removes the protective bag and lid in a sterile environment and fills the drug product directly in the nest.

Sterile Barrier System: The standard explicitly defines it as the combination of "tub + lid"; the protective bag is not included. The significance of this definition is that the protective bag is removed before the tub enters the sterile filling area, so the barrier that actually maintains syringe sterility until filling is the tub and lid. Lid integrity verification and tub seal testing are therefore at the core of packaging validation.

Standardization of the 160-hole nest: Annex A defines standard dimensions (230.2 mm × 198.5 mm) for the 160-hole nest (for 1 ml syringes). The aim is to promote market standardization so that ready-to-fill syringes from different suppliers can run on the same filling equipment, reducing the CDMO's equipment investment risk.

Analogy

The tub-and-nest system is like a supermarket egg carton: the eggs (syringes) sit in the holes of the cardboard (nest) so they cannot knock against each other; the whole tray goes into a clear container (tub) that is closed with a cover (lid); finally everything goes into a shopping bag (protective bag). The cover is the key barrier that keeps the eggs fresh, while the shopping bag is just an outer layer of protection. This is the intuitive meaning of the ISO 11040-7 definition "sterile barrier system = tub + lid".

Key Notes

Purpose of the multi-layer insert: The standard allows multi-layer inserts, one important purpose of which is to "shield the glass from damage by electron beam irradiation" (Clause 4.4). E-beam decontamination is a commonly used surface decontamination method for tubs before they enter the sterile filling area, but the electron beam can damage the glass syringe barrel. Multiple layers of nonwoven material can absorb part of the radiation energy and protect the glass.

The "completely peelable" requirement for the lid: Clause 4.5 requires the lid to minimize particle release during peeling. On a CDMO sterile filling line, operators (or automated equipment) peel the lid in a grade A environment, and any particle shedding could contaminate the drug product. The choice of lid material (usually nonwoven Tyvek®) and confirmation of seal strength are key parameters of packaging validation.

Serial number and batch number on the tub label: Clause 6 mentions that a serial number may be included, which provides a basis for serialized traceability of syringe packaging and is consistent with the current regulatory trend toward drug serialization (such as the EU FMD and DSCSA).

Formula and Calculation

Typical tub-and-nest system specifications (1 ml syringe as an example):

Nest dimensions: 230.2 mm × 198.5 mm × 17.5 mm (height)
Capacity: 160 syringes (standard format)
Hole pitch (I): 12.7 mm (160 holes)

Example calculation of quantity received per batch:
  10 cartons × 4 tubs/carton × 160 syringes/tub
  = 6,400 ready-to-fill syringes delivered per batch

EO sterilization permeability requirement:
  The lid and protective bag must be permeable
  → allows EO gas to enter and reach the syringe surface
  → the vent design ensures sufficient EO exchange

E-beam decontamination (before entry into the area):
  The insert protects the glass
  The protective bag material must withstand E-beam energy
  (generally a surface decontamination dose of <30 kGy)

Practical Application

When qualifying a new ready-to-fill syringe supplier, a CDMO should carry out the following packaging system verification: (1) seal integrity testing, including lid peel strength and bubble leak testing; (2) microbial barrier testing to confirm the microbial barrier capability of the lid and tub; (3) nest dimension confirmation to ensure compatibility with the filling line's robotic handler; (4) E-beam decontamination compatibility, confirming that the protective bag and insert materials do not release additional particles after E-beam; (5) shelf-life testing of the packaging system's ability to maintain sterility under the specified storage conditions. These tests usually require 12–24 months of accelerated and real-time aging studies.

  2. A CDMO plans to introduce an electron beam (E-beam) decontamination system to replace hydrogen peroxide (VHP) vapor decontamination for the entry of ready-to-fill syringes into the filling area. Against the requirements of ISO 11040-7, what packaging material requalification tests are needed?
  3. Why does the standard require the lid to be "completely peelable" and not allow the lid to tear? Analyze the risks of lid tearing from a GMP perspective.

Section Summary

  • ISO 11040-7 defines the "tub-and-nest" packaging system for ready-to-fill sterile syringes; the core "sterile barrier system" consists of the tub + lid (the protective bag is not included)
  • The packaging system must comply with ISO 11607-1 (packaging for terminally sterilized medical devices); the lid must be completely peelable and minimize particle release
  • The standardized 160-hole nest dimensions (230 mm × 198 mm) help promote market standardization and ensure interchangeability of ready-to-fill syringes from different suppliers with CDMO filling equipment
  • The packaging system must be compatible with EO sterilization (permeability requirement) and E-beam decontamination (material tolerance), and the insert must protect the glass syringe barrel from electron beam damage

Part 8 Clauses 1–4 — Scope, User Requirements, Risk Management, Usability

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11040 is applicable to aseptically filled or terminally sterilized finished prefilled syringes (intended for single use only) based on ISO 11040-4 or ISO 11040-6, together with ISO 11040-5, for parenteral injection preparations with focus on quality, functional performance and safety requirements.

NOTE 1: Can also be used as guidance for other types, designs and/or sizes (e.g. dual chamber prefilled syringes).

NOTE 2: If used in a needle-based injection system, see also ISO 11608-3.

NOTE 4: Finished prefilled syringes containing so-called borderline products (e.g. hyaluronic acid) are included, though not always regulated as pharmaceutical products.

3 Terms and Definitions

3.1 Finished prefilled syringe: Prefilled container closure system for parenteral injection preparations as it is marketed, including e.g. assembly of components, terminal sterilization and final packaging.

3.2 Manufacturer: Natural or legal person holding the licence for the pharmaceutical product with responsibility for the design, manufacture, packaging, and labelling of a finished prefilled syringe, before it is placed on the market.

4 User Requirements

4.1 Definition of intended use: Manufacturer shall define intended use considering:

  • Users including their health status
  • Route of administration
  • Additional medical devices/application aids (needle-based injection systems, infusion tubes, plunger rod/finger flange)
  • Characteristics of environment during transport, storage and use
  • Point of use (hospital, homecare)
  • Frequency of application; criticality of medication

4.2 Risk management: Manufacturers shall follow a risk-based approach per ISO 14971 during design, development, manufacture and life cycle. Risk management shall consider intended use, interactions between content and container, and environmental conditions. This can result in product-specific requirements and test methods that differ from this part of ISO 11040.

4.3 Application of usability engineering: The usability of the prefilled syringe shall be considered and validated according to a process compliant with IEC 62366. Instructions for use are part of usability testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dual regulatory identity of the finished prefilled syringe: The Introduction of ISO 11040-8 states that a finished prefilled syringe requires a marketing authorization as a drug, a combination product or a medical device, depending on the contents and intended use. In the EU, drug-led combination products are approved under the medicinal products legislation (2001/83/EC) and must also meet the relevant requirements of the MDR (EU 2017/745); in the US, after classification under 21 CFR 3.2(e), the review is led by the relevant FDA center (CDER/CDRH/CBER).

ISO 14971 (risk management for medical devices): Requires systematic identification, evaluation, control and monitoring of risks across the whole life cycle. For prefilled syringes, the risk assessment must cover: (1) container-drug interactions (such as the interaction between silicone oil and proteins); (2) use error, i.e. patient handling mistakes in the home environment; (3) manufacturing process risks (such as the effect of terminal sterilization on seal integrity).

IEC 62366 (usability engineering): Requires systematic evaluation, during design, of the ability of intended users to operate the product in the intended use environment. For prefilled syringes with an autoinjector device (such as a pen-injector), formative and summative usability studies must be performed to confirm that users can use the product safely and correctly.

Analogy

ISO 11040-8 is like the acceptance standard for a complete car, while Parts 1–7 are the specifications for the individual components (engine, tires, steering wheel). Components that each meet their standards do not mean the whole car can be driven safely; systematic whole-vehicle functional testing (braking performance, steering responsiveness and so on) is needed, just as ISO 11040-8 requires systematic functional performance testing of the finished prefilled syringe (break-loose force, burst pressure, seal integrity and so on).

Key Notes

Broad definition of manufacturer: ISO 11040-8 defines the "manufacturer" as "the legal entity holding the marketing authorization for the drug product", not "the owner of the production facility". This means that even when a CDMO performs the manufacturing, final responsibility remains with the marketing authorization holder (MAH). The division of responsibilities between the CDMO and the MAH should be clearly specified in the Quality Agreement.

Inclusion of borderline products: ISO 11040-8 explicitly brings "borderline products" such as hyaluronic acid into its scope; these are sometimes regulated as medical devices (for example, intra-articular filler injections). This reflects the intent of ISO 11040-8 as a "neutral framework" applicable to any prefilled syringe, regardless of regulatory status.

Part 8 Clauses 5–8 — System Characterization, Performance, Pharmaceutical, and Documentation Requirements

Original Text

5 System Characterization

5.1 Critical dimensions: Defined considering intended use. Special focus on interfaces with other devices (e.g. auto-injectors, needleless connectors) and plunger stopper position.

5.2 Description of components and materials: ISO 11040-4 and ISO 11040-6 shall apply for barrel. Barrel can include staked needle. Needle dimensions (inner/outer diameter) consistent with intended use (route, injection systems, viscosity).

5.3 Description of content: Critical parameters of content: viscosity, density, surface tension, fill volume (with tolerances), pH. These are temperature dependent and can change over time.

6 Performance Requirements

6.1 General: Manufacturer shall ensure finished prefilled syringe meets appropriate functional requirements until end of shelf life.

6.2 Break loose and extrusion forces: Tested with final system (assembled plunger + rod) at application temperature. Test speed defined based on intended use and content properties. Forces might change over shelf life.

6.3 Burst resistance: Tested per ISO 11040-4:2015, G.2 with sealed front end. Test pressures defined for intended use including high viscosity content and use in injection systems.

6.4 Break resistance: Flange break per ISO 11040-4:2015, 5.4.4; Luer cone breakage per ISO 11040-4:2015, 5.4.5.

6.5 Closure system forces and torques: Test methods of ISO 11040-4:2015, G.3 to G.6 apply.

6.6 Connectivity with fluid path connectors: Additional connectors tested per ISO 80369-1, ISO 80369-7, ISO 80369-20.

6.7 Residual volume: Tested in combination with components as considered in intended use. ISO 11040-4:2015, 6.5.1.3 applies.

6.8 Needle penetration force: Per ISO 11040-4:2015, Annex F. NOTE: Terminal sterilization (moist heat) can affect silicone layer on needle.

6.9 Needle pull-out force: ISO 11040-4:2015, 6.5.2.5 applies.

6.10 Sharps injury protection: If integrated sharps injury protection features, shall meet ISO 23908.

6.11 Liquid leakage beyond plunger: Per ISO 7886-1:2015, Annex B; with blocked fluid path, axial force on plunger stopper consistent with maximum force generated during use.

6.12 Markings: All graduation/indicator lines shall be verified for accuracy appropriate for intended use.

7 Pharmaceutical Requirements

7.1 General: Container closure system suitable for content (protection, safety, compatibility, performance) considering intended use.

7.2 Drug-container interaction: Compatibility of drug product with all container components, including leachables, adsorption, and degradation. Study conditions shall reflect actual use conditions including shelf life and storage temperature.

7.3 Biological requirements: Per ISO 10993-1; biocompatibility evaluation shall consider all materials in contact with drug product and patient.

7.4 Container closure integrity (CCI): Integrity shall be maintained throughout shelf life. Test methods and acceptance criteria shall be established per ICH Q1A, ISO 11607-1, or USP <1207>. Probabilistic methods (e.g. CCIT) preferred over sterility testing.

7.5 Deliverable volume: Shall comply with stated fill volume within appropriate tolerances, per national/regional regulations.

7.6 Particles (visible and subvisible): Per applicable pharmacopoeia. Visible inspection per applicable methods. Sub-visible per USP <788>, <787> or Ph. Eur. 2.9.19/2.9.20.

8 Documentation

Documentation shall demonstrate that the finished prefilled syringe meets requirements of this standard and applicable regulatory requirements. Documentation shall include results of tests, process controls, and any deviations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Break-Loose Force and Extrusion Force:

  • Break-loose force: The force required to start the plunger stopper moving from rest, usually higher than the sustained gliding force. During static storage (especially after terminal sterilization), the plunger stopper develops some adhesion to the glass/silicone layer, which increases the break-loose force.
  • Extrusion force: The force required to keep the plunger stopper moving. If it is too high, the patient cannot complete the injection; if it is too low, the seal may be inadequate and the product may leak.

Container Closure Integrity (CCI): ISO 11040-8 explicitly requires that CCI be maintained throughout the shelf life, and recommends probabilistic CCI testing methods (such as helium leak testing, headspace analysis and high voltage leak detection) rather than sterility testing as the primary CCI test method, which is consistent with the guidance in USP <1207>.

Drug-Container Interaction: Studies must cover three dimensions: leachables, adsorption (such as protein adsorption to the inner wall of the syringe barrel or to the plunger stopper) and degradation (such as drug oxidation by silicone oil). Study conditions must include the worst case for shelf life and storage temperature.

Analogy

Performance testing of a finished prefilled syringe is like the full technical inspection of a race car before it goes on the track:

  • Break-loose/extrusion force = clutch and throttle responsiveness test
  • Burst pressure = maximum pressure test of the hydraulic system
  • CCI = fuel tank seal and airtightness test
  • Drug-container interaction = chemical compatibility test of the fuel with the fuel tank material
  • Visible particles = visual inspection of the fuel tank for foreign matter (residue/particles)

Key Notes

Effect of terminal sterilization on syringe performance: ISO 11040-8 specifically notes that terminal steam sterilization (moist heat sterilization) may affect the silicone oil layer on the needle (Clause 6.8 NOTE) and may increase the plunger stopper break-loose force. Performance testing must therefore be carried out on the sterilized syringe in its "as marketed" state, not only immediately after filling. This is critical to the design of the CDMO's performance validation plan.

Needlestick injury protection (Clause 6.10): ISO 23908 requires the needlestick protection feature (safety feature) to activate automatically after use to protect healthcare workers from needlestick injuries. More and more regulators (especially in the EU and US) require prefilled syringes with an autoinjector device to be equipped with this feature, and the CDMO needs to confirm compliance with this requirement during design review.

Importance of choosing the CCI test method: ISO 11040-8 states that CCI should be confirmed by a test method validated over the shelf life, and prefers deterministic methods (such as helium leak and high voltage leak detection) over probabilistic sterility testing. This is consistent with the direction of the 2016 update of USP <1207>. When designing the CCI validation protocol, the CDMO should give priority to deterministic methods and establish acceptance criteria for sensitivity and specificity.

Practical Application

When taking on validation work for the release batch of a finished prefilled syringe, a CDMO should establish a complete Finished Product Specification based on ISO 11040-8, containing at least the following tests and acceptance criteria: (1) appearance and visible particles; (2) fill volume accuracy; (3) subvisible particles (HIAC or membrane filtration method); (4) sterility test; (5) endotoxin; (6) break-loose/extrusion force (at each time point of the accelerated stability study); (7) container closure integrity (CCI, deterministic methods preferred); (8) product-specific compatibility indicators (such as protein content, purity and potency). The acceptance criteria for these tests should be established jointly on the basis of ICH Q6A/Q6B (biologics) and the requirements of ISO 11040-8.

Section Summary

  • ISO 11040-8 is the "system-level" standard of the ISO 11040 series, covering the functional performance (12 items) and drug product requirements (6 items) of finished prefilled syringes, and complementing the component-level requirements of Parts 1–7
  • Risk management (ISO 14971) and usability engineering (IEC 62366) are the core methodologies for the design and validation of finished syringes; all performance requirements and test methods can be adjusted on the basis of risk assessment
  • Container closure integrity (CCI) testing should preferably use deterministic methods (such as helium leak), and its ability to be maintained should be validated over the entire shelf life
  • Drug-container interaction studies must cover leachables, adsorption and degradation, and be performed under worst-case shelf-life conditions; this is one of the most complex technical tasks before commercial launch
