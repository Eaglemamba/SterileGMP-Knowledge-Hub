Foreword

ISO 15378:2017 is the fourth edition, prepared by ISO/TC 76 (Transfusion, infusion and injection, and blood processing equipment for medical and pharmaceutical use). It cancels and replaces ISO 15378:2015 (third edition).

Key technical changes from the 2015 edition:

  • Integration of sector-specific GMP requirements directly into the ISO 9001:2015 framework
  • Deletion of the requirement for a quality manual
  • Inclusion of all annexes from ISO 9001:2015
  • Terminology aligned with ISO 9000:2015
  • Addition of an alphabetical index of defined terms

The document uses a visual convention: clauses quoted directly and unchanged from ISO 9001:2015 appear in boxes. GMP-specific additions appear outside boxes.

Foreword Analysis

Significance of the fourth edition: The 2017 edition fully integrates GMP requirements into the ISO 9001:2015 structure. They are no longer an add-on document but a true "dual-track standard".

Typographic Convention - How to Read This Standard

Text inside the boxes: quoted directly from ISO 9001:2015, unmodified.

Text outside the boxes: GMP-specific requirements or recommendations added by ISO 15378, which primary packaging material manufacturers must meet in addition.

When reading the standard, look first at the text outside the boxes, because that is the part where ISO 15378 goes "beyond ISO 9001" and is the focus of attention in supplier audits.

Removal of the "Quality Manual" Requirement

Before the 2015 edition, organizations were required to have a quality manual. The 2017 edition, aligned with ISO 9001:2015, no longer mandates a quality manual as such, but the documentation requirements for the quality management system still exist; the form is simply more flexible.

Introduction 0.1 - General

This document identifies Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) principles and specifies requirements for a quality management system applicable to primary packaging materials for medicinal products.

The realization of GMP principles in production and control of primary packaging materials is of great importance for patient safety, because of their direct product contact. The application of GMP for pharmaceutical packaging materials helps ensure that these materials meet the needs and requirements of the pharmaceutical industry.

"This document is an application standard for primary packaging materials, which contains the text of ISO 9001:2015."

Verbal forms used:

  • "shall" - a requirement
  • "should" - a recommendation
  • "may" - a permission
  • "can" - a possibility or capability

The phrase "if appropriate" appears throughout the document. When a requirement is qualified by this phrase, it is deemed appropriate unless the organization can document a justification otherwise.

0.1 Analysis - The Nature of the Standard

ISO 15378 = ISO 9001 + GMP: The Dual-Track Architecture

Think of ISO 15378 as a building:

  • Foundation = ISO 9001:2015 (the generic quality management system framework)
  • Superstructure = GMP-specific requirements (tailored for pharmaceutical packaging materials)

A supplier holding ISO 15378 certification has a QMS that satisfies both sets of requirements, reaching a pharmaceutical-grade level of quality management.

Practical Application in CDMO Audits

When you carry out supplier qualification for a rubber stopper or glass vial supplier:

Scenario A: The supplier holds ISO 15378 certification → the audit scope can be reduced. Focus on confirming the validity of the certificate and recent surveillance reports, without reviewing the QMS framework from scratch.

Scenario B: The supplier holds only ISO 9001 certification → additional audit of GMP-related requirements is needed (production controls, batch records, retention samples, etc.), so the workload increases significantly.

ISO 15378 certification serves as the supplier's "GMP pre-qualification evidence".

The Legal Implication of "if appropriate"

This phrase is important: ISO 15378 presumes that every requirement marked "if appropriate" applies, unless the organization can give a documented justification for why it does not. This means the burden of proof for "not applicable" rests on the organization, and requirements cannot be skipped lightly.

Introduction 0.2 - Quality Management Principles

ISO 9001:2015 is based on seven quality management principles described in ISO 9000. These principles form the philosophical foundation of the standard:

  1. Customer focus - the customer is at the center
  2. Leadership - leadership
  3. Engagement of people - involvement of personnel
  4. Process approach - managing activities as processes
  5. Improvement - continual improvement
  6. Evidence-based decision making - decisions based on evidence
  7. Relationship management - managing relationships

A key objective of this document is to specify GMP for primary packaging materials.

Introduction 0.3 - Process Approach

0.3.1 General

This standard promotes the adoption of a process approach — understanding and managing interrelated processes as a system. This enables the organization to control interrelationships and interdependencies among processes, enhancing overall performance.

The application of the process approach enables:

  • Understanding and consistency in meeting requirements
  • Consideration of processes in terms of added value
  • Achievement of effective process performance
  • Improvement of processes based on evaluation of data and information

0.3.2 Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) Cycle

The PDCA cycle can be applied to all processes and to the QMS as a whole:

  • Plan: Establish objectives and processes, identify risks/opportunities
  • Do: Implement what was planned
  • Check: Monitor and measure processes against policies and objectives
  • Act: Take actions to improve performance

0.3.3 Risk-Based Thinking

Risk-based thinking is essential for achieving an effective QMS. It enables an organization to determine factors that could cause deviation from planned results and put in place preventive controls.

"Because of the nature of primary packaging materials the risk-based approach is applied throughout all processes of the organization."

0.3 Analysis - PDCA and Risk-Based Thinking

The PDCA Cycle in Glass Vial Manufacturing

  • Plan: Set the dimensional specification and particulate standard for each glass vial batch
  • Do: Run production according to SOPs, with in-process control (IPC)
  • Check: Final inspection against specification, with statistical process capability (Cpk)
  • Act: When a nonconformance trend is found, adjust forming parameters or equipment and update the SOP

Putting Risk-Based Thinking into Practice

ISO 15378 stresses that, because primary packaging materials come into direct contact with the medicinal product, risk-based thinking must run through all processes, not only the design or validation stage.

For example: a rubber stopper supplier must assess the compatibility risk of siliconization; an aluminum cap manufacturer must assess the effect of crimp force on container closure integrity.

This is why 3.11 has a dedicated subsection on "terms relating to risk management".

CDMO Audit Checkpoints

  1. Ask the supplier to show its PDCA improvement records (CAPA list + completion rate)
  2. Check whether there is a documented risk assessment report covering the critical process steps
  3. Confirm that the risk assessment tools are appropriate (FMEA, FTA or HACCP are all acceptable)

Introduction 0.4 - Relationship with Other Standards

This document incorporates the requirements of ISO 9001:2015 and, additionally, particular requirements for primary packaging materials derived and adapted from GMP for the production and control of medicinal products.

ISO 15378 relates to other ISO standards as follows:

  • ISO 9000:2015 — provides essential background for understanding ISO 9001

Section 1 - Scope

ISO 9001:2015 Scope (incorporated)

This International Standard specifies requirements for a quality management system when an organization:

  • (a) needs to demonstrate its ability to consistently provide products and services that meet customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements, and
  • (b) aims to enhance customer satisfaction through the effective application of the system, including processes for improvement and assurance of conformity to requirements.

All requirements are generic and intended to be applicable to any organization, regardless of type, size, or the products and services it provides.

ISO 15378 Additional Scope

In addition to ISO 9001, this document specifies GMP requirements applicable to primary packaging materials for a quality management system where an organization needs to demonstrate its ability to provide primary packaging materials for medicinal products, which consistently meet:

  • Customer requirements
  • Regulatory requirements
  • International Standards

This document is an application standard for the design, manufacture and supply of primary packaging materials for medicinal products.

Section 1 Analysis - Who Needs to Comply?

Target Audience: Primary Packaging Material Manufacturers

ISO 15378 applies to organizations that design, manufacture and supply primary packaging materials. Common primary packaging materials include:

  • Glass vials, ampoules
  • Rubber stoppers / closures
  • Aluminum crimp caps / seals
  • Plastic containers (HDPE/PP/PET bottles)
  • Prefilled syringe components: barrel, plunger stopper, needle shield, tip cap
  • Blister packaging materials (blister foils): aluminum foil, PVC/PVDC base film
  • IV bags (intravenous bags)

Out of Scope

  • Secondary packaging (cartons, leaflets, labels) → no direct contact with the medicinal product
  • Contract laboratories (testing only, not manufacturing packaging materials)
  • Distributors (resale only, with no involvement in design or manufacturing)
  • Pharmaceutical manufacturers themselves (who should follow EU GMP, ICH Q10, etc.)

CDMO QA Practice: Where a Supplier Audit Starts

  1. Confirm that the supplier holds a valid ISO 15378 certificate (Certificate of Registration)
  2. Check that the certification scope covers the specific product category you purchase (for example, rubber stoppers and glass vials are different categories)
  3. Confirm that the certification body (CB) is an IAF-accredited body
  4. Check the certificate expiry date and the result of the most recent surveillance audit

Section 2 Analysis - The Meaning of Normative References

Normative vs Informative References

Normative reference: the referenced document forms part of ISO 15378 and must be complied with.

Informative reference: provides supplementary background and is not mandatory.

ISO 14698-1 and -2 are listed as normative references, which means the cleanroom biocontamination control methods are a mandatory component of ISO 15378.

Why Is ISO 14698 Included?

The production environment for primary packaging materials (especially rubber stoppers and syringe components) needs microbial contamination control. ISO 14698 defines a systematic approach to biocontamination control, including environmental monitoring (EM) programs, sample collection and data interpretation.

This means that biocontamination control in the cleanrooms of an ISO 15378-certified plant must follow the ISO 14698 methodology, rather than an arbitrarily designed EM program.

Section 3 Overview - Why Definitions Matter

The Practical Value of Consistent Terminology

In supplier agreements (Quality Agreement, Technical Agreement), both parties must use consistent terminology, otherwise ambiguity and disputes easily arise. The definitions in ISO 15378 provide an objective basis for this.

For example: "batch" and "lot" are synonyms in this standard, but if the pharmaceutical company and the supplier understand them differently, traceability records may be incomplete.

New ISO 15378 Terms vs Original ISO 9000:2015 Terms

ISO 15378 redefines only the terms from ISO 9000:2015 that it has modified (for example organization, validation). Terms not listed are taken directly from the original definitions in ISO 9000:2015.

Therefore, when reading Section 3 of ISO 15378, you should refer to ISO 9000:2015 at the same time.

3.1 Terms Related to Organization

3.1.1 organization

Person or group of people that has its own functions with responsibilities, authorities and relationships to achieve its objectives.

Note 2 (ISO 15378 specific): In this document the organization is the company manufacturing the primary packaging material (3.6.4).

[Modified from ISO 9000:2015, 3.2.1]

3.1.2 quality unit

Organizational unit which fulfils both quality assurance (QA) and quality control (QC) responsibilities.

Note: The quality unit(s) can consist of separate QA and QC units or of a single individual (or group), depending upon the size and structure of the organization.

3.1 Analysis

organization - its specific meaning in this standard

In ISO 9001, "organization" refers to any type of company; ISO 15378 specifies that in the context of this standard, the organization is the company that manufactures primary packaging materials. This clarification matters because it prevents the subject of the standard from being misread as the pharmaceutical manufacturer or another party.

quality unit - the core organizational structure of GMP

The QU (quality unit) is a core concept in a GMP environment. It is equivalent to the "QA department" of a pharmaceutical company, though in a small packaging material plant it may be one person holding multiple roles.

Key functions:

  • Batch release decisions
  • Deviation investigation and approval
  • Supplier audits
  • Document control and SOP review

During an audit, confirm the independence of the QU within the organization; it must not report directly to production.

3.2 Terms Related to Activity

3.2.1 assembly

Fitting together of primary packaging materials and/or components.

Example: Pipette assemblies for filling, prepared components of injection systems, positioning of needle shields on prefillable syringes.

3.2.2 change control

Documented control of changes with an appropriate risk management.

Note: Changes can include changes in raw materials, specifications, facilities, equipment, production processes and test methods.

3.2.3 Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)

Manufacturing, quality control and quality assurance applied in quality control.

Note 3: GMP for primary packaging materials requires, in addition to suitable provision of personnel, premises and equipment, a QMS that includes controls for incoming starting materials, manufacture, documentation, factory hygiene, final inspection, distribution records, processing of complaints and self-inspection.

Note 4: GMP and cGMP are equivalent.

3.2.4 installational qualification (IQ)

Process of obtaining and documenting evidence that equipment has been provided and installed in accordance with its specification.

[SOURCE: ISO/TS 11139:2006, 2.22]

3.2.5 operational qualification (OQ)

Process of obtaining and documenting evidence that installed equipment operates within predetermined limits when used in accordance with its operational procedures.

[SOURCE: ISO/TS 11139:2006, 2.27]

3.2.6 origination / artwork

All preparative activities prior to print.

Note: These include concept, design, graphics, reprographics, film, plate making, silk screens and digital files and masters.

3.2.7 performance qualification (PQ)

Verification that the proposed specification for the facility, equipment or system is suitable for the intended use.

[SOURCE: ISO/TS 11139:2006, 2.30]

3.2.8 qualification

Process to demonstrate the ability to fulfil specified requirements.

Note 2: Qualification comprises design qualification (DQ), IQ (3.2.4), OQ (3.2.5) and could include PQ (3.2.7) as well as re-qualification.

Note 3: Qualification can be applied to facilities, equipment and utilities.

3.2.9 quality control

Part of quality management focused on fulfilling quality requirements.

Note: Quality control includes checking or testing that specifications are met.

[Modified from ISO 9000:2015, 3.3.7]

3.2 Analysis - Activity Terms

change control - its GMP significance

Change control is one of the most essential management tools in GMP. ISO 15378 stresses that change control must be documented and must involve appropriate risk management.

Why is it so important? Any change to a primary packaging material (even just a change of raw material supplier) may affect the safety and stability of the medicinal product. The pharmaceutical company may need to repeat compatibility testing, or even notify the regulatory authority.

From the CDMO QA perspective: the supplier must notify you in a timely manner before making a change and provide enough technical data for you to assess whether revalidation is needed.

qualification vs validation - clearing up a common confusion

Qualification: applied to equipment, facilities and utilities - demonstrating that they meet specified requirements.

Validation: applied to manufacturing processes, cleaning procedures and analytical methods - demonstrating that the process consistently delivers the intended results.

In short: you qualify your vial washer (IQ/OQ/PQ), and you validate the vial washing process (cleaning validation).

IQ / OQ / PQ - the qualification trilogy

These are the standard steps of equipment qualification in a GMP environment:

ISO 15378 states specifically in 3.2.7 that PQ is a form of verification, that is, providing objective evidence that the specified requirements are fit for the intended use.

3.3 Terms Related to System

3.3.1 airlock

Enclosed space to control air-flow.

Note: Typically has at least two interlocked doors between two or more rooms, used either by people or for goods, to control different conditions (cleanliness, air-flow).

3.3.2 calibration

Process of checking or adjusting (by comparison with a reference standard) the accuracy of a measuring instrument.

Note: Calibration establishes the relationship between values indicated by a measuring instrument and corresponding known values of a reference standard.

3.3.3 cleanroom

Room within which the number concentration of airborne particles is controlled and classified, and which is designed, constructed and operated in a manner to control the introduction, generation and retention of particles inside the room.

[SOURCE: ISO 14644-1:2015, 3.1.1]

3.3.4 clean zone

Defined space within which the number concentration of airborne particles is controlled and classified, and which is constructed and operated in a manner to control the introduction, generation and retention of contaminants inside the space.

Note 3: A clean zone can be a defined space within a cleanroom or achieved by a separative device (isolator, RABS).

[SOURCE: ISO 14644-1:2015, 3.1.2]

3.3.5 controlled area / controlled environment

Area or environment constructed and operated to control the possible introduction of potential contaminants.

3.3.6 working area

Defined area where origination, production, packaging, test or inspection operations are carried out and where such activity will usually be subject to a line clearance.

3.3 Analysis - System Terms

airlock - what it does

An airlock is a key facility for preventing contamination from crossing between cleanliness grades. ISO 15378 defines its core function as "controlling airflow", not merely serving as a transition space.

In a primary packaging material plant, airlocks are used for:

  • Gowning/disinfection of personnel before entering the clean area
  • Transfer of materials from non-clean areas into clean areas
  • Preventing loss of control of the pressure differential between different cleanliness grades

cleanroom vs clean zone - the difference

Cleanroom: a complete room whose structural design is itself intended to control particles.

Clean zone: a specific space within a cleanroom, or an area formed by a separation device (such as an isolator or RABS).

For example: the whole filling room is a cleanroom (ISO Class 8), and the RABS filling area within it is a clean zone (ISO Class 5).

Note: ISO 15378 refers to the definitions in ISO 14644-1, ensuring that the terminology is consistent with the cleanroom standards.

calibration - the link to GMP

In a GMP environment, all measuring instruments used for product release and process control must be calibrated regularly. ISO 15378 defines calibration as a process of comparison against a reference standard, emphasizing traceability to national/international standards.

During an audit, confirm: Is the calibration program complete? Are any instruments overdue for calibration? Are the calibration results within the acceptance criteria?

3.4 Terms Related to Requirement

3.4.1 expiration date

Expected suitable use limit; shelf-life.

Note 2: This is typically the period during which a primary packaging material is expected to remain suitable for use if stored under defined conditions, and after which it should not be used.

3.4.2 shelf-life

Period during which a primary packaging material is expected to comply with the requirements (specifications).

3.4 Analysis

expiration date vs shelf-life - dating of primary packaging materials

Everyone is familiar with the expiry date of a medicinal product, but primary packaging materials themselves also have a shelf-life, a point many people overlook.

Take rubber stoppers as an example: the effect of siliconization may decline over time, or extractables may exceed limits after long-term storage. The supplier must therefore define:

  • Under what storage conditions (temperature, humidity, light)
  • How long the material remains in conformance with specification (shelf-life)
  • After which it must not be used (expiration date)

The CDMO's receiving procedure must check the expiry of packaging materials to avoid using expired material.

3.5 Terms Related to Process

3.5.1 batch / lot

Defined quantity of primary packaging material manufactured in one process or series of processes intended to have uniform characteristics with consistent, homogeneous quality.

Note 1: A batch can be divided into sub-batches that are later combined to form a single consistent batch.

Note 2: In continuous production, the batch is a fraction of production defined as a fixed quantity or fixed time interval.

3.5.2 contamination

Introduction of any unwanted material into the primary packaging material.

Note 1: A finished product can be contaminated by physical (particulate), chemical or biological (bioburden and endotoxin) action.

3.5.3 cross-contamination

Contamination of a material or of a product with another material or product.

Note 1: Cross-contamination can also be referred to as mix-up or admixture.

3.5.4 line clearance

Removal (line purge) of everything associated with the prior production run.

Note: Line clearance is done prior to a production run to prevent any error and cross-contamination. The production facility (line) and its associated working area must be completely clear of all materials, waste, products, samples, documents from the previous run.

3.5.5 manufacturing

All operations including purchasing and receipt of materials to production, packaging, labelling, quality control, release, storage, distribution of products and the related controls.

3.5.6 process aids

Material used to facilitate process realization. The material is not included in the product specification and can be removed at or before the final processing stage.

Example: Mould release agents, compressed air, rolling lubricants.

3.5.7 production

Processes resulting in primary packaging material, from receipt of starting materials through processing and packaging, to completion as a finished product.

3.5.8 quarantine

Status of materials or products isolated pending a decision on their subsequent approval or rejection.

Note: Quarantined material is typically isolated by physical or other effective means.

3.5.9 realization

Result of applying all processes required to achieve the desired output from design to product delivery.

3.5.10 reconditioning

Processing or reprocessing primary packaging material to meet specification requirements.

3.5.11 reprocessing

Repeating part of a production process.

Note: Continuation of part of a process after an in-process control test has shown that the part is incomplete is considered normal process, not reprocessing.

3.5.12 retained samples

Materials or finished products stored for future reference.

Note: Samples are taken in a sufficient amount and stored under recommended conditions for reference during a defined period of time.

3.5.13 starting material

Raw material, components and substances used to produce primary packaging materials.

3.5.14 surface treatment

Process to improve primary packaging material surface.

Example: Siliconization or other treatment of internal glass surfaces, coating of internal or external surfaces of glass containers or rubber parts.

3.5 Analysis - Process Terms

batch/lot - the central role of the batch

The batch is the basic unit of GMP traceability. All quality records, test results and release decisions are tied to a specific batch.

The ISO 15378 definition emphasizes two key characteristics:

  • Uniform characteristics: all products in the same batch should have consistent quality attributes
  • Homogeneous quality: no systematic quality differences are allowed within a batch

The batch definition is critical for designing the sampling plan - you must know clearly where the batch boundaries lie.

The three forms of contamination

ISO 15378 specifically lists three types of contamination, which is particularly important for packaging material plants:

  • Physical (particulate) contamination: glass fragments, metal particles, fibers
  • Chemical contamination: leachables, residual cleaning agents, plasticizers
  • Biological contamination: microorganisms (bioburden), endotoxin

All three types can compromise the safety of the medicinal product, so a packaging material plant must establish controls for each of them.

reprocessing vs reconditioning - an important distinction

Reprocessing: repeating part of the production steps. For example, if the first siliconization of rubber stoppers does not meet the standard, they are siliconized again. A documented approval procedure is required.

Reconditioning: processing or reprocessing a primary packaging material to bring it to specification. The concept is broader and includes treatment carried out externally.

Both require evaluation and approval by the quality unit and must be recorded in the batch record.

surface treatment - its effect on compatibility

Surface treatment is a concept specific to primary packaging materials. Take siliconization as an example:

  • Purpose: reduce friction between the rubber stopper and the syringe barrel to ensure smooth sliding
  • Risk: silicone oil may migrate into the medicinal product (leaching), affecting the stability of protein drugs (biologics)
  • GMP requirement: the degree of siliconization must be within the specification range and supported by validation data

When conducting extractables & leachables (E&L) studies, pharmaceutical companies must understand the surface treatment of the packaging materials used.

quarantine - practical management of the quarantine status

Quarantine status is a key concept in GMP material management. From receipt until release, materials must be held in quarantine to prevent unreleased materials from being used by mistake.

ISO 15378 states that quarantine can be "physical segregation" (a separate area) or "other effective means" (such as a quarantine flag in a computer system). Modern warehouse management systems (WMS) usually implement quarantine electronically, but this requires appropriate system validation (IT system validation).

3.6 Terms Related to Results

3.6.1 finished product

Primary packaging material which has completed all stages of production.

3.6.2 intermediate product

Primary packaging material which has completed some but not all production stages.

Note: An intermediate product needs further processing before it becomes a finished product.

3.6.3 medicinal product

Substance or combination of substances presented for treating or preventing disease in human beings or animals.

Note 3: Medicinal products can also be referred to as pharmaceutical or drug products, including clinical trial products.

3.6.4 primary packaging material

Material used in pharmaceutical packaging which will contain, seal or be used for dose application of a medicinal product and which will have direct contact with the medicinal product.

Secondary packaging examples (NOT primary): Printed or unprinted cartons, labels, leaflets or inserts (or outserts), over-wraps, and transit containers such as folding boxes.

3.6.5 secondary packaging materials

Non-contact packaging materials.

Example: Printed or unprinted cartons, labels, leaflets, inserts, over-wraps, transit containers (folding boxes).

3.6 Analysis - Result Terms

primary packaging material - the core concept of the standard

This is the most important definition in the whole standard. Direct contact with the medicinal product is the defining characteristic of a primary packaging material.

Typical primary packaging materials:

  • Glass containers: vials, ampoules, cartridges
  • Rubber products: stoppers, plungers, O-rings
  • Metal products: aluminum seals/crimp caps
  • Plastic containers: HDPE/PP medicine bottles, PET containers, IV bags
  • Blister materials: PVC/PVDC base film, aluminum lidding foil
  • Syringe components: barrel, plunger stopper, needle shield

Secondary packaging materials are outside the scope of ISO 15378

Packaging materials that do not directly contact the medicinal product, such as cartons, labels and leaflets, are outside the jurisdiction of ISO 15378. Suppliers of these materials only need to comply with ISO 9001 or other applicable standards.

Note, however: a label printed with medicinal product information is secondary packaging, but because it carries information critical to product safety, it is still subject to the relevant provisions of EU GMP Annex C (Annex 15378 Annex C) and EU GMP Part II.

intermediate product - in-process quality control

Take rubber stopper manufacturing as an example:

  • Compounded rubber → starting material
  • Molded, not yet siliconized stoppers → intermediate product
  • Stoppers after siliconization, washing and packing → finished product

Each stage requires in-process controls and appropriate quarantine management.

3.7.1 approved

Confirmed conformity status for any stage of the process [starting materials, process aids, packaging material or finished product].

Documents and records that provide a history of the batch, including information relating to its production and control, and which facilitate its traceability.

Unique identifier to identify a batch or lot. Can be a combination of numbers, letters and/or symbols; from this the production and distribution history can be determined.

Date on which one of the first stages in the process of manufacture of the primary packaging material, or the packaging, or the final release, occurs. Can be subject to customer agreement.

Departure from an approved standard operating procedure (SOP) or established standard.

3.7.6 documented procedure

Procedure that is established, documented, authorized, implemented and maintained.

Note: Documentation can be in any form or type of medium. See SOP (3.7.10).

Documented verification of an activity, result or record by a second person or system.

Note: Typically, double-checks are signed by a second person. Includes in-process control check signatures and quality records signed by a second person.

3.7.8 out of specification (OOS)

Test results that do not comply with the specification.

3.7.9 rejected

Status of starting materials, quality (process aids), intermediate products or finished products whose test results do not comply with one or more specification requirements, and which have been deemed — usually by the quality unit — as not suitable for use.

Authorized documented procedure, or set of procedures, work instructions and test instructions.

3.7.11 user requirement specification (URS)

Approved document that states the product specifications of the material produced on this equipment as well as functional, operational and/or technical aspects of the equipment or process required to produce the desired product.

3.7.12 validation

Confirmation, through the provision of objective evidence, that the requirements for a specific intended use or application have been fulfilled.

Note 4 (ISO 15378 specific): Validation can be applied to processes, products and software.

3.7.13 verification

Confirmation, through the provision of objective evidence, that specified requirements have been fulfilled.

Note 4 (ISO 15378 specific): In this document the term verification is used to ensure manufacturing systems are properly installed and operating correctly; alternatively this could be done by IQ and OQ.

[Modified from ISO 9000:2015, 3.8.12]

3.7 Analysis - Document Terms

batch record - a core GMP document

The batch record is the foundation of GMP traceability. ISO 15378 defines the batch record as one that must:

  • Provide the complete history of the batch (production + control)
  • Facilitate traceability

A complete batch record should include: raw material information, equipment numbers, operating steps, IPC results, deviation records and final release sign-off.

When evaluating a packaging material supplier, the CDMO should ask to review sample batch records to confirm completeness and legibility.

deviation - regulatory expectations for deviation management

A deviation is defined as a departure from an approved SOP or an established standard. This means that even if the final product meets specification, the deviation itself must be investigated and recorded.

Steps in GMP deviation management:

  1. Deviation detected → record immediately
  2. Quarantine the affected batches (if needed)
  3. Root cause analysis (RCA)
  4. Assess the impact on product quality
  5. Establish CAPA
  6. Release only after approval by the quality unit

OOS - handling out-of-specification results

The OOS definition is clear: a test result that does not meet the specification. This triggers a formal OOS investigation procedure, including:

  • Phase 1: laboratory investigation (rule out analytical error)
  • Phase 2: manufacturing investigation (check for process problems)
  • Final decision: retest (if there is a justified reason) or reject/scrap

The ISO 15378 OOS management framework corresponds directly to the FDA OOS guidance (21 CFR context) and the requirements of EU GMP Chapter 6.

URS - the starting document for equipment procurement

The URS (user requirement specification) is the first step of the qualification lifecycle. It defines:

  • What products the equipment needs to produce
  • Functional requirements (capacity, speed)
  • Operational requirements (environmental conditions, cleaning method)
  • Technical requirements (materials of construction, electrical specifications)

The URS must be approved before purchase and serves as the baseline document for the subsequent IQ/OQ/PQ.

validation vs verification - the most common confusion

ISO 15378 adds in 3.7.13 that verification can also be carried out through IQ and OQ, illustrating how these two concepts are linked in the GMP equipment qualification framework.

3.8 Terms Related to Action

3.8.1 batch release

Decision to release the batch for sale or supply, following a formal review of the batch document performed by the quality unit or a person authorized by the quality unit.

3.8.2 rejection

Process whereby starting materials, process aids, intermediate products or finished products which have been deemed — usually by the quality unit — as not suitable for use.

3.8.3 return

Process for sending back primary packaging material(s) to the organization.

3.8.4 rework

Action on a nonconforming product or service to make it conform to the requirements.

Note 1: Rework can affect or change parts of the nonconforming product.

Note 2 (ISO 15378 specific): Sorting can be considered to be rework.

[Modified from ISO 9000:2015, 3.12.8]

3.8 Analysis - Action Terms

batch release - the decision framework

Batch release is the most important decision point in the quality system. ISO 15378 clearly requires that:

  • A formal review must be performed (not an informal verbal confirmation)
  • The batch record must be reviewed (not just the test results)
  • It must be performed by the quality unit or its authorized personnel (to ensure independence)

From the CDMO's perspective as a pharmaceutical company: behind the CoA (certificate of analysis) you receive with packaging materials is the supplier's quality unit having reviewed and signed off all the batch records. During an audit, review the release SOP and the list of authorized persons.

rework - special provisions

ISO 15378 adds that sorting can be regarded as rework. This is common in primary packaging material plants:

For example: bubble defects are found in part of a batch of glass vials, requiring vial-by-vial visual inspection and removal of defective units. Under the ISO 15378 definition, this sorting process is rework and must have an approved SOP and records.

Any rework requires the quality unit to assess the impact on product quality and decide whether retesting is needed.

3.9 Terms Related to Characteristic

3.9.1 homogeneity

Uniformity of characteristics and their values throughout a defined quantity of material.

Note: Homogeneity can include uniformity of materials or certain characteristics of materials of special significance.

3.9.2 sterile

Free from viable microorganisms.

3.9 Analysis

homogeneity - the link to the batch definition

Homogeneity is the core characteristic of the batch definition. Homogeneity of the rubber formulation ensures consistent mechanical properties (hardness, elasticity, penetration force) across all stoppers in a batch; homogeneity of the glass composition ensures a consistent coefficient of thermal expansion and prevents thermal stress breakage.

sterile - sterility requirements for primary packaging materials

Not all primary packaging materials need to be sterile, but materials used for sterile products usually do. ISO 15378 adopts the ISO/TS 11139 definition: sterile = free from viable microorganisms.

Rubber stoppers and glass vials are usually terminally sterilized by the pharmaceutical company before use (steam, dry heat, EO, etc.), or supplied in a form already sterilized by irradiation. Either way, the supplier must provide bioburden data that meets specification.

3.10 Terms Related to Determination

3.10.1 automated inspection

Conformity evaluation performed by inspection equipment without manual intervention.

Note: Equipment can include optoelectronics (cameras), laser systems, ultrasonics and their associated data processing functions.

3.10.2 final inspection

Tests carried out on the finished product to determine compliance with the specification.

3.10.3 in-process control

Actions taken during the production process to test product conformity to its specification.

Note 1: Monitoring processes and adjusting means of production can be necessary to meet product requirements.

Note 2: The control of the environment or equipment can also be regarded as part of in-process control.

3.10.4 reconciliation

Comparison between the amount of finished product theoretically and actually produced or used, making allowance for normal variation.

Note: The comparison considers waste, samples or other losses inherent in the process.

3.10 Analysis

automated inspection - the core of modern quality control

Automated inspection is now standard in primary packaging material plants. ISO 15378 defines this term specifically and gives concrete technology examples (optical, laser, ultrasonic) to illustrate the variety of automated inspection systems.

For CDMO QA, when auditing an automated inspection system, confirm that:

  • The equipment has been qualified (IQ/OQ/PQ)
  • The rejection logic and threshold settings have a documented basis
  • The false reject and false accept rates are evaluated regularly
  • System calibration records are complete

in-process control (IPC) - the quality checkpoints within the process

IPC is quality confirmation performed during production (rather than on the final product). ISO 15378 adds that environmental controls and equipment controls are also part of IPC.

Typical IPC items in glass vial manufacturing:

  • Dimensional checks (volume, wall thickness, neck dimensions)
  • Visual or automated inspection (bubbles, stones, cracks)
  • Mold weight monitoring
  • Cleanroom temperature, humidity and differential pressure records (environmental IPC)

reconciliation - the importance of material balance

Reconciliation is an important GMP tool for preventing mix-ups and calculation errors. The formula: theoretical yield = actual yield + waste + samples + other accountable losses.

If the reconciliation falls outside the acceptable range, you must investigate whether product has been lost or batches mixed. This is also one of the controls that prevent pharmaceutical packaging materials from flowing into unintended channels.

3.11 Analysis - Risk Management Terms (a Distinctive Feature of ISO 15378)

quality critical - one of the most important terms

"Quality critical" is a term unique to ISO 15378 and is uncommon in other ISO standards. The concept corresponds directly to the pharmaceutical industry's Critical Quality Attributes (CQA) and Critical Process Parameters (CPP).

Criterion: if a parameter fails to meet its requirement and may have a significant adverse effect on patient safety, it is quality critical.

Examples of quality critical parameters for packaging materials:

  • Coring tendency of rubber stoppers → directly affects injection safety
  • Container closure integrity of glass vials → relates to the sterile barrier
  • Crimp force of aluminum caps → determines the sealing of the closure
  • Seal strength of blister aluminum foil → prevents exposure of the medicinal product to moisture

Correspondence with ICH Q9

The risk management terms in ISO 15378 are highly consistent with ICH Q9 (Quality Risk Management), because both refer to ISO Guide 73. This means pharmaceutical professionals already familiar with ICH Q9 need almost no relearning of terminology to understand the risk management requirements of ISO 15378.

The risk tools commonly used by CDMOs (FMEA, fishbone, FTA) are all acceptable methods within the ISO 15378 risk management framework.

Supplier audit focus: assessing risk management maturity

  1. Ask to review the most recent formal risk assessment report for the process (especially the identification of quality critical parameters)
  2. Confirm that the change control process includes a systematic risk assessment step
  3. Check whether risk assessments were performed for new raw materials or new equipment
  4. Assess whether the supplier can clearly explain which process steps are quality critical and what the control strategy is

Clause 4 — Context of the Organization

4.1–4.4 Standard Requirements

4.1 Understanding the Organization and Its Context

The organization shall determine external and internal issues that are relevant to its purpose and strategic direction and that affect its ability to achieve the intended results of its quality management system. The organization shall monitor and review information about these external and internal issues.

GMP Addition: The organization's overall policy, intentions and approach to risk management, validation and change control shall be documented in order to fulfil the GMP requirements.

4.2 Understanding the Needs and Expectations of Interested Parties

The organization shall determine: (a) the interested parties that are relevant to the quality management system; (b) the requirements of these interested parties that are relevant to the quality management system. The organization shall monitor and review information about these interested parties and their relevant requirements.

4.3 Determining the Scope of the QMS

The organization shall determine the boundaries and applicability of the quality management system to establish its scope. When determining this scope, the organization shall consider: (a) external and internal issues (4.1); (b) requirements of relevant interested parties (4.2); (c) the products and services of the organization.

The scope shall state the types of products and services covered, and provide justification for any requirement determined not applicable.

4.4 Quality Management System and Its Processes

4.4.1 — The organization shall establish, implement, maintain and continually improve a QMS, including the processes needed and their interactions. The organization shall:

  • Determine inputs/outputs and sequence of processes
  • Determine and apply criteria, monitoring, measurement and performance indicators
  • Ensure resources and assign responsibilities for each process
  • Address risks and opportunities (Clause 6.1)
  • Describe how the quality policy and objectives are implemented, cascaded and monitored to provide assurance of product quality and meet agreed customer specifications
  • Document the structure of the quality management system (e.g. quality manual, master SOP)

4.4.2 — The organization shall maintain documented information to support operations and retain documented information to provide confidence that processes are being carried out as planned.

Commentary

Clause 4.1 — Context Analysis in Practice: PESTLE for a Packaging Supplier

ISO 15378 requires a supplier to analyze its external and internal environment systematically. For a primary packaging manufacturer, key external issues include:

  • Regulatory trends: upgrades to EU MDR/IVDR, new NMPA rules in China, revisions to FDA 21 CFR Part 211
  • Raw material availability: supply chain concentration risk for borosilicate glass and halobutyl rubber
  • Market concentration: the commercial vulnerability of a customer base that depends too heavily on a single pharmaceutical company
  • Competitive environment: the impact of alternative packaging (COP/COC syringes) on traditional glass

Strengthened GMP requirement (specific to this standard): the overall policy for risk management, validation and change control must be documented. This goes beyond what ISO 9001 originally requires.

Clause 4.2 — Identifying Interested Parties

A three-tier structure of interested parties for a primary packaging supplier:

  • Direct customers (pharmaceutical companies/CDMOs): set specifications, conduct audits, require CoA/CoC
  • Regulators (FDA/EMA/NMPA): set the regulatory floor and indirectly shape supplier requirements
  • End patients: the ultimate beneficiaries; any packaging defect can affect drug safety

Key insight: although the patient is not a party to the contract, the patient is the core subject that the whole of ISO 15378 protects.

Clause 4.4 — QMS Scope: The Core Difference Between ISO 15378 and ISO 9001

This is the most critical point of difference in ISO 15378: the QMS scope must explicitly cover GMP requirements.

The QMS of a packaging supplier certified only to ISO 9001 may not cover validation, batch records, the ALCOA documentation principles or cleanroom classification. ISO 15378 explicitly requires all of these to be within the QMS scope.

CDMO Audit Perspective

When auditing a stopper or glass vial supplier, a concrete way to ask about compliance with Clause 4.4:

"Please show me your quality manual or master SOP. Does it explicitly list GMP requirements as part of the QMS scope?"

If the supplier's QMS documents mention only quality objectives and customer satisfaction, and say nothing about GMP, validation or batch release, this is a major gap against Clause 4.4.

Clause 5 — Leadership

5.1–5.3 Standard Requirements

5.1.1 General — Leadership and Commitment

Top management shall demonstrate leadership and commitment with respect to the quality management system by:

  • Taking accountability for the effectiveness of the QMS
  • Ensuring quality policy and objectives are established and compatible with context and strategic direction
  • Ensuring integration of QMS requirements into business processes
  • Promoting the use of the process approach and risk-based thinking
  • Ensuring resources needed for the QMS are available
  • Communicating the importance of effective quality management
  • Ensuring the QMS achieves its intended results
  • Promoting improvement

5.1.2 Customer Focus

Top management shall ensure that customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements are determined, understood and consistently met; and that risks affecting conformity of products are addressed.

NOTE (GMP): Key customer requirements include suitable facilities, competent and trained personnel, processes designed to ensure product security and avoidance of cross-contamination, and the ability to consistently produce product conforming to customer specifications.

5.1.3 Customer Audits

Top management shall approve access by mutual agreement for existing/prospective customers or their nominated representatives to conduct audits to review and assess the organization's QMS.

5.2 Policy

5.2.1 Establishing the quality policy: Top management shall establish, implement and maintain a quality policy that is appropriate to the purpose and context of the organization, provides a framework for quality objectives, includes a commitment to satisfy applicable requirements, and includes a commitment to continual improvement.

5.2.2 Communicating the quality policy: The quality policy shall be available as documented information, communicated, understood and applied within the organization, and available to relevant interested parties.

5.3 Organizational Roles, Responsibilities and Authorities

Top management shall ensure that responsibilities and authorities for relevant roles are assigned, communicated and understood. Top management shall assign responsibility and authority for: (a) ensuring QMS conforms to requirements; (b) ensuring processes deliver intended outputs; (c) reporting QMS performance to top management; (d) ensuring customer focus throughout the organization; (e) maintaining QMS integrity when changes are planned.

GMP Addition: The organization shall maintain a current record of signatures of responsible persons. Signature/user identification lists of all personnel checking or double-checking process steps and in-process controls are recommended.

GMP Addition: The quality unit(s) with responsibility for quality critical decisions shall have the authority to make those decisions independently.

Commentary

Clause 5.1 — Leadership Commitment: The Spirit of GMP Beyond Formality

Leadership commitment under ISO 15378 is not just signing the quality policy. The standard implies that when production targets conflict with product quality, product safety must take priority over output targets.

Measurable indicators of management commitment:

  • Does management attend quality review meetings in person?
  • Is the quality department adequately staffed?
  • Do deviation investigations have time limits that management tracks?
  • Is the CAPA completion rate part of management KPIs?

What the Data Show: Management Commitment and Quality Outcomes

Industry studies show that when supplier management is highly visible (regular gemba walks, quality issues reported to the board), the annual deviation rate is on average 30-40% lower, and the complaint rate 50% lower, than at suppliers with passive management.

This indicates that Clause 5.1 is not merely a documentation requirement but a leading indicator of a supplier's long-term performance.

Clause 5.1.3 — Customer Audits: Proactive Openness vs. Passive Acceptance

The standard requires top management to approve access for customer audits. This means the supplier's senior leaders must endorse an audit culture, rather than leaving the quality department to deal with audits alone.

From the CDMO's point of view: an audit request that is delayed or declined is often a major risk signal. Good suppliers typically have a standard audit hosting procedure, and top management attends the opening meeting.

Clause 5.3 — Independent Decision-Making Authority of the Quality Unit

This is the clause in ISO 15378 that comes closest to the Qualified Person (QP) concept at pharmaceutical companies.

The standard clearly specifies that the quality department has independent decision-making authority over quality-critical decisions, free from pressure from production or commercial departments. In practice this means:

  • The quality department can reject a batch even when production demands shipment
  • The head of quality should not report to the production director
  • The conclusions of deviation investigations should not be changed by commercial pressure

Audit question: "Please describe a case in which the quality department vetoed a shipment decision."

Signature Records — Signature List

The standard requires maintaining a current record of the signatures of responsible persons, and recommends establishing a signature/user identification list for all personnel who perform checks or double checks.

This links directly to the ALCOA principle of GMP (Attributable): every record must be traceable to a named individual.

Clause 6 — Planning

6.1–6.3 Standard Requirements

6.1 Actions to Address Risks and Opportunities

6.1.1 — When planning for the QMS, the organization shall consider the issues (4.1) and requirements (4.2) and determine the risks and opportunities that need to be addressed to: (a) give assurance that the QMS can achieve its intended results; (b) enhance desirable effects; (c) prevent or reduce undesired effects; (d) achieve improvement.

6.1.2 — The organization shall plan actions to address risks and opportunities and evaluate their effectiveness. Actions shall be proportionate to the potential impact on conformity of products and services.

6.1.3 — GMP Addition: The organization shall ensure that risk management is included in all processes, associated with the design/development, manufacturing and delivery of primary packaging materials. Records shall be maintained.

Risk management applies to, for example:

  • Change control & cleaning
  • Complaints & contamination
  • Design control (new products/processes)
  • Health and hygiene
  • Labelling & maintenance
  • Manufacturing planning & materials management
  • Nonconformities and quality defects
  • Pest control & purchasing/supply chain
  • Rework & traceability
  • Validation, verification and qualification

NOTE: Risk management principles can be found in ISO 31000, ISO 14971, ICH Q9, and GAMP5. Methods for hazard identification and assessment: IEC 31010.

6.2 Quality Objectives and Planning to Achieve Them

6.2.1 — The organization shall establish quality objectives at relevant functions, levels and processes. Quality objectives shall:

  • Be consistent with the quality policy
  • Be measurable
  • Take into account applicable requirements
  • Be relevant to conformity of products/services and to customer satisfaction enhancement
  • Be monitored, communicated and updated as appropriate

6.2.2 — When planning how to achieve quality objectives, the organization shall determine: what will be done; what resources are required; who is responsible; when it will be completed; how results will be evaluated.

6.3 Planning of Changes

When the organization determines the need for changes to the QMS, changes shall be carried out in a planned manner. The organization shall consider:

  • The purpose of the changes and their potential consequences
  • The integrity of the quality management system
  • The availability of resources
  • The allocation or reallocation of responsibilities and authorities

For change control, see Clause 8.5.6.2.

Commentary

Clause 6.1.3 — End-to-End Risk Management in Packaging Manufacture

The strengthened GMP requirements of ISO 15378 explicitly bring the thinking of ISO 14971 (risk management for medical devices) into the packaging field. For a primary packaging manufacturer, this means risk management cannot be just one FMEA but must run through all processes.

Practical mapping (using a glass vial for injection as the example):

  • Design control: FMEA of the effect of a new glass formulation on pH or delamination risk
  • Manufacturing planning: risk assessment of the effect of furnace temperature excursions on defect rate
  • Validation: risk-based validation strategy for the washer / depyrogenation process
  • Traceability: a lot numbering system that ensures a defective batch can be fully traced back

Practical Considerations for Risk Options

The risk treatment options listed in NOTE 1 of the standard include: avoiding the risk, taking the risk (to pursue an opportunity), removing the risk source, changing the likelihood or consequences, sharing the risk, or retaining the risk.

Implication for CDMO purchasing decisions: if a supplier adopts a "retain the risk (informed decision)" strategy for a high-severity risk, require written risk assessment rationale and control measures. A verbal explanation alone is not enough.

Clause 6.3 — Change Control: QMS Level vs. Process Level

Clause 6.3 deals with changes at the QMS system level (such as organizational restructuring or the introduction of a new process), whereas Clause 8.5.6.2 deals with change control at the process/product level.

Both require changes to be carried out in a "planned manner"; ad hoc or informal systemic changes are not allowed. This is consistent in spirit with the change control procedures of pharmaceutical CDMOs (including impact assessment, customer notification and assessment of validation triggers).

Risk Flag During Audits

If a supplier's quality objectives consist only of business metrics (on-time delivery rate, capacity utilization) with no product safety or GMP compliance metrics, this is a gap against Clause 6.2 and also points to a quality culture problem.

Clause 6.2 — SMART Quality Objectives: The GMP Add-On

ISO 9001 requires quality objectives to be measurable and communicated. ISO 15378 goes further in spirit: quality objectives must include product safety and compliance metrics, not just commercial KPIs.

Clause 7 — Support (Part 1): Resources, Infrastructure & Environment

7.1 Resources — General to Environment

7.1.1–7.1.2 General Resources & People

The organization shall determine and provide the resources needed for the establishment, implementation, maintenance and continual improvement of the QMS. The organization shall consider: (a) capabilities and constraints on existing internal resources; (b) what needs to be obtained from external providers.

The organization shall determine and provide the persons necessary for effective implementation of its QMS and for the operation and control of its processes.

7.1.3 Infrastructure

7.1.3.1 General: The organization shall determine, provide and maintain the infrastructure necessary for the operation of its processes and to achieve conformity of products and services.

7.1.3.2 GMP-related requirements on infrastructure:

  • Infrastructure shall be managed, operated and maintained to avoid product contamination
  • Facilities shall be protected against entry of unauthorized personnel
  • Personnel entering defined production/storage/QC areas shall only access wearing appropriate clothing
  • Layout, design and operation shall minimize the risk of errors and permit effective cleaning and maintenance to avoid cross-contamination, based on a risk assessment
  • Changing facilities, toilets and hand-washing facilities shall be provided; where product quality is affected, these shall be separated from manufacturing areas and not ventilated directly to them

Storage areas shall be of adequate capacity to allow orderly storage of starting materials and products, and appropriate with regard to material and product quality.

7.1.4 Environment for the Operation of Processes

7.1.4.2 Work environment:

  • Documented requirements for health, cleanliness, clothing and access control shall be established
  • If work environment conditions can adversely affect primary packaging material quality, appropriate conditions shall be defined and effectively monitored and controlled
  • Special conditions shall be established for control of contaminated or potentially contaminated material
  • Where primary packaging materials are exposed, covers shall be used unless justified by documented risk assessment

7.1.4.3 Classification of clean zones/cleanrooms: Clean zones/cleanrooms shall be classified and monitored/operated. See ISO 14644-1, -2, -3, -5. For biocontamination monitoring: ISO 14698-1 and -2.

7.1.4.4 Risk control of contamination: The organization shall determine and control risks that can result in contamination, including: personal hygiene and health; personal clothing, jewellery and make-up; smoking, eating, chewing, drinking and personal medication; handling and disposal of waste; microbiological contamination; protective clothing appropriate to the process area classification.

7.1.4.5 Pest control: An effective, documented pest control programme shall be implemented and maintained.

7.1.4.6 Materials and utilities: All utilities (air, gases, steam, water) shall be assessed for their potential impact on primary packaging material quality. If water comes into direct contact with the primary packaging material or is used for cleaning contact equipment, its quality shall be determined and controlled. Processing aids shall be subject to a documented risk assessment.

7.1.4.7 Maintenance and cleaning: Documented requirements for maintenance activities shall be established. Maintenance operations shall not introduce contamination and, on completion, shall include a documented cleanliness check. A cleaning schedule that takes into account contamination risk shall be defined and documented. Defective quality critical equipment shall be removed from service and/or clearly labelled; prior to reintroduction it shall be verified as suitable for use.

Commentary

Clause 7.1.3.2 — GMP Premises Design Principles

The standard requires the premises layout to be based on risk assessment, so as to minimize the risk of errors and allow effective cleaning. This is a core design principle of pharmaceutical GMP.

Specific implications for a primary packaging manufacturer:

  • Production lines for different materials (rubber vs. glass) need physical separation to prevent cross-contamination
  • The design of personnel and material flows must prevent mix-ups between contaminated and acceptable product
  • The location and ventilation design of changing rooms and toilets must not open directly onto the production area

Clause 7.1.4.3 — Cleanroom Classification and ISO 14644

ISO 15378 explicitly cites the ISO 14644 series as the technical basis for cleanroom management, creating an integrated structure of standards referencing standards:

  • ISO 14644-1: cleanroom classification (ISO 5-9)
  • ISO 14644-2: monitoring specifications
  • ISO 14644-3: test methods
  • ISO 14644-5: operations
  • ISO 14698-1/2: biocontamination monitoring

For manufacturers of sterile packaging components (such as sterile prefilled syringe plants), the cleanroom classification is usually ISO 5 or ISO 7, and regular airborne particle counting and microbiological monitoring are required.

Water Quality Management: From WFI to Process Water

Clause 7.1.4.6.3 requires that where water comes into direct contact with primary packaging materials or is used to clean product-contact equipment, the water quality must be determined and controlled.

Practical scenario (glass vial washer): if the final rinse water is water for injection (WFI), regular microbial, endotoxin and conductivity testing is needed, with records retained. If purified water is used instead as a downgrade, a documented risk assessment must support it.

Post-Maintenance Cleanliness Verification: A Frequently Overlooked Requirement

Clause 7.1.4.7.3 explicitly requires that a documented cleanliness check must be performed after maintenance is completed. This is a common gap in audits.

Common problem: after the engineering department completes repairs, production resumes without notifying the quality department to confirm cleanliness, so contamination risks such as lubricants and tools left behind go uncontrolled.

Clause 7 — Support (Part 2): Measurement, Competence & Documented Information

7.1.5–7.5 Standard Requirements

7.1.5 Monitoring and Measuring Resources

7.1.5.1 General: The organization shall determine and provide resources to ensure valid and reliable results when monitoring or measuring is used to verify conformity of products.

7.1.5.2 Measurement traceability: When measurement traceability is required, measuring equipment shall be:

  • Calibrated or verified at specified intervals or prior to use, against measurement standards traceable to international or national measurement standards
  • Identified in order to determine their status
  • Safeguarded from adjustments, damage or deterioration that would invalidate calibration status

The organization shall determine the knowledge necessary for the operation of its processes and to achieve conformity of products and services. This knowledge shall be maintained and made available. 7.2.1 General: The organization shall: (a) determine the necessary competence of persons doing work that affects QMS performance; (b) ensure persons are competent based on appropriate education, training, or experience; (c) take actions to acquire necessary competence and evaluate effectiveness; (d) retain appropriate documented information as evidence of competence.

7.2.2 GMP Training: Additional training shall be conducted regularly and shall include:

  • Awareness of applicable GMP and all procedures/policies affecting product quality

  • The impact of deviations from specified procedures, processes or specifications on customer's product quality or the end user

Particular attention shall be given to training of personnel involved with manufacture of sterile components or components to be subsequently sterilized. Specific training on microbiological and particulate contamination and the patient risk shall be provided.

Additional refresher training shall be carried out at defined intervals. Temporary personnel shall be trained or supervised by a trained person. Contractors and visitors shall receive appropriate instructions prior to entering manufacturing/production facilities.

7.3 Awareness

The organization shall ensure that persons doing work are aware of: (a) the quality policy; (b) relevant quality objectives; (c) their contribution to QMS effectiveness; (d) the implications of not conforming with QMS requirements.

7.4 Communication

The organization shall determine internal and external communications relevant to the QMS. GMP Addition: GMP and regulatory requirements shall be communicated, as appropriate, to each level of the organization. Top management shall be notified of quality critical situations in a timely manner.

7.5 Documented Information

7.5.1–7.5.2: The QMS shall include documented information required by this Standard and as determined necessary by the organization. When creating and updating documented information, the organization shall ensure appropriate identification, description, format, review and approval. Changes to documents shall be reviewed and approved by the original or another designated function. If electronic signatures are used, they shall be controlled to provide equivalent security to a hand-written signature.

7.5.3 Control of documented information:

  • 7.5.3.1: Documented information shall be available and suitable for use, and adequately protected
  • 7.5.3.2: The organization shall address distribution, access, retrieval and use; storage and preservation including legibility; version control; retention and disposition
  • 7.5.3.3: The organization shall define the period for retaining at least one copy of obsolete controlled documents; obsolete documents shall be stored and marked to prevent unintended use
  • 7.5.3.4: Records shall be controlled to provide evidence of conformity. Records shall remain legible, readily identifiable and retrievable
  • 7.5.3.5: Electronic records shall be subject to the same controls as other records
  • 7.5.3.6 (GMP): Entries in records shall be clear, indelible, made directly after performing the activity, dated, and initialled or signed. Corrections shall be dated, initialled or signed and explained, leaving the original entry still legible
  • 7.5.3.7: The organization shall define quality critical processes/parameters where a double-check is necessary for batch release
  • 7.5.3.8: For each batch, a record shall be established providing traceability and identifying quantity manufactured and quantity approved for distribution
  • 7.5.3.11: All manufacturing, control, testing, distribution and investigation records shall be retained for at least five years after the date of manufacture, or as agreed with the customer

7.5.4 Administration of IT systems and data: There shall be documented procedures for: responsibility assignment for IT and data security; network/file access control; file integrity on shared systems; password management and sleep mode; back-up and recovery of electronic product-related data (frequency, method, media, physical process for safe storage). The organization shall have a documented IT recovery plan. Access to legacy systems shall be defined as part of change control when IT systems are changed.

Commentary

Clause 7.1.5 — Calibration of Measuring Equipment: GMP in Practice

All equipment used for quality-critical measurements must be calibrated and records kept. In packaging manufacture this covers a wide range:

  • Glass vial production: wall thickness gauges, volume gauges, burst strength testers
  • Rubber stopper production: durometers, penetration force testers, dimensional measuring instruments
  • Environmental monitoring: particle counters, incubator thermometers, differential pressure gauges
  • Analytical laboratory: HPLC, balances, pH meters

Identification of calibration status (labels) and the list of measuring equipment are basic items checked in audits.

Clause 7.2.2 — GMP Training: Patient Safety Awareness Beyond the SOP

What is special about the GMP training requirements of ISO 15378: it requires employees not only to know "how to do it" but also to understand "why", including the potential harm to patients.

These three levels of awareness map onto training design:

  • Level 1 (Knowledge): the definition, types and sources of contamination
  • Level 2 (Understanding): how contamination passes through packaging materials to the drug product and harms patients
  • Level 3 (Action): report deviations immediately rather than concealing them, because the consequence is patient safety

For personnel working on sterile components, the standard requires especially intensive training on microbial and particulate contamination. This is the requirement for the highest risk level.

GMP Control of Temporary Staff and Contractors

Clause 7.2.2 explicitly requires that temporary workers must be trained or work under the supervision of trained personnel, and that contractors and visitors must receive appropriate instruction before entering production facilities.

Practical risk: hiring large numbers of temporary workers in the peak season without an effective rapid training procedure is a common GMP compliance gap and a frequent audit observation.

Record Retention Period: Five-Year Minimum

Clause 7.5.3.11 requires all manufacturing, control, testing, distribution and investigation records to be retained for at least five years, or as agreed with the customer (which may be longer).

Practical note: if a pharmaceutical customer requires retention until two years after the drug product expiry (which may be 10-15 years), the supplier must reflect this requirement in the quality agreement and ensure that its storage systems (including IT backups) can support long-term retention.

Clause 7.5.4 — IT System Management: The Modern Challenge of Data Integrity

ISO 15378:2017 explicitly requires the establishment of IT system management procedures covering:

  • Access control for networks and files (authorized personnel only)
  • Document integrity protection on shared servers
  • Password management and screen locking (when personnel step away)
  • Electronic data backup: frequency, method, media and secure storage
  • An IT system disaster recovery plan, with regular verification

These requirements are highly consistent in spirit with the FDA data integrity guidance (2018) and the MHRA data integrity guidance (2021), reflecting regulators' growing attention to electronic GMP environments.

8.1 Operational Planning and Control

Original Text

ISO 9001:2015 base text:

The organization shall plan, implement and control the processes needed to meet the requirements for the provision of products and services, and to implement the actions determined in Clause 6, by:

  1. determining the requirements for the products and services;
  2. establishing criteria for:
    1. the processes;
    2. the acceptance of products and services;
  3. determining the resources needed to achieve conformity to the product and service requirements;
  4. implementing control of the processes in accordance with the criteria;
  5. determining, maintaining and retaining documented information to the extent necessary:
    1. to have confidence that the processes have been carried out as planned;
    2. to demonstrate the conformity of products and services to their requirements.

The output of this planning shall be suitable for the organization's operations.

The organization shall control planned changes and review the consequences of unintended changes, taking action to mitigate any adverse effects, as necessary.

The organization shall ensure that outsourced processes are controlled (see 8.4).

Additional GMP-Related Requirements

Product realization planning shall consider the requirement for consistent processing of primary packaging materials. Planning shall also take account of the need for taking and retaining samples in appropriate conditions.

The organization shall ensure that risk management processes are included in the planning and implemented throughout product realization; records shall be maintained (see 7.5.3.4).

Educational Commentary

Core Concept: The Layered Structure

The structure of ISO 15378 is quite unique: each clause first quotes the original text of ISO 9001:2015 and then adds additional GMP-related requirements.

This means a company making pharmaceutical primary packaging materials must satisfy both:

  • The ISO 9001 quality management system foundation: general manufacturing requirements
  • The ISO 15378 additional GMP clauses: special requirements added because of drug product safety

Think of it as a "driver's license plus a professional driver's license": the latter includes the former but sets stricter standards.

Five Key Actions in Operational Planning

8.1 requires the organization to do the following for each product realization process:

  • Confirm requirements: What are the product specifications and regulatory requirements?
  • Establish criteria: process acceptance criteria plus product acceptance standards
  • Ensure resources: personnel, equipment, environment
  • Implement control: operate according to the criteria
  • Retain records: demonstrate that the process was carried out as planned and that the product conforms to requirements

Analogy: How Do You Make a Batch of Glass Vials?

Imagine you receive an order for 10 mL glass vials for injection. 8.1 requires you to plan before starting work: the glass melting temperature range, forming dimensional tolerances, the annealing curve, cleaning conditions, and when to sample and how to store retention samples.

If you find partway through that the oven temperature is running high (an unplanned change), you must assess the impact on the product, take mitigating action, and keep a record.

Key Points of the Additional GMP Requirements: Sample Retention & Risk Management

Sample Retention: retention samples of primary packaging materials are not just quality records; they are the basis for investigation when a problem arises in the market. ISO 15378 requires storage under "appropriate conditions", which means considering storage temperature, humidity, light and other factors.

Risk management throughout the process: it is not enough to do a single risk assessment at the design stage. Risk management must be continuous across the entire product realization process (from incoming raw materials to finished product release), and records must be retained (7.5.3.4).

Original Text

  3. obtaining customer feedback relating to products and services, including customer complaints;
  4. handling or controlling customer property;
  5. establishing specific requirements for contingency actions, when relevant.

8.2.1.2.1 — The organization shall establish and maintain a documented feedback system to provide early warning of potential and actual quality problems and to facilitate customer input into the corrective and preventive action system.

Educational Commentary

Why Do Pharmaceutical Packaging Materials Need a Stricter Customer Communication Mechanism?

In general manufacturing, customer communication is mostly commercial (orders, quotations, complaint handling). But customers of pharmaceutical primary packaging (drug manufacturers) use these materials directly on drug products, and if packaging quality fails, the ultimate impact falls on patient safety.

ISO 15378 therefore adds three GMP requirements on top of ISO 9001: a feedback system, a change confirmation mechanism, and support for regulatory submission documents.

8.2.1.2.1 — Documented Feedback System

This is not the passive practice of simply handling complaints as they arrive. ISO 15378 requires a proactive early warning system that can:

  • Detect potential quality problems early (not only those that have already occurred)
  • Feed customer feedback into the CAPA (corrective and preventive action) system
  • Form a closed loop: problem, analysis, improvement, effectiveness check

Analogy: What Are a DMF and a CoS?

Imagine a drug manufacturer applying for marketing authorization for a new drug, which requires detailed manufacturing information on the glass vial used (material composition, test methods, process conditions).

DMF (Drug Master File): the packaging supplier submits a technical file directly to the US FDA, and the drug manufacturer cites the DMF number in its application. The supplier's confidential information is not disclosed to the drug manufacturer, and the FDA can review it directly.

CoS (Certificate of Suitability): a certificate of suitability issued under the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph.Eur.), certifying that a material complies with the Ph.Eur. monograph and can be cited in marketing applications in Europe.

These two mechanisms allow packaging suppliers to protect trade secrets while still supporting their customers' regulatory submissions.

Classification of Change Notifications

8.2.1.2.2 requires both parties to agree in advance which changes need "written confirmation before implementation" and which only require "notification". This should be written into the technical/quality agreement (Quality Agreement).

Common classification in practice:

  • Written approval required: replacing a raw material supplier, major changes to process parameters, changing equipment models
  • Notification only: minor process optimization, replacing non-critical auxiliary materials

Printed Primary Packaging — Annex C Applies

8.2.1.2.4 specifically points out that primary packaging carrying printed text (such as printed vial labels or dosage information printed directly on the packaging) must follow the GMP provisions of Annex C, including version control, prevention of use of obsolete versions, and reconciliation of printed materials. This is because a printing error can directly lead to a medication error (wrong dose, wrong drug).

Original Text

When determining the requirements for the products and services to be offered to customers, the organization shall ensure that:

  1. the requirements for the products and services are defined, including:
    1. any applicable statutory and regulatory requirements;
    2. those considered necessary by the organization;
  2. the organization can meet the claims for the products and services it offers.

Requirements related to the product, including changes requiring notification, shall be determined and documented.

Customer requirements to avoid unauthorized use of waste primary packaging material (including samples, print media, labels) shall be determined and documented.

Educational Commentary

Requirements for Safe Destruction of Obsolete Packaging Materials

This additional GMP requirement looks minor at first glance but is very important. Typical scenarios of "prohibiting unauthorized use of obsolete primary packaging materials":

  • Rejected glass vials (Rejects) enter the market and are illegally filled with drug product
  • Obsolete printed labels are recovered and used on counterfeit drugs
  • Trial samples (Samples) are resold without authorization

The organization therefore needs to agree clearly with the customer how obsolete packaging materials are handled (for example, destruction records, certified destruction by a third party) and set this out as a documented requirement.

Identifying Regulatory Requirements

"Applicable regulatory requirements" for pharmaceutical packaging may include:

  • Packaging monographs in national pharmacopoeias (USP, Ph.Eur., JP)
  • FDA 21 CFR (for example, provisions on plastic containers used for pharmaceuticals)
  • Requirements for packaging materials in the EU GMP annexes
  • Specific regulations of the customer's market

The organization shall ensure that it has the ability to meet the requirements for products and services to be offered to customers. The organization shall conduct a review before committing to supply products and services to a customer, to include:

  2. requirements not stated by the customer, but necessary for the specified or intended use, when known;
  3. requirements specified by the organization;

Educational Commentary

Feasibility Review Before Accepting an Order

The core spirit of this clause is to confirm that you can deliver before committing to supply. For a pharmaceutical packaging manufacturer, the key review questions include:

  • Can our process meet the particle specification the customer requires?
  • Does our cleanroom grade meet the requirement?
  • Have we obtained the validation needed to produce a product to this specification?
  • Are we capable of performing the test items the customer requires?

Requirements That Are "Not Stated but Necessary"

A customer says, "I want a batch of 2 mL ampoules that comply with Ph.Eur. 3.2.1" but does not mention thermal shock resistance testing. Yet when glass ampoules are used in a terminal sterilization process, thermal shock resistance is a necessary property.

Under 8.2.3(b), even if the customer does not mention it, the organization should proactively identify and confirm this requirement. This is where a packaging supplier shows its value as a "professional partner".

8.2.4 Changes to Requirements

Original Text

The organization shall ensure that relevant documented information is amended, and that relevant persons are made aware of the changed requirements, when the requirements for products and services are changed.

Educational Commentary

Practical Points on Communicating Changes

This clause looks simple, but in practice the problem often arises that "the specification was revised but the operators on the floor did not know". The organization needs an effective change communication mechanism to ensure:

  • Related documents (SOPs, specifications, quality control plans) are updated in step
  • Relevant personnel (production, QC, sales) receive the notification and understand the change
  • Training records are updated (if needed)
  • Traceable records of the change are retained

The responsibility for design and risk assessment should be agreed between the customer and the organization.

Educational Commentary

Design and Development of Pharmaceutical Packaging — Why Is a Formal Process Needed?

Developing a new PFS (prefilled syringe) or a new pharmaceutical vial is not simply an engineering design question. The following must be considered:

  • Compatibility of the packaging with the drug product (E&L testing)
  • Tolerance to the sterilization method
  • Usability in clinical use (can patients/nurses use it correctly?)
  • Feasibility and consistency of the manufacturing process
  • Documentation needed to support regulatory submissions

Without a formal D&D process, it is easy to end up with a "product that was designed but cannot be manufactured" or "compatibility problems discovered after launch".

The Core of the GMP Additional Requirement: Patient Impact Assessment

ISO 9001 design planning only requires consideration of customer requirements and resources. ISO 15378 additionally requires "potential impact on the patient" to be built into the documented procedures of the design process.

Example: developing a new rubber stopper. From an ISO 9001 perspective, you only need to make sure the dimensions meet the specification and the mechanical properties are acceptable. ISO 15378 also requires you to assess whether the stopper's extractables could migrate into the drug solution, and whether they pose a toxicity risk to patients.

This patient-first way of thinking is the core difference between GMP and general quality management.

Customer-Supplier Agreement on Design Responsibilities

The clause recommends that both parties agree in advance on the division of responsibility for design and risk assessment. In practice, this is usually written into the Quality Agreement or Technical Agreement.

A typical division: the drug manufacturer provides the functional requirements and compatibility requirements, the packaging supplier is responsible for material design and process validation, and both share responsibility for the final risk assessment document.

Educational Commentary

The Quality of Design Inputs Determines the Success of the Design

"Garbage in, garbage out" applies equally to design and development. The key requirements for design inputs are:

  • Complete: no missing items, especially safety-related requirements
  • Unambiguous: nothing vague; for example, "sufficient strength" must be turned into a specific value
  • Conflict-resolved: if a customer asks for "as thin and light as possible" but regulations require a minimum thickness, the conflict must be resolved

(e) Assessment of Consequences of Failure — The Core GMP Mindset

It requires considering "what are the consequences if it fails?" already at the design input stage. This is the starting point of FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis).

Take vial closure as an example: if the seal fails, sterility may be lost, leading to drug contamination and then patient infection. This severity assessment directly affects the design safety factor and acceptance criteria.

8.3.4 Design and Development Controls

Original Text

The organization shall apply controls to the design and development process to ensure that:

  1. the results to be achieved are defined;
  2. reviews are conducted to evaluate the ability of the results to meet requirements;
  3. verification activities are conducted to ensure that outputs meet the input requirements;
  4. validation activities are conducted to ensure that the resulting products meet the requirements for the specified application or intended use;
  5. any necessary actions are taken on problems determined during reviews or V&V activities;
  6. documented information of these activities is retained.
NOTE: Design and development reviews, verification and validation have distinct purposes. They can be conducted separately or in any combination, as is suitable for the products and services of the organization. Verification and validation in this context is limited to design and development. For verification and validation of production and service provision, see 8.5.

Educational Commentary

The Difference Between Review / Verification / Validation

These three activities are often confused, but their purposes differ:

  • Review (design review): evaluates whether the design results can meet the requirements. It can be carried out at each stage of design, usually as a cross-functional review meeting. It asks: "Is the design heading in the right direction?"
  • Verification: confirms that the outputs meet the input specifications. It is usually a check at the level of documents, calculations or tests. It asks: "Does what we made meet the design specification?"
  • Validation: confirms that the product can meet its intended use under actual application conditions. It asks: "Can this packaging material protect the drug under real conditions of use?"

Example: Developing a Pharmaceutical Rubber Stopper

Designing a rubber stopper for a lyophilized powder injection:

  • Review: a design review meeting confirms that the formulation and dimensional design meet the customer's requirement specification
  • Verification: test whether the stopper dimensions meet the tolerances on the design drawing (for example, inner diameter ±0.1 mm)
  • Validation: fit the stopper onto the actual vial and run it through a lyophilization cycle to confirm that sealing, recovery after vacuum, and compatibility with the drug product meet the intended use

Original Text

The organization shall ensure that design and development outputs:

  1. meet the input requirements;
  2. are adequate for the subsequent processes for the provision of products and services;
  3. include or reference monitoring and measuring requirements, as appropriate, and acceptance criteria;
  4. specify the characteristics of the products and services that are essential for their intended purpose and their safe and proper provision.
  5. are verified as suitable before finalizing product specifications.

NOTE 2: Design and development outputs can include records (specifications, manufacturing procedures, engineering drawings, engineering or research logbooks) and samples.

Educational Commentary

What Are Design Outputs?

Design outputs are the "deliverables" of the design process and usually include:

  • Product specification (Specification)
  • Manufacturing procedure (Manufacturing Procedure / SOP)
  • Engineering drawings (Engineering Drawings)
  • Control plan (Control Plan / Inspection Plan)
  • Acceptance criteria (Acceptance Criteria)
  • Samples for confirmation (Validation Samples)

(e) Verifying Suitability Before the Specification Is Finalized — An Added GMP Requirement

This is an additional requirement of ISO 15378 over ISO 9001. A product specification cannot be set on design calculations alone; before it is "finalized", its suitability must be confirmed through actual verification (testing or trial production).

Practical meaning: you cannot simply turn design drawings into a formal specification; it must first be supported by the results of trial sample verification.

NOTE 1 in Practice: Supplier-Customer Joint Validation

When a packaging supplier develops a new PFS barrel (Cartridge), the customer needs to run compatibility tests on its own filling line (filling, freezing, transport vibration). Such "joint validation" is industry best practice: it can reveal problems in production use early and avoid systemic nonconformities appearing only after launch.

Original Text

The organization shall retain documented information on:

  1. design and development changes;
  2. the results of reviews;
  3. the authorization of the changes;
  4. the actions taken to prevent adverse impacts.

Educational Commentary

Four Key Elements of Design Change Control

Any change made late in design or development must be handled through a formal change control process:

  1. Identify: who changed what, and when
  2. Review: assess the impact on product quality and regulatory compliance
  3. Authorize: approval by authorized personnel
  4. Act: update documents, retrain, and revalidate where necessary

Key GMP Requirement: Obligation to Notify Regulators

This is one of the most important additional GMP requirements in ISO 15378. If you have provided customers with a DMF or technical file, any change affecting that information must be notified directly to the regulatory authority.

Typical violation: a raw material supplier is quietly replaced, and the packaging supplier neither notifies the customer nor updates the DMF. The technical requirements in the drug manufacturer's marketing authorization then no longer match reality, triggering an FDA Warning Letter.

"Validation Impact Assessment" Is a Precondition for Implementing a Change

When a change may affect a validated process or equipment, you must:

  • Assess whether the existing validation documents are still valid
  • Decide whether revalidation (full or partial) is needed
  • Update all affected related documents (SOPs, specifications, validation reports)
  • Retrain the personnel concerned and keep records

This is why change control needs joint assessment across functions (design, production, QA, RA).

8.4.1 Control of Externally Provided Processes — General

Original Text

  1. products and services from external providers are intended for incorporation into the organization's own products and services;
  2. products and services are provided directly to the customer(s) by external providers on behalf of the organization;
  3. a process, or part of a process, is provided by an external provider as a result of a decision by the organization.

Educational Commentary

Three Types of External Providers

8.4 covers every form of "outside purchasing or outsourcing":

  • Service providers: sterilization services, testing laboratories, calibration services

  • Evaluation: qualification review before a new supplier is brought in (audit, sample testing)
  • Selection: decide on approval based on the evaluation results
  • Performance Monitoring: continuously track CoA pass rate, delivery time and abnormality records

For example, if the final washing of glass vials is outsourced, and washing is a critical step affecting particulate contamination, then the contractor's washing procedures, equipment, environment and records must all meet the ISO 15378 standard.

8.4.2.2.1 — Three Categories of Suppliers That Must Be Approved

This clause explicitly lists three categories of suppliers that must go through a formal approval procedure:

  • Starting Materials: raw materials that make up the final packaging material, such as glass raw materials, rubber compound ingredients and polymer pellets
  • Quality Critical Process Aids: for example, mold lubricants, cleaning agents and silicone oil (if used for syringe coating)
  • Packaging materials for cleanroom use: the packaging materials themselves that are used in the cleanroom, such as bags, trays and shipping containers

"Approval" typically includes supplier audit, sample testing, risk assessment and approval by an approval committee, followed by listing on the approved vendor list (AVL).

8.4.2.2.2 — Customer Must Be Notified Before Outsourcing

This is a requirement that many packaging suppliers easily overlook. Before outsourcing any part of any production process, the customer must be notified in advance.

Typical scenario: to increase capacity, a decision is made to subcontract one processing step to another company. The customer must be informed first, because the customer's marketing authorization may specify the manufacturing site or process description.

8.4.2.2.4 — Consultants Are Suppliers Too!

This is a frequently overlooked requirement. If you engage an external consultant to assist with production or quality control, that consultant is a "supplier" under the ISO 15378 framework and must be evaluated and managed.

In practice, the consultant's qualifications (education, professional licenses, relevant experience) should be evaluated and recorded, and the advice and reports they provide should also be brought into the quality management system.

8.4.2.2.6 — Laboratory Competence Assessment

Outsourced laboratories (such as CROs and third-party testing organizations) must pass a competence assessment. Common methods include:

  • Accreditation: ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation (the best approach)
  • Proficiency Testing: comparison against samples with known results
  • Audit: on-site review of the laboratory's SOPs, equipment and personnel qualifications
  • Method Validation: confirming that the test methods used are fit for purpose

8.4.2.2.9 — Quarantine of Incoming Materials in Practice

"Physical or administrative segregation" means:

  • Physical segregation: set up a quarantine warehouse area, apply "Quarantine - Pending Test" labels, and prevent the material from entering the production area before release
  • Administrative segregation: lock the inventory status in the ERP/warehouse system so that warehouse staff cannot pick the material unless the system releases it

The "use under exceptional circumstances" in the NOTE must have strict error-proofing mechanisms, for example a batch traceability system that ensures, if the test result turns out to be nonconforming, all intermediates made with this material can be quickly identified and dispositioned.

8.4.2.2.10 — Periodic Verification of CoA/CoC

Simply accepting the supplier's CoA is not enough; "periodic verification" is also needed. There are two acceptable approaches:

  • Approach 1 (full testing): periodically perform full testing of the supplier's material, in-house or through a third party, and compare it with the CoA
  • Approach 2 (minimum requirement): accept the CoA, but for every batch perform at least one specific identity test (Identity Test) in-house. For example, FTIR identification of a rubber compound confirms that the material is what it claims to be.

The frequency of "periodic" verification should be based on risk assessment: new suppliers or suppliers with a problem history need more frequent verification.

8.4.2.2.8 — Change of Raw Material Supplier Triggers Change Control

Changing the supplier of a quality-critical raw material must go through the full change control process, including: re-approving the new supplier, comparing old and new material specifications, assessing the impact on downstream processes and product quality, revalidating where necessary, and notifying customers and regulators (where applicable).

The background to this requirement is the many historical quality problems caused by suppliers quietly changing materials, for example a small formulation adjustment that altered the extractables profile.

Section Summary

  • Understanding the layered structure: every clause in Section 8 of ISO 15378 is built on ISO 9001:2015 and then adds GMP requirements specific to pharmaceutical packaging materials. Understanding why these additional requirements are needed (because they ultimately affect patient safety) is the core mindset for learning this standard.
  • Risk management throughout the process (8.1): starting from the planning stage, risk management must be built into the entire product realization process, and records must be retained. Sample retention must be carried out under appropriate conditions.
  • Documented feedback system (8.2.1): establish a proactive early warning mechanism rather than passively waiting for complaints. DMF and CoS are important tools that protect trade secrets while supporting regulatory submissions. Control of obsolete packaging materials is an important safeguard against drug counterfeiting.
  • Design and development must assess patient impact (8.3): Review / Verification / Validation have different purposes but complement one another. Design changes must be assessed for their impact on regulatory submission documents and, where necessary, notified directly to regulators.
  • Strengthened GMP requirements for supplier control (8.4): suppliers of quality-critical materials/services must be approved by the quality unit; the customer must be notified in advance before any production process is outsourced; consultants are also suppliers; outsourced laboratories must be assessed for competence; incoming materials must be quarantined until release; when a CoA is accepted, at least one identity test must be performed; changing a raw material supplier must go through change control.
  • Supply chain responsibility is not transferred by outsourcing: whichever step is outsourced, ultimate responsibility for quality remains with the organization. Outsourced quality-critical processes must meet all requirements of ISO 15378.
Core insight: ISO 15378 moves the supply chain control mindset of pharmaceutical GMP forward to the packaging manufacturer. Packaging suppliers can no longer just "make a good product"; they need to manage the whole supply chain like a drug manufacturer, build a complete documentation system, and proactively assess the impact on patient safety. This shift in mindset is the key to understanding and implementing ISO 15378.

Cleanliness and Contamination Control (continued): Line Clearance, Regrind, Pallet Controls

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p37)

8.5.1.3.13 — Partial Line Clearance / Automated Changeover

Partially reduced line clearances or automated changeover systems that are designed to reduce make-ready time and do not permit a total line clearance shall be subject to a documented risk assessment and operated with controls to ensure product quality.

8.5.1.3.14 — Pallet Materials

Pallets shall be constructed of materials appropriate to the product being handled, sourced and controlled to reduce the risk of contamination.

NOTE: Wooden pallets could be contaminated through migration of any chemicals used in other pallet treatments.
Commentary

What Is Line Clearance and Why Does It Matter?

Line clearance is the comprehensive check of the production line carried out after one batch ends and before the next begins, to ensure that all materials, labels and documents from the previous batch have been completely removed.

In a packaging plant this includes: confirming that all molds/tools have been removed; confirming that labels have been removed; confirming that no material from the previous batch remains; and completing the clearance record, which is verified by QC.

Analogy: Turning Over a Restaurant Table

Line clearance is like a waiter resetting a table after guests leave: the plates, cutlery and leftovers from the previous party must all be cleared before the next party is seated. If only a partial clearance is done, there must be clear rules that guarantee nothing from the previous table is left for the next one.

In pharmaceutical primary packaging, "carry-over from the previous batch" can lead to medicinal products being packed in the wrong material or to labeling mix-ups, which is a serious GMP violation.

GMP Key Point: Automatic Changeover Does Not Remove the Need for Risk Assessment

Some modern production lines use automated changeover systems that are claimed to shorten changeover time and avoid a full line clearance. However, ISO 15378 explicitly requires that such systems still undergo a documented risk assessment and that control measures be established. "Automated" does not mean "no validation needed".

The Hidden Risk of Wooden Pallets

Clause 8.5.1.3.14 specifically warns about contamination from wooden pallets: wood can absorb chemicals (for example insecticides and preservatives) that may then migrate onto the surface of packaging materials during storage or transport. Many high-specification packaging plants have switched to plastic pallets or apply strict source control and inspection to wooden pallets.

GMP

Why Pharma Packaging Manufacturers Face Higher GMP Standards

Why Primary Packaging Manufacturers Carry a Higher GMP Burden than General Packaging Plants

The Unique Position of Primary Packaging Manufacturers

What Sets Pharma Packaging Apart

ISO 15378 exists because primary packaging materials come into direct contact with the medicinal product — they are the final barrier between the drug and the patient. Unlike secondary or tertiary packaging, primary packaging can directly affect:

  • Drug purity (contamination migration)
  • Drug stability (moisture, oxygen permeation)
  • Drug identity (label mix-ups)
  • Drug sterility (particulate contamination)

The ISO 15378 Layered Structure

Throughout this standard, every clause follows a predictable architecture:

  1. ISO 9001:2015 base text — the generic quality management requirement applicable to any manufacturing organization
  2. Additional GMP-related requirements — pharmaceutical-specific overlays that go beyond ISO 9001

Reading only the ISO 9001 base text gives an incomplete picture. The GMP additions are where the pharmaceutical regulatory requirements live.

Commentary

The Logic of the Two-Layer Structure of ISO 15378

ISO 15378 deliberately uses a structure of "ISO 9001 base clauses + additional GMP requirements", for these reasons:

  • Manufacturers already certified to ISO 9001 can clearly see the "upgrade gap"
  • It lets auditors compare ISO 9001 baseline compliance with the medicinal-product-specific requirements
  • It allows the standard to be updated in step with revisions of ISO 9001 (the 2017 edition corresponds to ISO 9001:2015)

In practice, a general packaging plant only needs to comply with ISO 9001, whereas a pharmaceutical primary packaging manufacturer must meet the ISO 9001 base clauses plus all of the additional GMP requirements.

Analogy: An Ordinary Cook vs. an Operating-Theatre Caterer

A general packaging plant is like an ordinary restaurant cook, who only has to follow food hygiene regulations. A pharmaceutical primary packaging manufacturer is more like someone preparing meals for an operating theatre: in addition to food hygiene, they must follow aseptic practice, wear cleanroom garments and work in a controlled environment.

The end use of the product (a medicinal product that contacts the patient) determines the standard the manufacturing environment must reach.

8.5.1.4 Particular Requirements for Sterile Primary Packaging Materials

Special Requirements for Sterile Primary Packaging: Sterilization Validation, Records and Outsourcing Control

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p40)

8.5.1.4.1 — Sterilization Records

The organization shall maintain records (see 7.5.3) of the process parameters for the sterilization process, which was used for each sterilization batch. Sterilization records shall be traceable to each batch of primary packaging material.

8.5.1.4.2 — Validation of Sterilization Processes

If sterilization is a requirement, the organization shall establish documented procedures for the validation of sterilization processes. Sterilization processes shall be validated prior to initial use and revalidated periodically. Records of the results of sterilization process validation shall be maintained (see 7.5.3).

See ISO 11135, ISO 11137-1 or ISO 11137-2.

8.5.1.4.3 — Validated Sterilization Process & Outsourcing

Where sterilization is a requirement, the organization shall subject the primary packaging materials to a validated sterilization process and record all the control parameters of the sterilization process. If the sterilization process is outsourced, the organization shall ensure that the process complies with the appropriate requirements of this document.

See ISO 14937.
Commentary

Which Packaging Materials Need to Be Sterilized?

Primary packaging materials that require sterilization mainly include: rubber stoppers for sterile injectables; vial components for sterile powders; syringe barrels for pre-filled syringes; and certain medical-grade bags (IV bags).

Key point: these materials contact the sterile medicinal product directly after leaving the plant, so the packaging manufacturer itself must also ensure that they achieve the specified sterility assurance level (SAL).

Three Key Reference Standards for Sterilization Validation

  • ISO 11135: validation and routine control of ethylene oxide (EO) sterilization
  • ISO 11137-1/-2: validation requirements for radiation sterilization (gamma, e-beam)
  • ISO 14937: general validation requirements for other sterilization methods (for example steam, dry heat)

Each sterilization method requires its own IQ/OQ/PQ validation, with operating ranges established according to critical parameters such as sterilization dose, exposure time and temperature/humidity.

Analogy: A Hospital Autoclave Usage Log

Just as a hospital autoclave must record temperature, pressure and time for every cycle and link the record to specific batches of surgical instruments, ISO 15378 requires that the packaging manufacturer's sterilization process records be traceable "one to one" to each batch of packaging material. If a contamination problem is later found in a batch of medicinal product, investigators can trace back to the corresponding sterilization batch record.

Outsourced Sterilization: Responsibility Cannot Be Outsourced

Clause 8.5.1.4.3 specifically provides that even if the organization outsources the sterilization process to a third-party sterilization service provider, the organization remains responsible for ensuring that the contractor's procedures meet the requirements of ISO 15378. This means the packaging manufacturer must:

  • Audit the qualification and validation documents of the contract sterilizer
  • Specify compliance requirements clearly in the contract
  • Audit the contractor periodically
  • Ensure that the sterilization records for every batch are obtained

"Outsourced" does not mean "not accountable". This is a basic GMP principle.

8.5.2 Identification and Traceability

Identification and Traceability: Batch Records, Cross-Contamination Prevention and Returned Material Management

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p41)

8.5.2.1 General (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall use suitable means to identify outputs when it is necessary to ensure the conformity of products and services.

The organization shall identify the status of outputs with respect to monitoring and measurement requirements throughout production and service provision.

The organization shall control the unique identification of the outputs when traceability is a requirement, and shall retain the documented information necessary to enable traceability.

8.5.2.2 Additional GMP-related Requirements

8.5.2.2.1 — The organization shall establish and maintain a system to trace all production materials from source to product realization, defining the extent and the records required based on risk assessment (see 7.5.3, 10.2.3 and 10.3).

8.5.2.2.2 — Batch production records shall be identified with a unique batch or identification reference.

8.5.2.2.3 — Records of the use of quality critical equipment shall be retained (see 7.5.3). These records shall also include cleaning and maintenance activities in sequence with the manufacturing operations. Maintenance activities shall be documented and traceable to a particular manufacturing operation or piece of equipment.

8.5.2.2.4 — The organization shall establish and maintain documented procedures to ensure that primary packaging materials returned to the organization for e.g. reprocessing to specified requirements are identified and distinguished from normal production at all times.

8.5.2.2.5 — In order to reduce the risk of cross-contamination of materials, intermediates and finished products, they shall be segregated by suitable means based on a risk assessment e.g. physical segregation, labelling, barcoding, electronic locations.

Commentary

Traceability in Practice at a Packaging Plant

ISO 15378 requires full-chain traceability from "raw material source" to "product realization". For a packaging plant, this means:

  • Upstream traceability: the lot numbers of the raw materials (resin, glass frit, rubber compound) used in each batch of packaging material
  • Production traceability: the equipment used (including calibration status), the operators and the time stamps
  • Downstream traceability: which pharmaceutical manufacturer and which batch each batch of packaging material was shipped to

This two-way traceability system allows the affected packaging batches to be identified within hours when a medicinal product is recalled.

The Importance of a Unique Batch Reference

Clause 8.5.2.2.2 requires that each batch production record have a unique identifier. This is not merely a matter of document management convenience; it is a regulatory requirement. When a pharmaceutical customer investigates a batch of packaging material (for example, suspecting that the packaging caused a medicinal product failure), the packaging manufacturer must be able to quickly retrieve the complete batch production record, including raw material lot numbers, equipment status, operators and environmental monitoring data.

8.5.2.2.3: The "Chronological" Requirement for Equipment Use Records

This clause specifically requires that equipment use records include the "sequence of cleaning and maintenance activities", that is, recorded in the actual order in which they occurred. This matters because:

If equipment undergoes emergency repair after a production run and is then put straight into the next batch, this sequence must be clearly recorded. Could the repair have introduced contaminants? Is additional cleaning validation needed after the repair? All of this needs to be traceable.

Returned Material Management (8.5.2.2.4)

Packaging material returned by customers (for example returns for quality problems or exchange returns) must be physically or systemically fully segregated from normal production material and clearly identified. This is a key control point to prevent "returned nonconforming product from being used by mistake in normal production". Common practice: a dedicated holding area for returned material (with red "HOLD" labels) plus quarantine in the ERP system.

Analogy: A Supermarket RFID Tracking System

Modern supermarkets use barcodes/RFID to track every item from supplier to shelf to checkout; once a problem food is found, they can determine within minutes which stores and which shelves hold the affected goods. The traceability requirements of ISO 15378 for packaging materials work on the same principle, but the consequences are more serious: a packaging batch that cannot be traced could lead to a medicinal product recall or even harm to patients.

8.5.3 Customer Property: Common Scenarios in a Packaging Plant

In the pharmaceutical packaging business, "customer property" most commonly takes these forms:

  • Customer-supplied molds: dedicated packaging molds supplied by the pharmaceutical manufacturer, which the packaging manufacturer must keep safe after use
  • Customer-supplied raw materials: a specific rubber compound or resin supplied by the pharmaceutical manufacturer for the packaging manufacturer to process
  • Intellectual property: the customer's packaging design drawings and technical specifications (subject to confidentiality obligations)
  • Test samples: medicinal products or drug solutions sent by the customer for compatibility testing

Preservation System (8.5.4.2.1): Shelf-Life Management

Many packaging materials (especially rubber closures and pre-sterilized packaging) have a defined shelf life; for example, the shelf life of sterile products is usually 12-24 months. ISO 15378 requires:

  • A documented procedure must be in place to track expiry dates
  • Expired material must be segregated and disposed of
  • The shelf life must be supported by data (stability studies)

Delivery Documents Must Be Batch-Specific (8.5.4.2.2)

"Delivery documents must be batch-specific" is a key GMP requirement. The delivery note for each shipment must refer to specific batch numbers, not merely list the product name and quantity. This ensures that the pharmaceutical manufacturer can match the physical goods to the batch records at goods receipt, and it is one link in the end-to-end traceability chain.

Reuse of Packaging Containers (8.5.4.2.3)

The returnable crates/containers used to deliver some packaging materials may be reused. ISO 15378 requires that, if containers are reused, old labels be completely removed or obliterated (to prevent identification confusion) and that container cleanliness be verified according to a documented procedure. This seemingly minor requirement is in fact an important control against "residual contaminants in the container" and "identification errors caused by old labels".

ISO 15378 requires that, in determining the scope of these activities, the "potential undesirable consequences" be considered, that is, the possible impact of a packaging problem on patients. This reflects risk-based thinking.

Change Control: One of the Most Important Systems in GMP

The four additional GMP requirements of 8.5.6.2 outline a complete change control system architecture:

  • Trigger: any change that may affect product quality (raw material supplier, equipment, process parameters, facility)
  • Evaluation: risk assessment plus a decision on whether validation/revalidation is needed
  • Supporting data: supporting data must be generated (such as compatibility tests and engineering batches)
  • Approval: formal approval by an authorized function (usually the QA department) is required
  • Implementation: carried out in a controlled manner, with notification of the customer (where applicable)

Analogy: Packaging Changes Seen from the Pharmaceutical Manufacturer's Side

Imagine you are the QA head at a pharmaceutical company. Your rubber stopper supplier suddenly tells you they have changed the rubber formulation. This change would affect the rubber's extractables/leachables, sealing force and compatibility with the drug solution.

ISO 15378 requires the packaging manufacturer to complete a risk assessment and the necessary supporting data before making such a change, and to notify the customer (the pharmaceutical manufacturer). This is why many pharmaceutical supply contracts state that "the supplier shall not make changes without approval".

8.5.6.2.4: Who Has the Authority to Approve Changes?

"Defined functions" usually means the Quality Assurance (QA) department. This ensures that approval of changes is not driven solely by engineering or production; the quality department must be involved and hold final approval authority. In practice, many companies set up a cross-functional Change Control Board to review major changes.

8.6 Release of Products and Services

Product Release: Batch Documentation Review and Release Authorization

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p43)

8.6 Release of Products and Services (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall implement planned arrangements, at appropriate stages, to verify that the product and service requirements have been met.

The release of products and services to the customer shall not proceed until the planned arrangements have been satisfactorily completed, unless otherwise approved by a relevant authority and, as applicable, by the customer.

The organization shall retain documented information on the release of products and services. The documented information shall include:

  • a) evidence of conformity with the acceptance criteria;
  • b) traceability to the person(s) authorizing the release.
GMP Addition (from 9.1.3.5): A review of batch documentation shall be performed in order to release the batch. Shipment prior to batch release can occur in accordance with a customer agreement.
Commentary

Batch Release: The Packaging Plant's Equivalent of the QP Function

In a pharmaceutical plant, batch release is authorized by the Qualified Person (QP). ISO 15378 establishes a similar mechanism for packaging plants: a batch can be released only after a documentation review. This review normally includes:

  • Confirmation that the Batch Production Record is complete
  • All incoming materials have passed incoming inspection
  • In-process control data are within specification
  • Final inspection results meet the specification
  • Deviations have been investigated and closed, or their impact has been assessed

Conditions for "Shipment Prior to Release"

ISO 15378 (through 9.1.3.5) permits shipment prior to batch release under a "customer agreement". This arrangement is commonly called Preliminary Release or Ship-to-Test in the industry and is often used in urgent supply situations. However, it must be supported by written customer contract terms, and the final batch release conclusion must be completed afterwards.

Analogy: The Bank's Dual-Signature System

Batch release is like a large bank transfer that requires dual authorization: even when all the documents are ready, an authorized person must sign to confirm before the goods can "go out the gate" to the customer. This authorization record (who approved which batch, and when) is key evidence for any later audit.

8.7 Control of Nonconforming Outputs

Nonconforming Product Control: Segregation, Concession and Disposition, Three Pathways

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p44)

8.7.1 (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall ensure that outputs that do not conform to their requirements are identified and controlled to prevent their unintended use or delivery.

The organization shall take appropriate action based on the nature of the nonconformity and its effect on the conformity of products and services. This shall also apply to nonconforming products and services detected after delivery of products, during or after the provision of services.

The organization shall deal with nonconforming outputs in one or more of the following ways:

  • a) correction;
  • b) segregation, containment, return or suspension of provision of products and services;
  • c) informing the customer;
  • d) obtaining authorization for acceptance under concession.

Conformity to the requirements shall be verified when nonconforming outputs are corrected.

8.7.2 (Documented Information)

The organization shall retain documented information that:

  • a) describes the nonconformity;
  • b) describes the actions taken;
  • c) describes any concessions obtained;
  • d) identifies the authority deciding the action in respect of the nonconformity.
Commentary

The Three Pathways for Nonconforming Product

Once nonconforming product is found, ISO 15378 provides three handling options:

  • Correction/Rework: repair, then re-verify conformity to the specification (the most common)
  • Concession/Waiver: with written customer authorization, accept material that does not fully meet the specification (must be recorded)
  • Disposal/Destruction: when it cannot be corrected and the customer does not accept a concession, destroy it according to a documented procedure

Concession: Customer Authorization Is Required

Concession is a strictly controlled concept in GMP. ISO 15378 explicitly requires that any concession for nonconforming product be authorized in writing by the customer; it cannot be decided unilaterally by the packaging manufacturer.

This reflects an important principle of allocating responsibility: the end user (the pharmaceutical manufacturer) must be informed of and agree to whether nonconforming product may be used, because the risk of using nonconforming packaging material is borne by the pharmaceutical manufacturer's finished product.

Nonconforming Product Detected After Delivery

Clause 8.7.1 specifically covers "nonconforming product detected after delivery", that is, where a problem is found only after the packaging material has reached the customer or even been used in medicinal product manufacture. In this case, the packaging manufacturer must: notify the customer promptly, keep investigation records, cooperate with the customer in assessing the scope of impact, and take CAPA. This links directly to the customer complaint handling procedure in Section 10.

Analogy: The Automotive Parts Recall Process

When an automotive parts supplier discovers that delivered parts are defective, it cannot decide on its own that "it is fine" and keep shipping. It must notify the automaker (the customer) immediately, and the automaker decides how to deal with parts already installed in vehicles. ISO 15378 follows the same logic for packaging materials.

Section 9: Performance Evaluation — Overview

Section 9 Overview: A Complete Framework for Monitoring, Measurement, Analysis and Evaluation

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p44–45)

9.1.1 General (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall determine:

  • a) what needs to be monitored and measured;
  • b) the methods for monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation needed to ensure valid results;
  • c) when the monitoring and measuring shall be performed;
  • d) when the results from monitoring and measurement shall be analysed and evaluated.

The organization shall evaluate the performance and the effectiveness of the quality management system. The organization shall retain appropriate documented information as evidence of the results.

9.1.2 Customer Satisfaction (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall monitor customers' perceptions of the degree to which their needs and expectations have been fulfilled. The organization shall determine the methods for obtaining, monitoring and reviewing this information.

NOTE: Examples of monitoring customer perceptions can include customer surveys, customer feedback on delivered products and services, meetings with customers, market-share analysis, compliments, warranty claims and dealer reports.
Commentary

The Three Pillars of Section 9

Section 9 of ISO 15378 establishes a complete performance evaluation architecture at three main levels:

  • 9.1 Monitoring and evaluation: routine data collection, OOS investigation, incoming/in-process/batch controls
  • 9.2 Internal audit: systematic, periodic audit of QMS compliance
  • 9.3 Management review: periodic evaluation by top management of the overall effectiveness of the QMS

These three levels form a bottom-up quality monitoring system: routine data → audit findings → management decisions.

Customer Satisfaction Monitoring (9.1.2) in the Packaging Business

For a packaging plant, the key customer satisfaction indicators typically include: batch rejection rate, the number and severity of customer complaints, on-time delivery rate, and response time to quality notifications.

Many large pharmaceutical customers run a regular "Supplier Scorecard" rating system for their suppliers, and these scores feed directly into the packaging plant's customer satisfaction monitoring data.

9.1

9.1.3 Analysis and Evaluation — OOS, Incoming Inspection & In-Process Controls

Analysis and Evaluation: Out-of-Specification (OOS) Investigation, Incoming Inspection, In-Process Controls

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p45–46)

9.1.3.1 General

The organization shall analyse and evaluate appropriate data and information arising from monitoring and measurement. The results of analysis shall be used to evaluate: conformity of products and services; degree of customer satisfaction; performance and effectiveness of the QMS; whether planning has been implemented effectively; effectiveness of actions taken to address risks and opportunities; performance of external providers; the need for improvements.

The quality unit(s) shall ensure that quality critical deviations are investigated, resolved and documented. The organization shall establish and maintain documented procedures, including requirements for the analysis of data, to identify existing or potential causes of nonconforming product or other quality problems.

9.1.3.2 Investigation of OOS Results

Any out-of-specification (OOS) result shall be investigated according to a documented procedure and the outcome recorded (see 7.5.3).

9.1.3.3 Incoming Inspection and Testing

Requirements shall be established and maintained for all materials used. Incoming materials shall be inspected or otherwise verified as conforming to specified requirements.

9.1.3.4 In-Process Controls

9.1.3.4.1 — The organization shall, as required by documented procedures, inspect and test the product during processing.

9.1.3.4.2 — Sampling procedures shall be defined to ensure that samples are representative of the process being assessed. Samples shall not be returned to the production area if removed to a separate testing location.

9.1.3.4.3 — Additional in-process controls shall be carried out after an equipment breakdown or an unscheduled interruption which stops the process.

Commentary

OOS Investigation (9.1.3.2): A Mandatory GMP Requirement

Out-of-specification (OOS) investigation is a core requirement of US FDA 21 CFR Part 211 and EU GMP, and ISO 15378 brings it within the obligations of packaging plants. The basic OOS investigation procedure is:

  • Step 1 (laboratory investigation): confirm whether there is an identifiable laboratory error such as a calculation error, instrument malfunction or operator error
  • Step 2 (manufacturing investigation): if a laboratory cause cannot be ruled out, extend the investigation to the production process
  • Conclusion: confirm the validity of the OOS result, take CAPA and decide the disposition of the batch

Key point: OOS investigations must have a documented procedure, and the result of every investigation must be recorded and filed.

Incoming Inspection (9.1.3.3): Two Approaches in a Packaging Plant

ISO 15378 requires that incoming requirements be established for "all materials used" and that "inspection or other means of verifying conformity" be carried out. "Other means" usually refers to:

  • Supplier release certificate (Certificate of Analysis, CoA): for approved, qualified suppliers, accepting the CoA in place of batch-by-batch inspection (backed by periodic audits)
  • Skip-lot testing: for suppliers with a good history, reducing the sampling frequency (requires documented justification)

Three Key Points of In-Process Control

Representative samples (9.1.3.4.2): sampling must represent the whole process; for example, in injection molding each cavity may have different process characteristics, so the sampling plan must cover all cavities.

Samples must not be returned (9.1.3.4.2): samples removed from the production area must not be put back, because they may have been contaminated or damaged during testing.

Additional controls after equipment failure (9.1.3.4.3): after an equipment failure or unplanned stoppage, additional in-process controls must be performed to confirm that quality is stable once production resumes.

Analogy: The Quality Control Pyramid

Incoming inspection (9.1.3.3) is the first line of defense; in-process control (9.1.3.4) is real-time monitoring during manufacture; final inspection (9.1.3.5) is the last confirmation before shipment. OOS investigation runs through all levels: an OOS result at any level must trigger a systematic investigation, not just "retest once".

9.1.3.5–9.1.3.7: Batch Release, Retained Samples & Process Data

Batch Release, Retained Samples and the Principle of Open Process Data

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p47)

9.1.3.5 Batch Release

The organization shall implement an approval process for the batch release of products from the organization.

NOTE 1: Shipment prior to batch release can occur in accordance with a customer agreement.

If final inspection is a requirement, it shall be completed prior to batch release. Sampling procedures shall be defined to ensure that samples are representative of the batch being assessed. Samples shall not be returned to the production area if removed to a separate testing location.

A review of batch documentation shall be performed in order to release the batch.

NOTE 2: Final inspection might not include all specification parameters on basis of the control system and control strategy.

9.1.3.6 Retained Samples

Retained samples shall be taken in accordance with the organization's and/or customer's requirements.

9.1.3.7 Process Data

When required by the customer or their representative, production and control data related to the product (excluding the organization's confidential intellectual property) shall be made available for verification that the production process, in-process and final control and test equipment meet the requirements.

Commentary

Batch Release: The Core of Documentation Review

ISO 15378 stresses that batch release must include a Review of Batch Documentation. This is not merely confirming that final inspection passed; it is a comprehensive review of the whole production history of the batch:

  • Raw material receipt records + CoA
  • Production batch record (equipment, parameters, operators)
  • In-process control data
  • Deviation records (if any) and their evaluation
  • Final inspection report
  • Environmental monitoring data (if applicable)

The Significance of Note 2: Risk-Based Release (RTRT)

NOTE 2 of 9.1.3.5 alludes to the modern GMP concept of Real-Time Release Testing (RTRT): final inspection does not need to test every specification parameter as long as the control system and control strategy can assure quality. This is consistent with the process analytical technology (PAT) and QbD approaches of ICH Q8/Q10.

The Purpose of Retained Samples

Retained samples are the "time capsule" of a shipped batch. When a customer later raises a complaint or a problem arises, retained samples can be used to:

  • Retest the quality of the original batch (to confirm it was conforming at release)
  • Carry out reproduction tests of the problem described in the customer report
  • Serve as a reference for medicinal product recall investigations

The quantity, storage conditions and retention period of retained samples are usually specified in the customer contract or internal procedures (usually at least 1 year beyond the product shelf life).

9.1.3.7 The Principle of Open Process Data

Customers have the right to request access to production and control data to verify process compliance, but this "does not include the organization's confidential intellectual property". In practice this needs careful definition: which data can be shared (such as production records and test data) and which are trade secrets (such as formulation ratios and special process technology). A Quality Technical Agreement usually agrees the scope of data sharing in advance.

9.2 Internal Audit

Internal Audit: Frequency, Method, Objectivity and Follow-Up

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p47–48)

9.2.1 (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall conduct internal audits at planned intervals to provide information on whether the quality management system:

  • a) conforms to:
    • 1) the organization's own requirements for its quality management system;
    • 2) the requirements of this International Standard;
  • b) is effectively implemented and maintained.

9.2.2 (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall:

  • a) plan, establish, implement and maintain an audit programme(s) including the frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements and reporting, which shall take into consideration the importance of the processes concerned, changes affecting the organization, and the results of previous audits;
  • b) define the audit criteria and scope for each audit;
  • c) select auditors and conduct audits to ensure objectivity and impartiality of the audit process;
  • d) ensure that the results of the audits are reported to relevant management;
  • e) take appropriate correction and corrective actions without undue delay;
  • f) retain documented information as evidence of the implementation of the audit programme and the audit results.
NOTE: See ISO 19011 for guidance.
Commentary

The Dual Purpose of Internal Audit in ISO 15378

Clause 9.2.1 clearly lists two purposes of internal audit: to confirm conformity to the requirements of ISO 15378 (compliance confirmation), and to confirm that the QMS is effectively implemented and maintained (effectiveness evaluation).

Many organizations' internal audits only look at "whether the procedure exists" and ignore "whether the procedure actually works effectively". ISO 15378 requires equal attention to both.

Four Considerations for the Audit Program (9.2.2.a)

When drawing up the annual internal audit plan, the following must be considered:

  • Importance of the process: high-risk processes (such as sterilization and cleanroom production) should be audited more frequently
  • Organizational changes: new facilities, new equipment and staff changes need to be audited as a priority
  • Results of previous audits: areas where major nonconformities were found last time need stronger follow-up
  • Customer audit/regulatory inspection results: external audit findings should be built into the focus of internal audits

Objectivity and Impartiality (9.2.2.c)

Auditors must not audit their own area of work; this is the most basic independence requirement. In organizations with few staff this is often a challenge, and common solutions include: cross-auditing (different departments audit each other), engaging external auditors to supplement internal audits, and setting auditor qualification requirements to ensure competence.

Analogy: The Annual Financial Audit

Internal audit is like a company's internal financial audit: rather than waiting for an external inspection to find problems, it is a proactive, regular self-check. The difference is that a financial audit looks at the compliance of cash flows, while a GMP internal audit looks at the compliance of the quality system. Both need independence, both report results to management, and in both, problems found must be corrected within a reasonable time.

9.3 Management Review

Management Review: Inputs, Output Decisions and Top Management Responsibility

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p48–49)

9.3.1 General (ISO 9001:2015 base)

Top management shall review the organization's quality management system, at planned intervals, to ensure its continuing suitability, adequacy, effectiveness and alignment with the strategic direction of the organization.

9.3.2 Management Review Inputs

The management review shall be planned and carried out taking into consideration:

  • a) the status of actions from previous management reviews;
  • b) changes in external and internal issues relevant to the QMS;
  • c) information on the performance and effectiveness of the QMS, including trends in:
    • 1) customer satisfaction and feedback;
    • 2) the extent to which quality objectives have been met;
    • 3) process performance and conformity;
    • 4) nonconformities and corrective actions;
    • 5) monitoring and measurement results;
    • 6) audit results;
    • 7) the performance of external providers;
  • d) the adequacy of resources;
  • e) the effectiveness of actions taken to address risks and opportunities (see 6.1);
  • f) opportunities for improvement;
  • g) effectiveness of training.

9.3.3 Management Review Outputs

The outputs of the management review shall include decisions and actions related to:

  • a) opportunities for improvement;
  • b) any need for changes to the quality management system;
  • c) resource needs;
  • d) training needs.

The organization shall retain documented information as evidence of the results of management reviews.

Commentary

Management Review: The Top Management's Annual Health Check of the QMS

Management review is the formal mechanism by which top management periodically evaluates whether the whole QMS remains suitable, adequate and effective. Note that this is not a matter for the quality department alone: "Top management" must take part personally.

For a packaging plant, management review is normally held at least once a year (large or complex organizations may hold it every six months), and produces a written management review record and an action plan.

9.3.2(g) Effectiveness of Training: An ISO 15378 Addition

It is worth noting that ISO 15378 adds item g), "effectiveness of training", to the management review inputs. This is one more input than in the base ISO 9001:2015 and reflects the great importance GMP places on personnel competence and training. Management needs to evaluate whether the training program has really improved employee competence, and not just whether the paper target for training hours has been met.

Inputs vs. Outputs: The Closed Logic Loop of Management Review

ISO 15378 applies PDCA logic to management review:

  • Inputs (9.3.2): collect the quality performance data of the past year
  • Review (9.3.1): top management evaluates the data and identifies problems and opportunities
  • Outputs (9.3.3): make decisions: what improvements are needed? what resources are needed? what QMS changes are needed?

The outputs must include specific decisions and actions, not just "we talked about it and there is nothing much to worry about". The first input of the next management review (9.3.2.a) is the status of actions from the previous review.

Analogy: The Board of Directors' Annual Report

The structure of management review is similar to a company board's annual report: review the previous year's performance (financial indicators → quality indicators), assess changes in the internal and external environment, and decide resource allocation and strategic adjustments for the coming year. The difference is that the "indicators" of management review are quality performance data (nonconformity rate, number of complaints, audit findings), and the "strategic adjustments" are the QMS improvement plan.

Section 10: Improvement — Nonconformity, CAPA & Continual Improvement

Section 10: Improvement: Nonconformity, Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) and Continual Improvement

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p50–51)

10.1 General (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall determine and select opportunities for improvement and implement any necessary actions to meet customer requirements and enhance customer satisfaction. These shall include:

  • a) improving products and services to meet requirements as well as to address future needs and expectations;
  • b) correcting, preventing or reducing undesired effects;
  • c) improving the performance and effectiveness of the quality management system.

10.2 Nonconformity and Corrective Action

10.2.1 (ISO 9001:2015 base) — When a nonconformity occurs, including any arising from complaints, the organization shall:

  • a) react to the nonconformity: 1) take action to control and correct it; 2) deal with the consequences;
  • b) evaluate the need for action to eliminate the cause(s): 1) reviewing and analysing the nonconformity; 2) determining the causes; 3) determining if similar nonconformities exist, or could potentially occur;
  • c) implement any action needed;
  • d) review the effectiveness of any corrective action taken;
  • e) update risks and opportunities determined during planning, if necessary;
  • f) make changes to the quality management system, if necessary.

Corrective actions shall be appropriate to the effects of the nonconformities encountered.

10.2.2 — The organization shall retain documented information as evidence of: the nature of the nonconformities and any subsequent actions taken; the results of any corrective action.

Commentary

The Three Levels of Section 10

The improvement framework in Section 10 of ISO 15378 has three progressive levels:

  • Correction: immediate fixing of a problem that has already occurred (treating the symptom)
  • Corrective Action: eliminating the root cause of the nonconformity to prevent recurrence (treating the cause)
  • Continual Improvement: proactively identifying and implementing opportunities for improvement even when there is currently no problem (going beyond compliance)

GMP requires that all three levels be in place, not just correction.

The Closed-Loop CAPA Process (10.2.1)

Clause 10.2.1 in fact describes a complete closed-loop CAPA process:

  1. Immediate response: control and correct the nonconformity, and deal with the consequences
  2. Root cause analysis: not just "what happened" but "why it happened"
  3. Extension to other areas: determine whether similar problems exist or could occur elsewhere
  4. Implement action: carry out the measures that eliminate the root cause
  5. Effectiveness check: verify that the corrective action really worked (the step most often overlooked)
  6. Update the QMS: if necessary, update the risk assessment, procedure documents or the QMS
10.2.3 — Additional GMP-Related Requirements (p51)
ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text

The following additional GMP-related requirements apply to nonconformity and corrective action:

  • a) Nonconforming material or products shall be quarantined pending determination of corrective or other actions. When considering correction via rework or reconditioning, a risk assessment of any adverse effect of the reworking on the products shall be performed and recorded.
  • b) Rework and/or reconditioning shall be in accordance with a documented procedure that has been approved by the quality unit(s). The rework procedure shall be agreed with the customer, where this is a specified requirement.
  • c) If primary packaging material has been produced under cleanroom conditions, any rework shall be carried out under the same conditions.
  • d) Any proposal to release nonconforming product shall be via a documented concession, authorized by the customer.
  • e) Following rejection, primary packaging materials shall be disposed of or destroyed in accordance with a documented procedure.
  • f) The organization shall establish and maintain documented procedures for the analysis of data, to identify existing or potential causes of nonconforming product or other quality problems.
  • g) The organization shall investigate all customer complaints in a timely manner and communicate identified corrective action to all production and production-related sites. Action(s) shall be implemented as soon as practical and to an agreed timetable; records of investigation shall be maintained.
  • h) Customer complaints not followed by corrective and/or preventive action shall be justified and also recorded.
Commentary

10.2.3(c): Mandatory Requirement for Rework in the Cleanroom

This is a very specific and strict requirement in ISO 15378: if the packaging material is produced in a cleanroom environment, rework must also be carried out under the same cleanroom conditions.

This is because the quality assurance of cleanroom-produced packaging material (for example pre-sterilized components) depends on the clean environment. If rework is done in an ordinary environment, then even if the appearance of the material is restored, its cleanliness may already be compromised, and this cannot be accepted as a substitute.

10.2.3(d): Concession Must Be Authorized in Writing by the Customer

This clause echoes 8.7.1(d) and stresses once more that a concession for nonconforming product must be authorized in writing by the customer. "Our internal assessment is that it is acceptable" is not enough; the customer must be informed and give authorization.

This has important implications in the supply chain: after accepting a concession, the customer (the pharmaceutical manufacturer) is obliged to reflect this information in its own batch records and to assess the impact on the quality of the final medicinal product.

10.2.3(g): Time Requirements for Customer Complaint Handling

"In a timely manner" and "as soon as practical" are the wording used in ISO 15378. Although no specific number of days is prescribed, the usual industry practice in GMP is: initial acknowledgment within 24-48 hours; preliminary investigation report within 5-10 working days; final CAPA report within 30 days. These time limits are usually specified explicitly in the Quality Technical Agreement (QTA) between customer and supplier.

10.2.3(h): "No Action" Also Needs a Justification

This is a frequently overlooked requirement: even if the organization determines that a customer complaint does not require corrective or preventive action, that decision itself must be documented with the reasons stated. The complaint record cannot simply be "closed" without leaving the basis for the disposition decision.

10.3 Continual Improvement

Continual Improvement: Going Beyond Compliance and Building a Quality Culture

ISO 15378:2017 — Original Text (p51)

10.3 Continual Improvement (ISO 9001:2015 base)

The organization shall continually improve the suitability, adequacy and effectiveness of the quality management system.

The organization shall consider the results of analysis and evaluation, and the outputs from management review, to determine if there are needs or opportunities that shall be addressed as part of continual improvement.

Changes proposed as part of continual improvement shall be subject to risk management.
Commentary

The Essential Difference between Continual Improvement and Corrective Action

Many organizations confuse "Corrective Action" with "Continual Improvement":

  • Corrective action: triggered by a nonconformity that has already occurred, and aims to eliminate the root cause and prevent recurrence; it is reactive
  • Continual improvement: proactively identifies opportunities for enhancement even when there is currently no problem; it is proactive

ISO 15378 requires the organization to build a culture of continual improvement, not just "fix it when something goes wrong".

Changes from Continual Improvement Also Need Risk Management

The last sentence of 10.3 is very important: "Changes proposed by continual improvement must be subject to risk management."

This means that even a "well-intentioned improvement" may introduce unforeseen risks. For example, modifying a production process to increase efficiency could inadvertently affect the quality of the packaging material. Every improvement proposal must go through risk assessment and be linked to the change control procedure in 8.5.6.

Analogy: Combining Lean Production and Quality Management

The concept of continual improvement originates from the Japanese culture of "Kaizen", looking for small opportunities to improve every day. In the pharmaceutical packaging industry this means: not waiting for a customer complaint before making a change, but proactively analyzing process data (SPC, statistical process control), finding trends that are drifting but still within specification, and taking preventive action before the problem erupts.

This is the essence of the PA (Preventive Action) in CAPA: prevention, not just correction.

Section 3B Summary: Key Takeaways

Section Summary: Key Points on the Second Half of Production, Performance Evaluation and Improvement in ISO 15378

Key Requirements by Clause
Core Insights

Insight 1: Responsibility Cannot Be Outsourced

A theme running through this section is that, whether it is sterilization validation, concession or rework, responsibility always remains with the organization itself and does not disappear through outsourcing or customer authorization. The packaging manufacturer is a key node in the quality chain, and its quality decisions have a direct impact on the downstream pharmaceutical manufacturer and the end patient.

Insight 2: All the Additional GMP Requirements in ISO 15378 Point to the Same Thing

On careful review of all the additional GMP requirements in Sections 8-10, they can be seen to point to the same goal: to ensure that every batch of packaging material delivered to a pharmaceutical manufacturer carries complete, traceable quality assurance records, so that the pharmaceutical manufacturer can trust that the material will not endanger patients.

This is why ISO 15378 is harder to get certified to than ordinary ISO 9001: it requires not just "having procedures" but a quality assurance system that is effectively implemented, documented and auditable.

Insight 3: Sections 9 and 10 Form a Closed Loop

Section 9 (Performance evaluation) collects data, and Section 10 (Improvement) uses the data to drive action. This closed loop embodies the PDCA logic of ISO management systems:

  • P (Plan): Sections 6-8 establish strategy and processes
  • D (Do): the operational requirements of Section 8
  • C (Check): the performance evaluation of Section 9
  • A (Act): the improvement measures of Section 10

  • Your downstream pharmaceutical customer asks you to provide all production data for a certain batch. Which data can you provide, and which is confidential intellectual property?
  • Your supplier wants to change the glass tubing supplier. Which 8.5.6 change control requirements does this trigger?

Original Text (ISO 15378:2017)

This Annex is reproduced from ISO 9001:2015, Annex A. It provides informative clarification of the new structure, terminology and concepts introduced in the 2015 revision.

The International Standards described in this annex explain why the clause structure and some of the terminology of ISO 9001:2015 — and by extension ISO 15378:2017 — have been changed to improve alignment with other management systems standards.

There is no requirement in this International Standard for its structure and terminology to be applied to the documented information of an organization's quality management system. Organizations can choose to use terms which suit their operations, e.g. using "records", "documentation" or "protocols" rather than "documented information"; or "supplier", "partner" or "vendor" rather than "external provider".

Commentary

Why does ISO 15378 include annexes from ISO 9001?

The body of ISO 15378 is "built on ISO 9001:2015, with particular requirements added for pharmaceutical primary packaging." Annex A and Annex B therefore come directly from ISO 9001:2015 and help ISO 15378 users understand the logic of the underlying framework.

In other words: understanding Annex A = understanding the design philosophy of ISO 9001:2015.

Important reminder: freedom of terminology

The standard states explicitly that the organization does not need to convert all its internal documents to ISO terminology. Terms familiar to pharmaceutical manufacturers, such as "SOP", "batch record" and "specification", can all be kept, as long as their function meets the requirements of the standard.

Commentary

The logic behind the terminology changes

The terminology changes in the 2015 edition are not just renaming; they reflect a shift in QMS philosophy:

  • Products → Products and services: service organizations (such as CROs and CDMOs) are explicitly brought into scope
  • Supplier → External provider: a broader term that covers contract research organizations and outsourced service providers
  • Documented information: merges four concepts (documents / procedures / records / quality manual), reducing the administrative burden of document management
  • Management representative removed: responsibility is distributed across functions rather than assigned to one person

Key terminology mapping for pharmaceutical auditors

EU GMP Annex 15 and FDA 21 CFR Part 211 still use the older terms (such as "procedures", "records" and "supplier"). During audits you need to understand how the terms map to each other:

  • FDA "records" = ISO "retain documented information"
  • FDA/EU "procedures" = ISO "maintain documented information"
  • GMP "supplier qualification" = ISO "control of external providers"

Understanding this mapping avoids unnecessary confusion in cross-standard audits.

Analogy: from "filing folders" to a "knowledge base"

The 2008 edition was like having five separate folders (SOP folder, records folder, quality manual, plans...), each with its own management rules. The 2015 edition says: call all of it "documented information", and you decide which items to "maintain" and which to "retain"; what matters is that it fits its purpose.

A.2 Products and Services

Why "services" were explicitly added | p52

Original Text

ISO 9001:2008 used the term "product" to include all output categories. This edition of this International Standard uses "products and services". "Products and services" include all output categories (hardware, services, software and processed materials).

The specific inclusion of "services" is intended to highlight the differences between products and services in the application of some requirements. The characteristic of services is that at least part of the output is realized at the interface with the customer. This means, for example, that conformity to requirements cannot necessarily be confirmed before service delivery.

In most cases, products and services are used together. Most outputs that organizations provide to customers, or are supplied to them by external providers, include both products and services. For example, a tangible or intangible product can have some associated service or a service can have some associated tangible or intangible product.

Commentary

Impact on primary packaging material manufacturers

ISO 15378 is aimed at manufacturers of pharmaceutical primary packaging materials (such as glass vials, syringes and plastic closures). Besides supplying physical products, these companies often also provide:

  • Technical support (e.g., recommendations on material compatibility testing)
  • Packaging design services
  • Customized development agreements

The 2015 edition explicitly brings these "accompanying services" within the scope of the QMS.

Service characteristics vs product characteristics

The key difference with services: compliance cannot be fully verified before delivery (because a service is only completed at the moment it is delivered). This differs from the GMP logic of release before shipment, and it requires service quality to be assured at the design stage rather than relying solely on after-the-fact inspection.

A.3 Understanding the Needs and Expectations of Interested Parties

Needs and expectations of interested parties (Clause 4.2) | p53

Original Text

Subclause 4.2 specifies requirements for the organization to determine the interested parties that are relevant to the quality management system and the requirements of those interested parties. However, 4.2 does not imply extension of quality management system requirements beyond the scope of this International Standard.

As stated in the scope, this International Standard is applicable where an organization needs to demonstrate its ability to consistently provide products and services that meet customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements, and aims to enhance customer satisfaction.

There is no requirement in this International Standard for the organization to consider interested parties where it has decided that those parties are not relevant to its quality management system. It is for the organization to decide if a particular requirement of a relevant interested party is relevant to its quality management system.

Commentary

Scope of "interested parties" in the pharmaceutical packaging supply chain

For packaging material manufacturers to which ISO 15378 applies, interested parties (stakeholders) can include:

  • Direct customers: pharmaceutical manufacturers, CDMOs
  • Regulatory authorities: FDA, EMA, PMDA
  • End users: patients (through drug product safety)
  • Suppliers: raw glass manufacturers, plastic resin suppliers
  • Certification bodies: ISO / GMP audit organizations

Important boundary: this does not mean unlimited expansion of scope

The standard states explicitly that identifying interested parties does not mean the QMS must expand without limit to meet every requirement of every interested party. The organization has the right to decide on its own which requirements are relevant to the QMS. This clarification is very important in scope discussions during audits.

A.4 Risk-Based Thinking

Risk-based thinking - the design logic that replaced the "preventive action" clause | p53–p54

Original Text

The concept of risk-based thinking has been implicit in previous editions of this International Standard, e.g. through requirements for planning, review and improvement. This International Standard specifies requirements for the organization to understand its context (see 4.1) and determine risks as a basis for planning (see 6.1). This represents the application of risk-based thinking to planning and implementing quality management system processes (see 4.4) and will assist in determining the extent of documented information.

One of the key purposes of a quality management system is to act as a preventive tool. Consequently, this International Standard does not have a separate clause or subclause on preventive action. The concept of preventive action is expressed through the use of risk-based thinking in formulating quality management system requirements.

The risk-based thinking applied in this International Standard has enabled some reduction in prescriptive requirements and their replacement by performance-based requirements. There is greater flexibility than in ISO 9001:2008 in the requirements for processes, documented information and organizational responsibilities.

Although 6.1 specifies that the organization shall plan actions to address risks, there is no requirement for formal methods for risk management or a documented risk management process. Organizations can decide whether or not to develop a more extensive risk management methodology than is required by this International Standard, e.g. through the application of other guidance or standards.

Not all the processes of a quality management system represent the same level of risk in terms of the organization's ability to meet its objectives, and the effects of uncertainty are not the same for all organizations. Under the requirements of 6.1, the organization is responsible for its application of risk-based thinking and the actions it takes to address risk, including whether or not to retain documented information as evidence of its determination of risks.

Commentary

Why did the 2015 edition remove the "preventive action" clause?

Clause 8.5.3 of ISO 9001:2008 was "Preventive Action", which required identifying potential nonconformities and taking action. In practice, however, this clause was often executed mechanically (filling in forms and opening PAs without any real risk analysis).

The 2015 edition takes a more fundamental approach: it builds risk-based thinking into the whole QMS framework, in particular:

  • 4.1: Understanding the organization and its context
  • 4.2: Identifying the needs of interested parties
  • 6.1: Identifying risks and opportunities during planning

The result: the QMS itself is a continuous "preventive mechanism", rather than a separate preventive-action form system.

Analogy: from "fire extinguishers" to "fire-safe design"

The old preventive action was like placing a fire extinguisher in every room (an after-the-fact remedy mindset). The new risk-based thinking is like planning escape routes, fire-resistant materials and sprinkler systems from the building design stage: safety is built into the structure instead of being added on with tools.

Relationship with ICH Q9 risk management

The standard states clearly that it does not require formal risk management methodology or a documented risk management process. This differs from the formal FMEA/HACCP methods of ICH Q9: the risk-based thinking in ISO 15378 is an "attitude and judgment", whereas ICH Q9 provides "tools and methods". The two are complementary, and ISO 15378 does not replace ICH Q9.

Practical impact on pharmaceutical packaging manufacturers

The flexibility that risk-based thinking brings includes:

  • Packaging materials from low-risk processes (such as cartons) can have simpler documentation requirements
  • Packaging materials in direct contact with the drug product (such as syringe plungers) require stricter documentation
  • The organization can decide for itself which risk assessments need retained records and which can be confirmed verbally

A.5 Applicability

Applicability - replacing the old concept of "Exclusions" | p54

Original Text

This International Standard does not refer to "exclusions" in relation to the applicability of its requirements to the organization's quality management system. However, an organization can review the applicability of requirements due to:

  • the size or complexity of the organization
  • the management model it adopts
  • the range of the organization's activities
  • the nature of the risks and opportunities it encounters

The requirements for applicability are addressed in 4.3, which defines conditions under which an organization can decide that a requirement cannot be applied to any of the processes within the scope of its quality management system.

The organization can only decide that a requirement is not applicable if its decision will not result in failure to achieve conformity of products and services.

Commentary

The essential difference between "exclusion" and "not applicable"

The 2008 edition allowed the quality manual to declare an "exclusion" of particular clauses, but this led some organizations to simply skip requirements that were relevant to their business.

The 2015 edition changes this: the word "exclusion" no longer exists. You can only say that a clause is "not applicable to the scope of your QMS", and only on condition that this decision does not affect the conformity of products/services.

Key audit practice points

When an auditee claims that a clause is "not applicable", the auditor should confirm:

  1. Whether there is a written justification for why it is not applicable
  2. Whether the decision affects product conformity
  3. Whether it is addressed in the QMS scope statement

This differs from the old practice of "listing exclusion clauses in the quality manual" in both form and spirit.

A.6 Documented Information

Documented information - consolidating documents, procedures, records and the quality manual | p54

Original Text

As part of the alignment with other management system standards, a common clause on "documented information" has been adopted without significant change or addition (see 7.5). Consequently, "documented information" is used for all document requirements.

Where ISO 9001:2008 used specific terminology such as "document" or "documented procedures", "quality manual" or "quality plan", this edition of this International Standard defines requirements to "maintain documented information".

Where ISO 9001:2008 used the term "records" to denote documents needed to provide evidence of conformity with requirements, this is now expressed as a requirement to "retain documented information". The organization is responsible for determining what documented information needs to be retained, the period of time for which it is to be retained and the media to be used for its retention.

A requirement to "maintain" documented information does not exclude the possibility that the organization might also need to "retain" that same documented information for a particular purpose, e.g. to retain previous versions of it.

Where this International Standard refers to "information" rather than "documented information" (e.g. in 4.1), there is no requirement that this information is to be documented. In such situations, the organization can decide whether or not it is necessary or appropriate to maintain documented information.

Commentary

What happened to the quality manual?

ISO 9001:2015 formally removed the mandatory requirement for a "quality manual". Organizations may still choose to maintain one, but it is no longer a required element of the standard. For organizations already certified to ISO 15378 that have a quality manual, it is enough to make sure its coverage is consistent with the documented information required by the standard.

Analogy: recipe vs cooking log

Maintain = the chef's manual (recipes): keep the latest version, take it out when someone needs it, update it after changes, and discard old versions. Retain = the restaurant's daily service records: each day's records must be kept as proof for food safety inspections and cannot be disposed of at will.

A.7 Organizational Knowledge

Organizational knowledge - a new requirement in the 2015 edition (7.1.6) | p54–p55

Original Text

In 7.1.6, this International Standard addresses the need to determine and manage the knowledge maintained by the organization, to ensure the operation of its processes and that it can achieve conformity of products and services.

Requirements regarding organizational knowledge were introduced for the purpose of:

a) safeguarding the organization from loss of knowledge, e.g.

  • through staff turnover;
  • failure to capture and share information;

b) encouraging the organization to acquire knowledge, e.g.

  • learning from experience;
  • mentoring;
  • benchmarking.
Commentary

Organizational knowledge - an entirely new requirement in the 2015 edition

This is one of the most innovative additions of ISO 9001:2015 compared with the 2008 edition. Clause 7.1.6 requires the organization to:

  1. Determine the knowledge it needs
  2. Maintain this knowledge (and make it available to those who need it)
  3. When addressing new needs, consider how to acquire knowledge

Common knowledge-loss risks in pharmaceutical companies

For primary packaging material manufacturers, the following situations need particular attention:

  • A senior process engineer leaves, and the specialized material processing know-how is lost
  • The history of customer specifications is not documented, so the basis for decisions cannot be traced at the next certification
  • Root cause analysis results from audit findings are not systematized, so similar problems recur
  • Historical data from supplier change assessments is scattered across departments

Analogy: organizational memory vs personal memory

If key knowledge exists only in one employee's head, it is not "organizational knowledge"; it is "hostage risk". The essence of 7.1.6 is to require that "individual knowledge" be turned into "organizational knowledge".

A.8 Control of Externally Provided Processes, Products and Services

Control of externally provided processes, products and services (8.4) | p55

Original Text

All forms of externally provided processes, products and services are addressed in 8.4, e.g. whether through:

  • a) purchasing from a supplier;
  • b) an arrangement with an associate company;
  • c) outsourcing processes to an external provider.

Outsourcing always has the essential characteristic of a service, since it will have at least one activity necessarily performed at the interface between the provider and the organization.

The controls required for external provision can vary widely depending on the nature of the processes, products and services. The organization can apply risk-based thinking to determine the type and extent of controls appropriate to particular external providers and externally provided processes, products and services.

Commentary

Three forms of external provision

ISO 15378 broadens the scope of control here, from "purchasing" to "all forms of external provision" in the wider sense:

  • Purchasing: buying raw materials and components from suppliers (traditional supplier management)
  • Arrangements with affiliated companies: processes or services provided across sites within a group
  • Outsourcing: entrusting a process to an external organization to perform

Risk-based thinking determines the extent of control

Not all external provision needs the same intensity of control. Risk-based assessment:

  • Packaging materials in direct contact with the drug product (such as glass vials for injection) → rigorous supplier audits and material certification
  • Outsourced office cleaning services → contractual agreement is sufficient, no GMP audit needed
  • Outsourced sterilization process → control close to GMP level, audit capability required

This proportionate control logic is consistent with the spirit of the ICH Q10 pharmaceutical quality system.

Annex C (Normative) — GMP Requirements for Printed Primary Packaging Materials

Annex C (Normative): GMP Requirements for Printed Primary Packaging Materials | p60-p63

Annex C — Introduction & Status

This annex sets out GMP requirements for printed primary packaging materials. It is normative — meaning its requirements are mandatory and binding on organizations that manufacture printed packaging materials covered by this standard.

The annex covers three main areas:

  • C.1 — Artwork/origination and print impression media controls
  • C.2 — Print and conversion processes (including digital printing)
  • C.3 — Security code systems and reel material controls

Commentary: Why Is Annex C Especially Important?

Normative = Mandatory

In ISO standards, annexes fall into two categories:

  • Normative: carries the same weight as the clauses of the main text and must be complied with
  • Informative: provides guidance only and is not mandatory

Annex C is normative, so every organization that manufactures printed primary packaging materials must comply with it. This contrasts with Annex D (informative validation guidance).

Patient Safety at the Core

The text on printed packaging materials directly affects patient medication safety: wrong dosing instructions, an incorrect expiry date format, or mix-ups with other products can all cause serious harm. Every requirement in Annex C points to the same goal: ensuring that "the right label goes on the right product".

C.1

C.1 Artwork/Origination and Print Impression Media

Control of Artwork/Origination and Printing Media

C.1.1 General — Artwork File Control

C.1.1.1 Artwork/origination files shall be named and stored according to a documented procedure that enables them to be readily identified, issue controlled and traceable.

C.1.1.2 All print impression media shall be:

  • a) clearly and uniquely identified such that it is traceable to the origination material,
  • b) produced from, and traceable to, the master origination material held by the customer,
  • c) verified against the customer approved hard copy or electronic data and recorded (see 7.5.3), and
  • d) stored in a secure area within a defined system for authorized issue and return to store.

Commentary: The Print Plate Traceability Chain

Traceability Chain Overview

ISO 15378 requires a complete traceability chain from the customer master to the production printing plate:

Customer-approved master origination
↓ Copy and verify
Print plate/cylinder
↓ Unique identifier
Production plate (authorized issue/return)

Every level must be documented to ensure that the plate used in production is identical to the design approved by the customer.

Analogy: Managing Bank Signature Specimens

It works like a bank managing seals: there is a "master seal" (the customer master), and "working seals" (production plates) are copied from it once the account is opened. Every loan and return is logged, and old seals are destroyed and recorded when retired. Print plate management for packaging materials follows exactly the same logic.

C.1.2 Multiple Plates/Cylinders

Where more than one printing plate/cylinder is required, there shall be a documented system for ensuring that all plates/cylinders within the set are used.

Where a set of plates/cylinders contains the generic design for several jobs, each individual plate/cylinder within the set shall be clearly, uniquely identified and documented.

Commentary: Register Control in Multi-Colour Printing

Risks of Multi-Colour Printing

Pharmaceutical packaging materials usually need several colours (for example, a black text plate, a blue colour-block plate and a red warning plate). If one colour plate is missing or the wrong version is used, the result may be:

  • Missing information (such as absent dosing instructions)
  • Colour identification errors (colour is an important element of medicinal product identification)
  • Mixing of versions from different batches

Each set of plates must therefore be managed as a complete set, and the full set must be present before it goes onto the press.

C.1.3 Copy/Design Change

Where a design requires several plates/cylinders and some of them are to be replaced because of a copy/design change, there shall be a documented procedure to allow for the replacement of the affected plate(s)/cylinder(s) and the retention of the other media within the set.

The original plates/cylinders shall be subject to a procedure that allows re-identification.

Commentary: The Complexity of Version Changes

The Challenge of Partial Changes

A design change does not always require every colour plate to be replaced. For example, only the expiry date format (the text plate) may be updated while the colour-block plate stays the same. In this case you need to:

  • Record clearly which plates are replaced and which are retained
  • Re-identify the old plates (to avoid their misuse on the new design)
  • Never mix old and new versions within the same set of plates

C.1.4 Verification, Quarantine and Destruction

C.1.4.1 General — Verification of the design on print impression media shall be carried out during the printing machine make-ready and before the approval to run the product is given.

C.1.4.2 Quarantine and destruction — The organization shall have:

  • a) a documented procedure which ensures that origination and print media, for a design undergoing revision, are subject to formal quarantine, and
  • b) a documented system detailing the method for disposal of the unwanted origination and print impression media; such items shall be rendered unusable and disposed of in a controlled and secure manner.

Commentary: Preventing the Old Version from Coming Back

Risks of Obsolete Plates

If an obsolete plate is not destroyed, it may be taken out and used by accident, producing packaging materials with an outdated design. "Rendered unusable" is the key requirement:

  • Metal plates: physical destruction (bending, perforating)
  • Electronic files: formal deletion with a record
  • Photographic film: cutting or chemical treatment

Plates held in quarantine must carry clear quarantine identification to prevent misuse.

Make-Ready Verification

The plate design must be verified as correct before every start-up. This is not a one-time event: it must be re-confirmed after a plate change, after a shift change and after a production interruption. The verification results must be recorded (clause 7.5.3).

C.2

C.2 Print and Conversion Processes

GMP Requirements for Printing and Converting Processes | Make-ready, plate changes, retained samples, gang printing

C.2.1 Print Machine Set-Up (Make-Ready)

C.2.1.1 Initial print make-ready shall be performed using unprinted components or material.

C.2.1.2 Make-ready for subsequent processes may use material from the initial print process of the same batch.

C.2.1.3 Initial make-ready material may be reused during the make-ready process in order to achieve correct colour.

C.2.1.4 Material used for make-ready shall be segregated and then disposed of as production waste.

Commentary: Managing Make-Ready Waste

Purpose of Make-Ready (Trial Printing)

Each time the press is started, a certain quantity must be run as "make-ready" before colour is stable and register is accurate. This make-ready material must not be mixed into the production batch. It must be:

  • Clearly identified as "waste"
  • Physically segregated
  • Disposed of as production waste

Make-ready material from an earlier operation (such as printing) may be used to set up a later operation (such as hot-foil stamping or cutting), but it must likewise be disposed of as waste once the later operation is finished.

C.2.2 Changeover Systems

Changeover systems that are designed to reduce make-ready time (e.g. automated plate changing), and which do not permit a total line clearance, shall be subject to a documented risk assessment and operated with controls to ensure product security.

All print media from the previous job shall be removed from the line prior to formal approval of the print run being given.

All controls shall be recorded (see 7.5.3).

Commentary: Risks of Automatic Plate Changing

Automation Does Not Remove GMP Risk

Automatic plate-changing systems can greatly shorten changeover time, but if the system design does not allow a "total line clearance", the organization must:

  • Perform a documented risk assessment
  • Establish specific controls to ensure that no product mix-up occurs
  • Confirm that all plates from the previous job have been removed from the production line
  • Record the results of all control measures

Key principle: technical convenience must not come at the expense of product safety. The risk assessment is mandatory.

C.2.3 Retained Samples

C.2.3.1 All in-process printed samples which are to be retained shall be clearly identified and securely stored.

C.2.3.2 Samples used for other purposes (e.g. administration/sales) shall be voided if they leave the control of the organization.

Commentary: Dual Management of Samples

Two Types of Retained Samples, Two Treatments

"Voiding" usually means printing or stamping "SAMPLE" or "VOID" on the sample so that it cannot be mistaken for production packaging material. This prevents samples from reaching the market or being used to pack medicinal products by mistake.

C.2.4 Replacement Print Media

C.2.4.1 During a production run, if replacement plates are made from an existing fixed approved source (e.g. negatives, or using computer-to-plate technology from an existing stepped image), the job may be continued after a new "first off" check has been carried out.

C.2.4.2 During a production run, if plates are created from a new source (e.g. re-stepping a "one-up" image), the existing job shall be lifted; the replacement plates shall be treated as new origination. Subsequent production shall be treated as a new batch. The introduction of all replacement print media shall be recorded (see 7.5.3).

Commentary: Batch Determination When Plates Are Changed During Production

Comparison of Two Plate Change Scenarios

Logic: traceability of the source determines the continuity of the batch. A new source means a new design origin, which must be re-verified, so a new batch must be opened.

C.2.5 Gang Printing

Gang printing (the process of printing more than one design on a substrate at one production run) is recognized and classified as an acute contributory risk relating to admixtures.

Therefore this shall only be permitted:

  • in agreement with the customer, and
  • on completion of a documented risk assessment to evaluate and mitigate the risk of cross-contamination (see 8.2.1).

Commentary: Gang Printing - Efficiency versus Patient Safety

The Serious Risk of Gang Printing

"Gang printing" means printing several different designs on the same substrate in a single print run. It is common in commercial printing (it saves cost), but for pharmaceutical packaging it is an acute mix-up risk:

  • Insufficient physical separation between designs
  • Cutting errors may carry part of design A into the batch of design B
  • "Admixture" = the labels of one product getting mixed into another product

Analogy: "Cross-Contamination" in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

Just as in sterile manufacturing different products must not be made on the same line (unless strict segregation and line clearance are in place), gang printing puts the identification information of different medicinal products on the same sheet, and its essential risk is the pharmaceutical equivalent of cross-contamination. The standard explicitly calls it an "acute contributory risk", which is very strong wording.

Compliance Route: Both Conditions Are Required

  • Written customer agreement: a verbal agreement is not enough
  • Documented risk assessment: the specific mix-up risks must be assessed and the mitigation measures recorded

C.2.6 Batch Production and Stock Holding

C.2.6.1 Batched production and holding of product in stock shall only be practised if contractually agreed.

C.2.6.2 The organization shall control the storage to ensure security and integrity of the product and maintain its traceability back to manufacture and the materials used.

Commentary: Control of Stock-Based Production

Batched Production (Production for Stock)

Some packaging material manufacturers "pre-produce" and hold stock in the warehouse, shipping it once the customer places an order. This model must:

  • Be contractually agreed
  • Have a stock management system that ensures traceability (to the manufacturing batch and the raw materials)
  • Prevent the release of old-version stock during an unauthorized design change

The biggest risk of the stock-based model is that after a design change, old-version stock is not intercepted and is shipped to the customer.

C.2.7

C.2.7 Digital Printing — Emerging Control Challenges

Digital Printing: New Technology Brings New Control Requirements | p62

C.2.7 Digital Printing Requirements

C.2.7.1 The flexible capabilities of digital printing introduce new activities, which shall be controlled and documented to ensure the accuracy and security of the printed products.

C.2.7.2 The use of digital printing and any special requirements for the product shall be agreed with the customer.

C.2.7.3 The organization shall establish a secure file access system, which is designed to prevent unintentional use of incorrect origination files.

C.2.7.4 Unless alternative security is designed, the controlling computer within the digital printing machine shall have only the specific origination file in its memory for the current print run; removal of this file shall form part of documented line clearance.

C.2.7.5 Operational settings to achieve acceptable colours shall be established through a formal process and recorded (see 7.5.3).

C.2.7.6 For reel-fed production on a continuous basis there shall be a suitable method to ensure product separation; the subsequent processes shall be verified to ensure correct separation of products and removal of defined gap material.

EXAMPLES: Defined change-over or gap between each printed job e.g. printed dummy text or blank material, code reading and code verification.

Commentary: Digital Printing - Where Traditional Plate Controls Break Down

Unique Risks of Digital Printing

Traditional printing relies on the physical plate as an error-proofing mechanism: the wrong plate simply cannot be mounted on the press. Digital printing relies entirely on computer files, so a single keystroke error can print the design of product A on the packaging material of product B, with a perfectly normal appearance.

Four Additional Control Requirements

  • Secure file access system: prevents operators from accidentally selecting the wrong design file (for example, strict file permissions and double confirmation for job changeover)
  • Single file in memory: the press computer may hold only the file of the current job during printing, and it must be cleared before switching
  • Formalized colour settings: colour management in digital printing (colour profiles, ICC settings) must be established through a formal process and recorded, and must not depend on the operator experience
  • Product separation verification: in continuous web production, it must be confirmed that the interval material between different designs is correctly identified and removed

Analogy: Access Control for Electronic Medical Records

A hospital electronic medical record system must ensure that doctors do not accidentally view or modify the wrong patient record, and a digital printing system must likewise ensure that operators do not accidentally load the design file of the wrong product. Both are problems of "compliant access control for information systems".

C.3 Security Code Systems & Reel Material Controls

Security Code Systems, Online Scanning Verification and Web Control | p62-p63

C.3.1 General & C.3.2 Verification Methods

C.3.1 To ensure the security of the product and prevent cross-contamination, security code systems may be included in the design of printed packaging materials for verification either by the organization during manufacture and/or by the customer during the packaging operation.

NOTE: Common security code systems include barcode systems, data matrix codes, and tag technology.

C.3.2.1 Where practical, every security coded item shall be verified by online scanning equipment to ensure that the codes are readable and that the correct product is being produced. Scanning should be carried out during the last feasible production process.

C.3.2.2 The scanning equipment software/control configuration shall be controlled to prevent unauthorized tampering. Codes shall be loaded from an independent source, e.g. specification or approved proof.

C.3.2.3 There shall be an effective system for rejecting any product that fails the scanning process. Any product rejected by the online scanning system shall be inspected, subsequently scrapped, and findings recorded and reviewed prior to product release.

C.3.2.4 The online scanning equipment and its associated reject mechanism shall be subjected to a challenge test during production to verify whether its operation is effective in detecting and removing incorrectly coded material. Such monitoring shall take place at the start of the process, at regular intervals, and be recorded.

C.3.2.5 Any product produced where electronic scanning is specified, but has not been performed, shall be properly authorized and recorded. The customer shall be notified and documented approval obtained before product release.

C.3.2.6 For reel-fed multi-lane production, all lanes should be subject to security code verification. Where this cannot be performed, one lane only may be verified, in agreement with the customer.

C.3.2.7 / C.3.3 "Point of measure" codes and/or verification of sample codes from all lanes shall be carried out.

Where "point of sale" codes (GS1/EAN, Code 39, PZN, etc.) are incorporated into the design, a documented sample verification shall be carried out during the production process.

Commentary: Core Requirements for Online Scanning

Online Scanning = 100% Verification

"Every security code item shall be verified by online scanning equipment." This is 100% online verification, not sampling. For security codes on pharmaceutical packaging materials (such as barcodes and Data Matrix), missing even one could mean that a wrong product leaves the plant.

The Importance of Challenge Tests

Scanning equipment must not only confirm that it "can read the code"; it must also be challenge-tested periodically to confirm that it:

  • Detects incorrect codes (not just missing codes)
  • Has a reject mechanism that actually operates and removes nonconforming items
  • Is tested at the start of each production run and at regular intervals, with the results recorded

Analogy: a fire drill does not just confirm that the alarm sounded; it also confirms that employees can really evacuate to a safe place.

Handling Exceptions When Scanning Is Not Performed

If scanning is required but was not performed, the material cannot be released automatically:

  • Formal authorization (documented approval) must be obtained
  • The customer must be notified and written agreement obtained
  • This is a formal deviation and must be recorded in the quality records

C.3.4 Reel Materials and Procedures

C.3.4.1 Unless otherwise specified by the customer, splices shall be:

  • a) made using a brightly coloured adhesive tape on both sides of the web, and
  • b) checked either side of the splice to ensure that identical materials are joined and in register.

A limit on the maximum number of splices may be specified.

C.3.4.2 The quantity of material (length, weight or numerical) produced on each reel shall be determined within accuracy limits agreed with the customer and recorded on the reel.

C.3.4.3 The batch identity, reel number and production date shall be recorded on the inner face of the core for each individual reel.

C.3.4.4 To prevent cross-contamination, the web shall be run to plain material at the end of the run to ensure that no printed material remains in the printing equipment.

C.3.4.5 Where it is necessary to leave printed material in the converting equipment due to the difficulty in carrying out re-webbing (e.g. slitters), there shall be a formal documented procedure for removal and disposal of the material used to pull the new design through the machine.

C.3.4.6 If material with missing print can be produced as a consequence of the design or operation of the printing equipment, the organization shall have a secure system for the detection, removal and segregation of product produced with missing colours or text.

Commentary: Special Controls for Web (Reel) Production

Why Do Splices Need Coloured Tape?

In web production, when one reel of raw material runs out and a new reel is spliced on, the packaging material at the splice may have printing shifts, misregistration and similar problems. Marking the splice with brightly coloured tape lets the customer quickly identify and remove the material at these positions during later filling operations.

Reel Core Traceability Information (C.3.4.3)

The batch number, reel number and production date are recorded on the inside of the reel core (not the outside), because:

  • Labels on the outside are easily damaged or detached during transport
  • The core is the physical item that remains after the whole reel is used up, so the traceability information must be kept with it
  • The traceability chain stays intact until the last turn of material is used

Analogy: The Logic of "Clearing Down to Plain Material"

C.3.4.4 requires that, at the end of production, the line is run out to "plain material" before it stops, just as a kitchen must clean its pots before switching cuisines. Printed material left in the printing equipment, if it enters the next job, is the source of an "admixture" event.

Detection Systems for Missing Print

Under certain conditions, some printing equipment may produce packaging materials with missing characters or missing colours. If such material is released, patients end up with incomplete label information. The organization must have a systematic detection and rejection mechanism and must not rely on visual inspection.

Annex D (Informative): Guidance on Verification, Qualification and Validation | p64-p74

D.1 General — Scope of Annex D

This annex describes the approach when verification or qualification and validation are required. The guidance relates to the requirements given in 8.5.1.2 (additional GMP-related requirements on verification, qualification and validation).

Items which can require verification/qualification include:

  • equipment used for production of primary packaging materials, utilities and facilities, and
  • test equipment used for determining the acceptance of quality critical starting materials, intermediate/in-process or finished product.

Items which can require validation include:

  • validation of processes, and
  • validation of individual product, where appropriate or as agreed with the customer.

The aim of verification/qualification/validation is to confirm through documented evidence that pre-determined specifications are consistently fulfilled.

Commentary: What the V/Q/V Framework Means in Packaging Material Manufacturing

Link to EU GMP Annex 15

The V/Q/V (verification, qualification and validation) framework of ISO 15378 Annex D closely parallels EU GMP Annex 15 (Qualification and Validation). Both emphasize:

  • Risk assessment-driven decisions
  • Sequential confirmation through IQ → OQ → PQ
  • A documented plan, protocol and report structure
  • Change control to maintain the qualified state

Readers familiar with EU GMP Annex 15 will find the same logical framework in ISO 15378 Annex D.

D.2 Prerequisites and Considerations

Prerequisites and Considerations: Preconditions for Equipment Qualification and Process Validation

D.2.1 General

A documented risk assessment is used to determine which equipment and processes need to be verified/qualified/validated. Product validation is optional, either after an internal decision of the organization or at the request of the customer.

Verification/qualification of equipment and validation of processes/product can be performed independently of each other, e.g. validation of a new process/product does not require re-verification/re-qualification of existing equipment.

D.2.2 Prerequisites for Verification/Qualification/Validation

Prerequisites to verification/qualification of the equipment:

  • approved/agreed upon requirement specifications,
  • identification of roles and agreed responsibilities (organization and supplier),
  • definition of critical process parameters,
  • training such as GMP and verification/qualification.

Prerequisites to validation of the process/product:

  • approved/agreed upon process/product specifications,
  • verified/qualified equipment,
  • identification of roles and agreed responsibilities (organization and customer),
  • definition of critical process parameters,
  • training (operators, quality personnel, engineers, etc.),
  • standard operating procedures to provide detail on how to produce/operate and clean the equipment.

D.2.3 Software Validation

Software can be validated/verified by functional tests. Software which is part of the equipment can be included in the equipment verification/qualification.

NOTE: The GAMP guideline might give guidance on how the functional test can be executed.

D.3 Validation Master Plan — Planning, Types & Decision Criteria

Validation Master Plan: Types, Roles and Criteria for Critical Equipment/Processes | p65-p68

D.3.1 VMP — General Elements

The Validation Master Plan (VMP) describes the validation activities and the order of execution in accordance with the overall validation approach. It is recommended to revise the plan on a regular basis, for example yearly.

It should usually include the following elements:

  • validation planning (see D.3.2) and scheduling;
  • organizational structure of validation activities — roles and responsibilities (see D.3.3);
  • summary of quality critical equipment, process (see D.3.4 and D.3.5) and product;
  • references to existing documents (e.g. reference to an existing validation report), if applicable;
  • references to applicable procedures (e.g. overall validation approach, documentation format).

Commentary: The VMP Is the "Map" of Qualification and Validation Activities

Positioning of the VMP

The VMP is not the qualification itself; it is the overall planning document for qualification activities. It answers three questions:

  • What to do? (which equipment and processes need qualification)
  • How to do it? (which methods and standards to apply)
  • Who does it? (allocation of roles and responsibilities)

An annual update ensures that the VMP reflects the latest equipment status, newly introduced processes, and any completed or planned qualification activities.

D.3.2 Validation Planning — Three Approaches

D.3.2.1 General — Prospective validation is recommended as the preferred approach but under certain circumstances a concurrent or retrospective validation can be accepted (this statement is not valid for sterilization processes).

D.3.2.2 Prospective validation — Prospective validation occurs prior to commercial production.

D.3.2.3 Concurrent validation — Concurrent validation should only apply to performance qualification and process validation. Concurrent validation consists of activities conducted in parallel with the manufacture of commercial product where the commercial product is released before the conclusion of validation activities.

Concurrent validation should follow the principles and procedures associated with prospective validation. Validation that takes place in campaigns represents a speciality in the manufacture of primary packaging material — concurrent validations may be interrupted and resumed with the next production campaign.

D.3.2.4 Retrospective validation — This approach includes establishing documented evidence that installed and operating equipment produces a consistent product by performing a historic review of data generated over the range of operating parameters and raw materials. This review can include maintenance and engineering records, quality records, and customer complaints.

Commentary: Logic for Choosing Among the Three Validation Approaches

A Special Feature of Packaging Material Manufacturing: Campaign Production

Many packaging material manufacturers use "campaign production": a given product is made in a large batch once a quarter or once every six months. In this case, concurrent validation may be interrupted when the campaign ends and resumed when the next campaign starts. This flexibility is specific to the packaging industry, and the standard explicitly allows it.

Exception: Sterilization Processes Must Use Prospective Validation

The standard clearly states that this principle that "all three approaches are acceptable" does not apply to sterilization processes (see 8.5.1.4.2 and 8.5.1.2.5). Sterilization processes must use prospective validation, which is also consistent with the positions of the FDA and EU GMP.

D.3.2.5 Bracketing/Matrix Approach

In general a bracketing/matrix approach can be appropriate based on risk analysis. Where the equivalence of equipment has been proven, a bracketing/matrix approach can be used to:

  • reduce the number of installation and operational testing during equipment verification/qualification,
  • reduce the number of PQ batches during equipment qualification,
  • reduce the number of PV batches during product or process validation, and
  • increase the re-validation period for individual equipment and performing the re-validation on equivalent equipment alternately.

One of the Most Valuable Tools for Manufacturers

For packaging material manufacturers with several machines of the same model, the matrix approach can greatly reduce the validation workload while maintaining compliance. The key prerequisite is: the equivalence of the equipment must be demonstrated first.

In the pharmaceutical industry, bracketing is commonly used in stability studies: only the strongest and weakest strengths are tested, and the intermediate strengths are assumed to fall within the range. D.3.4 Quality Critical Equipment — Decision Criteria

Risk assessment is an inherent part of the decision-making process. Equipment is critical in case one of the following questions is answered with "yes":

  • Would malfunction of the equipment impact directly on product quality as defined for the type of products it is producing?
  • Is the equipment used to guarantee sterility of the products?
  • Does the equipment control or measure quality critical processing steps or parameters?
  • Does the equipment produce data/records for acceptance or rejection?
  • Is equipment in direct contact with the product?
  • Is the equipment used to prevent contamination, to remove contamination or to clean?

When equipment is considered critical, verification or qualification should be performed and it is included in the VMP. When equipment is not critical, good engineering practices apply and it is not included in the VMP.

  • Calibration (where applicable)

D.4.1.3 Requirement Specification

D.4.1.3.1 General — The requirement specifications can be combined in one or more specification documents depending on the criticality of the equipment, whether it concerns standardized or customized equipment, or depending on responsibilities. Every specification should contain well defined, specific, testable, achievable acceptance criteria.

D.4.1.3.2 User Requirement Specification (URS) — A URS (as defined in 3.7.11) is an approved document that states the product specifications of the material produced on this equipment as well as functional, operational and/or technical aspects of the equipment or process required to produce the desired product. The URS can include the consideration for equipment lay-out with sufficient space for material flow and operators, availability of spare parts, and ease of access for cleaning and line clearance.

Verification/qualification can be split in two sub-stages: installation qualification and operational qualification, but it is also possible to combine them in one stage (typically done during testing at the supplier and testing on site). Leveraging of data are allowed — data obtained during testing at the supplier does not have to be obtained again on site unless the transfer of the equipment influences the testing.

Commentary: The Three Stages of IQ/OQ/PQ

URS: The Starting Point of Qualification

The URS (user requirements specification) must be completed before the equipment is purchased. It defines:

  • The product specifications the equipment must produce
  • Functional requirements (speed, accuracy and so on)
  • GMP-related requirements (cleanability, material compatibility)
  • Operator access/safety requirements
  • Layout and space considerations

The URS is the baseline document for all later qualification activities.

The Principle of "Leveraging" Data

If factory acceptance testing (FAT) at the supplier has already collected sufficient data, that data can be "carried over" and used in on-site qualification without being collected again. The condition is that no event occurred during transport or installation that could affect the test results. This is a pragmatic, risk-based approach that avoids unnecessary duplication of work.

D.4.1.4 Performance Qualification (PQ)

PQ testing uses production materials to verify that the equipment is robust and the primary packaging material can be consistently produced under routine operating conditions. PQ challenges the overall line performance to ensure that it can produce consistently at the quality standard required.

The test process and results of an appropriate number of consecutive production batches (typically three) are formally documented and approved.

Based on risk management, performance qualification can be skipped or can also be combined with process or product validation.

An exception to using three subsequent batches may be made where the production process is lengthy — for example a single batch may require several weeks of continuous production. In this case PQ may be conducted on sub-batches of a minimum of one day's duration each.

Commentary: Flexibility in PQ

"Typically Three" Batches Is Not a Hard Rule

"Typically three" batches is a common benchmark in the GMP context, but ISO 15378 explicitly provides exceptions. For processes that take several weeks to complete one batch (for example, long print runs of large reels), a "sub-batch of at least one day" can replace the full three batches.

This balances science and pragmatism: the qualification principle is not skipped because the production cycle is long, but is carried out in an equivalent way.

PQ Can Be Combined with Process Validation

Based on risk management, PQ can be:

  • Skipped: if the equipment is highly standardized and OQ is sufficient to confirm performance
  • Combined with PV: the PQ batches also serve as process validation batches, achieving both at once

The decision to skip or combine must be supported by a risk assessment and approved by the quality unit.

D.4.2 Process and Product Validation

D.4.2.1 Process validation — A process validation consists of a number of consecutive production batches (typically three) produced under normal conditions with a higher level of sampling and additional testing compared to routine production. Process validation can be combined with PQ.

Process validation has to be based on a firm base of knowledge of the process and its variables/variations. Process validation is independent of the product. Bracketing or matrix approach may be used (see D.3.2.5).

D.4.2.2 Product validation — The approach is in line with process validation; however specific customer requirements can be added.

Commentary: Process Validation versus Product Validation

Key Difference: Process Validation Is Independent of the Product

Process validation verifies the capability of the "process itself" and is independent of any specific product. For example:

  • Validating that the "rotogravure printing process" consistently achieves a given colour accuracy and register tolerance
  • Once process validation is complete, a new product made on this process usually needs only product qualification, not revalidation of the process

Product validation adds the requirements of a specific customer or product (such as specific ink compatibility or special substrates) on top of process validation.

D.5 Documentation Hierarchy

D.5.1 General — All documents should be reviewed and approved prior to the commencement of verification/qualification/validation/release. Any release decisions should be the responsibility of the quality unit. Any revision of a document should be tracked by version control.

D.5.2 Plan (optional) — One or more plans can be created to detail the steps to be taken in the different stages. These plans can include:

  • description of the project/scope;
  • roles and responsibilities;
  • planning;
  • risk analysis;
  • overall acceptance criteria;
  • validation approach;
  • documents to be created;
  • reference to procedures and change control;
  • definition of conditions for quarantine status of batches subject to PQ or validation.

Plan and protocol may be combined in one document.

D.5.3 Protocol — A written protocol should be established that specifies how verification/qualification/validation will be conducted. The protocol can include:

  • description of the project/scope;
  • roles and responsibilities;
  • justification of the approach taken;
  • tests to be performed with test methods and test conditions;
  • detailed acceptance criteria for each test;
  • sampling plan;
  • critical process parameters;
  • reference to procedures, change control and requirement specifications;
  • prerequisites.

D.5.4 Report — A report that cross-references the protocol should be prepared. The report can include:

  • summary of test results;
  • raw data;
  • deviations observed and corrective actions taken or to be taken;
  • conclusion;
  • changes to the plan as defined in the protocol with appropriate justification.

After completion of satisfactory testing, a formal release for the next step should be made as a written authorization.

D.5.5 Validation Summary Report — VSR (optional) — The VSR follows the completion of all stages of V/Q/V and concludes the project. The VSR can include:

  • cross-reference to protocols and reports;
  • changes to plans and justification for any change;
  • formal approval and closure of the project.

D.5.6 Archiving — Verification or qualification and validation records should be maintained according to 7.5.3.11.

Core Difference between Protocol and Report

The protocol must be approved before execution: it defines "how we plan to do it".
The report is written after execution: it records "what we actually did and what the results were".

This sequence of "approve the protocol, then execute, then report" is a basic GMP principle. It ensures that acceptance criteria are clearly defined before testing and prevents the practice of "changing the criteria after the fact".

Analogy: Pre-Registration of Clinical Trials

Clinical trials also require the protocol to be registered in advance, and the statistical methods and success criteria for the results must be defined beforehand. The protocol-first principle in qualification and validation comes from the same logic of scientific integrity.

Principles for Handling Deviations

A deviation recorded in the report does not necessarily mean that the qualification has failed. The key points are:

  • Every deviation must have a written root cause analysis
  • The impact on product quality must be assessed
  • Corrective actions must be documented and tracked to completion
  • The quality unit must approve any "release with deviations"

D.6 Validation Maintenance — Change Control & Re-qualification

Maintaining Qualification and Validation: Change Control, Preventive Maintenance and Requalification | p74

D.6 Validation Maintenance

D.6.1 General — The verification/qualification of equipment and validation of process(es)/product should be maintained. Any changes should be risk assessed for their impact on the validation status. If necessary, appropriate verification/qualification/validation or re-qualification/re-validation should be performed.

Changes/improvements to validation procedures should also be risk assessed to determine if re-validation is required.

D.6.2 Change control — Specific controls should be implemented to cover changes during verification/qualification/validation. Formal, documented change control should be implemented after release of the equipment/process/product and kept throughout the lifetime of the equipment/process/product.

D.6.3 Follow-up — Action should be taken to complete any outstanding actions, e.g. deficiencies following verification/qualification/validation. All actions taken should be documented.

D.6.4 Preventive maintenance — After release of equipment a preventive maintenance plan (including calibration) should be established to guarantee equipment performance.

D.6.5 Re-qualification/re-validation — As long as the equipment/process operates in a state of control and no changes have been made to the equipment/process or output product, the equipment need not be re-qualified and process need not be re-validated.

Whether the equipment/process is operating in a state of control is determined by analysing day-to-day process control data and any product testing data for conformance with specifications and for variability.

When changes or deviations occur, the equipment/process should be reviewed and evaluated, and re-qualification/re-validation should be performed where appropriate. Periodic qualifications/validations can be required e.g. clean rooms, sterilization processes.

Commentary: Qualification and Validation Is Not a One-Time Activity

"State of Control" = Precondition for Not Needing Requalification

The position of ISO 15378 Annex D is consistent with ICH Q10: if equipment/processes run stably in a state of control, periodic requalification is not needed (except in special cases such as cleanrooms and sterilization processes).

How to judge the "state of control":

  • Routine process control data meet specifications
  • Product test data show no statistical trends
  • No significant deviations or complaints
  • No changes to equipment or process

Change Control = Gatekeeper of the Qualified State

Once qualification and validation are complete, the change control system becomes the core mechanism protecting the qualified state:

  • Any change to qualified equipment/processes must go through formal change control
  • The change risk assessment must evaluate the impact on the qualified state
  • Major changes (such as equipment overhaul or process parameter adjustment) may trigger requalification
  • Change control must run through the whole lifecycle of the equipment/process

Analogy: Regular Car Servicing

The preventive maintenance programme (D.6.4) is like regular car servicing: you do not wait for the car to break down before repairing it, but maintain it at regular intervals as the manufacturer recommends, so that the equipment always operates within its designed performance range. Calibration is part of this too: measuring instruments must be calibrated regularly, so that when the equipment says "pass", it really is a pass.

EU GMP Annex 15 is aimed mainly at medicinal product manufacturers (finished product plants).
