Introduction

Introduction

Cleanrooms and associated controlled environments provide for the control of contamination of air and, if appropriate, surfaces, to levels appropriate for accomplishing contamination-sensitive activities. Contamination control can be beneficial for protection of product or process integrity in applications in industries such as aerospace, microelectronics, pharmaceuticals, medical devices, healthcare and food.

This part of ISO 14644 specifies classes of air cleanliness in terms of the number of particles expressed as a concentration in air volume. It also specifies the standard method of testing to determine cleanliness class, including selection of sampling locations.

This edition is the result of a response to an ISO Systematic Review and includes changes in response to user and expert feedback validated by international enquiry. The nine ISO cleanliness classes are retained with minor revisions. Table 1 defines the particle concentration at various particle sizes for the nine integer classes.

The most significant change is the adoption of a more consistent statistical approach to the selection and the number of sampling locations; and the evaluation of the data collected. The statistical model is based on adaptation of the hypergeometric sampling model technique, where samples are drawn randomly without replacement from a finite population. The new approach allows each location to be treated independently with at least a 95% level of confidence that at least 90% of the cleanroom or clean zone areas will comply with the maximum particle concentration limit for the target class of air cleanliness.

The revised version of this part of ISO 14644 addresses the ≥ 5 µm particle limits for ISO Class 5 in the sterile products annexes of the EU, PIC/S and WHO GMPs by way of an adaptation of the macroparticle concept.

Commentary

Why do we need ISO 14644-1?

Imagine if every pharmaceutical company and every country defined "how clean is clean" in a different way: international compliance verification would be chaos. ISO 14644-1 was created to solve exactly this problem. It provides a common language so that cleanrooms anywhere in the world can be described by the same standard.

This standard applies to many industries, including aerospace, microelectronics, pharmaceuticals, medical devices and food, but in the pharmaceutical field it is closely linked to EU GMP Annex 1, the FDA aseptic processing guidance, PIC/S and other regulations.

2015 edition vs. 1999 edition: key changes

The most important changes in the 2015 second edition (the current version):

  • New statistical approach: The 1999 assumption that particle counts follow a normal distribution is dropped. A hypergeometric sampling model is adopted instead, and each sampling location is evaluated independently
  • Explicit confidence level: A 95% confidence level plus 90% area compliance replaces the inconsistent application of the 95% UCL in the 1999 edition
  • Change in the number of sampling locations: Table A.1 was redesigned and the minimum number of sampling locations was adjusted
  • ≥5 µm particles: Through the M descriptor concept, the standard explicitly addresses the ≥5 µm limit for EU GMP Grade A

Analogy: ISO Classes are like hotel star ratings

Just as five-star and three-star hotels each have clear service standards, ISO Class 1 (cleanest) to ISO Class 9 (least stringent) each have a clear particle concentration standard. The lower the number, the cleaner the air and the stricter the requirement, just as a five-star hotel is held to a higher standard than a three-star hotel.

Section 1 — Scope

1 Scope

This part of ISO 14644 specifies the classification of air cleanliness in terms of concentration of airborne particles in cleanrooms and clean zones; and separative devices as defined in ISO 14644-7.

Only particle populations having cumulative distributions based on threshold (lower limit) particle sizes ranging from 0.1 µm to 5 µm are considered for classification purposes.

The use of light scattering (discrete) airborne particle counters (LSAPC) is the basis for determination of the concentration of airborne particles, equal to and greater than the specified sizes, at designated sampling locations.

This part of ISO 14644 does not provide for classification of particle populations that are outside the specified lower threshold particle-size range, 0.1 µm to 5 µm. Concentrations of ultrafine particles (particles smaller than 0.1 µm) will be addressed in a separate standard to specify air cleanliness by nano-scale particles. An M descriptor (see Annex C) may be used to quantify populations of macroparticles (particles larger than 5 µm).

This part of ISO 14644 cannot be used to characterize the physical, chemical, radiological, viable or other nature of airborne particles.

2 Normative References

  • ISO 14644-2:2015 — Monitoring of air cleanliness by particle concentration
  • ISO 14644-7 — Separative devices (clean air hoods, gloveboxes, isolators and mini-environments)

Key Points Commentary

Three elements of scope

The boundary of this standard is very clear. You can decide whether it applies by asking three questions:

  1. Location: Is it a cleanroom, a clean zone, or a separative device? → Yes
  2. Particle size range: Are the particles between 0.1 µm and 5 µm? → The standard applies; <0.1 µm or >5 µm → other methods are used respectively
  3. Measurement method: Is a LSAPC (light scattering airborne particle counter) used? → Yes

Analogy: an LSAPC is like an ultra-precise "particle camera"

How an LSAPC works: air passes through a laser beam, and each time a particle passes through it scatters a light signal. The particle size and number are calculated from the intensity of the scattered light. Just as a traffic camera records the size of every car, an LSAPC records the size of every "particle vehicle".

CDMO practical scenario

During cleanroom qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) at a CDMO facility, the first compliance check is to confirm that the particle counter used is an LSAPC with a valid calibration certificate (per ISO 21501-4). All classification reports must clearly state that they are based on ISO 14644-1:2015 (note the year; it is not the 1999 edition).

Section 3 — Terms and Definitions

3.1 General Terms

3.1.1 cleanroom: Room within which the number concentration of airborne particles is controlled and classified, and which is designed, constructed and operated in a manner to control the introduction, generation and retention of particles inside the room.

Note 1: The class of airborne particle concentration is specified.
Note 2: Levels of other cleanliness attributes such as chemical, viable or nanoscale concentrations in the air, and also surface cleanliness might also be specified and controlled.
Note 3: Other relevant physical parameters might also be controlled as required, e.g. temperature, humidity, pressure, vibration and electrostatic.

3.1.2 clean zone: Defined space within which the number concentration of airborne particles is controlled and classified, and which is constructed and operated in a manner to control the introduction, generation and retention of contaminants inside the space.

Note 3: A clean zone(s) can be a defined space within a cleanroom or might be achieved by a separative device.

3.1.3 installation: Cleanroom or one or more clean zones, together with all associated structures, air-treatment systems, services and utilities.

3.1.4 classification: Method of assessing level of cleanliness against a specification for a cleanroom or clean zone.

3.2 Airborne Particle Terms

3.2.1 particle: Minute piece of matter with defined physical boundaries.

3.2.2 particle size: Diameter of a sphere that produces a response, by a given particle-sizing instrument, that is equivalent to the response produced by the particle being measured. Note: For discrete-particle light-scattering instruments, the equivalent optical diameter is used.

3.2.3 particle concentration: Number of individual particles per unit volume of air.

3.2.4 particle size distribution: Cumulative distribution of particle concentration as a function of particle size.

3.2.5 macroparticle: Particle with an equivalent diameter greater than 5 µm.

3.2.6 M descriptor: Designation for measured or specified concentration of macroparticles per cubic metre of air, expressed in terms of the equivalent diameter that is characteristic of the measurement method used. Note: M descriptors cannot be used to define ISO Classes, but the M descriptor may be quoted independently or in conjunction with ISO Classes.

3.2.7 unidirectional airflow: Controlled airflow through the entire cross-section of a cleanroom or a clean zone with a steady velocity and airstreams that are considered to be parallel.

3.2.8 non-unidirectional airflow: Air distribution where the supply air entering the cleanroom or clean zone mixes with the internal air by means of induction.

3.3 Occupancy States

3.3.1 as-built: Condition where the cleanroom or clean zone is complete with all services connected and functioning but with no equipment, furniture, materials or personnel present.

3.3.2 at-rest: Condition where the cleanroom or clean zone is complete with equipment installed and operating in a manner agreed upon, but with no personnel present.

3.3.3 operational: Agreed condition where the cleanroom or clean zone is functioning in the specified manner, with equipment operating and with the specified number of personnel present.

3.4–3.5 Instrument Terms

3.5.1 LSAPC: Light scattering discrete airborne particle counter — instrument capable of counting and sizing single airborne particles and reporting size data in terms of equivalent optical diameter. Note: Specifications given in ISO 21501-4:2007.

3.5.2 discrete-macroparticle counter: Instrument capable of counting and sizing single airborne macroparticles.

3.5.3 time-of-flight particle sizing apparatus: Discrete-particle counting and sizing apparatus that defines the aerodynamic diameter of particles by measuring the time for a particle to accommodate to a change in air velocity.

In-Depth Commentary on the Terms

Cleanroom vs. clean zone: what is the difference?

A cleanroom is a separate room with physical walls and an air-handling system.

A clean zone has a broader scope. It can be:

  • A specific area within a cleanroom (for example, the Grade A zone around a filling line)
  • The protected space created by an isolator or RABS
  • The interior of a laminar flow workstation

In the aseptic manufacturing environment of EU GMP Annex 1, Grade A usually refers to a "clean zone" rather than the whole room.

Analogy: the three states are like three situations in a restaurant

As-built = the restaurant has just been fitted out: no tables or chairs yet, the kitchen equipment is not installed, and only the HVAC is running.

At-rest = the restaurant is set up, the tables and chairs are in place and the kitchen equipment is fully on, but it has not opened yet and no chefs or waiters have come in.

Operational = the restaurant is open for business, the chefs are cooking in the kitchen and the waiters are moving between tables. This is the real production environment!

Compliance requirements for LSAPC calibration

The LSAPC (light scattering airborne particle counter) is the only reference measurement instrument recognized by this standard. Its calibration requirements are:

  • Calibration must be performed per ISO 21501-4:2007
  • A valid calibration certificate is required
  • A zero count check must be performed before each test

LSAPC calibration records are a common inspection item in FDA inspections and EU GMP reviews.

Section 4 — Classification of Air Cleanliness

4 Classification

4.1 Occupancy State(s): The air cleanliness class by particle concentration of air in a cleanroom or clean zone shall be defined in one or more of three occupancy states, viz. "as-built," "at-rest" or "operational" (see 3.3).

4.2 Particle Size(s): One, or more than one, threshold (lower limit) particle sizes situated within the range from ≥0.1 µm to ≥5 µm are to be used to determine air cleanliness particle concentration for classification.

4.3 ISO Class Number: Air cleanliness class by particle concentration shall be designated by an ISO Class number, N. The maximum permitted concentration of particles for each considered particle size is determined from Table 1.

Particle number concentrations for different threshold sizes in Table 1 do not reflect actual particle size and number distribution in the air and serve as criteria for classification only.

Core Concepts of Classification

Classification Formula

All values in Table 1 come from the following formula (Formula E.1):

Cn = 10^N × (K/D)^2.08

where:
Cn = maximum permitted concentration (particles/m³), rounded to three significant figures
N  = ISO class number (1 to 9)
D  = the particle size considered (µm)
K  = 0.1 µm (constant)

Worked example: ISO Class 5, D = 0.5 µm

Cn = 10^5 × (0.1/0.5)^2.08
   = 100,000 × (0.2)^2.08
   = 100,000 × 0.03520
   = 3,520 particles/m³  ✓ (agrees with Table 1)

The physical meaning of this formula: the larger the particle, the lower the permitted concentration in the air (because larger particles are harder to filter out and pose a more direct contamination risk to the product).

How to correctly "designate" an ISO Class

Per 4.4 Designation, a complete ISO class designation must include three elements:

  1. ISO Class number: for example "ISO Class 5"
  2. Occupancy state: at-rest, operational or as-built
  3. Particle size(s) considered: for example ≥0.5 µm

Complete example: "ISO Class 5; at-rest; ≥0.5 µm"

If several particle sizes are measured at the same time, the larger size must be at least 1.5 times the smaller one: D2 ≥ 1.5 × D1. For example, 0.3 µm and 0.5 µm can be selected (0.5/0.3 = 1.67, which complies), but 0.3 µm and 0.4 µm cannot (0.4/0.3 = 1.33, which does not comply).

Section 5 — Demonstration of Compliance

5 Demonstration of Compliance

5.1 Principle

Compliance with air cleanliness (ISO Class) requirements specified by the customer is verified by performing specified testing procedures and by providing documentation of the results and conditions of testing.

At-rest or operational classification may be performed periodically based upon risk assessment of the application, typically on an annual basis.

For monitoring cleanrooms, clean zones and separative devices, ISO 14644-2:2015 shall be used.

NOTE: Where the installation is equipped with instrumentation for continuous or frequent monitoring, the time intervals between classification may be extended provided that the results of the monitoring remain within the specified limits.

5.2 Testing

The reference test method for demonstrating compliance is given in Annex A (normative). Alternative methods or instrumentation, having at least comparable performance, may be specified. If no alternative is specified or agreed upon, the reference method shall be used.

Tests performed to demonstrate compliance shall be conducted using instruments which are in compliance with calibration requirements at the time of testing.

5.3 Airborne Particle Concentration Evaluation

Upon completion of testing in accordance with Annex A, the concentration of particles (expressed as number of particles per cubic metre) in a single sample volume at each sampling location shall not exceed the concentration limit(s) given in Table 1 or Table E.1 for intermediate decimal classes for the considered size(s).

If multiple single sample volumes are taken at a sampling location, the concentrations shall be averaged and the average concentration must not exceed the concentration limits.

Particle concentrations used for determination of compliance with ISO Classes shall be measured by the same method for all considered particle sizes.

5.4 Test Report

The results from testing each cleanroom or clean zone shall be recorded and submitted as a comprehensive report, along with a statement of compliance or non-compliance with the specified designation of air cleanliness class by particle concentration.

The test report shall include:

  1. the name and address of the testing organization, and the date on which the test was performed,
  2. the number and year of publication of this part of ISO 14644, i.e. ISO 14644-1:2015,
  3. a clear identification of the physical location of the cleanroom or clean zone tested (including reference to adjacent areas if necessary), and specific designations for coordinates of all sampling locations,
  4. the specified designation criteria, including the ISO Class number, the relevant occupancy state(s), and the considered particle size(s),
  5. details of the test method used, with any special conditions, departures from the test method, and identification of the test instrument and its current calibration certificate, and
  6. the test results, including particle concentration data for all sampling locations.

Commentary on Demonstrating Compliance

Classification vs. monitoring: two different activities

Many people confuse "classification" with "monitoring":

  • Classification: performed per ISO 14644-1, usually once a year, using the reference method specified in Annex A. It is the formal act of "confirming the cleanroom class".
  • Monitoring: performed per ISO 14644-2. It is a routine or periodic check that "maintains cleanliness", at a higher frequency (it can be continuous, per shift, per batch, etc.) and does not necessarily require the full Annex A procedure.

Classification is "obtaining the qualification"; monitoring is "maintaining the qualification". Both are indispensable.

5.3 The core of the evaluation criterion: every sampling location must pass

This is a very important compliance principle:

  • The (average) concentration at each sampling location must not exceed the Table 1 limit
  • If several samples are taken at the same sampling location, take the average, and the average must still not exceed the limit
  • You must not judge compliance using the "average of all sampling locations" (each location must be evaluated individually)

In other words: if 1 of 10 sampling locations exceeds the limit, the whole cleanroom is judged as not meeting that ISO Class, even if the average concentration at the other 9 locations is far below the limit.

This is completely different from the 95% UCL approach used in the 1999 edition and is one of the most substantive changes in the 2015 edition.

Six elements of the test report (5.4)

In an FDA inspection or EMA GMP review, the test report is the first document examined. Missing any one item can lead to an observation:

  1. Name, address and date of the testing organization
  2. Reference to the standard: clearly state "ISO 14644-1:2015" (the year must not be omitted)
  3. Location of the cleanroom and a map with the coordinates of all sampling locations
  4. Designation: ISO Class N + occupancy state + particle size(s)
  5. Test method, any special conditions, instrument identification and calibration certificate number
  6. Particle concentration data for all sampling locations

CDMO practice: annual classification plan

A typical CDMO annual classification plan includes:

  • Timing: usually performed when the facility restarts after the annual maintenance shutdown, so that it can be completed with minimal disruption to the production schedule
  • Document preparation: the SOP should predefine the sampling plan, including a floor plan with grid showing the sampling locations
  • Dual qualification: if the same cleanroom is declared for the products of several clients, results may need to be recorded separately under different occupancy states
  • Deviation management: any location exceeding the limit must immediately trigger a deviation investigation, and the root cause and corrective actions must be recorded in the report

Annex A (Normative) — Reference Method for Classification

Annex A (normative) — Reference Method

A.1 Principle

A discrete-particle-counting instrument is used to determine the concentration of airborne particles, equal to and greater than the specified sizes, at designated sampling locations.

A.2 Apparatus Requirements

A.2.1: The instrument shall have a means of displaying or recording the count and size of discrete particles in air with a size discrimination capability to detect the total particle concentration in the appropriate particle size ranges for the class under consideration.

A.2.2 Calibration: The particle counter shall have a valid calibration certificate; the frequency and method of calibration should be based upon current accepted practice as specified in ISO 21501-4.

A.3 Preparation for Particle Count Testing

Prior to testing, verify that all relevant aspects of the cleanroom or clean zone that contribute to its integrity are complete and functioning in accordance with its performance specification.

A.4 Establishment of Sampling Locations

A.4.1 Deriving the Number of Sampling Locations: Derive the minimum number of sampling locations, NL, from Table A.1. Table A.1 provides the number of sampling locations related to the area of each cleanroom or clean zone to be classified and provides at least 95% confidence that at least 90% of the cleanroom or clean zone area does not exceed the class limits.

Annex A Commentary

The status of Annex A: why is it "Normative"?

ISO standards have two kinds of annexes:

  • Normative: must be followed and carries the same weight as the main text
  • Informative: provides guidance but is not mandatory

Annex A is "Normative", which means that the classification test must follow the Annex A procedure strictly, unless both parties agree to use an alternative method (and the alternative performs no worse than Annex A). In FDA and EMA reviews, use of a non-normative alternative method must be backed by sound scientific justification.

95% confidence level + 90% area compliance: what does it mean?

This is the core statistical concept of the 2015 edition and is more rigorous than the 1999 edition:

  • 95% confidence level: the reliability of the test result. If the test were repeated many times, the conclusion would be correct 95% of the time
  • 90% area compliance: at least 90% of the area of the whole cleanroom meets the class requirement

This statistical model is based on the hypergeometric distribution and does not assume that particles are evenly distributed in space, which is closer to real conditions.

Minimum sample volume formula (A.2)

Vs = (20 / C_n,m) × 1000

where:
Vs    = minimum single sample volume per sampling location (liters)
C_n,m = class limit for the largest considered particle size (particles/m³)
20    = number of particles expected to be counted if the concentration is exactly at the class limit

Example: ISO Class 5, ≥0.5 µm (limit 3,520 particles/m³)
Vs = (20 / 3,520) × 1000
   = 0.005682 × 1000
   = 5.68 liters

Minimum sample volume: max(Vs, 2 liters); minimum sampling time: 1 minute

If Vs is very large (for example, sampling at 0.1 µm for ISO Class 3), the sequential sampling procedure in Annex D can be used instead to save time.

Four steps for locating sampling points (A.4.2)

  1. Determine the minimum number of sampling locations NL from Table A.1
  2. Divide the cleanroom into NL grid cells of equal area
  3. Select a representative position within each cell (considering layout, equipment positions and airflow)
  4. Place the sampling probe at the working activity level or at another specified position

In non-unidirectional airflow environments, sampling locations should not be placed directly under an undiffused air supply outlet.

Note 1: If area falls between two values, select the greater number of locations.
Note 2: For unidirectional airflow, area = cross-section of moving air; for all other cases, area = horizontal plan area.

A.5 Sampling Procedure

A.5.1 Set up the particle counter in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions including performing a zero count check.

A.5.2 The sampling probe shall be positioned pointing into the airflow. If the direction of the airflow being sampled is not controlled or predictable (e.g. non-unidirectional airflow), the inlet of the sampling probe shall be directed vertically upward.

A.5.3 Ensure normal conditions for the selected occupancy state are established before sampling.

A.5.4 Sample the volume of air determined in A.4.4, as a minimum, for each sample at each sampling location.

A.5.5 If an out-of-specification count is found at a location due to an identified abnormal occurrence, then that count can be discarded and noted as such on the test report and a new sample taken.

A.5.6 If an out-of-specification count found at a location is attributed to a technical failure of the cleanroom or equipment, then the cause should be identified, remedial action taken and retesting performed of the failed sampling location, the immediate surrounding locations and any other locations affected.

A.6 Processing of Results

A.6.1.1 Average concentration at each sampling location (when ≥2 samples taken):

x̄i = (xi,1 + xi,2 + ... + xi,n) / n

A.6.1.2 Concentration per cubic metre:

Ci = (x̄i / Vt) × 1000

where Vt is the selected single sample volume in litres.

A.6.2.1 Classification requirements: The cleanroom or clean zone is deemed to have met the specified air cleanliness classification requirements if the average of the particle concentrations measured at each of the sampling locations does not exceed the concentration limits determined from Table 1.

A.6.2.2 Out-of-specification result: In the event of an out-of-specification count, an investigation shall be undertaken. The result of the investigation and remedial action shall be noted in the test report.

Key Points of the Sampling Procedure

Zero count check: why is it important?

Before each sampling, a zero count check must be performed: the instrument draws clean HEPA-filtered air, and the reading is confirmed to be close to zero. This step verifies that the instrument itself does not generate false particle counts (such as electronic noise from the optical system or residue in the tubing). If the zero count check fails, all subsequent measurement data are invalid.

Handling two kinds of OOS (out-of-specification) results

Case A: an identified abnormal event (A.5.5)

  • For example: someone sneezed during sampling, or a door was opened accidentally
  • Permitted: discard the data, record it in the report, and resample
  • Condition: the cause of the abnormal event must be clearly documented; unfavorable data must not be discarded "selectively"

Case B: equipment or cleanroom failure (A.5.6)

  • For example: an HVAC system malfunction or a damaged HEPA filter
  • Required: identify the root cause, take corrective action, and retest the failing location and the surrounding locations
  • Prohibited: continuing to use the cleanroom without taking corrective action

Detailed explanation of the concentration calculation formula (A.6.1.2)

C_i = (x̄_i / V_t) × 1000

Example (Annex B Example 1, sampling location 1):
  Count: 245 particles (in 28.3 liters)
  Vt = 28.3 liters
  C_i = (245 / 28.3) × 1000
      = 8.658 × 1000
      = 8,649 particles/m³ (≈ 8,649, which is 8,649 in the Table)

Conversion:
  1 m³ = 1,000 liters
  Therefore: particles/m³ = (count / sample volume in L) × 1000

Or equivalently:
  multiplier = 1000/28.3 ≈ 35.3
  Therefore: 8,649 = 245 × 35.3

This multiplier of 35.3 (1000/28.3) appears repeatedly in the Annex B example tables, so remember its physical meaning.

Commentary on the Calculation Examples

Mastering the calculation flow of Example B.1

The Annex B examples are the references most often used in exams and in practice. Taking B.1 as an example, the flow is:

  1. Look up Table A.1: 18 m² → NL = 6 sampling locations
  2. Confirm the limit: ISO Class 5, ≥0.5 µm → 3,520 particles/m³
  3. Calculate Vs: (20/3,520) × 1,000 = 5.68 L
  4. Decide the actual sample volume: instrument flow rate 28.3 L/min × 1 min = 28.3 L (≥ Vs and ≥ 2 L, so it meets the requirement)
  5. Calculate the concentration: count × (1,000/28.3) = count × 35.3
  6. Compare location by location: the concentration at each location must not exceed the limit

The key lesson of Example 4: an average cannot mask a location that exceeds the limit

In Example 4, the average concentration at sampling location 4 (4,165 particles/m³) exceeded the ISO Class 5 limit (3,520 particles/m³), even though the other 9 locations all passed. The result is that the whole cleanroom does not meet the requirement.

Even more noteworthy is sampling location 9: the three samples were 54, 159 and 78 particles. The second, 159 × 35.3 = 5,612 particles/m³, clearly exceeds the limit. But the average of the three, 97 × 35.3 = 3,424 particles/m³, is below the limit, so the location passes.

Conclusion: the judgement is based on the "average at the sampling location", not on "each individual sample". This is contrary to some people's intuition.

Verifying the intermediate class calculation (Example 5)

ISO Class 7.5, D = 0.5 µm

Cn = 10^7.5 × (0.1/0.5)^2.08
   = 31,622,777 × (0.2)^2.08

Calculation of (0.2)^2.08:
  = exp(2.08 × ln(0.2))
  = exp(2.08 × (-1.6094))
  = exp(-3.3476)
  = 0.03517

Cn = 31,622,777 × 0.03517
   = 1,112,096
   ≈ 1,110,000 particles/m³ (three significant figures)

The 2,100 m² cleanroom in Example 6 needs 57 sampling locations. Imagine dividing this cleanroom into 57 grid cells of equal area (each about 36.8 m², roughly a 6 m × 6 m square) and selecting one representative position in each cell for sampling. This is the intuitive meaning of "systematic sampling".

When one cleanroom at a CDMO facility serves several clients at the same time, the classification report must reflect the needs of the most demanding client. If client A requires ISO Class 5 operational and client B requires ISO Class 7 at-rest, the whole cleanroom must pass classification in both states (ISO Class 5 operational and ISO Class 7 at-rest), and the documentation must record them separately.

Annex C — Macroparticle Descriptor (M Descriptor)

Annex C — Counting and Sizing of Airborne Macroparticles

C.1 Principle

In some situations, typically those related to specific process requirements, alternative levels of air cleanliness may be specified on the basis of particle populations that are not within the size range applicable to classification. The maximum permitted concentration of such particles and the choice of test method to verify compliance are matters for agreement between the customer and the supplier.

C.2 M Descriptor Format

The M descriptor is expressed in the format:

"ISO M (a; b); c"

  • a = maximum permitted concentration of macroparticles (macroparticles/m³)
  • b = equivalent diameter (µm) associated with the specified method
  • c = specified measurement method

Example 1: "ISO M (29; ≥5 µm); LSAPC" — 29 particles/m³ at ≥5 µm, measured by LSAPC.

Example 2: "ISO M (2,500; ≥10 µm); time-of-flight aerosol particle counter"

Example 3: "ISO M (1,000; 10 to 20 µm); cascade impactor followed by microscopic sizing and counting"

Note: If the population contains fibres, supplement with "Mfibre (a; b); c".

C.3 Macroparticle Measurement Methods

In situ measurement (C.4.1):

  • LSAPC — reports equivalent optical diameter
  • Time-of-flight apparatus — reports aerodynamic diameter

Collection methods (C.4.2):

  • Filter collection + microscopic measurement
  • Cascade impactor collection + microscopic measurement

C.7 ISO Class 5 and ≥5 µm Particles

In order to express an airborne concentration of 29 particles/m³ in the particle size range ≥5 µm based on the use of an LSAPC, the designation would be "ISO M (29; ≥5 µm); LSAPC" and for 20 particle/m³ the designation would be "ISO M (20; ≥5 µm); LSAPC" (see Table 1, Note f).

M Descriptor Commentary

Why do ≥5 µm particles need an M descriptor?

Recall footnote e of Table 1:

"Sample collection limitations for both particles in low concentrations and sizes greater than 1 µm make classification inappropriate, due to potential particle losses in the sampling system."

Macroparticles (>1 µm, especially >5 µm) tend to deposit in the sampling tubing through gravity and inertia (impacting the tubing wall), so the number of particles actually reaching the sensor is too low and the measurement is unreliable.

For this reason, ISO 14644-1 does not include particles ≥5 µm in the formal ISO Class designation, and addresses them separately with the "M descriptor".

Annex D — Sequential Sampling Procedure

Annex D — Sequential Sampling

D.1 Background and Limitations

Sequential sampling is useful when classifying a clean controlled environment with a very low particle concentration at the class limit. It allows reduction of the sample volume and sampling time by measuring the rate of counting and predicting the likelihood of passing or failing.

Sequential sampling is most appropriate for air cleanliness of ISO Class 4 or cleaner. It may also be used for other classes when the limit for the chosen particle size is low.

Limitations:

  • Only applicable when expected counts from a single sample are <20
  • Each sample measurement requires supplementary monitoring and data analysis
  • Particle concentrations are not determined as precisely as with conventional sampling

D.2 Basis for the Procedure

Reference values are derived from formulae for upper- and lower-limit boundaries:

Upper limit (FAIL): Cfail = 3.96 + 1.03E    (D.1)

Lower limit (PASS): Cpass = −3.96 + 1.03E    (D.2)

where E = expected count = Q × t × Cn,m / 1,000    (D.5)

D.3 Procedure

  1. Record the total number of particles counted as a function of time
  2. Calculate the expected count E (Formula D.5)
  3. Plot the total count versus E (Figure D.1)
  4. Compare with upper and lower limit lines
  5. If cumulative count crosses upper line → FAIL
  6. If cumulative count crosses lower line → PASS
  7. If between the lines → continue sampling until E reaches 20 or count reaches 20

If the total count is 20 or fewer at the end of the prescribed sampling period and has not crossed the upper line, the air is judged to have complied with the class limit.

Sequential Sampling Commentary

Analogy: sequential sampling is like an "interim vote count"

Imagine an election count: if one candidate is far ahead after 30% of the votes are counted, you can predict the result with confidence without waiting for 100% of the votes. Sequential sampling follows a similar logic:

  • Very clean air → the particle count grows very slowly and quickly reaches the "pass line", so sampling can stop early
  • Clearly contaminated air → the particle count rises quickly and soon reaches the "fail line", so a failure can be declared early
  • Air close to the limit → sampling must continue until the full sample volume is reached

Sequential sampling formulas explained

Pass/fail boundaries (vertical axis: cumulative observed count C; horizontal axis: expected count E):

Upper limit (FAIL): C_fail = 3.96 + 1.03 × E
Lower limit (PASS): C_pass = −3.96 + 1.03 × E

Expected count:
E = Q × t × C_n,m / 1000

where:
Q   = sampling flow rate (liters/second)
t   = sampling time (seconds)
C_n,m = ISO Class limit (particles/m³)

Example (D.4.1, ISO Class 3, 0.1 µm, 1,000 particles/m³):
Q = 28.3 L/min = 0.472 L/s
t = 5 s → E = 0.472 × 5 × 1000 / 1000 = 2.4

Upper limit: C_fail = 3.96 + 1.03 × 2.4 = 3.96 + 2.47 = 6.43 → 7 (rounded up)
Lower limit: C_pass = −3.96 + 1.03 × 2.4 = −3.96 + 2.47 = −1.49 → N.A. (negative)

When should sequential sampling be used?

Sequential sampling is particularly useful when the conventional sample volume is too large (the sampling time is too long):

For ISO Class 3 at ≥0.5 µm, or stricter classes, sequential sampling can greatly shorten the validation time. In actual testing, if the air is much cleaner than the limit, a pass may be determined in only a few minutes.

Commentary on Intermediate Classes

When are intermediate classes needed?

The nine whole ISO Classes (1–9) cover most applications. In some special cases, however, a customer or regulation may require a cleanliness level between two whole classes, and intermediate classes can then be used.

Common cases:

  • Some semiconductor processes need conditions stricter than ISO Class 7 (352,000 particles/m³) but do not require ISO Class 6 (35,200 particles/m³)
  • Annex B Example 5 uses ISO Class 7.5 (limit 1,110,000 particles/m³ at ≥0.5 µm) as its example

Note: the increment must not be smaller than 0.5 (for example, ISO Class 7.3 is not permitted), because measurement uncertainty in particle counting makes smaller increments meaningless.

Complete worked example of Formula E.1

Problem: calculate the ISO Class 5 limit at ≥0.4 µm (not a standard Table 1 particle size)

Cn = 10^5 × (0.1/0.4)^2.08
   = 100,000 × (0.25)^2.08

(0.25)^2.08:
  = exp(2.08 × ln(0.25))
  = exp(2.08 × (-1.3863))
  = exp(-2.8834)
  = 0.05612

Cn = 100,000 × 0.05612
   = 5,612 particles/m³
   ≈ 5,610 particles/m³ (three significant figures)

Note: D2(0.4)/D1(0.3) = 1.33 < 1.5, so 0.3 and 0.4 µm
cannot be selected together as considered particle sizes (this violates the requirement in clause 4.4).
But if only the single particle size ≥0.4 µm is considered, calculating with this formula is legitimate.

Practical significance of intermediate classes in the pharmaceutical field

In the correspondence between EU GMP Annex 1 and ISO 14644-1, pharmaceutical facilities normally use only three whole classes, ISO Class 5, 7 and 8. Intermediate classes are not common in pharmaceutical environments.

Still, it is important to understand the principle behind the formula. It shows that all the values in Table 1 come from the same mathematical formula, and understanding the formula is more valuable than memorizing the values.

Commentary on Instrument Specifications

The core standard for LSAPC: ISO 21501-4:2007

The detailed technical specifications of an LSAPC (sensor sensitivity, counting efficiency, flow rate accuracy, sampling rate, etc.) are specified in ISO 21501-4:2007. ISO 14644-1 cites this standard directly, which means:

  • The instrument must meet all the technical requirements of ISO 21501-4
  • Calibration must be performed using the methods of ISO 21501-4
  • The calibration certificate must clearly state that it is based on this standard

In GMP inspections of pharmaceutical facilities, inspectors often ask to see the LSAPC calibration certificate and confirm that it includes the relevant ISO 21501-4 test items.

Why is Annex F "Informative" rather than "Normative"?

Instrument technology advances quickly, and today's best LSAPC may outperform the 2015 specification. Making Annex F normative could restrict the use of better-performing new instruments.

Annex F therefore gives only a "reference for minimum requirements" and allows users to choose instruments with better performance (subject to agreement between customer and supplier). This reflects the flexible approach of ISO standards.

CDMO instrument management practice

Key points in how a typical CDMO facility manages its LSAPCs:

  • Calibration frequency: usually every 12 months, per ISO 21501-4
  • Pre-use check: perform a zero count check before each classification test
  • Flow rate verification: periodically verify that the actual instrument flow rate meets the specification (for example, 28.3 L/min ± 5%)
  • Tubing length limit: the longer the sampling tubing, the more macroparticles are lost; for particles >1 µm, the tubing generally should not exceed the length recommended by the manufacturer (usually ≤ 1 m)
  • Calibration traceability: calibration must be traceable to a national metrology standard (NIST or equivalent)

  • Core of ISO 14644-1:2015: Based on particle concentration, it defines nine classes, ISO Class 1–9, uses an LSAPC to measure particles in the 0.1–5 µm range, and uses the M descriptor to supplement for particles >5 µm.

  • Three occupancy states: as-built (no personnel, no equipment) → at-rest (equipment, no personnel) → operational (personnel and equipment, in production); pharmaceutical validation must record the relevant states separately.

  • M descriptor: the format is "ISO M (a; b); c" and is used for macroparticles >5 µm; ISO Class 5 is paired with the EU GMP Grade A/B ≥5 µm limit (20 or 29 particles/m³).
  • Sequential sampling (Annex D): suitable for low-concentration environments of ISO Class 4 or stricter; the count rate is used to predict pass/fail in real time, saving sampling time; boundary formulas: Cfail = 3.96 + 1.03E, Cpass = −3.96 + 1.03E.
  • Key differences between the 2015 and 1999 editions: the statistical model changed from an assumed normal distribution to a hypergeometric sampling model; the confidence level is explicitly 95% × 90%; the 95% UCL was removed; the number of sampling locations was updated; the M descriptor was formally incorporated into the standard.

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Pages 1-2 | First Edition

Introduction

Industries and organizations of all kinds utilize cleanrooms. Operational procedures have a profound effect on the cleanliness levels achieved during the operation of the cleanroom and equipment. Consistent quality is cleanliness dependent. Operational cleanliness can only be attained and maintained through a deliberate programme established to specify, measure and enforce defined operational procedures.

Regulatory agencies that have authority over processes and products produced in the cleanroom may require additional procedures and measures of cleanliness not covered in this general operating standard.

This part of ISO 14644 addresses normative and informative operational requirements related to:

  1. providing a system that defines policies and operational procedures;
  2. clothing used to isolate human-generated contamination from the cleanroom environment;
  3. training of personnel inside the cleanroom and monitoring their compliance to specified procedures and disciplines;
  4. transfer, installation and maintenance of stationary equipment;
  5. selection and use of materials and portable equipment in the cleanroom;
  6. maintaining the cleanliness of the cleanroom through systematic cleaning and monitoring procedures.

Section 1 — Scope

This part of ISO 14644 specifies basic requirements for cleanroom operations. It is intended for those planning to use and operate a cleanroom. Aspects of safety that have no direct bearing on contamination control are not considered. This document considers all classes of cleanrooms used to produce all types of products.

Methods and programmes for routine monitoring within cleanrooms are not covered in detail in this part but reference is made to ISO 14644-2 and ISO 14644-3 for monitoring particles and ISO 14698-1 and ISO 14698-2 for monitoring micro-organisms.

Analysis and Regulatory Links

Core Concept: Why Do Operating Procedures Determine Cleanroom Quality?

The core logic of ISO 14644-5: however perfect the cleanroom design, cleanliness will still collapse if operating procedures are inadequate. Personnel, equipment, materials, and cleaning are the four dynamic factors that are the main sources of contamination in a cleanroom in the "operational state".

  • At-rest ≠ operational: in a typical cleanroom, particle counts at rest are far below the limits, but once people enter, particle counts can rise 10-100 times
  • Regulators (such as FDA and EMA) require more than the minimum requirements of ISO 14644-5, particularly in sterile pharmaceutical environments
  • This standard is a "general operating standard": it is not specific to any industry and is the foundational framework for all cleanrooms

Analogy: a cleanroom is like an operating room

However advanced a hospital operating room is (negative pressure, HEPA, stainless steel walls), surgical infection rates will still soar if surgeons do not scrub, nurses enter in street clothes, or equipment brings in bacteria. ISO 14644-5 is the "operating room code of practice": it specifies every action concerning people, clothing, materials, and cleaning.

Regulatory Focus: ISO 14644-5 vs. EU GMP Annex 1

ISO 14644-5 is a general standard, while EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) is the mandatory requirement for sterile pharmaceutical manufacturing. The relationship between the two:

  • EU GMP Annex 1 Section 4 "Personnel" corresponds directly to Section 4.3 of this standard

  • EU GMP Annex 1 requirements for Grade A are far stricter than the basic requirements of ISO 14644-5
  • Section IV (Personnel) of the FDA Aseptic Processing Guide (2004) corresponds closely to this standard

Terminology Analysis: Commentary

The Three Occupancy States: Core Concepts for Monitoring and Qualification

ISO 14644-1 defines three states, each with different permissible particle concentrations:

  • As-built: no equipment, no personnel; the cleanest state, used for acceptance testing
  • At-rest: equipment installed and running, no operating personnel; the EU GMP at-rest standard
  • Operational: normal production with personnel working; the EU GMP in-operation standard (the strictest)

EU GMP Annex 1 Grade A requirement: the in-operation limit for particles ≥ 0.5 µm is 3,520 /m³ (equivalent to ISO Class 5 at-rest and operational)

Analogy: the three states are like three situations in a restaurant

"As-built" = just finished fitting out, no tables or chairs placed, the cleanest; "at-rest" = tables and chairs in place but not yet open to customers; "operational" = the restaurant is open for business, with cooks and waiters all in place. The level of "mess" is completely different in each situation, and so is the meaning of cleanroom monitoring.

The Importance of the Cross-Over Bench

The cross-over bench is the core element of changing room design. Its function is to establish a physical boundary between the "clean side" and the "contaminated side":

  • Before the bench (contaminated side): remove street shoes or ordinary shoe covers
  • After the bench (clean side): put on cleanroom-dedicated shoes or shoe covers
  • Prevents contamination carried on the floor from following people into the clean side of the changing room
  • EU GMP Annex 1 requires that Grade B/A changing areas be designed with adequate segregation to prevent contamination transfer

Section 4.2 - Cleanroom Clothing

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Pages 4-5 | Normative Requirements

4.2 Cleanroom Clothing — Normative Requirements

4.2.1 Cleanroom clothing shall protect the environment and products from contamination generated by the personnel and their everyday clothing. To maximize this containment, the choice of barrier fabric, the clothing style and extent of coverage of personnel by the cleanroom clothing shall be established.

4.2.2 Cleanroom clothing shall be made of fabrics and materials that will resist breakdown (minimal linting) and therefore not shed contamination.

4.2.3 The frequency of changing into fresh cleanroom clothing before entering the cleanroom shall be determined in accordance with the product and process cleanliness requirements.

4.2.4 Reusable cleanroom clothing shall be processed at regular intervals to remove contamination.

4.2.5 The necessary cleaning, processing (including sterilization or disinfection where required) and packaging of clothing shall be defined.

4.2.6 Cleanroom clothing shall be transported and stored in a specified manner to minimize contamination.

4.2.7 Cleanroom clothing (clean packaged or dirty) shall not be removed beyond the confines of the storage area and cleanroom except for laundering, repair or exchange purposes.

4.2.8 Cleanroom clothing shall be put on and taken off in such a way that the spread of contamination is avoided or minimized.

4.2.9 If clothing is to be reused, it shall be removed and stored to ensure that contamination is minimized.

4.2.10 Cleanroom clothing shall be checked at regular intervals to ensure that it retains acceptable contamination control characteristics.

4.2.11 Consideration shall be given for the comfort of personnel wearing the cleanroom clothing.

4.2.12 Consideration shall be given to special (e.g. chemical, physical or microbiological) properties of the clothing that may be necessary for specific applications.

4.2.13 Consideration shall be given to special concerns for cleanroom clothing during and after emergency evacuations.

NOTE: Informative guidance concerning cleanroom clothing requirements listed in 4.2.1 to 4.2.13 can be found in Annex B.

Analysis and Regulatory Links

The Core Function of Cleanroom Clothing: A "Filter" for Human Contamination

The human body is the largest source of contamination in a cleanroom:

  • At rest: about 100,000 particles per minute (≥0.3 µm)
  • Normal walking: about 5 million particles per minute
  • Fast walking: up to 10 million particles per minute
  • Bacteria: hundreds of microorganism-carrying particles per minute (mainly from skin flakes)

The design goal of cleanroom clothing is to intercept this contamination so that it cannot enter the environment. The "barrier fabric" emphasized in 4.2.1 embodies this principle.

Analogy: cleanroom clothing is a full-body version of an N95 mask

An N95 mask filters particles exhaled from the nose and mouth; cleanroom clothing is a full-body "N95" that intercepts contaminants shed from skin, hair, and clothing fibers. The tighter the material (the higher the weave tightness), the better the filtration efficiency, but the lower the comfort (the comfort consideration in 4.2.11). This is a trade-off that always has to be balanced.

Regulatory Link: EU GMP Annex 1 Gowning Requirements

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) sets clear requirements for clothing in sterile manufacturing areas:

  • Grade A/B: coverall, hood, face mask, goggles, overboots, and two layers of powder-free gloves; the coverall must be sterilized
  • Grade C: hair cover, coverall or jacket + trousers, and appropriate shoe covers; sterilization is not required, but disinfection is
  • Grade D: basic cleanroom clothing, including a hair cover
  • FDA Aseptic Guide §IV.A: gowning for areas equivalent to Grade A/B requires "gown qualification" to confirm that each person's gowning procedure achieves the required microbial control

Garment Quality Verification Indicators

Key garment material tests (Annex B.3):
• Air permeability test
  → the lower the permeability, the higher the filtration efficiency, but the lower the comfort
• Particle retention test
• Pore size measurement
• Electrostatic properties
  → a mandatory test item for semiconductor plants
• Durability test
  → tests performance degradation after repeated laundering

Dispersal chamber (body box) test:
• The person wears the garment and moves inside a closed box
• The number of particles/bacteria shed is measured
• The filtration performance of different garments is compared

Section 4.3 - Personnel

4.3 Personnel — Normative Requirements

4.3.1 Personal and other items not intended for cleanroom use shall not be allowed inside the cleanroom, unless approved.

4.3.2 Personnel shall be instructed in hygiene-related issues that will prepare them for properly working in the cleanroom environment.

4.3.3 A policy concerning jewellery, cosmetics and similar materials that can cause contamination problems shall be determined.

NOTE: Informative guidance concerning personnel requirements listed in 4.3.1 to 4.3.5 can be found in Annex C.

Analysis and Regulatory Links

Personnel: The Hardest Source of Contamination to Control in a Cleanroom

Section 4.3 looks brief (only five clauses), but the principles behind it are extremely important:

  • 4.3.1 Bringing personal items (mobile phones, keys, wallets) into the cleanroom is a serious violation, because the particles and microorganisms on their surfaces are difficult to control
  • 4.3.3 Jewelry policy: rings and watches may puncture gloves or protrude from the cuffs, increasing particle and microbial risk; this clause requires a clear jewelry prohibition policy
  • 4.3.4 Behavioral training is central: every movement a person makes can increase particle shedding (fast walking, bending over, sneezing, etc.)

EU GMP Annex 1 §4 Personnel Requirements in Brief

  • §4.2: personnel entering aseptic areas must receive regular training in microbiological contamination control and gowning procedures
  • §4.4: personnel with wounds, skin infections, or colds must not enter Grade A/B areas
  • §4.5: jewelry, false eyelashes, nail polish, etc. are prohibited
  • §4.6: scratching the skin or touching the face in the aseptic area is prohibited
  • §4.9: gowning qualification (microbiological confirmation of gowning) must be performed annually to confirm that the microbial monitoring results after gowning are within limits
  • FDA Aseptic Guide §IV.A: personnel must receive both didactic training and hands-on training, with documented records of qualification

Quantifying Contamination Generated by Personnel

Human particle shedding rates (reference values, Annex B.1):
• Standing still:   ~100,000 particles/min (≥0.3 µm)
• Normal walking:   ~500,000 particles/min
• Fast walking:  ~1,000,000 particles/min (1 million/min)
• Sneezing:    >100,000 particles/sneeze

Shedding of microorganism-carrying particles (person inside gowning):
• Cleanroom clothing: ~10-100 CFU/min (depending on garment design and the individual)
• Ordinary clothing:  ~1,000-10,000 CFU/min

Conclusion: wearing qualified cleanroom clothing can reduce
microorganism-carrying particles by about 100-1000 times, but cannot bring them to zero

Section 4.4 - Stationary Equipment

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Page 5 | Normative Requirements

4.4 Stationary Equipment — Normative Requirements

4.4.1 All equipment, with its associated moving and rigging equipment, shall be thoroughly cleaned or decontaminated, or both, before being transported into the cleanroom environment.

4.4.2 Procedures relating to the entry of equipment into a controlled environment shall be specified to ensure that all equipment undergoes the necessary cleaning and decontaminating.

NOTE: Informative guidance concerning stationary equipment requirements listed in 4.4.1 to 4.4.6 can be found in Annex D.

Analysis and Regulatory Links

Contamination Risks and Controls for Stationary Equipment

Stationary equipment (filling machines, freeze dryers, conveyors, compressed air systems, etc.) is a key focus for managing fixed sources of contamination in a cleanroom:

  • Entry contamination (4.4.1/4.4.2): the equipment is manufactured outside the facility, and its surfaces carry a large amount of external environmental contamination
  • Installation contamination (4.4.3): the installation process (welding, drilling, screwing) generates large amounts of particles and metal shavings
  • Post-use contamination (4.4.4/4.4.6): equipment ageing, wear, and lubricant leaks are all potential contamination sources

The "preventive maintenance" in 4.4.6 is key: replace parts before they become a source of contamination, rather than waiting for failure.

Analogy: equipment entry is like sterilization management of surgical instruments

Unsterilized surgical instruments entering the operating room = large equipment entering the cleanroom without cleaning. Both need "pre-entry treatment" (layered unpacking + cleaning/disinfection). Because large equipment is bulky, it must be processed layer by layer in a "transfer room", just as surgical instruments are packaged and processed step by step in the sterile processing department (CSSD).

Key Principles for Equipment Entry (with Annex D)

  • Remove the outer packaging first (cartons/wooden battens) in an uncontrolled area, then bring the equipment into the Transfer Area
  • Layered cleaning: wipe layer by layer in the Transfer Area (from top to bottom) to remove surface contamination
  • Cleanrooms already in production need special protection (D.2.1): for a cleanroom already in production, equipment entry may affect work in progress, so an isolation plan must be developed
  • Isolation during installation (D.4): installation of large equipment requires a temporary isolation wall, maintaining negative pressure to prevent contamination from spreading to adjacent cleanrooms

Section 4.5 - Materials and Portable Equipment

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Page 5-6 | Normative Requirements

4.5 Materials and Portable and Mobile Equipment

4.5.1 All materials, as well as portable and mobile equipment, shall be appropriate for the level of cleanroom cleanliness, and in use, shall not compromise the product and process.

4.5.2 Procedures shall be established to ensure materials and portable and mobile equipment entering the cleanroom are not contaminated.

4.5.3 Procedures shall be established to minimize the quantities of materials stored in the cleanroom. Consideration shall be given to shelf-life limitations, if applicable.

4.5.4 Materials stored in the cleanroom shall be subject to defined procedures and, where necessary, shall be held in protective storage or isolation. The risk of contamination arising from the storage and subsequent use of materials and portable and mobile equipment in the cleanroom shall be considered.

4.5.5 All used and waste materials shall be collected, identified and removed in accordance with defined procedures. Waste materials shall be removed frequently and in a manner that does not compromise the cleanliness of the product or process. Procedures for hazardous materials must conform to statutory requirements set by local and other regulatory agencies.

NOTE: Informative guidance concerning materials and portable equipment requirements listed in 4.5.1 to 4.5.5 can be found in Annex E.

Analysis and Regulatory Links

Core Principles of Materials Management: Minimum Quantity, Minimum Time, Effective Entry Procedures

The three core principles of Section 4.5:

  • Compatibility (4.5.1): materials must be suitable for the cleanroom grade: no cartons into Grade B, no uncleaned tools into Grade A
  • Minimization (4.5.3): a cleanroom is not a warehouse; every item of material is a potential contamination source, so keep only the necessary quantity of work-in-process/raw materials
  • Prompt waste removal (4.5.5): waste is an established source of contamination and must not accumulate in the cleanroom

Analogy: cleanroom materials management is like consumables management in an ICU ward

An ICU does not allow unopened medical consumables to pile up beside the bed; used consumables must be thrown immediately into the infectious waste bin, not left at the bedside. The principles of cleanroom materials management are exactly the same: "just enough, just in time", with waste removed frequently.

The Importance of the Material Entry Procedure

4.5.2 requires a material entry procedure to be established. A typical double-wrapped entry flow (modeled on Annex E.4.1):

  1. In the warehouse (uncontrolled area), verify the materials and remove cartons/wooden pallets
  2. In the external Transfer Area: remove the outer packaging (cartons, foam) and wipe the outer surface of the inner packaging with a damp wipe
  3. In the Grade C/D Transfer Area: strip off the outer plastic bag, wipe the outer surface of the inner bag with disinfectant, and pass it into the Grade B airlock
  4. Grade B airlock: remove the last layer of packaging; the materials can then enter the Grade B area for use

This procedure is usually called the "double-bag / triple-bag entry" method.

4.6 Cleanroom Cleaning — Normative Requirements

4.6.1 Cleaning methods and procedures shall be specified and routinely followed to maintain cleanroom surfaces at acceptable cleanliness levels.

4.6.2 Personnel responsible for the cleaning operation shall be designated and receive specific training for accomplishing the task.

Analysis and Regulatory Links

The Six Elements of Cleaning Management (All Clauses of 4.6)

  • Standardized methods (4.6.1): the SOP must specify exactly which cleaning agents and tools to use and how to wipe
  • Designated trained personnel (4.6.2): cleaning personnel must receive dedicated training; untrained personnel must not be assigned to clean at random
  • Frequency plan (4.6.3): establish a cleaning schedule, defining frequency according to surface criticality and contamination rate
  • Contamination verification (4.6.4): after cleaning, perform contamination checks (particles, microorganisms) to confirm that cleaning is effective
  • Protection of work in process (4.6.5): work in process must be removed or covered during cleaning to prevent the cleaning process from contaminating the product
  • Emergency cleaning (4.6.6): emergency response cleaning procedures for accidental spills and contamination events

Regulatory Link: PDA TR70 and EU GMP Annex 1

PDA Technical Report No. 70 (Cleaning and Disinfection of Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Facilities) is the most detailed guidance for pharmaceutical cleaning:

  • Disinfectant Rotation: rotate at least two disinfectants to prevent microorganisms from developing resistance
  • Disinfectant Efficacy Testing: efficacy must be tested against microorganisms isolated in the facility
  • Cleaning Validation: demonstrate that the cleaning procedure consistently achieves the predetermined cleanliness standard

EU GMP Annex 1 §4.34-4.36: the efficacy of cleaning agents and disinfectants must be confirmed regularly; disinfectants must not be a source of microbial growth (they need preservatives or must be freshly prepared).

Annex A (Informative) - Operational Systems: Detailed Guidance

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Pages 7-12 | Informative

A.1 General

It is essential that management provides leadership that will focus the attention of its staff on generating and maintaining systems that will encourage good cleanroom practices. A management structure should be defined and published to ensure all parties are aware of their responsibilities.

A.2 Assessing Contamination Risks

A.2.1 Methods for assessing risks

A risk assessment should be made to determine any relevant contamination control factors. Some examples of methods include:

  • a) HACCP (Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point)
  • b) FMEA (Failure Mode Effects Analysis)
  • c) FTA (Fault Tree Analysis)

A.2.2.1 General Assessment Criteria

Improper control of the critical elements of an operational cleanroom can pose a risk. Key considerations:

  • a) concentration of contamination in or on the risk factor;
  • b) distance from the risk to the product;
  • c) importance of the method used to protect product from the risk.

A.2.2.2 Cleanroom Clothing Risk Factors

Risk factors include: required human containment (coveralls, frocks, hoods, gloves, boots, masks, etc.); material performance (weave characteristics, filament types, sterility, antistatic, etc.); design and construction; comfort; usage (launderable versus disposable); choice of personal clothing worn under cleanroom clothing; time interval before laundering; choice of cleanroom clothing laundry; renewing, packaging, storage and distribution.

A.2.2.3 Personnel Risk Factors

Risk factors include: selection of personnel; education and training; safety (including emergency procedure); personal attire, hygiene and behaviour; chronic and acute medical conditions; personnel who shed significantly more contamination; who is allowed to enter; special procedures for visitors; maximum occupancy; entry and exit procedures; movement and activity of personnel within the cleanroom.

A.2.2.4 Stationary Equipment Risk Factors

Risk factors include: entry and exit procedures; installation; cleaning techniques; contamination generation; generation of heat, humidity and electrostatic charge; maintenance and repair; cleanliness of process material and utilities delivery systems; potential equipment failures.

A.2.2.5 Materials and Portable Equipment Risk Factors

Risk factors include: compatibility and selection; entry, exit and movement procedures; storage factors while in the cleanroom; contamination factors during use; generation of electrostatic charges; liquid and gas purity supplied by delivery systems; waste disposal; packaging.

A.2.2.6 Cleanroom Cleaning Risk Factors

Risk factors include: routine environmental contaminating factors (airflows, airborne particles, out-gassing, hazardous gas, micro-organisms, vibration, electrostatic charges, molecular contamination, etc.); personnel and material flow; service, maintenance and repair; cleaning methodology; emergency and planned shutdown; facility expansion and modification; frequency for monitoring the results of cleaning.

A.3 Monitoring and Corrective Action

A routine monitoring programme should be followed that encompasses personnel, cleaning and other operational systems. Monitoring should be sufficiently frequent and comprehensive to detect actual or emerging unacceptable conditions in a timely manner. Exceeding specified action levels should result in a prompt response, including investigative and corrective action. Investigative and corrective action should include the effect on product quality.

A.4 Education and Training

A.4.1 Involvement

General personnel activity within the cleanroom has a profound effect on the integrity of the clean environment. Management is responsible for implementing a comprehensive programme to train all personnel. Certification should be based on successful completion of testing to demonstrate understanding and compliance. Personnel groups requiring training:

  • operators; technicians; engineers and scientists;
  • quality assurance personnel; supervisors and managers;
  • facilities personnel; contractors; field service personnel; visitors.

A.4.2 Training Course Contents

Subjects that can be included:

  • a) how the cleanroom works (design, airflow and air filtration);
  • b) cleanroom standards;
  • c) sources of contamination;
  • d) personal hygiene;
  • e) cleaning;
  • f) cleanroom clothing procedures;
  • g) maintenance procedures;
  • h) how a cleanroom is tested and monitored;
  • i) how to act in a cleanroom;
  • j) explanation of the work process and how the process can become contaminated;
  • k) safety and emergency response.

A.4.3 Monitoring of Cleanroom Personnel

The ability of personnel to incorporate all elements of cleanroom training into practice is essential. Personnel, although properly trained, may not fully comprehend all requirements or may lapse into poor procedural habits. Therefore, actions of personnel should be monitored to ensure compliance. Internal auditors can monitor the actions based upon the written procedures. Reports can be issued to management on a regular basis.

A.4.4 Training Documentation

A concise, comprehensive system that documents the training progression and level of each individual should be used. Basic documentation should include course contents, personnel identification information, training and certification dates, and schedules for retraining.

A.5 Cleanroom Support Services

A.5.1-A.5.6

Management is responsible for ensuring cleanroom support services consistently function as designed. Support services may include clean and conditioned air systems, compressed air and gases, water and other utilities. Topics covered:

  • A.5.2 Record of the installation — drawings, classification, acceptance test results, spare parts lists
  • A.5.3 Operating and maintenance instructions — including emergency shutdown and start-up procedures
  • A.5.4 Performance monitoring — documented schedules and procedures for specified tests and frequencies
  • A.5.5 Maintenance procedures — preventive maintenance to minimize contamination from equipment failures
  • A.5.6 Maintenance records — problem diagnoses, parts replaced, dates, times and personnel

A.6 Upgrading and Modifying the Cleanroom

All upgrades or modifications, including the addition of stationary equipment and changes to floor plans, can affect the cleanliness. Management should ensure these changes are planned and carried out in a controlled manner and that requalification is in compliance with ISO 14644-2 and ISO 14644-4. Personnel involved may include: facility engineers; manufacturing engineers; equipment engineers; contamination control engineers; process engineers and scientists; quality assurance engineers and scientists; manufacturing managers; contractors.

A.7 Safety

Normal operation of cleanroom facilities often includes the use of hazardous, toxic or infectious materials. A good safety programme should include:

  • a) centralized, readily-available safety data sheets;
  • b) evacuation plans and practice evacuations;
  • c) accident reporting system;
  • d) feedback suggestion systems for personnel;
  • e) appropriate monitoring of potentially hazardous conditions;
  • f) rapid response to emergencies by trained personnel;
  • g) documentation that supports improvements and corrections to safety-related issues.

Annex A In-Depth Analysis

The Role of Annex A: Turning Normative Requirements into a Management System

Section 4.1 has only seven short normative requirements, while Annex A provides detailed implementation guidance, ranging from:

  • Risk assessment methodologies (HACCP/FMEA/FTA)
  • A detailed list of six categories of contamination risk factors

  • Nine categories of training audience and 11 training course topics
  • Documentation requirements for facility support services
  • Change control and requalification

A.3 Monitoring and Corrective Action: Alert Level vs. Action Level

EU GMP Annex 1 §9 establishes the concept of two tiers of monitoring limits, which corresponds closely to A.3:

  • Alert Level: an excursion is an early warning of a possible departure from normal operation; the cause must be investigated but production need not necessarily stop
  • Action Level: an excursion requires immediate action, including stopping operations, investigation, and correction
  • Both must be defined in writing in the EM SOP and set on the basis of trend analysis and qualification data
  • An event exceeding the Action Level must trigger the OOS/OOT investigation procedure

A.4 Training System: The GMP Training Structure in the Pharmaceutical Industry

The training system proposed in A.4 corresponds to EU GMP Part 1 Chapter 2 "Personnel" and the personnel management requirements of ICH Q10:

  • Training course design (A.4.2): 11 topics covering theory (cleanroom science) and practice (gowning, cleaning, behavior)
  • Certification system (A.4.1): after training, personnel must pass a test to be deemed qualified; it is not just "attending class and signing in"
  • Behavior monitoring (A.4.3): after training, personnel behavior must continue to be observed and an audit mechanism established
  • Document management (A.4.4): each person's training history, certification dates, and retraining plan must be filed

Pharmaceutical plants usually use a training management system (such as Veeva Vault Training or ComplianceWire) to manage these records.

Analogy: A.5 support services are like an aircraft's ground crew system

Ground services for an aircraft (refueling, mechanical checks, cleaning) determine flight safety; the "support services" of a cleanroom (HVAC, compressed air, purified water, equipment maintenance) determine whether the cleanroom can operate normally. The documentation requirements of A.5 (A.5.2-A.5.6) are the "ground crew manual": making sure each support system has operating instructions, maintenance records, and emergency response procedures.

A.6 Change Control: Links to ICH Q10 and GMP

The "upgrading and modifying" management in Annex A.6 corresponds to the pharmaceutical industry's Change Control system:

  • Any change affecting cleanroom classification requires requalification
  • Typical Change Control triggers: changing the HEPA filter model, expanding cleanroom space, changing the material transfer route, adding equipment
  • EU GMP Part 1 §13: Change Control must assess the impact of the change on product quality and obtain QA approval before implementation
  • Major changes may require supplementary clinical/analytical studies (Regulatory Filing)

B.1 Function of Cleanroom Clothing

Personnel disperse fragments from their skin and particles from their indoor, non-cleanroom clothing. This airborne dispersion will vary from person to person and from time to time but can be several million particles per minute and several hundred bacteria-carrying particles per minute. The prime function of cleanroom clothing is to act as a barrier filter that protects product and process from human contamination.

Personnel also emit inert and microbe-carrying particles from the mouth through sneezing, coughing and talking. Therefore, cleanroom clothing should be made from a fabric that filters the contamination dispersed. Touching will transmit contamination from the hands to surfaces in the cleanroom.

B.2 General Choice of Cleanroom Clothing

The best design of cleanroom clothing will completely envelop the person and have good closures at the wrist, neck and ankle. The choice will depend on the class of cleanroom but cleanrooms with higher cleanliness requirements are typified by a one-piece coverall, overboots and a hood with yoke or skirt that tucks under the neck of the garment.

There are two broad categories of clothing used in cleanrooms:

  • Disposable (or limited use) — made from non-woven materials; used once or a few times, then discarded.
  • Reusable — usually made from tightly woven synthetic fabrics, constructed with lint-free, continuous filament materials (such as polyester or polyamide).

Natural fabrics made from fibres, such as cotton, would not normally be used in cleanrooms as they easily break up and disperse contamination. More critical applications may require the use of membrane barrier technology, which may be either disposable or reusable.

B.3 Properties of Fabric

B.3.1 Barrier Properties

The fabric used in cleanroom clothing should prevent personnel-generated contamination from being dispersed into the cleanroom. Woven fabric acts as a filter; effectiveness is related to the tightness of the weave. Fabric effectiveness can be assessed by a measurement of air permeability, particle retention and pore size. As air permeability decreases, there is a corresponding increase in pressure within the garment which can result in an outward pumping action of unfiltered air through the closures.

B.3.2 Durability

Cleanroom clothing should be resistant to breakdown and tearing. The fabric should disperse the minimum of particles.

B.3.3 Electrostatic Properties

In some types of cleanrooms (e.g. microelectronics or rooms with flammable or explosive chemicals), the electrostatic charges that build up on the surface of clothing will be harmful to the components being manufactured. Fabrics are available with woven-in, static-dissipative threads to discharge any induced voltage potentials on the fabric surface.

B.3.4 Other Physical Properties

The effectiveness of a fabric will deteriorate due to aging, wear, washing, drying, sterilization, etc. This deterioration should be monitored. Another physical property that should be considered is the resistance of the fabric to chemicals.

B.4 Design and Construction

B.4.1 Construction of Clothing

All raw edges of the fabric should be covered, interlocked and heat seared or laser cut to prevent fraying. Seams should be double-needle stitched, bound or taped. Threads should be synthetic continuous filament. Zippers, clips and fasteners should not shed, chip or corrode, and should stand up to multiple launderings and sterilization.

B.4.2 General Design

To minimize the retention of contamination, pockets, pleats, darts, hook and pile fasteners, and action backs should not be used. Elasticized or knitted cuffs should not trap or shed contaminants. Other design parameters: zipper material and location; sleeve construction (set-in or raglan); cuff closures; collar style; hood style (open or closed face).

B.4.3 Dispersal Chamber (Body Box)

This simulation procedure can be used to demonstrate the combined effect of fabric, construction and design of clothing. A person will enter the box, which is ventilated at a known flow rate of filtered air, and exercise to a given routine. The number of particles or bacteria dispersed can be measured.

B.5 Thermal Comfort

Whenever possible, the comfort of people working in the cleanroom should be considered when choosing cleanroom clothing materials. Air and water vapour permeability specifications can help in this determination. The use of relevant personnel parameters and environmental parameters can be used to derive theoretical clothing comfort level according to ISO 7730.

B.6 Cleanroom Clothing Cleaning Process and Frequency of Change

During use, cleanroom clothing will become contaminated. If it is to be reused it should be cleaned. Final treatment and packaging operations for cleanroom clothing should be carried out in cleanroom conditions that are compatible with the standards of the cleanroom in which they will be used. The processing cycle in the cleanroom laundry should include, as appropriate:

  • a) disinfection;
  • b) hot water cycles;
  • c) sterilization.

Cleaning procedures should include sample testing at the laundry for the appropriate type and level of contamination. Increasing the cleaning frequency will cause additional stress to the cleanroom clothing, contributing to premature breakdown of the fabrics.

B.7 Gloves

Cleanroom gloves are required in most cleanrooms. They cover that part of the human body that is often closest to the product and critical surfaces. Properties to consider: surface contamination, outgassing, sterility, tactility, strength, comfort, fit as well as the method of packaging. Gloves can be constructed of latex, vinyl, polyurethane, or other materials such as nitrile rubber.

B.8 Face Masks and Other Headgear

Face masks and exhaust headgear provide a barrier against saliva and contamination dispersed from the mouth, nose and face. Masks can be surgical-style masks with tie straps, elasticized straps or loops. Headgear is available that provides an active barrier to contamination from the mouth and head — a helmet with hood and clear face-shield enclosed with a filtered exhaust system that prevents contamination from escaping into the cleanroom.

B.9 Storage of Clothing

B.9.1 If reused, clothing should be stored or hung using appropriate techniques that will maintain cleanliness. Methods include: clothing racks with high-efficiency, self-contained, filtered air supply; fixed and portable racks utilizing hangers; locking/non-locking hooks; bins or storage slots.

B.9.2 The space required for temporary storage depends on the number of people working in the cleanroom and the frequency of change.

B.9.3 An area large enough to contain the total inventory of packaged cleanroom clothing should be set aside. Lockers should be placed on the cleaning schedule.

B.9.4 Cleaned clothing should be packaged in clean, non-shedding bags. The shelf life for sterilized products should be defined. Storage should be in a controlled environment adjacent to or in the changing area.

Annex B In-Depth Analysis

Garment Filtration Mechanism: Why Is Fabric Density Critical?

B.3.1 describes the filtration mechanism of cleanroom garments:

  • Fabric = filter: the pores between fibers intercept skin squames and particles shed from the skin
  • The smaller the pores, the higher the filtration efficiency, but the stronger the "pumping action": when the limbs move, the pressure inside the garment rises, forcing unfiltered air out through the collar and cuffs
  • Design compensation: good gown design must balance high-efficiency filtration against sealed openings (collar, cuffs, ankles)

Correspondence Between Garment Grade and Pharmaceutical Grade

EU GMP grade vs. garment requirements (Annex 1, 2022)

Grade A/B aseptic core area:
• Full-coverage coverall (whole body)
• Hood (completely covering head and neck)
• Face mask (covering nose and mouth)
• Goggles
• Powder-free gloves (2 layers, disinfected regularly)
• Cleanroom boots (overboots)
• Garments must be sterilized (gamma irradiation or autoclave)

Grade C support area:
• Coverall or jacket + trousers
• Hair cover/hood
• Suitable shoe covers
• Garments must be cleaned; disinfection optional

Grade D general pharmaceutical area:
• Basic cleanroom garment
• Hair cover
• No special glove requirement (depends on the operation)

Glove material selection considerations (B.7):
• Latex: highly elastic, but allergy concerns
• Nitrile: non-allergenic, chemical resistant
• Polyurethane (PU): thin, high tactile sensitivity, suited to delicate operations
• Vinyl: inexpensive, weaker chemical protection

Analogy: garment laundering is like reprocessing surgical instruments

Reusable cleanroom garments need "reprocessing" (laundering + sterilization) after every use, just as surgical instruments must be cleaned and disinfected/sterilized before they can be used again. The key principle: the clean environment of the laundry facility must be compatible with the environment in which the garments are used (B.6), just as surgical instruments must be washed in the CSSD rather than in an ordinary restroom.

B.4.3 Body Box Test: The Gold Standard for Verifying Garment Performance

The body box (dispersal chamber) test is currently the most direct method of verifying garment performance:

  • A person wearing the garment under test enters a closed test chamber
  • Standard movements (marching on the spot, bending, etc.) are performed in a chamber supplied with filtered air (of known cleanliness)
  • The air leaving the chamber is measured with a particle counter or microbial sampler
  • The "barrier efficiency" of different garments can be compared quantitatively
  • The FDA Aseptic Guide recommends using a similar method to verify the barrier performance of Grade A/B garments

Practical Points for Garment Storage Management (B.9)

  • Shelf-life management of sterilized garments (B.9.4): garments sterilized by gamma irradiation or autoclave must have a defined "shelf life", usually set according to packaging material and storage conditions (for example, vacuum packaging can last 6-12 months)
  • First-in, first-out (FIFO): inventory management must ensure that older garments are used first, to avoid expiry
  • Storage environment control: a garment storage area is recommended near the temperature- and humidity-controlled changing room, to prevent garments from failing prematurely due to environmental effects (humidity, ultraviolet light)
  • Damage inspection (4.2.10): visually check garment integrity before every use, and scrap damaged garments immediately

C.1 Training

Only trained personnel should be allowed to enter and work in a cleanroom. All personnel should be given an introductory course when initiated into the cleanroom and further periodic retraining (see A.2).

C.2 Access by Personnel

People generate contamination. Therefore, only essential personnel should enter the cleanroom. If the total number of people allowed in the cleanroom is controlled, access should be documented and enforced. Visitors and maintenance people can be allowed into the room by permission and under supervision. They should be given an appropriate level of training.

C.3 Clothing and Personal Items

The type of clothing worn under cleanroom clothing will affect the dispersion of airborne particles and fibres. Personal indoor clothing manufactured from natural fibres such as wool or cotton worn underneath cleanroom clothing will shed contamination. The provision for special cleanroom undergarments should be considered.

Personal items should be left outside the cleanroom in a secure area. Jewellery, such as rings, watches, and chains, can puncture cleanroom gloves or dangle outside face masks, hoods or sleeves. Cosmetics, talcum powder, hair sprays, nail polish or similar materials are undesirable in a cleanroom.

C.4 Hygiene

Cleanroom personnel are expected to have good personal hygiene. Personnel should keep dandruff controlled, and, as necessary, use specially formulated skin lotion to replace skin oils after washing and showering.

Personnel arriving for work should report problems that might increase contamination in the cleanroom, including:

  • a) conditions such as flaking skin, dermatitis, sunburn or bad dandruff;
  • b) cold, flu or chronic coughing;
  • c) allergic conditions that cause sneezing, itching or scratching;
  • d) in a biocleanroom — high microbial bioburden on personnel.

Depending on the seriousness of the condition, it may be necessary to reassign personnel with such conditions to work outside the cleanroom. In some cleanrooms, it may be required that personnel refrain from smoking for a defined period of time before entering.

C.5 Cleanroom Clothing Changing Procedures

Cleanroom personnel will change into cleanroom clothing before proceeding into a cleanroom. Usually the process begins at the head and proceeds downward to the feet:

  1. Remove contamination from shoes by use of a shoe cleaner, cleanroom mat or cleanroom flooring.
  2. Remove unnecessary street clothing.
  3. Remove jewellery, etc. if required.
  4. Remove cosmetics and put on moisturizer, if required.
  5. Put on hair cover, if applicable.
  6. Wash hands and put on suitable moisturizer, if applicable.
  7. Put on cleanroom underclothing, if applicable.
  8. Put on cleanroom-dedicated under-shoes, or shoe covers.
  9. Select cleanroom clothing.
  10. If required, put on gloves for handling cleanroom clothing.
  11. Put on face and head covering.
  12. Put on coverall or gown.
  13. Put on shoe coverings or special cleanroom shoes, using a cross-over bench.
  14. Using a full-length mirror, ensure that all items of clothing are properly adjusted.
  15. Gloves used for putting on cleanroom clothing can now either be removed, or left on, so that process gloves can be put on.
  16. Enter the cleanroom.

C.6 Discipline and Conduct

Cleanroom personnel should conduct themselves in a cleanroom in such a way as to minimize the possibility of contaminating the product. Minimum disciplines include:

  • Doors should not be opened and closed quickly, nor left open.
  • When using a transfer area, the entry door should be allowed to close and the air to purge before the exit door is opened.
  • Personnel should not position themselves between clean air supplies and product or process surfaces.
  • Methods should be devised for moving or manipulating the product. "No-touch" techniques should be used where appropriate.
  • Personnel should not support material against their bodies or contamination may be transferred.
  • Personnel should not talk when working close to the product.
  • Personnel should not allow anything to trail over the product.
  • Nose blowing should be done outside the cleanroom. Gloves should always be changed afterwards.
  • Personnel should also refrain from touching, scratching or wiping any skin areas while in the cleanroom.
  • Glove and cleanroom clothing surfaces can easily become contaminated. Personnel should not touch contaminated surfaces that will transfer contamination to critical areas.
  • A cleanroom wipe should be used as specified and then discarded in the proper waste receptacle.
  • All personnel movements should be deliberate and methodical. Rapid walking and movements should not be allowed since it will disrupt the airflow.
  • The room should be kept neat and tidy.
  • Products stored or left standing in a cleanroom should be protected from contamination and kept in an identifiable closed cabinet, container or unidirectional cabinet.
  • Waste material should be placed into easily identifiable containers and not allowed to collect unnecessarily.

C.7 Safety

C.7.1 Preventive measures required by statutory regulations must be implemented to protect personnel from hazards that may occur or may be in use in the cleanroom, such as microbes, radioactivity and chemicals.

C.7.2 Emergency situations may arise and emergency response personnel, trained in all aspects of potential emergencies, can minimize the effects of mishaps. All employees should be trained for an orderly evacuation. If an evacuation is necessary, provisions should be made for the orderly return to the cleanroom once the situation is cleared. An emergency procedure for supplying fresh cleanroom clothing should be implemented.

C.8 Personnel Initiatives

Personnel should understand that they could have a positive influence on the effectiveness of the cleanroom. Helpful coaching of one to another can have a positive effect on conformance to personnel procedures. Personnel should be encouraged and empowered to immediately report any observed deficiencies, whether personnel or facility related.

Annex C In-Depth Analysis

The Logic of the C.5 Gowning Procedure: Outside to Inside, Top to Bottom

The 16-step gowning procedure in C.5 follows one core logic: touch the cleanest parts first, and the parts most likely to be contaminated last. Once you understand this logic, you understand the reason for the order of each step:

  • Step 1 (clean shoe soles): remove contamination from the soles before entering the changing room, to prevent contamination being carried into it
  • Step 5 (put on the hood first): hair and scalp are the hardest areas for garments to cover, so they are dealt with first
  • Step 13 (put on shoe covers using the cross-over bench): the cross-over bench ensures that feet already covered do not touch the "contaminated floor" again
  • Step 14 (full-length mirror check): confirm that all garments fit tightly and no skin is exposed

C.6 Behavioral Rules: The Most Easily Overlooked Contamination Risk

The behavioral rules in C.6 correspond directly to the requirements of EU GMP Annex 1 §4.5-4.7. The following are the violations most often found by pharmaceutical inspectors:

  • Incorrect personnel positioning: standing between the HEPA air supply and the product (contaminating the product), rather than downstream of the product
  • Fast movement: disrupts laminar flow and lifts floor contamination up to working height
  • Talking in the aseptic area: even with a face mask, talking still increases the shedding of exhaled contaminants
  • Leaning on equipment/walls: the outer surface of the garment becomes contaminated through contact with surfaces carrying microorganisms
  • Gloves not changed promptly: continuing to handle sterile items after touching non-sterile surfaces

Common FDA 483 observation: personnel behavior in the aseptic area that does not meet "aseptic technique" requirements is one of the most common inspection deficiencies.

Analogy: C.6 behavioral rules are like "aseptic technique" in surgery

The core of surgical "aseptic technique" is: never touch a sterile area with a contaminated hand or instrument. The C.6 behavioral rules are the industrial version of the same logic: avoid forming any "contamination transfer path". "No-touch technique" embodies exactly this principle.

Quantitative Monitoring of Personnel Contamination Contribution (Gowning Qualification)

EU GMP Annex 1 §4.9 gowning qualification process:

1. Gowning: complete gowning following the standard SOP gowning procedure
2. Sampling sites: gloves (fingertips of both hands) + chest (coverall surface)
3. Sampling method: fingertips pressed onto culture medium (5 fingers / hand)
                     coverall surface by contact plate or swab
4. Acceptance criteria (EU GMP Annex 1 Table 1):
   • Grade A/B: ≤ 1 CFU / contact plate
   • Grade C: ≤ 25 CFU / contact plate
   • Grade D: ≤ 50 CFU / contact plate
5. Frequency: initial qualification + requalification at least once a year
6. Failure handling: retraining → retesting → access restricted if still failing

CDMO Scenario: Training Qualification Plan for New Employees Entering a Grade B Area

Design a training qualification plan for new employees entering the Grade B aseptic filling area, according to Annex C:

  • Training content (C.1/A.4.2): cleanroom science principles (2 h) + aseptic technique fundamentals (4 h) + gowning SOP practice (2 h)
  • Knowledge test (A.4.1): written test, pass mark ≥ 80; those who fail must be retrained
  • Gowning qualification: sampling after 3 consecutive gowning events, with results meeting the Grade B standard (≤1 CFU)
  • Behavior monitoring (A.4.3): for the 3 months before entering the aseptic room, behavior is observed by senior personnel or QA, with records and feedback provided
  • Annual requalification: repeat gowning qualification every year; retrain immediately after a major violation

Annex D (Informative) - Stationary Equipment: Detailed Guidance

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Pages 22-25 | Informative

D.1 General

Equipment that is large enough to be stationary or relatively immovable, once located within the cleanroom. Stationary equipment may be in the form of automated and mechanical processing equipment, separative devices, and exhaust hoods as well as other large equipment. When possible, equipment to be used in the cleanroom should be manufactured under clean conditions and packaging procedures should be adapted for the requirements of the intended cleanroom.

D.2 Clean Entry Process

D.2.1 Planning

The process of bringing equipment into the cleanroom should not add contamination. Equipment entering a cleanroom that is "as-built" or "at-rest" should be properly unpacked and cleaned. However, special considerations should be made before bringing equipment into an "operational" cleanroom. Failure to do so will expose not only the cleanroom to contamination risks but may affect products in process. This will also necessitate additional cleaning and may require the cleanroom to be requalified under ISO 14644-2.

D.2.2 Inspection and Removal of Non-Cleanroom Packaging

All equipment should be checked for damage in transport. Whenever possible, shipping crates and packaging should be removed in the uncontrolled environment adjacent to the cleanroom. All cardboard and heavily shedding materials should be removed before being transported into the controlled environment. When not pre-packaged, all surfaces of the equipment should be pre-cleaned prior to entry.

D.2.3 Removing Cleanroom Packaging — Step-by-Step

Unpacking of equipment should be done in steps to control contamination:

  1. The outer protective covering should be vacuumed, beginning at the top surface and then proceeding to the sides.
  2. The protective cover should be wiped, using the appropriate cleaning agent.
  3. The outer layer of packaging film should be slit at the top in an "I" form and peeled from the top to the bottom edge.
  4. The unpacking procedures in steps 2 and 3 should be repeated for each additional layer. All exterior surfaces of the equipment should be thoroughly cleaned.
  5. All personnel should be wearing the proper cleanroom clothing prior to entering the transfer area from the cleanroom.
  6. All moving and handling equipment should also be cleaned.
  7. The transfer area should be cleaned before opening the doors to the cleanroom for transferring the equipment inside.

D.3 Transporting Equipment

Large equipment should be dismantled (if possible) to a size that will enable safe entry. Any special equipment used for lifting, hauling or positioning large equipment should be thoroughly cleaned before being allowed into the cleanroom. Soft rubber wheels can be coated with cleanroom tape to avoid leaving trails of rubber or plastic particles on the flooring.

D.4 Installation Procedures

Ideally, the cleanroom should be closed down during the installation. If work within the cleanroom must continue during installation, the rest of the operational cleanroom must be effectively isolated from the work area through a temporary isolation wall or partition:

  • a) Access to the isolation area should be from a service aisle or other non-critical area. Airflow to this isolation area should be maintained at a neutral or negative pressure to reduce the possibility of contamination being forced outside the work area.
  • b) A completely sealed isolation area should not be pressurized from within. The clean-air supply inside the isolated area should be blocked.
  • c) A method and frequency for monitoring the areas surrounding the isolated area should be instituted.
  • d) All facility services (electricity, water, gas, vacuum, compressed air) will then be attached. Care should be taken to ensure fumes and debris generated by this operation are controlled.
  • e-k) Cleaning procedures should be used to decontaminate the entire isolation area. All surfaces should be vacuumed, wiped and mopped, working from the top to the bottom.

D.5 Maintenance and Repair

With time, equipment wears out and becomes dirty or emits contamination unless it is maintained. Key measures:

  • a) Equipment being repaired should be removed from the area whenever possible before making repairs.
  • b) If necessary, stationary equipment should be suitably isolated from the surrounding cleanroom operations.
  • c) Adjacent cleanroom areas should be suitably monitored.
  • d) Maintenance personnel should not come into contact with personnel performing manufacturing procedures.
  • e) All personnel should follow appropriate practices including wearing cleanroom-approved protective clothing.
  • f) A determination of conditions should be made before technicians lie or crawl under equipment.
  • g) Steps should be taken to protect the cleanroom clothing from lubricating oils or processing chemicals.
  • h) All tools, boxes and carts should be thoroughly cleaned. No rusted or corroded tools should be allowed. Sterilization or disinfection may be necessary in a biocleanroom.
  • m) Vacuum cleaners should be used during all drilling or sawing operations.

Open spaces remaining after holes are drilled in floors, walls, sides of equipment should be properly sealed afterwards to prevent contamination from entering the cleanroom.

D.6 Equipment Removal

Removing stationary equipment from the cleanroom often stirs up or loosens contamination from internal or other inaccessible surfaces that have not been routinely cleaned. Steps should be taken to isolate, clean, and contain such equipment before and during removal to avoid contaminating the surrounding cleanroom. Regulatory considerations may be involved if the contamination is of a hazardous nature.

Annex D In-Depth Analysis

Equipment Entry Procedure: The Logic of the "Layer-by-Layer Stripping" Method

The layered unpacking procedure in D.2.3 is the core technique for equipment entry, and its logic is exactly the same as double-wrapped material entry:

  • Principle: each time a layer of packaging is removed, advance to the next cleaner area
  • Direction: from outside to inside (uncontrolled area → transfer room → cleanroom)
  • Cleaning direction: from top to bottom (to prevent contamination from above falling onto surfaces already cleaned below)
  • Combined vacuum + wipe: first vacuum off large particles, then wipe with a damp wipe to remove adhering contamination

D.4 Isolation During Installation: The GMP Principle of "Protecting Work in Process"

The biggest challenge when installing equipment in a cleanroom that is in production: how to protect work in process while the construction generates contamination?

  • Temporary isolation wall: build a sealed isolation zone to confine construction-generated contamination locally
  • Negative-pressure isolation zone: the isolation zone is kept at negative pressure to ensure contamination does not spread outward to adjacent cleanrooms
  • Monitoring plan (D.4.c): environmental monitoring must continue outside the isolation zone to confirm that contamination has not leaked
  • EU GMP Annex 1 §7.4: construction near an aseptic manufacturing area must have a written contamination control plan approved by QA

Analogy: equipment installation isolation is like "replacing operating room equipment during aseptic surgery"

Imagine that during heart surgery a large piece of equipment in the operating room has to be replaced: obviously impossible. But some plants really are under pressure to keep producing, and in that case the temporary isolation wall is the industrial version of "hanging a sterile curtain in one corner of the operating room". The guiding principles of D.4 help plants maintain contamination control in this difficult situation.

Contamination Verification After Equipment Maintenance

Cleaning verification procedure after equipment maintenance/repair:

Post-maintenance cleaning flow:
1. Remove all tools, spare parts, and waste
2. Vacuum the external surfaces of the equipment (top to bottom)
3. Wipe the external surfaces of the equipment (IPA or the designated disinfectant)
4. Wipe the internal surfaces of the equipment (if accessible)
5. Wipe adjacent floors/walls

Verification items:
• Visual inspection: no visible contamination
• Wipe sampling: particle count
• Microbial sampling: mandatory for a biocleanroom
• Particle count: confirm that the environment has returned to its pre-operation level

Documentation:
• Maintenance log
• Cleaning record
• Monitoring results
• QA release sign-off

Annex E (Informative) - Materials and Portable Equipment: Detailed Guidance

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Pages 26-32 | Informative

E.1 General

Items that can easily be transported into and out of the cleanroom can compromise the cleanliness of the cleanroom if they are not properly selected, handled and stored in the correct quantities. This includes consumable and disposable supplies, and production and cleaning materials, as well as hand tools and portable equipment. The ability to sterilize or disinfect reusable materials and portable equipment should be considered in biocleanroom applications.

E.2 Criteria for Selection

E.2.1 Characteristics

To protect a cleanroom from contamination, materials should have the following characteristics:

  • surfaces and moving parts that shed or generate as little contamination as possible;
  • unbroken, impervious and clean surfaces;
  • properties that minimize generation of contamination by shedding and cutting;
  • suitable cleanroom packaging;
  • compatibility with the cleanroom environment.

E.2.2 Other Criteria

Additional criteria include: free from undesirable chemicals; acceptable anti-static properties; low outgassing properties; free from micro-organisms; compatible with sterilization or disinfection procedures in biocleanrooms.

E.3 Preliminary Testing

Preliminary testing and auditing should be performed as agreed upon between customers and suppliers. Incoming inspection criteria and sampling methods should be fully documented. A secure storage location may be necessary to avoid unauthorized use while materials are waiting for acceptance. Strict quarantine measures may be necessary for biologically sensitive materials.

The supplier should notify the customer prior to making critical changes to approved materials or supplies used in the cleanroom.

E.4 Entry and Exit Procedures

E.4.1 Unpacking and Entry Procedures

Outer contamination-generating packaging, such as wood, cardboard, paper and other materials, should be removed before entry to any part of the controlled or cleanroom environments. Inner plastic wrappers should not be removed at this time. Any interior packaging should be wiped with appropriately moistened cleanroom wipers to remove any gross contamination before being carried into the controlled environment.

A designated transfer area, adjacent to the cleanroom, should be used for final wiping procedures. The outer wrappers of double-wrapped packaging can now be removed and placed in an appropriate rubbish receptacle. Final packaging should only be removed prior to use of the material or object.

Any wheeled, portable equipment, including carts and trolleys, should be thoroughly cleaned before being allowed to enter the cleanroom. Sticky mats or flooring will help prevent this from occurring.

E.4.2 Entry of Materials through Pipes

Materials such as bulk chemicals, compressed gases and water generally enter the cleanroom through pipes. Such materials are subject to the procedures that govern the introduction and intended use of those materials to the facility.

E.4.3 Exit Procedures for Materials and Portable Equipment

Personal and small equipment items should be protected from becoming contaminated through the use of approved plastic bags or other suitable means. Certain waste materials may have a higher risk of transferring contamination — such objects should preferably leave the cleanroom (after being packaged) via transfer areas for material rather than via changing rooms for personnel.

E.5 Types of Materials and Portable Equipment

E.5.3 Solutions and Finishes Used in Cleaning

Types of cleaning solutions and finishes include:

  • a) Clean-filtered, distilled or deionized water — desirable properties but can corrode certain surfaces and may be ineffective without a surfactant or disinfectant.
  • b) Surfactants and detergents — most reasonably priced, non-toxic; non-ionic surfactants are generally preferred as they are the least reactive.
  • c) Organic solvents — for removing organic contamination on hard surfaces. Detergents tend to leave behind a film.
  • d) Disinfectants — used to kill micro-organisms. Care should be taken to select an appropriate material that will not contaminate the process or become harmful to personnel or equipment.
  • e) Synthetic sealers — highly resistant to wear, can be used on certain cleanroom floors.

E.5.4 Wipers

Wipers are used to remove contamination from surfaces in the cleanroom. Characteristics to consider when selecting wipers:

  • a) wiper material; b) solution or solvent compatibility; c) absorption rate of liquids; d) particle generation (both wet and dry); e) extractable molecular contamination; f) sterilization compatibility, if necessary; g) packaging.

E.5.5 Vacuum Cleaners

Cleanroom-compatible vacuum cleaning equipment: portable vacuum cleaners constructed of stainless steel or plastic, with outlet air flowing through a terminal HEPA or ULPA filter. Built-in vacuum cleaner systems employ a large, centralized vacuum pump in a service area outside the cleanroom. The HEPA or ULPA filters should be tested and/or replaced on a regular basis.

E.5.6 Mops

Standard commercial or industrial grade mops should not be used. Mops should be carefully selected to resist shedding of fibres and the effects of sterilization. Floor mop heads should be constructed of polyester fibres or open-celled hydrophilic (synthetic) materials. Handles and fittings should be made of stainless steel, anodized aluminium, or other non-shedding plastics. Polyester offers better resistance to autoclaving than polyvinyl acetate — PVA mop heads will prematurely deteriorate when used with cleaning agents containing high levels of isopropyl alcohol.

E.5.7-E.5.18 Other Equipment

Other equipment types covered include: buckets and wringers (plastic or stainless steel, not galvanized); floor scrubbers with built-in HEPA/ULPA vacuum (standard commercial machines prohibited); stepladders (stainless steel or anodized aluminium); brooms or brushes (should NOT be used in operational cleanrooms — generate gross particle contamination); cleanroom mats and sticky flooring; clean containers and packaging; hand tools (kept clean, stainless steel or synthetic); written documentation (lint-free, cleanroom-compatible media, non-retractable ballpoint pens); electronic documentation (computer cooling fans may affect cleanroom air; flexible keyboard covers recommended).

E.6 Storage

Materials can become contaminated or ineffective if improperly stored while waiting to be used. They should be stored in an environment that protects them from degradation and contamination. The accumulation of unused materials in the cleanroom presents a risk of contamination. Certain classes and types of waste materials are stored in the cleanroom until specified limitations are reached, often regulated by agencies or by recycling programmes.

Annex E In-Depth Analysis

E.2 Material Selection Criteria: Cleanroom Compatibility Assessment

The selection criteria in E.2 provide a technical framework for material procurement. In practice, selecting materials for a pharmaceutical cleanroom usually requires evaluating:

  • Particle shedding: the particle shedding rate of the material surface, which affects the cleanliness grade of the cleanroom
  • Extractables / leachables: chemicals the material may release on contact with cleaning agents or product, which is especially important for injectables and cell therapy products
  • Electrostatic properties: critical in semiconductor plants; in injectable filling, static electricity can attract particles onto glass containers
  • Sterilization compatibility: whether the material can withstand sterilization methods such as autoclave/gamma irradiation/EO
  • Chemical compatibility: whether the material can withstand common disinfectants such as IPA and H₂O₂ VHP

E.3 Regulatory Importance of Supplier Change Notification

  • ICH Q10 §2.7: Supplier Management is one of the elements of the pharmaceutical quality system
  • EU GMP Part 1 §7: requirements for the Approved Suppliers List and control of material purchasing
  • A change in material specification caused by a supplier failing to give notice of a change is a common major compliance problem in pharmaceutical regulation (and may affect the marketing authorization)

Annex F (Informative) - Cleanroom Cleaning: Detailed Guidance

ISO 14644-5:2004 | Pages 33-42 | Informative

F.1 Overview

Cleanrooms are designed to be as free from contamination as possible. Facility and maintenance operations, manufacturing processes, the presence and activity of personnel and other factors may cause contamination to be generated and dispersed on surfaces. Therefore, all surfaces should be cleaned frequently enough to prevent this from becoming a risk to the manufacturing process. When feasible, cleaning should be avoided during manufacturing operations.

F.2 Surface Classification

F.2.2 Critical Surfaces

Surfaces classified as critical are located at and around the point of manufacture or production where contamination can gain direct access to the product or process. These surfaces should be kept the cleanest. Separative devices, including unidirectional airflow equipment, clean benches or workstations, usually help control the cleanliness of these surfaces.

F.2.3 General Cleanroom Surfaces

All surfaces within the cleanroom that are not at the point of production or localized by unidirectional airflow are considered "general". They should be cleaned on a regular basis to prevent transfer of contamination onto critical surfaces.

F.2.4 Surfaces of Changing Rooms and Transfer Areas

Surfaces of changing rooms and transfer areas can become highly contaminated due to the high level of activity. Frequent cleaning is necessary to minimize the level of contamination and to reduce the transfer of contamination into the cleanroom.

F.3 Basic Cleaning

F.3.2 Basic Cleaning Categories

The act of cleaning can be divided into three different categories:

  • Gross cleaning — removal of large particles of contamination usually greater than 50 µm in diameter. Typically found on floors. Construction and equipment maintenance activities also can often generate gross particle contamination.
  • Intermediate cleaning — removal of smaller particles of contamination, typically ranging from 10 µm to 50 µm in diameter. Performed on general cleanroom surfaces — walls, benches and clean hallways.
  • Precision cleaning — needed to remove remaining particulate contamination that is generally less than 10 µm in diameter. Generally employed on or near critical surfaces where product is stored and processed.

F.3.3 Vacuum Cleaning

Vacuum cleaning can be used in gross and intermediate cleaning operations as a basic first step. Vacuum cleaning is a prerequisite, not an alternative, to mopping or wet wiping. Vacuum cleaning should be performed in deliberate, unidirectional strokes to minimize air turbulence at the floor level and at operator height.

F.3.4 Wet Cleaning

Wet cleaning methods where liquid is applied to a surface and removed through wiping or vacuuming:

  • Scrubbing — gross cleaning method using machine or manual methods to remove stains or heavily soiled areas.
  • Mopping — effective method in gross or intermediate cleaning for removing contamination. The mop bucket should be filled with clean-filtered de-ionized or distilled water and changed frequently to avoid recontamination. Two (or multiple) bucket systems can be used to reduce the frequency of rinse water changes.

F.3.5 Damp Cleaning

Wiping produces results that support intermediate and precision cleanliness. Wiping should always be done in unidirectional, overlapping strokes, proceeding from most critical to least critical areas, following the direction of unidirectional airflow. Wipers should be folded to provide an unused surface area. The wiper should be replaced as frequently as needed.

F.4 Cleaning Specific Surfaces

F.4.2 Floors and Subfloors

Gross contamination can be removed first by vacuum cleaning. The floor should be wet or damp mopped. Cleaning should begin in critical areas and proceed through general areas. Larger floor areas should be divided into manageable segments.

F.4.3 Walls, Doors, Return Grilles

In unidirectional flow cleanrooms, surfaces upstream from product exposure should never be cleaned in the operational state. Upstream surfaces should only be cleaned in the at-rest state or after products have been removed.

F.4.4 Ceilings, Diffusers and Lamp Fixtures

Ceilings and other fixtures upstream or close to work areas should not be cleaned in the operational state but should wait for at-rest conditions. Some diffusers may require removal for washing or replacement.

F.4.5 Tables and Critical Horizontal Surfaces

Damp wipers can be used to remove contamination, working in unidirectional strokes from most to least critical areas.

F.4.7 Stationary Equipment

Surfaces of stationary equipment should be cleaned according to the risk presented. Fluid piping, electrical wires and connections — extreme care must be taken to avoid damaging during the cleaning operation.

F.4.11 Rubbish Bins and Containers

Rubbish should be removed before it collects in excess. Plastic bags or liners should never be removed from bins in the vicinity of critical areas. All bins should be removed to general, non-critical areas before any rubbish is removed.

F.5 Surface Treatment

F.5.3 Disinfection

Thorough cleaning programmes help to control micro-organisms. The effectiveness of the disinfectants and the methods used for disinfection should be determined. Disinfectant efficacy is a function of the type of disinfectant, the concentration of the disinfectant, the temperature of the solution and its contact time on the surface being disinfected. Some disinfectants can damage cleanroom surfaces (e.g. chlorine-based compounds on stainless steel). Disinfectants may be toxic not only when in direct contact with the product, but also when a residue remains on surfaces. It may be appropriate to remove such residues by properly rinsing the surfaces.

F.6 Cleaning Personnel

A specific training program should be provided for any personnel performing the cleaning operation. Specific personnel should be designated for each part of the cleaning programme. Operators with proper training are often assigned to clean the work surfaces.

F.7 Cleaning Programme

F.7.1 Preparing a Cleaning Programme — 11 Steps

  1. Classify all surfaces into critical, general or other surfaces.
  2. Determine the best cleaning and surface treatment method for achieving the desired cleanliness level.
  3. Determine the cleaning frequency required to maintain the desired cleanliness levels.
  4. Determine which cleaning operations can be accomplished during normal operating hours.
  5. Prepare the cleaning schedules.
  6. Decide which part of the cleaning schedule operators will execute and which part cleaning staff will execute.
  7. Choose the correct materials, machines, cleaning solutions and surface treatments.
  8. Train all personnel for the expected level of involvement in the cleaning programme.
  9. Provide adequate storage facilities for the required cleaning materials.
  10. Decide how to monitor the cleaning results and react to discrepancies.
  11. Organize all documents and schedules so that they can be reviewed and managed effectively.

F.7.3 Regular Cleaning

Changing areas and transfer areas should be cleaned at least once per day. These areas can harbour high contamination levels, due to the high level of personnel activity.

F.7.4 Periodic Cleaning

Many surfaces should be cleaned on a weekly basis. Arrangements should also be made to thoroughly clean the entire cleanroom facility, from top-to-bottom, on a scheduled basis. Continuously operating cleanrooms are only shut down sporadically.

F.7.5 Cleaning during and after Construction or Maintenance

Effective cleaning during cleanroom construction is essential. A sample 10-stage cleaning schedule in F.9 can be used to aid in planning, executing, and documenting efforts.

F.8 Monitoring Cleaning Effectiveness

F.8.1 Particle Contamination

An acceptable degree of cleanliness should be determined for each element. Visual inspection techniques: visual-clean surfaces demonstrate an absence of contamination seen without magnification. Wiper-clean surfaces can be demonstrated by passing a clean wiper over a clean surface. Other methods include tape lift and surface particle detector.

F.8.2 Microbiological Contamination

Methods for detecting microbiological contamination: contact plates (for flat surfaces); surface swabbing (for uneven surfaces).

F.9 Construction-Related Cleaning Programme (Table F.1)

A 10-stage programme for scheduling, assigning and documenting cleaning procedures during construction:

NOTE 1: During Stages 4 to 10, all high-efficiency and ultra-high-purity components should arrive on site protected by plastic or foil covers. Covers should only be removed when ready for use.

NOTE 2: During Stages 6 to 10, all activities should be done wearing prescribed cleanroom clothing.

Annex F In-Depth Analysis

Regulatory Link: PDA TR70 and EU GMP Annex 1 Disinfection Requirements

The core requirements of PDA Technical Report No. 70 correspond to F.5.3:

  • Disinfectant Rotation: rotate at least two disinfectants with different mechanisms of action to prevent resistance. For example: QAC (quaternary ammonium compound) alternating with IPA, or QAC alternating with sodium hypochlorite
  • Periodic use of a sporicidal agent: such as peracetic acid (PAA) or chlorine-based disinfectants, used regularly (for example weekly or monthly) to cover bacterial spores
  • Disinfectant efficacy validation: perform in-house efficacy testing against the strains isolated from the facility environment
  • EU GMP Annex 1 §4.34: disinfection procedures must be validated for effectiveness; §4.35: disinfectant efficacy must be confirmed at least once a year
  • Contact time in F.5.3: the disinfectant must remain in contact with the surface for sufficient time (generally ≥ 5-10 minutes) to achieve a bactericidal effect; a "quick wipe" is ineffective

The Four Determinants of Disinfectant Efficacy (F.5.3)

Disinfection efficacy = f(Type, Concentration, Temperature, Contact Time)

The four determinants of disinfection efficacy:
1. Type of disinfectant:
   • IPA 70%: effective against bacteria/fungi, not against spores
   • QAC (e.g. benzalkonium chloride): broad spectrum, but resistance issues
   • Sodium hypochlorite (bleach): broad spectrum including spores; highly corrosive
   • Peracetic acid (PAA): broad spectrum including spores; breaks down quickly, low residue
   • H₂O₂ VHP: broad spectrum including spores; suited to space disinfection

2. Concentration:
   • Too low: insufficient kill
   • Too high: toxicity/residue/material damage

3. Temperature:
   • Generally, the higher the temperature, the better the kill efficiency
   • Cold environments need special consideration

4. Contact time:
   • The most critical! The surface must stay wet for at least the specified time
   • "Wipe dry immediately after applying" = ineffective disinfection

Analogy: F.3.5 unidirectional wiping is like cleaning a medical wound

The correct way to clean a wound: wipe outward from the center of the wound (to prevent external contamination being carried into it), and change the cotton ball after each wipe (to prevent repeated contamination). The logic of unidirectional wiping in cleanroom precision cleaning is the same: wipe outward from the most critical area, fold the wiper to expose a fresh face and continue, and discard it after use.

The Significance of the F.9 Ten-Stage Construction Cleaning Plan

The 10-stage cleaning plan in Table F.1 is the standard framework for "progressive cleaning" of a newly built cleanroom. The cleanliness standard at each stage rises gradually from visual-clean to wiper-clean, ensuring that construction contamination has been progressively removed before the HEPA filters are installed.

  • Key turning point, Stages 5-6: before installing HEPA/ULPA filters, the wiper-clean standard must be reached, to prevent construction contamination from damaging or clogging the filters
  • Stage 7 Air Flushing: turn on the air handling system to "flush" the room, using airflow to carry away airborne contamination
  • Stage 9 Certification: the formal classification certification tests, including particle count, air velocity, temperature and humidity, and pressure differential
  • Stage 10: enter the routine cleaning mode; at this point the biocleanroom begins to build its microbiological baseline data

"Record and Verification" Requirements for Cleaning SOPs

The 11-step cleaning program in F.7 and the monitoring requirements in F.8 correspond to the documentation standards for pharmaceutical cleaning SOPs:

  • Cleaning Log: completed immediately after each cleaning, recording date and time, personnel, disinfectant used (lot number, concentration), area cleaned, and result
  • Environmental monitoring integration: EM sampling results after cleaning are direct evidence that cleaning is effective
  • Trend Analysis: statistical analysis of long-term environmental monitoring data to identify early signals of declining cleaning effectiveness
  • Cleaning Validation: demonstrates that the cleaning SOP repeatedly and effectively achieves the predetermined cleanliness standard, and must be supported by experimental data

Introduction

Why Separative Devices? The Separation Continuum Concept

Original Text

In the spirit of the generic requirements of an International Standard, the term separative devices was developed by Technical Committee ISO/TC 209 to encompass the wide continuum of configurations from open unrestricted air overspill to wholly contained systems. Common terms-of-trade, such as clean air hoods, gloveboxes, isolators and mini-environments, have different meanings depending on the specific industry.

Difficulties experienced in the manufacture and handling of certain products or materials have driven the development of separative devices. These difficulties include:

  • Product sensitivity to particles, chemicals, gases or microorganisms
  • Operator sensitivity to the process materials or byproducts
  • Both product and operator sensitivity

Separative devices provide assured protection in varying levels by utilising physical or dynamic barriers, or both, to create separation between operation and operator. Certain processes may require special atmospheres to prevent degradation or explosions. Some systems may be capable of providing 100% recirculation of the contained atmosphere to allow inert gas operation or biodecontamination with reactive gases.

Usually people do not work directly inside the separative-device environment during production. These separative devices may be movable or fixed, and used for transport, transfer and process. The product or process, or both, are manipulated remotely with access devices either manually, with protection by barrier technology such as wall-integrated personal interface systems (e.g. gloves, gauntlets, half-suits), or mechanically with robotic handling systems.

Air cleanliness definitions and test methods covered in ISO 14644-1, 14644-2 and 14644-3 generally apply within separative devices. In applications with biological contamination requirements, ISO 14698-1 and 14698-2 will apply. However, some applications can have special requirements for monitoring because of extreme conditions that may be encountered.

Transfer devices to move material in and out of separative devices form an important portion of this part of ISO 14644. Mini-environments are often used in the electronics industry with transport containers, called boxes or pods, to provide very clean process conditions.

From a unifying conceptual standpoint, a continuum of separation exists between the operation and the operator, ranging from totally open to totally enclosed systems depending on the application. Similarly, a continuum exists for containment.

Trainer's Analysis

Why Do We Need Separative Devices? Three Drivers

The opening of ISO 14644-7 names three practical drivers behind the development of separative devices:

  • Product protection: the product is sensitive to particles, chemicals, gases or microorganisms. A typical case is a sterile injectable, which needs an ISO 5 / Grade A environment
  • Operator protection: the operator is sensitive to process materials or by-products. Typical cases are highly potent APIs (HPAPI) and cytotoxic drugs, which need negative-pressure containment
  • Dual protection: both product and personnel must be protected. This is the most demanding scenario, for example radiopharmaceutical preparation

Once you understand these three drivers, you understand why separative device designs vary so widely.

Analogy: The "Separation Continuum" Is Like Grades of Protective Clothing

Imagine a chemist who has to handle a hazardous substance:

  • Gloves only = open unidirectional-flow hood (unrestricted air overspill)
  • Safety goggles + gloves = restricted-airflow device
  • Lab coat + gloves + goggles = semi-open device
  • Full protective suit = glovebox
  • Working inside a separate sealed chamber = fully enclosed isolator

The higher the level of protection, the better the resistance to outside disturbance, but the lower the operating flexibility and the higher the equipment cost. That is the essence of the "continuum": there is no single right answer, only the best choice for the risk.

Regulatory Link: Barrier Technology in EU GMP Annex 1 (2022)

Section 8, "Barrier Technology", of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) clearly distinguishes RABS from isolators:

  • RABS (Restricted Access Barrier System): a physical barrier restricts access, but the air is in communication with the background environment (Grade B)
  • Isolator: complete physical separation, with chemical decontamination (VHP) possible inside, which can lower the background environment requirement to Grade C or even D

Annex 1 clause 8.2: "Where isolators are used as an alternative to a conventional Grade A/B environment, the background environment for an isolator used for aseptic processing should be at least Grade D."

This means that using an isolator is not only a technical choice but also a regulator-recognised route to a lower-grade background environment.

Key Formula: Separation Descriptor [Aa:Bb]

This is one of the most important conceptual tools in ISO 14644-7:

Format: [Aa:Bb]
A = ISO class inside the device
a = measured particle size (micrometres)
B = ISO class outside the device
b = measured particle size (micrometres)

Example: [ISO5(0.5):ISO7(0.5)]
Meaning: inside the device ISO Class 5 (0.5 µm),
         outside the device ISO Class 7 (0.5 µm)

This descriptor tells you:
- This device maintains a difference of 2 classes between inside and outside
- A Grade A filling isolator typically has a separation capability of [ISO5:ISO8]

Note: the Separation Descriptor describes performance "under the specified test conditions". It is not a guarantee of continuous performance.

Key Formula: Hourly Leak Rate Rh

Rh = q / V

Rh = hourly leak rate (h⁻¹, reciprocal hours)
q  = leakage volume per hour (m³/h)
V  = volume of the separative device (m³)

ISO 10648-2 four-class system:
Class 1: Rh ≤ 5×10⁻⁴ h⁻¹ → high pressure integrity (High)
Class 2: Rh < 2.5×10⁻³ h⁻¹ → medium pressure integrity (Medium)
Class 3: Rh < 10⁻² h⁻¹ → low pressure integrity (Low)
Class 4: Rh < 10⁻¹ h⁻¹ → lowest class

Worked example:
Isolator volume = 0.5 m³
Pressure change after 1 hour of testing = 5 Pa (on a 1000 Pa basis)
Leak rate Rh ≈ 5/1000 = 5×10⁻³ h⁻¹ (Class 2)

Alert Level vs Action Level: Two Lines of Defence in Monitoring

  • Alert Level: once exceeded, it gives an early warning and calls for "increased attention". Like a yellow alert, it does not require production to stop immediately, but the trend must be recorded and investigated
  • Action Level: once exceeded, it requires "immediate intervention, investigation of the cause and corrective action". Like a red alert, it may require production to stop

EU GMP Annex 1 gives more detailed requirements for Alert/Action Levels, consistent with this standard. When drafting an EM (environmental monitoring) program, a CDMO must clearly set both levels.

Trainer's Analysis

Core Principle for Transfer Devices: They Must Not Become a Weak Point

The rule in 7.1, "must not reduce the performance of the separative device", is the golden principle. The transfer device is the most common integrity weak point of an isolator, because it must be periodically "opened" to let materials in and out. The design challenge is how to maintain the maximum degree of separation while it is open.

Another key principle of 7.2: the leak rate of the transfer device must not be greater than the leak rate of the separative device itself. This means:

  • If the isolator needs Class 1 (Rh ≤ 5×10⁻⁴), its RTP must also achieve the same or a better leak rate
  • You cannot "downgrade" a high-integrity isolator with a lower-class transfer port

Transfer Device Selection Criteria: Classification per Annex D

Transfer device leak requirement:
Transfer device Rh(transfer) ≤ isolator Rh(isolator)

Annex D classification of transfer devices (see the Annex D analysis for details):
Type A (A1, A2) = lowest separation: air can pass freely
Type B (B1, B2) = medium separation: double-door seal, can be evacuated
Type C (C1, C2) = with HEPA filtration: filters particles but not fully enclosed
Type D (D1, D2) = double HEPA filtration + time delay
Type E = can be sterilised/decontaminated

Selection rule: the higher the separation class of the isolator, the higher the class of transfer device it needs.

The "fail-safe" principle is critical in pharmaceutical isolator design:

  • On power failure, the electronic interlock must default to the locked state ("fail-locked")

Glove Breach Test: Detailed Look at 9.2

Test method:
Place an anemometer in the centre of the open glove port
Measure the air velocity through the opening

Guide value: ≥ 0.5 m/s (minimum protective velocity)

Physical meaning:
Positive-pressure isolator (for aseptic filling):

  - It prevents the external environment (particles/microorganisms) from entering the isolator

Negative-pressure isolator (for toxic HPAPI):
  - Negative internal pressure → air flows in from outside
  - When a glove breaks, the negative pressure ensures toxic material does not escape
  - It protects the operator from exposure to toxic drugs

reverse flow of the vast majority of particles
(ISO 14644-4 also cites this value)

9.3 Pressure Differential Monitoring: The Requirement for Continuous Monitoring

The phrase "should be continuously monitored and alarmed" in 9.3.2 is in practice mandatory for pharmaceutical aseptic applications:

  • EU GMP Annex 1: the pressure differential of an isolator must be continuously monitored with an alarm, and trend data must be recorded
  • Typical settings: positive-pressure isolator +15 Pa (range +10 to +30 Pa); negative-pressure isolator -15 Pa
  • Alert Level: usually set at the target value ±5 Pa; when triggered, increase observation
  • Action Level: usually set at the target value ±10 Pa; when triggered, immediately assess whether to stop production
  • Test in two states (9.3.1): the pressure differential may differ between the at-rest and operational states, and both must be recorded and confirmed

Induction Leak: An Easily Overlooked Risk

The NOTE in 9.4.2 explains the "induction leak" (Venturi effect):

  • Principle: high-velocity airflow through a small opening creates a local negative pressure, which instead "sucks" outside air into the device
  • Trigger: inserting or withdrawing a glove causes a brief change in volume/pressure inside the isolator
  • Danger: especially dangerous for devices with low differential pressure (such as some RABS), where a momentary reverse flow can carry in outside particles

This is why glove insertion technique (operator training) is so important: insert slowly and steadily, and avoid rapid movements that cause pressure shocks.

Original Text

A separative device utilises physical means, aerodynamic means, or both, to create improved levels of separation between the inside and outside of a defined volume. Physical separation means include both rigid and flexible barriers. Aerodynamic means include air/gas flow with or without filtration.

Generally, the assurance of maintaining separation increases with the degree of rigidity of the physical separation. Two measures of this separation are:

  • The separation descriptor [Aa:Bb] — a convenient measure when hourly leak rate is not appropriate
  • The hourly leak rate (Rh) — ISO 10648-2 provides a four-level classification system

ISO 10648-2 classification is generally applied to devices with rigid physical barriers.

Analogy: The Separation Continuum Is Like Upgrading a "Safe Room"

Imagine you want to protect a precious artefact (= the sterile product):

  • Display stand with a glass cover (RABS): there is a barrier, but it can be opened, and the background environment requirement is high

  • Bank vault (high-integrity isolator/nuclear glovebox): fully enclosed, leakage can be measured precisely, and an inert gas atmosphere can be maintained

From the display stand to the vault, the protection level keeps rising, but so do cost and operational complexity. Which one to choose depends on how rare the artefact is and how serious the environmental threat is.

Safe Change Filter: A Key Design Feature for Containment Devices

The "safe change filter" (bag-in bag-out filter, BIBO) mentioned in B.1.1 is a core element of containment isolator design:

  • A HEPA filter is itself contaminated when it is changed (especially in HPAPI applications)

B.3.1 Warning: Inert Gases Can Kill by Asphyxiation

C.1 Scope

This annex is limited to gloves, gauntlets, glove sleeve systems and half-suits. Gloves tend to form the weakest link in the pressure integrity of a separative device. Operator and product protection is limited by choice of glove system and glove material.

C.2 Glove materials

Common glove materials include:

  • a) Latex (natural rubber): Great flexibility and good mechanical properties. However, not impermeable to gas, perishes in ozone, no flame resistance, poor resistance to esters, acids and bases. Potential for life-threatening allergic reactions should be considered.

  • d) Poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC): Good electrical properties and chemical resistance. Certain elasticity.

The Core Statement of C.1: Gloves Are the Weakest Link in Integrity

"Gloves tend to form the weakest link in the pressure integrity" is the theme of the whole of Annex C.

Practical implications:

  • The overall integrity of the isolator is limited by the integrity of its gloves
  • Even if the device body has Class 1 pressure integrity, molecular containment cannot be achieved if the glove permeability is high (see 6.2.2.1)

  • The exchange of old and new gloves must ensure that a "barrier" is always present
  • The whole process must have a standard operating procedure and be practised regularly (C.7.2 explicitly requires "regular practice")

This corresponds directly to the training and qualification requirements of EU GMP Annex 1: operators must demonstrate their ability to change gloves.

Trainer's Analysis

Analogy: The Type F RTP (Alpha-Beta Port) Is Like a Space Station Airlock

How the type F transfer device (rapid transfer port, RTP, also called an Alpha-Beta port) works:

  • Alpha (female port): permanently mounted on the isolator body, with a sealing mechanism
  • Beta (male port): mounted on the container/transfer package, and detachable
  • Docking process: the Beta port approaches the Alpha port → the two sealing faces dock → the inner doors open in sync → material is transferred in under "zero exposure" conditions

It is like a space station airlock: the docking module (spacecraft) docks with the station (isolator), the inner doors open, and the astronaut (material) is transferred without being exposed to outer space (the external environment).

The external contact surface of the RTP (the outer surface of the Beta port) can be disinfected with VHP spray or alcohol before docking.

Serious Warning on C1: Not for Containment Negative-Pressure Devices

D.6 states clearly that "C1 transfer devices are NOT suitable for negative-pressure separative devices" and "NOT recommended where operator and third-party protection is required".

The reason: the HEPA filter of the C1 is on one side only (the isolator side). In negative-pressure mode, unfiltered air from the outside (the background environment) would enter the isolator directly. For a toxic drug this is catastrophic, because toxic material could leak outward through the background environment air.

At design review, confirm that an HPAPI isolator uses a C2 (negative pressure) or higher-class transfer device, and never a C1.

CDMO Practice: How to Choose the Type of Transfer Device

Transfer device selection guide by application scenario:

  • Aseptic filling isolator (positive pressure): routine material in/out → B1 or D1; containers/packaging in/out → type F RTP
  • HPAPI filling isolator (negative pressure): material in/out → B1 or C2; waste out → A1 (bag-out); special cases → type E (can be decontaminated)
  • Nuclear medicine preparation hot cell: material in/out → B2 (must be inert-gas compatible); waste → type E (needs decontamination)
  • Electronics mini-environment: wafer transfer → type F (SMIF/FOUP pod interface)

Acceptance Criteria for the Glove Leak Test (E.5 in Detail)

Judging a glove leak test is very straightforward:

  • Negative-pressure device (E.5.2): attach a sealing plate to the glove port and watch the connected pressure gauge. If the glove is intact, the gauge stays stable (±2-10 Pa); if it is damaged, the gauge reading gradually drifts negative (for example -10, -15, -19 Pa).
  • Positive-pressure device (E.5.3): cover the glove port with a sealing cap and inflate to 500-1000 Pa. If the glove is intact, the pressure holds steady; if it is damaged, the pressure gradually falls (500, 495, 490 Pa).
  • Repeat any doubtful result: a damaged glove shows the same decay pattern on every test, so it is easy to confirm.

Based on Annex E, when establishing a leak test program you need to decide:

  2. Glove test frequency: EU GMP requires it to be risk-based, usually before and after each batch or shift
  3. Overall device leak rate test: usually performed at IQ/OQ/PQ, with periodic re-confirmation (for example every six months or every year)

Annex F (Informative): Parjo Leak Test Method

Quick, non-intrusive leak rate assessment for devices operating near atmospheric pressure

Original Text

F.1 Background

Parjo is the name given to a method for the leak rate assessment of separative devices operating at pressures close to atmospheric. The method was originated by and named after K. Parkinson and W. F. Jones. This test provides a (relatively) quick and versatile method of determining leak rates. It can be used on contaminated devices providing the pressure tapping is suitably protected, thus avoiding lengthy down times — there are no intrusive test instruments. The short duration of the test tends to reduce effects of changes in temperature and atmospheric pressure. It is a sensitive test for minor leaks.

F.2 Principle

The Parjo method uses a pressure-sensitive detergent film (meniscus) injected into a glass tube of known dimensions and a reference vessel of known volume. With stable conditions and the device isolated at test pressure, any change in separative-device pressure is reflected by movement of the piston (meniscus) towards the lower pressure. This movement represents a volume change.

Keeping observations short (no more than 5 min), temperature and barometric variations can be ignored.

F.3 Equipment

F.3.2 Equipment list:

  • Parjo tube Type A (glass tube of known bore)
  • Metric scale (clip on)
  • Spring clips
  • Rubber stopper (bored for Parjo tube, 19mm or 21mm)
  • Clear glass bottle — volume 2500 cm³
  • Propipette rubber bulb with 3 valves
  • Rubber tubing (6mm bore)
  • U-tube manometer or capsule gauge
  • Stop watch
  • Needle valve (for introducing controlled leak)
  • Pressure/vacuum source
  • Bubble solution: 50/50 % household liquid soap and clean tap water

F.4 Procedure

F.4.2 Key steps:

  • Seal device and set test pressure: +1000 Pa or as specified
  • Allow ~30 min for temperature equilibration
  • Inject bubble into Parjo tube via propipette to form meniscus
  • Observe meniscus movement and record time (3-5 min observation window)
  • If device is negative pressure and leaking: bubble moves towards reference vessel
  • If device is positive pressure and leaking: bubble moves away from reference vessel
  • Measure deflection d (cm) over time t (min) and calculate Rh

F.5 Calculation of results

F.5.2 Formula:

Rh = (Ap × d × 60) / (Vr × t)

Where:

  • Ap = cross-sectional area of Parjo tube = 0.127 cm² (for 4mm bore tube)
  • d = deflection of meniscus (cm)
  • Vr = volume of reference bottle (cm³) — typically 2500 cm³ or 2700 cm³
  • t = time (min)

F.5.3 Examples:

  • 0.8 cm deflection in 5 min, 2500 cm³ bottle: Rh = 0.127 × 0.8 × 60 / (2500 × 5) = 4.88 × 10⁻⁴ h⁻¹
  • 1.0 cm deflection in 5 min, 2700 cm³ bottle: Rh = 0.127 × 1 × 60 / (2700 × 5) = 5.64 × 10⁻⁴ h⁻¹
  • 1.5 cm deflection in 3 min, 2700 cm³ bottle: Rh = 0.127 × 1.5 × 60 / (2700 × 3) = 1.41 × 10⁻³ h⁻¹

F.5.4 Test certification

If no detectable deflection, introduce a controlled leak within acceptable limits (via needle valve) to prove the test method is working. State on certificate: "actual leak rate does not exceed the introduced leak and is thus acceptable."

Known parameters (standard apparatus):

- Vr = 2500 cm³ (standard glass bottle)

Interpretation:
The smaller the Rh = the less the leakage = the better the integrity
The Rh of a Class 1 isolator should be ≤ 5×10⁻⁴ h⁻¹

Practical Tips and Common Problems with the Parjo Test

Key tips:

  • Choice of glass bottle: it must be a clear glass bottle (not coloured), so that the bubble can be observed
  • Soap solution preparation: 50/50 good-quality liquid soap and tap water; cheap dishwashing liquid leaves residues that affect the liquid film
  • Temperature equilibration: after setup, wait at least 30 minutes so that all components reach the same temperature
  • Secondary bubbles: before taking a reading, make sure there are no secondary bubbles at either end of the Parjo tube, otherwise they will interfere with the result

The wisdom of F.4.2: "Introduce a controlled leak even when no leak is visible"

If the leak rate of the device is below the detection limit of the test method, the standard allows a small controlled leak (needle valve) to be introduced to verify that the test method is working. This prevents the situation where the test method itself has failed but the device is wrongly believed to be intact.

CDMO Practice: When to Use the Parjo Method?

The Parjo method is especially suitable for the following scenarios:

  • Periodic in-production monitoring: quick integrity confirmation before and after each batch (without a 1-hour shutdown)
  • Quick confirmation after maintenance: rapid leak confirmation after isolator maintenance (such as a glove change)
  • Testing of contaminated equipment: an HPAPI containment isolator does not expose the test personnel to the contaminant
  • IQ/OQ acceptance tests: quickly confirm the baseline leak rate after installation

The Position of ISO 14644-7 in the Regulatory Ecosystem

ISO 14644-7 provides the "general skeleton", and industry-specific standards provide the "flesh":

ISO 14644-7 (general framework)
↓ Pharmaceutical application
ISO 13408-6 (aseptic processing)
↓ Regulatory requirements
EU GMP Annex 1 + FDA guidance + PDA TR34

In practice, the requirements of ISO 14644-7 are a "minimum standard", and pharmaceutical applications must layer stricter regulatory requirements on top.

RABS vs Isolator: Regulatory Considerations in the Technology Choice

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) does not ban RABS outright, but clearly favours isolators:

  • Annex 1 §8.2：「The use of isolators offers the greatest protection.」
  • Trend for new facilities: regulators increasingly expect to see isolators at PAI (Pre-Approval Inspection), especially for new products
  • Existing facilities: a well-qualified RABS + Grade B background environment + a rigorous EM program can still be acceptable

When choosing the technology route, a CDMO should consider the regulatory leaning of its main future markets (the positions of the FDA, EMA and NMPA may differ).
