Foreword: How ISO 11608-1 Developed

Original Text

Foreword

ISO (the International Organization for Standardization) is a worldwide federation of national standards bodies (ISO member bodies). The work of preparing International Standards is normally carried out through ISO technical committees.

The committee responsible for this document is ISO/TC 84, Devices for administration of medicinal products and intravascular catheters.

This third edition cancels and replaces the second edition (ISO 11608-1:2012), which has been technically revised.

ISO 11608 consists of the following parts, under the general title Needle-based injection systems for medical use — Requirements and test methods:

  • Part 1: Needle-based injection systems (this document)
  • Part 2: Needles
  • Part 3: Finished containers
  • Part 4: Requirements and test methods for electronic and electromechanical pen-injectors
  • Part 5: Automated functions

Key corrections in this third edition include: clarified dose accuracy testing conditions (70 °C and −40 °C), updated EMC sample sizes (5 NISs → 20 NISs), temperature range adjustment for cyclical testing.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Role of ISO/TC 84

ISO/TC 84 (Technical Committee 84) is responsible for international standards on administration devices for medicinal products and intravascular catheters. The ISO 11608 series is led by this committee, whose members include injection device manufacturers worldwide (BD, Becton Dickinson, etc.) and drug regulatory authorities.

ISO 11608 has five parts, each with a clear scope:

  • Part 1: Requirements for the overall system (this standard)
  • Part 2: Needle specifications
  • Part 3: Container (cartridge/syringe) specifications
  • Part 4: Electronic/electromechanical pen injectors
  • Part 5: Automated functions (autoinjectors)

Analogy

Think of the ISO 11608 series as a set of car safety standards: Part 1 governs the performance of the whole vehicle, Part 2 governs the tires, and Part 3 governs the fuel tank. Each part has its own responsibility, but system verification requires compliance with all of them.

For a CDMO, an autoinjector brought in by a customer involves Part 1 (the system) plus Part 5 (automated functions), and compliance with both must be confirmed during technology transfer.

Key Point: Important Changes in the Third Edition

The 2014 third edition made several key revisions that affect test plans:

  • The sample size for EMC testing increased from 5 to 20 units (more stringent)
  • The cycling test temperature changed from 25°C to 5°C ± 3°C
  • System designs B and D were removed from the free fall test

If a customer's Design Verification (DV) report is based on the 2012 edition (second edition), confirm whether the key differences have been retested.

Introduction: Design Philosophy and the Risk-Based Approach

Original Text

Introduction

This part of ISO 11608 covers needle-based injection systems (NISs) primarily intended for human use. It provides performance requirements regarding essential aspects so that variations of design are not unnecessarily restricted.

"The first edition... introduced the concept of interchangeability and the labelling designations 'Type A' (i.e. interchangeable)... Based on this experience, it is believed that the Type A designation does not represent adequate guidance to the user in making decisions on the compatibility of needles and containers with specific needle-based injector systems. As such, the labelling designation 'Type A' has been removed."

The design requirements related to system function have been maintained as a guide to assist manufacturers during the design phase, supporting the achievement of cross-platform compatibility. However, these design requirements are an insufficient replacement for system testing of the components and, where possible, direct communication and/or quality agreements between system component manufacturers.

Therefore, manufacturers of needles, containers and needle-based injectors shall label their products with the specific system components that have been tested and demonstrated to be functionally compatible.

The sampling plans for inspection selected for this part of ISO 11608 are intended to verify the design at a high confidence level. The sampling plans do not replace the more general manufacturing quality systems that appear in standards on quality systems, for example the ISO 9000 series and ISO 13485.

Manufacturers are expected to follow a risk-based approach during the design, development and manufacture of the product. This might result in product-specific requirements and test methods that differ from what is outlined in this part of ISO 11608.

Tutorial Commentary

Background: Why Was "Type A Compatibility" Withdrawn?

Earlier versions of ISO 11608-1 tried to establish a "universal needle specification" (Type A) so that needles and syringes from different manufacturers would be interchangeable. The idea was sound, but in practice:

  • Tolerance stack-up between components meant that overall function could not be guaranteed once parts from multiple manufacturers were combined
  • Customers often assumed Type A meant 100% interchangeability and skipped system-level testing
  • The result: more recalls and nonconformances

The third edition's solution: withdraw the Type A label, require manufacturers to state clearly which combinations of components have been tested, and encourage quality agreements between suppliers.

Key Point: Direct Impact on CDMOs

What this revision means for a CDMO:

  • A customer's autoinjector must come with an integrated test report covering the needle, container and injector together; a statement of "conforms to Type A" is not enough
  • During technology transfer, the CDMO should ask the customer for a Functional Compatibility Matrix
  • If the CDMO sources needles itself, it must confirm it has compatibility test data against the customer's injector system

Analogy: Risk-Based Design

The standard says that following a risk-based approach may lead to product-specific requirements that differ from those in this standard. This is not an evasion of responsibility; it acknowledges that:

An insulin pen injector (multiple injections per day, precise dosing) and a vaccine autoinjector (single use, fixed 0.5 mL dose) have completely different risk profiles, so one set of test specifications cannot be applied to both.

This is exactly why ISO 14971 (risk management) is listed as a normative reference: do the risk assessment first, then decide which tests apply.

Practice Questions

  1. A customer brings a new autoinjector and claims the needle conforms to the old Type A specification. Under the third edition of ISO 11608-1, what additional documents should the CDMO ask the customer to provide?
  2. ISO 11608-1 states that a sampling plan cannot replace an ISO 13485 quality management system. What does this mean within a CDMO's QMS framework?

1 Scope

This part of ISO 11608 specifies requirements and test methods for needle-based injection systems (NISs) intended to be used with needles and with replaceable or non-replaceable containers. Containers covered include:

  • Single- and multi-dose syringe-based systems
  • Cartridge-based systems
  • Manufacturer-filled and user-filled systems

Understanding the Scope of NIS

NIS types covered by ISO 11608-1 (using products common in CDMOs as examples):

  • ✅ PFS (prefilled syringe): manufacturer-filled, single dose, includes a needle system
  • ✅ Pen Injector: used with a replaceable cartridge, multiple doses
  • ✅ Autoinjector: preset dose, automatic drive → ISO 11608-5 also applies

  • ❌ Needle-free injectors (e.g., jet injectors)
  • ❌ User-filled devices (e.g., the BD Uniject filling machine)

Why Normative References Matter: ISO 14971 Is Central

ISO 14971 (risk management for medical devices) is listed as a normative reference, which means: without an ISO 14971 risk analysis, you cannot claim compliance with ISO 11608-1.

What this means for a CDMO:

  • The customer's Design History File (DHF) must include an ISO 14971 Risk Management File
  • During technology transfer review, confirm that the Risk Management File has been updated to reflect the CDMO's manufacturing environment
  • IEC 62366 (usability engineering) is also a mandatory reference → human factors study data is required

Analogy: Normative vs. Informative References

Normative references: like "in accordance with this Act..." in a legal text, the cited standard is a necessary condition for implementing this standard and cannot be followed selectively.

Informative references: like the "explanatory notes" in a legal text, they provide background knowledge but are not mandatory.

In the Normative References list of ISO 11608-1, both ISO 14971 and ISO 13485 are mandatory. This means that when an auditor reviews ISO 11608-1 compliance, they will also check the Risk Management File and QMS records.

Tutorial Commentary

Interpreting the Terms: NIS from a CDMO Perspective

NIS (needle-based injection system) is the central term of this standard, and it covers a wider range than most people expect:

  • A prefilled syringe (PFS) on its own is a container (governed by Part 3); adding a needle makes it an NIS (Part 1)
  • An insulin pen injector is a multi-dose NIS with a replaceable cartridge
  • An autoinjector is an NIS with an automatic drive function (it must additionally comply with Part 5)

A CDMO's responsibility usually ends at manufacturing and filling the container (PFS/cartridge), while the customer is responsible for device assembly. However, the device (NIS) design verification data still needs to be reviewed during technology transfer.

Why Dose Accuracy Matters

Dose accuracy is one of the core requirements of ISO 11608-1. Several key concepts:

  • Vset: the three set doses selected for testing (minimum, intermediate, maximum)
  • Dose Delivery Efficiency: applies especially to single-dose systems (B1, D1) and evaluates what proportion of the filled volume can be delivered
  • Minimum Deliverable Dose: the lowest dose the manufacturer guarantees to deliver, which directly affects safety claims

For a CDMO, precise control of fill volume directly affects the customer's Dose Delivery Efficiency data and is a key indicator in filling process validation.

Reading the Symbols: The k Value (Tolerance Limit Factor)

The k (tolerance limit factor) in the symbol table is the statistical core of the whole dose accuracy test:

  • The k value is looked up in the large statistical tables in Annex B, based on the sample size n and the probability content p
  • Acceptance criterion: x̄ + k·s ≤ upper limit, and x̄ − k·s ≥ lower limit
  • The larger the k value, the more lenient the test; the larger n is, the smaller k is, and the more stringent the test

The large tables in Annex B (3000+ rows) intimidate many engineers, but once the underlying statistical logic is understood, the core idea is simply to estimate the population distribution from sample statistics.

Section Summary

  • ISO 11608 is a series of standards: Part 1 (this document) governs the overall NIS, while Parts 2-5 cover needles, containers, electronic components and automated functions respectively; the PFS/autoinjector products a CDMO handles usually involve Parts 1 and 3, and autoinjectors also require Part 5
  • The Type A compatibility concept has been withdrawn: the third edition requires manufacturers to state the verified component combinations based on actual test results, and general compatibility assumptions can no longer be relied on
  • ISO 14971 is a mandatory prerequisite: the normative references of ISO 11608-1 include ISO 14971 (risk management), so without risk analysis documentation full compliance is not possible
  • Dose accuracy is the core: Vset (set dose), Vmeas (measured value) and the k value (tolerance limit factor) form the statistical backbone of the whole test system, and the CDMO's fill accuracy directly affects these indicators

Original Text

5.1 General Requirements

The NIS shall be designated according to 5.2. These designations form the basis for selecting the appropriate test requirements in Section 9 (Test Matrix).

5.2 System Designations

NISs shall be designated as follows, depending on the container type and dose configuration:

Designation A — Replaceable Container, Multi-Dose, Variable

Needle-based injection device with replaceable container. Each container holds multiple doses, the size of which may be fixed or variable (pre-set by the user).

Example: Insulin pen injector with replaceable cartridge

Designation B1 — Replaceable Container, Single Dose (Full Volume)

Needle-based injection device with replaceable container. Each container holds a single dose, whereby the entire deliverable volume is expelled.

Example: Prefilled syringe-based device, full-expulsion

Designation B2 — Replaceable Container, Single Dose (Partial Volume)

Needle-based injection device with replaceable container. Each container holds a single dose, whereby a portion of the deliverable volume is expelled.

Designation C — Integrated Non-Replaceable Container, Multi-Dose

Needle-based injection device with integrated non-replaceable container. Multiple doses, fixed or variable.

Example: Disposable insulin pen (non-refillable)

Designation D1 — Integrated Container, Single Dose (Full Volume)

Needle-based injection device with integrated non-replaceable container. Single dose, entire deliverable volume expelled.

Example: Most autoinjectors with prefilled PFS

Designation D2 — Integrated Container, Single Dose (Partial Volume)

Needle-based injection device with integrated non-replaceable container. Single dose, portion of volume expelled.

Special requirement: shall be designed so that it is impossible to deliver remaining volume after actuation, and impossible to reactivate the device.

Tutorial Commentary

The Most Common in CDMOs: The D1 System

In CDMO aseptic filling, the great majority of autoinjector products fall under designation D1:

  • Integrated container (the PFS is fixed inside the device and cannot be replaced)
  • Single dose (one unit per use)
  • Full delivery (0.5 mL or 1 mL, completely expelled)

Dose accuracy for a D1 system is assessed using Dose Delivery Efficiency (calculated from the residual volume relative to the fill volume), not by upper and lower limits on a fixed dose.

This means the CDMO's fill accuracy (uniformity of fill) directly affects whether a D1 system meets its Dose Delivery Efficiency requirement.

Analogy: A vs. D1

Designation A (insulin pen injector) is like a coffee capsule machine: the machine stays in your hand, you swap in a new capsule when one is used up, and you can choose a different serving size each time.

Designation D1 (autoinjector) is like a disposable lighter: the whole unit cannot be taken apart, it discharges once, and it is thrown away after use.

The dose accuracy test logic of the two is completely different: the former tests "the accuracy of every set dose", while the latter tests "whether the whole contents can be fully expelled".

Section 4: Statistical Symbols in Depth: The Mathematical Basis of Dose Accuracy

Original Text

Key Statistical Symbols

Key Equations

Volume from gravimetric measurement:

Vmeas = Gmeas / ρ   (Equation 1)

Specification limits (dual-mode):

  • If Vset ≤ TP: U = Vset + α; L = Vset − α
  • If Vset > TP: U = Vset × (1 + β/100); L = Vset × (1 − β/100)

Acceptance criterion (two-sided):

  • x̄ + k·s ≤ U and x̄ − k·s ≥ L → Pass
  • where k ≥ ktar from Annex B

kact calculation (two-sided):

kact = min[ (U − x̄)/s, (x̄ − L)/s ]

Tutorial Commentary

The Statistical Logic of Dose Accuracy: From Sample to Population

Dose accuracy verification in ISO 11608-1 uses a statistical tolerance interval method, not the simple "sample mean ± 3σ".

Core logic:

  • We measure n NIS (each with R replicates)
  • Calculate x̄ (mean) and s (standard deviation)
  • Use the k value to widen the confidence interval, ensuring that at least 95% of the population (p=0.95) falls within specification
  • The smaller n is, the larger k is (compensating for uncertainty), and the harder the test is to pass

Interpreting the Formula: Switching Between α and β

ISO 11608-1 uses a "two-segment error specification":

Small doses (V ≤ TP): use absolute error ±α mL
  Example: V_set = 0.1 mL, α = 0.02 mL
  Allowed range: 0.08 ~ 0.12 mL (±20%)

Large doses (V > TP): use relative error ±β%
  Example: V_set = 1.0 mL, β = 5%
  Allowed range: 0.95 ~ 1.05 mL (±5%)

Transition point TP = α / (β/100)

This design reflects a metrological fact: for small doses it makes more sense to express measurement accuracy as an absolute value, and for large doses as a percentage.

Analogy: An Intuitive Feel for the k Value

Imagine you are ordering a batch of cups, with a required capacity of 250 ± 10 mL. You can only measure 5 samples.

If all 5 samples fall between 248 and 252 mL (very uniform), you have high confidence in the whole lot.

But if the 5 samples range from 240 to 260 mL (widely scattered), even with a mean of 250 you cannot be sure the whole lot conforms.

The k value is the compensation factor for this "spread": the larger s is, the larger the k needed to guarantee the same conforming proportion. The large tables in Annex B are the k values precomputed for different combinations of n and p.

Gauge R&R Requirement: The Accuracy Threshold for Test Equipment

Section 6.1 requires Gauge R&R ≤ 20% of the tolerance range (30% for destructive tests).

What this means for CDMO QC:

  • If the allowable error range is ±0.05 mL (0.10 mL in total), the R&R of the test system must not exceed 0.02 mL
  • The customer's submitted DV report should include a Gauge R&R study, and the CDMO should verify it when the equipment is received
  • The gravimetric method is more accurate than the volumetric method, which is why ISO 11608-1 explicitly requires Gmeas to be recorded and converted to Vmeas

Practice Questions

  1. For an NIS with Vmin = 0.2 mL, Vmax = 1.0 mL, absolute error α = 0.04 mL and relative error β = 4%, calculate the transition point TP. (Hint: TP = α / (β/100))
  2. At Vset = 0.5 mL (greater than TP), what are the upper and lower specification limits U and L?
  3. Y = 20 NIS are tested, with 3 replicates each (N = 3). If x̄ = 0.495 mL, s = 0.008 mL, and kact = (0.524 - 0.495)/0.008 = 3.625, and from Annex B for n = 60 (20×3) and p = 0.95, ktar ≈ 1.95, does this batch pass?

Section Summary

  • Six system designations: container replaceability × single/multi-dose × full/partial delivery → determines the test matrix; the autoinjector most common in CDMOs is D1 (integrated, single dose, full delivery)
  • Two-segment error specification: absolute error ±α for small doses, relative error ±β% for large doses, with transition point TP = α/(β/100)
  • Statistical tolerance interval method: x̄ + k·s ≤ U and x̄ − k·s ≥ L; the k value is looked up in Annex B by n and p, compensating for the statistical uncertainty of small samples
  • Gauge R&R ≤ 20%: the accuracy requirement for test equipment, ensuring that the uncertainty of the test results does not dominate the compliance decision; the gravimetric method is the measurement method recommended by ISO 11608-1

Sections 5.3–5.4: Risk Analysis Requirements and Measurement Uncertainty

Original Text

5.3 Risk Analysis Requirements

The manufacturer shall conduct risk assessments in accordance with ISO 14971. These risk assessments shall consider all aspects of the development, manufacture and intended use of the NIS for medical use.

The NIS shall conform to the usability requirements specified in IEC 62366.

5.4 Uncertainty of Measurement and Conformance with Specifications

Measurement uncertainty shall be evaluated and expressed by the laboratory performing the test in accordance with ISO/IEC Guide 98-3 (GUM).

Conformance with specifications is established in accordance with ISO 14253-1.

Note: ISO 14253-1 defines decision rules for proving conformity (or nonconformity) when measurement uncertainty is considered. When the measured value ± uncertainty does not overlap the specification limit, a clear pass/fail can be declared.

Tutorial Commentary

Measurement Uncertainty: Its Effect on CDMO Compliance Decisions

In practice, the "decision rules for proving conformity" of ISO 14253-1 mean:

  • Conservative decisions: if the measured value ± the uncertainty crosses the specification limit, conformity cannot be declared, and either a more accurate test or a more conservative specification is needed
  • This directly affects how a CDMO sets its Internal Acceptance Criteria in filling process validation
  • A CDMO's IAC is usually tighter than the customer's Release Criteria, to leave a buffer for measurement uncertainty

Analogy: How Measurement Uncertainty Affects Conformity

A scale reads 99.5 kg and the specification upper limit is 100 kg. If the scale's measurement uncertainty is ±1 kg, the true value may lie anywhere between 98.5 and 100.5 kg.

The conservative decision rule of ISO 14253-1 says: conformity cannot be declared, because the uncertainty range extends beyond the specification limit.

In NIS dose accuracy testing, this means kact must be clearly greater than ktar, not merely equal to it.

ISO 14971 + IEC 62366: Dual-Track Risk Management

Section 5.3 requires two parallel but complementary risk tools:

For example, an autoinjector's "needle not fully retracted" is an ISO 14971 risk (harm to the patient), while "the user does not know the dose has been completely delivered" is an IEC 62366 usability issue (underdosing). Both must be verified in design verification.

Tutorial Commentary

The Requirements CDMOs Should Watch Most: u and v

Requirement u: Adverse effects of the drug on the device and of the device on the drug:

  • This is the regulatory basis for Extractables & Leachables (E&L) studies
  • The CDMO's drug product is in direct contact with the PFS's silicone coating, rubber plunger and stainless steel needle
  • The customer's Risk Assessment should cover all contact surfaces and comply with ISO 10993-1 (requirement v)
  • During technology transfer, the CDMO needs to confirm that the customer has completed the E&L study and that it covers primary packaging components from the same suppliers/lots as those the CDMO uses

Requirement w: Where the CDMO Can Add Value

Requirement w says: "for tests without acceptance criteria, the manufacturer establishes its own specifications."

This means that certain tests (such as the visual inspection criteria for the free fall test) are defined by the customer based on the risk analysis.

For a CDMO: the technology transfer protocol must clearly define which tests have Acceptance Criteria supplied by the customer and which are mandated by ISO. If the customer's AC is too lenient, the CDMO has a responsibility to challenge it with a risk assessment.

Analogy: The Design Philosophy of the Status Indication Requirements (e-h)

Requirements e-h are essentially "operational feedback design": the user needs confirmation at every step:

  • e: It is set (I selected 1 mL, and the dial makes a sound)
  • f: It is ready (the cap has been removed and the device is ready to inject)
  • g: The state has changed (the device looks different before and after injection, to prevent repeat injection)
  • h: It is finished (you hear a "click" or feel the device retract)

These requirements are the essence of IEC 62366 usability engineering: "an operating error at any step must not lead to harm".

  3. Requirement w says "the manufacturer establishes its own acceptance criteria", but the CDMO considers the customer's drop test criteria too lenient. How should the CDMO record this disagreement in the technology transfer agreement?

  • Dual-track risk management is mandatory: ISO 14971 (performance risk) and IEC 62366 (usability risk) are both mandatory under Section 5.3, and neither can be omitted; in technology transfer, the CDMO should ask the customer for the Risk Management File and the Usability Engineering File

Section 6: Reagents & Apparatus: Test Equipment and Test Liquid Specifications

Original Text

6.1 General — Gauge R&R Requirement

Any suitable test system can be used, when the required accuracy (calibration) and precision (Gauge R&R) can be obtained.

  • Gauge R&R ≤ 20% of the allowed tolerance range (standard)
  • Gauge R&R ≤ 30% for destructive test measurements
  • Gauge R&R shall cover at minimum ± 2 standard deviations (~95% of variation)

Example: Tolerance range = ±0.01 mL (total 0.02 mL). Gauge R&R ratio ≤ 20% → Gauge R&R ≤ 0.004 mL. Measurement uncertainty (±2 SD) = 0.002 mL.

All doses Vset delivered are recorded gravimetrically (Gmeas in grams), then converted to volumes:

Vmeas = Gmeas / ρ   (Equation 1)

6.2 Test Liquid

The test liquid is the original medicinal product intended to be injected by the NIS, or a liquid with similar physical properties.

6.3 Balance

The balance shall have a resolution of 1% of the minimum dose delivery.

Example: Minimum dose = 0.5 mL → Balance resolution ≤ 0.005 mL (≤ 0.005 g for aqueous solutions).

6.4 Test Surface for Free-Fall Testing

Test surface: smooth, hard, rigid steel of 3 mm thickness, backed by wood >10 mm thick.

Tutorial Commentary

Why Measure Dose Gravimetrically?

ISO 11608-1 explicitly requires weighing on a balance (Gmeas) and then converting to volume (Vmeas = G/ρ), rather than measuring volume directly (graduated cylinders, pipettes). The reasons:

  • Higher accuracy: a high-precision balance can have a resolution of 0.0001 g, far better than volumetric measuring tools
  • Better repeatability: it eliminates interference such as liquid surface tension and thermal expansion
  • Consistent with metrological practice: gravimetric measurements can be traced to international standards, whereas volumetric measurements are harder to trace

Key point in the conversion: the density ρ must be determined at the test environment temperature. A 1°C temperature difference changes the density of water by about 0.0002 g/mL, which introduces a systematic error of 0.02% on a 1 mL dose.

Choosing the Test Liquid: Actual Product vs. Surrogate Liquid

The standard says "the original medicinal product, or a liquid with similar physical properties". In CDMO design verification:

  • Use the original drug product where possible: it best represents actual use (taking viscosity and surface tension into account)
  • High-viscosity products (such as monoclonal antibody biologics at ~50 cP) must be tested with the original product or a surrogate liquid with matched viscosity; water cannot be substituted
  • If a surrogate liquid is used, the basis for "similar physical properties" (viscosity, density, surface tension) must be justified in the Risk Management File
  • A CDMO's filling process validation should use a Process Simulation Liquid rather than plain water, to ensure that the dose accuracy data reflect the characteristics of the actual product

An Intuitive Calculation of Balance Resolution

The logic behind the requirement "balance resolution ≤ 1% of the minimum dose":

If the minimum test dose is 0.1 mL (about 0.1 g), the smallest balance division must be ≤ 0.001 g. This ensures that the measurement error contributes no more than 1% of the specification, so that the results mainly reflect the actual performance of the NIS rather than the limits of the measuring tool.

For the CDMO QC laboratory: when performing fill volume verification, the balance specification used must cover a resolution of 1% of the minimum fill volume. This is an often overlooked equipment qualification point.

Section 7: Dose Accuracy: Three Evaluation Methods and the Statistical Framework

Original Text

7.1 General — Three Assessment Pathways

Dose accuracy is determined by testing a variable number of NISs. The standard approach uses statistical tolerance intervals: a 95% confidence that at least probability content p of all doses will fall within specification limits.

Three distinct evaluation methods apply depending on system type:

  1. Two-sided tolerance interval: Variable multi-dose systems (A, C) and partial-evacuation single-dose (B2, D2)
  2. Minimum deliverable dose (one-sided, lower limit only): Manufacturer-filled B1 and D1 designed to fully empty container
  3. Dose delivery efficiency (one-sided, lower limit): User-filled B1 and D1 designed to fully empty container

7.2 Dosing Regions (Multi-Dose Systems)

For multi-dose containers, dosing is performed from three regions:

  • Front 1/3 (container nearly full)
  • Middle 1/3 (container half-used)
  • Rear 1/3 (container almost empty)

NOTE: If maximum dose > 1/3 of labelled volume, two sections may be used instead of three.

Table 2 — Dose Accuracy Assessment Matrix

7.4.1 Acceptance Criteria (General)

  • Standard DA: 95% confidence that at least p of all doses fall within U and L limits
  • Minimum deliverable dose: 95% confidence that at least p of all doses are above the lower limit (labelled minimum)
  • Dose delivery efficiency: 95% confidence that at least p of all efficiencies are above the lower efficiency limit
  • Last-dose accuracy (A, C): 95% confidence that at least p of last-dose errors fall within upper/lower limits
  • Only one dose per injector per Vset per given test

7.4.4 Dose Delivery Efficiency (D1 user-filled, B1 user-filled)

Dose delivery efficiency = Vmeas / Vfill × 100%

where Vfill = volume filled by user (or manufacturer) into container.

Tutorial Commentary

Three-Segment Testing: Why Test the First, Middle and Last Third?

Multi-dose NIS (categories A and C) need to be tested separately when the container is in its first third, middle third and last third of use, because:

  • Container pressure differences: the plunger position differs, so the driving mechanical force differs, which may cause dose deviation
  • Liquid viscosity effects: the viscosity of high-viscosity products changes more at low or high temperatures, so testing of the last segment is especially important
  • Siliconization uniformity: the silicone layer may become unevenly distributed during use, affecting plunger sliding resistance

What this means for CDMO filling: Fill Volume Uniformity needs particular control in the last segment (the upper two-thirds of the container space), otherwise "the last dose" may be underdosed.

Worked Example: Dose Evaluation for a Manufacturer-Filled D1

Setup: 1 mL autoinjector (D1 system, manufacturer-filled)
Minimum Declared Dose (MDD) = 0.97 mL (see drug product labeling)
Test Y = 20 units, one measurement per unit (N = 1)

Gravimetric measurement:
G_meas = 1.003 g (test liquid density ρ = 1.002 g/mL)
V_meas = 1.003 / 1.002 = 1.001 mL

Statistical evaluation (results for N=20 units):
x̄ = 0.992 mL
s = 0.008 mL

Look up Annex B: n=20, p=0.95, one-sided
→ k_tar ≈ 2.208

k_act = (x̄ - L) / s
       = (0.992 - 0.970) / 0.008
       = 2.75

k_act (2.75) > k_tar (2.208) → ✅ PASS

Analogy: Dose Delivery Efficiency vs. Minimum Deliverable Dose

Minimum deliverable dose (manufacturer-filled D1): like ordering a cup of coffee with a specification of "at least 200 mL"; as long as every cup is ≥ 200 mL it passes, and a little extra does no harm.

Dose delivery efficiency (user-filled D1): like the user grinding coffee beans and putting the grounds into the machine, with a requirement to "expel at least 90% of the grounds"; what is assessed here is "whether the amount the user filled is used effectively", not the absolute quantity.

For a CDMO: manufacturer filling is the CDMO's scope of responsibility, so the most important compliance indicator is "whether the fill volume ensures that every unit can deliver a sufficient minimum deliverable dose".

Section Summary

  • Gravimetry is the only measurement method: Vmeas = Gmeas/ρ, the density must be determined at the actual test temperature; balance resolution ≤ 1% of the minimum dose
  • The test liquid must match the physical properties: high-viscosity biologics cannot be replaced with plain water; use of a surrogate liquid must be justified in the RMF with evidence of similar physical properties
  • Three dose evaluation routes: standard two-sided DA (A/B2/C/D2), minimum deliverable dose (manufacturer-filled B1/D1), and delivery efficiency (user-filled B1/D1); manufacturer-filled D1 is the most common route for a CDMO

Tutorial Commentary

Core Logic of the Test Matrix: "Stress + Dose Accuracy"

Most tests in Table 3 follow the same logic:

Apply environmental stress (pre-conditioning) → return to standard conditions → measure dose accuracy

The question behind this design is: "Can the NIS still deliver the drug accurately after real-world environmental conditions (refrigeration, tropical climate, drops, transport vibration)?"

Of particular note is the temperature test (10.2): testing at 5°C (use immediately after refrigeration) and at 40°C (hot environment) corresponds directly to actual patient use scenarios.

Required Tests for a D1 Autoinjector

Tests that Designation D1 (the most common autoinjector) must complete under Table 3:

  • ✅ Temperature test (Cool/Standard/Warm): 60 units, p=0.975
  • ✅ Free fall: 21 units (C/D versions), p=0.95
  • ✅ Dry heat/cold storage: 60 units, p=0.975
  • ✅ Vibration test: 20 units, p=0.95
  • ❌ Last-dose test: A/C systems only (multi-dose)
  • ❌ Life-cycle test: A/B systems only (replaceable container)
  • ❌ Damp heat/cycling test: A/B systems only
  • EMC: only when electronic components are present

Number of NIS needed for a complete D1 DV plan: up to ~200 units (20-60 units per test, plus spares).

Practical Significance of the Free Fall Test

The 1 m drop height is not arbitrary: it is roughly the height from the hand of a seated adult holding an injector to the floor. It represents the most common scenario of "a patient accidentally dropping the injector from the hand."

The 3 orientations (front/back, left/right, top/bottom) ensure that the test covers the most vulnerable impact directions.

For a CDMO: if the customer changes the primary packaging of the PFS (for example, from BD Hypak to a different supplier), the free fall test results need to be re-evaluated, because container material and content weight affect the impact outcome.

Temperature Conditions: Revision in the 2014 Third Edition

Key revision in this edition: the temperature for the cycling test (10.8) changed from 25°C ± 3°C to 5°C ± 3°C.

Rationale: users of refrigerated drug products (stored at 2–8°C) usually use them directly after removal from the refrigerator, so 5°C better represents the actual lowest use temperature. 25°C is too lenient (room temperature) and cannot test performance after refrigeration.

If the customer's DV report is based on the old edition (2012 second edition), this difference in test conditions needs special attention and supplementary testing may be required.

EMC Testing: When Is It Required?

EMC testing (10.10) applies only to NIS containing electronic/electromechanical components, including:

  • Pen injectors with an electronic dose calculator
  • Autoinjectors with electronic sensors (detecting completion of injection)
  • Connectivity pen injectors (Bluetooth-paired)
  • Electrically driven large-volume wearable injectors

A purely mechanical autoinjector (D1, no electronic components) does not require EMC testing.

11.3 Dose Accuracy Acceptance Criteria (Final Formulae)

Two-sided (systems A, B2, C, D2):

One-sided (B1, D1):

  • x̄ − k·s ≥ L or x̄ + k·s ≤ U

Tutorial Commentary

What the 215 lux Lighting Condition Means

ISO 11608-1 requires visual inspection to be performed under ambient lighting of 215 ± 20 lux. This value is a typical office lighting level (general offices 200–500 lux) and represents a "normal use environment."

Implications for a CDMO's QC laboratory:

  • If the customer's DV tests were performed under special lighting (high or low), the results may not be representative of real use
  • If a CDMO supports the customer with QC visual inspection, it should confirm that the light meter meets this requirement
  • The 40–70 cm viewing distance is the normal reading distance for an adult; make sure NIS markings are clearly legible at this distance

Principle for Judging a "Significant Defect"

The defect checklist in Section 11.1 not only lists what to look for; more importantly, each item carries a qualifier: "impact safe functioning."

This means that not every cosmetic defect counts as a failure. If the device surface has slight wear that does not affect function, it may not be a "significant defect."

When a CDMO helps a customer establish release criteria:

  • The visual defect list should clearly distinguish "cosmetic defects" from "functional defects"
  • The definition of "significant defect" should be stated explicitly in the acceptance criteria of the DV report
  • The boundary between acceptable and unacceptable defects should be supported by photographic references (limit samples)

Summary of the Final Dose Accuracy Decision Formula

D1 manufacturer-filled (one-sided, lower limit):
  x̄ - k_act · s ≥ L (minimum delivered dose)
  k_act = (x̄ - L) / s
  Pass condition: k_act ≥ k_tar (Annex B, p=0.95, n=20)

Worked example:
  x̄ = 0.985 mL, s = 0.006 mL, L = 0.970 mL
  k_act = (0.985 - 0.970) / 0.006 = 2.50

  Look up Annex B (n=20, p=0.95, one-sided):
  k_tar ≈ 2.208

Tutorial Commentary

Annex A: Why Are 6 Replicate Sequences Needed?

In dose accuracy testing of multi-dose NIS (A/C systems), each injector must be tested through all 6 randomized sequences (R1–R6), delivering the three doses (Vmin, Vmid, Vmax) in turn.

Rationale: the order of injections changes the residual liquid volume in the container, which in turn affects the driving conditions for the next injection. Testing all combinations confirms that dose accuracy is not affected by injection order.

For D1/B1 systems (single dose, full-volume delivery): this issue does not arise, because each device injects only once and there is no sequence effect.

Statistical Meaning of the k Value: The Larger the n, the Smaller the k

k value as n → ∞ (p=0.95, one-sided) = 1.645
This is the 95th percentile of the normal distribution (Z score)

Actual test n=20: k = 2.208 (much larger than 1.645)
Actual test n=60: k = 1.933 (closer to the theoretical value)

Reason for the difference:
  At n=20, there is more uncertainty in estimating the population from the sample x̄ and s
  The k value compensates for this additional uncertainty, ensuring that:
  "with 95% confidence, at least 95% of the population is within specification"

  Small n → large statistical uncertainty → large k → harder to pass
  Large n → small statistical uncertainty → small k → easier to pass

Annex B Is a "Normative" Annex!

Annex A is "informative": it is used to understand the methodological background.

Annex B is "normative": it must be followed and carries binding force.

This means the k values must be taken from the official tables in Annex B. k values calculated with other statistical software must be shown to be consistent with the Annex B results, and cannot be substituted arbitrarily.

In practice, ISO 16269-6 (the statistical standard referenced by Annex B) provides the same k values, and calculation with statistical software is permitted as long as the method is equivalent.

Analogy: the k Value Table vs. a Height-Weight Chart

The k value table is like the medical "normal weight range for a given height" chart: it tells you what kind of statement you can make about a whole population based on a limited measured sample.

The larger the sample size (n), the more precise your statement, and the smaller the "safety factor" (k value) you need.

Statistics in the pharmaceutical industry follow the same logic: from limited batch data, a statistical tolerance interval is used to make a confident statement about the performance of the entire manufacturing process.

Overall Summary of ISO 11608-1

  • The six system classifications (A/B1/B2/C/D1/D2) determine the test matrix; D1 (manufacturer-filled, integrated, full-volume delivery) is the most common type in the CDMO autoinjector business
  • The statistical tolerance interval (k value method) is the core of every dose accuracy decision; kact ≥ ktar (Annex B) is the pass criterion; the larger the n → the smaller the ktar → the easier to pass
  • Type A compatibility has been eliminated: the manufacturer must label the verified component combination based on actual testing; a CDMO supplier change requires assessing the validity of the compatibility statement
  • ISO 14971 + IEC 62366 are mandatory prerequisites: the design verification report must have a corresponding Risk Management File and Usability Engineering File
  • The test report (an ISO 13485 record) must contain the 11 minimum items, with particular attention to test fluid identification and deviation records; every deviation must be supported by a risk assessment
