Introduction

This document addresses the presentation of certain items of information that are considered by regulatory authorities to be essential for the safe and proper use of medical devices. As such, the items are required to appear with the medical device in most regulatory domains. The information can be required to appear on the medical device itself, as part of the label, or provided with the medical device.

Many countries require that their own language be used to display textual information with medical devices. At the same time, manufacturers seek to take costs out of labelling by reducing or rationalizing variants. This can cause problems in relation to translation, design and logistics when multiple languages are included on a single label or piece of documentation. For example, users of medical devices labelled in a number of different languages can experience confusion and delay in locating the appropriate language.

This document proposes solutions to these problems through the use of internationally recognized symbols with precisely defined descriptions.

While compiling symbols to be included in this document, ISO/TC 210 recognized the need for systematic methodology for the selection, development and validation of symbols proposed for adoption. This is the subject of ISO 15223-2.

This document is primarily intended to be used by manufacturers of medical devices who market identical products in countries where there are different language requirements for medical device labelling. It can also be of assistance to:

  • Distributors of medical devices or other representatives of manufacturers
  • Healthcare providers responsible for training, as well as those being trained
  • Those responsible for post-market vigilance
  • Healthcare regulatory authorities, testing organizations, certification bodies and other organizations responsible for implementing regulations affecting medical devices and which have responsibility for post-market surveillance
  • Consumers or end users of medical devices who draw their supplies from a number of sources and can have varied language capabilities

Commentary — Why Do We Need Standardized Symbols?

Core driver: language barriers vs. a global market

The basic problem behind ISO 15223-1 is a very practical one: if a prefilled syringe is to be marketed in 60 countries, do you need 60 language versions of the label?

Not only is that prohibitively expensive, it also causes design confusion and an explosion in logistics complexity. Standardized graphical symbols are the only workable solution: a universal pictogram that users of any language can understand.

Analogy: international road signs, for medical devices

Like the road signs used worldwide (the red circle for no entry, the stop sign), which you can understand whether you drive in Japan or Brazil, ISO 15223-1 sets out to build the same kind of visual language for medical device labels.

A factory pictogram means "manufacturer" and an hourglass means "use-by date"; they are understood worldwide and need no translation.

Key updates in the third edition (2016)

  • Special IVD labelling provisions: in vitro diagnostic (IVD) devices have specific requirements for where the manufacturer's address is placed
  • eIFU (electronic instructions for use): a new 5.4.3 symbol meaning "consult electronic instructions for use", corresponding to the eIFU provisions of the EU MDR (EU 207/2012)
  • ISO 7000 registration dates: Table 1 adds a "registration date" column for each symbol, making symbol versions traceable

Relevance to CDMO practice

As a contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO), you produce, fill and label on behalf of your clients. Correct use of ISO 15223-1 symbols is your responsibility in the following areas:

  • Label review: confirm that the label design supplied by the client complies with ISO 15223-1
  • Batch record: the product label version number must correspond to the currently valid version of the symbols
  • Change control: when ISO 15223-1 is revised, assess whether existing labels need to be revised in step

Clause 1 & 2 — Scope & Normative References

1 Scope

This document identifies requirements for symbols used in medical device labelling that convey information on the safe and effective use of medical devices. It also lists symbols that satisfy the requirements of this document.

This document is applicable to symbols used in a broad spectrum of medical devices, which are marketed globally and therefore need to meet different regulatory requirements.

These symbols may be used on the medical device itself, on its packaging or in the associated documentation. The requirements of this document are not intended to apply to symbols specified in other standards.

2 Normative References

The following documents are referred to in the text in such a way that some or all of their content constitutes requirements of this document. For dated references, only the edition cited applies. For undated references, the latest edition applies.

Commentary — Scope and Normative References

Three ways to read the scope

  • "Identification requirements": the standard is not just a symbol library; it also sets out mandatory requirements on when symbols are to be used
  • "A broad range of medical devices": from syringes, catheters and implants to IVD reagents and ophthalmic devices, all are covered
  • "Not applicable to symbols specified in other standards": for example, the EN 60601 series (electrical safety) has its own symbol system, and the two are not mixed

The four normative references in detail

ISO 7000 — the source of each symbol's "ID number". Every symbol in ISO 15223-1 Table 1 carries its ISO 7000 registration number (for example, ISO 7000-3082 is the manufacturer symbol). This number lets you trace the official version of the graphic.

IEC 80416-1 — defines what is meant by "title", "description" and "characteristic information" (the terms in the next clause all originate from this standard).

ISO 14971 — risk management is the trigger for using symbols. Not all information has to be conveyed with symbols; they are used only when the risk assessment shows it is "appropriate".

ISO 8601 — standardizes date format. The date of manufacture and the use-by date must be in YYYY-MM-DD format, not the DD/MM/YYYY or MM/DD/YYYY customary in individual countries.

Correspondence with FDA 21 CFR Part 801

FDA 21 CFR Part 801 requires medical device labels to carry enough information for safe use. Although FDA does not mandate the use of ISO 15223-1 symbols, in 2016 it issued the guidance "Use of International Standard ISO 10993-1" and, in its labelling guidance, recognizes the use of ISO 15223-1 symbols provided that:

  • the symbol is accompanied by explanatory English text (accompanying text), or
  • the manufacturer can provide the end user with a written explanation of the symbol's meaning

In practice: for devices sold in the US market, it is advisable to keep the English title (such as "Manufacturer") next to the symbol to meet FDA's comprehension requirements.

Correspondence with EU MDR 2017/745

EU MDR Annex I (General Safety and Performance Requirements), section 23, requires labels to be clearly legible, and Annex I, 23.2(d), expressly permits the use of symbols. EU MDR 2017/745 refers directly to ISO 15223-1 as a harmonized standard for demonstrating conformity.

This means that using ISO 15223-1 symbols correctly in the EU market can serve as a technical documentation basis for compliance with the EU MDR labelling requirements.

Clause 3 — Terms & Definitions

3 Terms and Definitions

For the purposes of this document, the terms and definitions given in ISO 14971 and the following apply.

3.1 Characteristic Information

Information that represents the property or properties of a symbol.

3.2 Description

Normative text which defines the purpose, application and use of the symbol.

[SOURCE: IEC 80416-1:2008, 3.2]

3.3 Label

Written, printed or graphic information provided upon the medical device itself.

[SOURCE: GHTF/SG1/N43:2005]

3.4 Labelling

Information supplied by the manufacturer that is provided for, associated with, or affixed to, a medical device or any of its containers or wrappers.

Note 1 to entry: This information relates to the identification, technical description (3.2) and use of the medical device, but excludes shipping documents.

Note 2 to entry: Some regional and national regulations refer to "labelling" as "information supplied by the manufacturer."

3.5 Symbol Used in Medical Device Labelling

Graphical representation appearing on the label (3.3) and/or associated documentation of a medical device that communicates characteristic information (3.1) without the need for the supplier or receiver of the information to have knowledge of the language of a particular nation or people.

Note 1 to entry: The symbol can be an abstract pictorial or a graphical representation, or one that uses familiar objects, including alphanumeric characters.

3.6 Title

Unique name by which a graphical symbol is identified and spoken of.

[SOURCE: IEC 80416-1:2008, 3.9]

Commentary — The Six Terms Explained

3.1 Characteristic Information

This is the "essence of the message" that a symbol conveys. For example, the characteristic information of the factory pictogram (the manufacturer symbol) is "this is the entity that made this device".

CDMO application: in your label review SOP, reviewers need to confirm that the characteristic information conveyed by each symbol is consistent with the actual product situation (for example, confirm that the company name next to the factory pictogram really is the MAH and not the CMO).

3.2 Description vs. 3.6 Title

Title = the symbol's "name", used for identification and reference. For example, "Manufacturer" is the title of the 5.1.1 symbol.

Description = the symbol's "normative text", which defines its purpose, applicable situations and manner of use. This is the most important column in Table 1 because it carries normative force.

Why the distinction matters: when a regulation or contract refers to an ISO 15223-1 symbol, you must comply with the description, not merely produce something that "looks about right".

3.3 Label vs. 3.4 Labelling

Label = the physical label attached directly to the product itself, for example the paper label on a glass vial.

Labelling = a broader concept that includes the label, information printed on the packaging, the instructions for use (IFU) and even information on the product website. Excluded: shipping documents.

The term "labeling" in FDA 21 CFR Part 801 (note the American spelling with a single l) corresponds to "labelling" in ISO; the two definitions are very close.

Analogy: Label vs. Labelling

Imagine a prefilled syringe as a book:

  • Label = the book's front cover (the sticker attached directly to the product)
  • Labelling = the whole publishing information system (front cover + back cover + copyright page + accompanying booklet), but not the bookseller's delivery note

3.5 Symbol — the most central definition

Key phrase: "without the need for... knowledge of the language". This is the fundamental reason symbols exist. A symbol must cross the language barrier and achieve genuine visual communication.

Note 1 is important: a symbol can be an abstract graphic (such as a wavy line for non-sterile), a familiar object (such as a factory building for manufacturer), or even contain alphanumeric characters (such as "2°C–8°C" together with a snowflake pictogram).

Clause 4 — General Requirements

4 General Requirements

4.1 Proposal of Symbols for Adoption

Symbols proposed for adoption in this document shall be validated in accordance with ISO 15223-2.

Any symbol proposed for adoption in this document shall be applicable to a range of medical devices and have global or regional applicability.

4.2 Requirements for Usage

When risk management shows it to be appropriate for symbols to be used to convey information essential for proper use on the medical device, its packaging or in associated documentation, the symbols given in Table 1 may be used.

Symbols that are registered in ISO 7000 shall comply with the graphical representation in ISO 7000, especially with respect to:

  • Relative dimensions
  • Relative line thickness
  • Orientation
  • Absence or presence of filled or shaded areas
NOTE 1: ISO and IEC jointly maintain an online database of graphical symbols for use on equipment at https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/#search. This online collection shows each graphical symbol and identifies it by a reference number and a title (in English and French).

As part of risk management, the manufacturer should determine the appropriate size for the symbol to be legible for its intended function.

NOTE 2: This document does not specify colours or minimum size for the symbols in Table 1, nor does it specify the relative size of symbols and that of indicated information.

It is important that symbols be used properly. Guidance on appropriate use of the general prohibition symbol and the negation symbol is given in Annex B.

Before symbols are used, the manufacturer shall carry out a risk assessment that indicates that the use of the symbol does not introduce an unacceptable risk.

NOTE 3: Additional information regarding risk assessment can be found in ISO 14971.

Symbols may be used without accompanying text. Where regulations require accompanying text, the title of the symbol given in this document should be considered sufficient. All dates and times presented in association with symbols shall use the conventions set out in ISO 8601.

4.3 Other Symbols

Other standards specify additional symbols that are applicable to particular kinds or groups of medical devices or to particular situations. Examples of sources for such symbols are identified in the Bibliography. This listing is not exhaustive.

Commentary — The Five Core General Requirements

Core 1: risk management drives the use of symbols (4.2)

Key sentence: "When risk management shows it to be appropriate..."

This means symbols are not decoration to be plastered all over the label, but an output of risk management (ISO 14971). Your Risk Management File should record:

  • which information is critical to safe use
  • why symbols (rather than plain text) were chosen to convey this information
  • whether the use of a symbol could introduce new risks (for example, misinterpretation by users)

Core 2: ISO 7000 graphic compliance

"Looks similar" is not good enough: symbols registered in ISO 7000 have strict graphic specifications:

  • Relative proportions: the roof height and chimney width of the factory pictogram follow prescribed proportions
  • Line weight: the relative proportion of thin and thick lines must be consistent
  • Orientation: symbols may not be rotated at will (for some symbols, orientation carries meaning)
  • Fill / shading: some symbols must be solid and others outlined, and the two cannot be interchanged

CDMO practice: when your graphic designer redraws a symbol, they must work from the official graphics in the ISO Online Browsing Platform (OBP) database (downloadable in AI, DWG and EPS formats) and must not "improve" the symbol or alter its proportions.

Core 3: a risk assessment is required before use

"the manufacturer shall carry out a risk assessment that indicates that the use of the symbol does not introduce an unacceptable risk."

This is a shall (mandatory) requirement. In the technical documentation (Technical File / Design History File), you need to keep records of usability testing, or an equivalent assessment, showing that the intended user group can correctly understand each symbol.

Core 4: accompanying text

A symbol may be used on its own (without text), subject to conditions:

  • If a regulation requires accompanying text (for example, FDA's requirement for the US market), the symbol's title should be added
  • The title (such as "Manufacturer") is regarded as "sufficient" accompanying text
  • There is no need to add the full description text

EU MDR Annex I, 23.4(ab), permits the use of symbols, but it must be ensured that they can be correctly understood within the user group.

4.3 Other symbols — an important exclusion clause

ISO 15223-1 is not the only medical device symbol standard. Symbols from other standards (such as the EN 60601 electrical equipment symbols and the IEC 60417 electrical symbols) each have their own scope, fall outside the jurisdiction of ISO 15223-1, and do not need to meet the requirements of this standard.

In practice: the label of an electrical infusion pump may carry both ISO 15223-1 symbols (such as use-by date and manufacturer) and EN 60601 electrical safety symbols (such as Class II equipment). The two symbol systems coexist, each complying with its own standard.

ISO 7000-2947 | Registration date: 2004-01-15

Title: Importer

Description: Indicates the date when the medical device was manufactured. Indicates the entity that places the medical device on the market after it has been manufactured outside the jurisdiction where it is sold.

Graphical form: A stylized symbol showing an arrow pointing into a building or territory, representing an entity bringing goods across a border into a market. Often depicted as a factory-like building with an inward-pointing arrow.

5.1.4 Distributor

ISO 7000-2946 | Registration date: 2004-01-15

Title: Distributor

Description: Indicates the entity in the supply chain that makes the medical device available on the market after it has been placed on the market.

Graphical form: A stylized symbol showing multiple arrows or pathways radiating from a central point or building, representing distribution of a product to multiple downstream points.

5.1.3 Date of Manufacture

ISO 7000-2497 | Registration date: 2004-01-15

Title: Date of manufacture

Description: Indicates the date when the medical device was manufactured.

Graphical form: The factory/building icon (same as Manufacturer, 5.1.1) combined with a calendar or date indicator symbol beneath or beside it, signifying the date on which manufacture occurred.

Usage requirements:

  • This symbol shall be accompanied by a date expressed as year, or year and month, or year, month and day, as applicable.
  • The date shall be expressed as required in ISO 8601, with four digits for the year; and, where appropriate, two digits for the month and two digits for the day.
  • This symbol may be used to identify the year and month of manufacture for active implantable medical devices, or the year of manufacture for active implantable medical devices where no use-by date is given, as required by the applicable EU Directive.
  • The date shall be located adjacent to the symbol.
  • NOTE: The date of manufacture, as well as the name and address of the manufacturer, can be combined in one symbol.

Commentary — Symbol Category 5.1, the Manufacturer Group

5.1.1 Manufacturer — the most important label symbol

This factory pictogram is the most basic required element on a medical device label. The label of every device that meets the requirements of EU MDR 2017/745 and EU IVDR 2017/746 must show this symbol together with the manufacturer's full name and address.

Key CDMO distinction: the manufacturer shown on the label is not necessarily the contract manufacturer (CMO/CDMO). EU MDR Article 2(30) defines the "manufacturer" as the natural or legal person who designs, manufactures, packages and labels a device and places it on the market under their own name. If your client (the MAH) is the manufacturer as defined in the EU MDR, the client's company name and address should be entered next to the factory symbol, not your CDMO site address.

Analogy: the cast of roles on a label

Picture the four roles on the label of a biologic product:

  • 🏭 Manufacturer (5.1.1) = your CDMO's client (the MAH, the brand owner)
  • ◯EC EU authorized representative (5.1.2) = if the brand owner is a US company, the representative it appoints in the EU
  • ↔🏭 Importer (5.1.3) = the company that brings the product from the US (place of manufacture) into the EU market
  • 📦 Distributor (5.1.4) = the company that distributes within the EU market to pharmacies and hospitals

An important change in EU MDR 2017/745: compared with the old MDD, the MDR expressly requires the identity of the importer and distributor to be shown on the label, which is why the 5.1.3 and 5.1.4 symbols have come into wide use.

5.1.2 Authorized Representative

EU MDR Article 11 provides that a manufacturer established outside the EU must designate at least one authorized representative (AR) within the EU before placing a device on the EU market. The AR must:

  • be registered in EUDAMED (the European Database on Medical Devices)
  • enter into a written mandate with the manufacturer
  • have obligations towards EU competent authorities (such as notification of non-conforming products and market placement notifications)

Labelling requirement: the AR's name and EU address must be clearly shown on the label, next to the 5.1.2 symbol.

Note: the 5.1.2 symbol has no ISO 7000 registration number (N/A); it is a symbol defined specifically by ISO 15223-1 and is not in the general ISO 7000 graphics library.

Importer and Distributor (5.1.3 & 5.1.4) — new EU MDR obligations

EU MDR 2017/745 is considerably stricter than the old MDD on supply chain oversight. The importer and the distributor now each have independent compliance obligations:

  • Importer: must verify that the device bears the CE mark, that the technical documentation is complete and that the manufacturer has met the EU MDR requirements. The importer's name and address must be shown on the label or packaging.
  • Distributor: must verify that the labelling is complete, that the device is within its shelf life and that storage conditions are met. If the distributor repackages or relabels, it may take on the responsibilities of the manufacturer.

Impact on a CDMO: if your client exports devices made at your site to the EU, your client (or its EU-designated importer) must ensure that the 5.1.3 importer symbol and information are correctly shown on the packaging.

Comparison with FDA 21 CFR Part 801

The main differences between FDA's requirements for manufacturer identification (21 CFR 801.1) and ISO 15223-1:

  • FDA does not mandate the use of the ISO 15223-1 factory symbol; the manufacturer can simply be identified in text as "Manufactured by: [Company Name and Address]"
  • FDA requires the information to be presented in English, and symbols need an English explanation
  • If ISO 15223-1 symbols are used in an FDA 510(k)/PMA submission, a symbol list (Symbol Glossary) explaining the meaning of each symbol must be included in the technical documentation
  • In 2016 FDA issued the final guidance "Use of Symbols in Labeling", formally recognizing the ISO 15223-1:2012 symbols (the 2016 edition is now widely accepted)

Practical application scenarios on a CDMO fill line

Aseptic fill-finish:

Standard Definition

The symbol consists of an hourglass graphic derived from ISO 7000-1051, typically displayed as an hourglass with a cross or X mark at the top. The date format immediately follows the symbol, expressed as:

  • YYYY-MM-DD — full date (preferred)
  • YYYY-MM — year and month only
  • YYYY — year only (limited use cases)

Regulatory Requirement

Per ISO 15223-1:2016 Clause 5.2.1, the "use by" date symbol is mandatory when the device has a defined shelf life. The symbol shall appear on the label in a manner that is clear, legible, and indelible.

"The date after which the medical device is not to be used shall be indicated on the label." — ISO 15223-1:2016 § 5.2.1

Notes on Usage

  • This symbol is often rendered as the hourglass glyph followed by the date numerals
  • In print, "USE BY" text may be used as an alternative in some regional regulations
  • Must be clearly distinct from the date of manufacture symbol
  • For sterile products, shelf life is linked to sterility barrier integrity (ISO 11607)

5.2.1 — Use-by Date Symbol: Commentary

Meaning of the symbol

The use-by date (Use By / Expiry Date) is one of the most critical symbols on a medical device label. The hourglass with a cross conveys intuitively that "time is up; do not use any more".

This symbol derives from ISO 7000-1051 and is the globally recognized way of indicating an expiry date; it communicates across languages without any text.

Practical applications for pharmaceutical packaging

  • Sterile injectables: the glass vial or ampoule must carry an expiry date, usually in YYYY-MM format
  • Prefilled syringes (PFS): the outer layer of the sterile packaging pouch must also carry the expiry date, together with the sterilization mark
  • Single-use sterile devices: surgical packs, catheters and the like all need this symbol
  • Drug delivery devices: including auto-injector pens and inhalers

Everyday analogy

Like the "best before" date on a milk carton, the use-by symbol on a medical device tells the user that beyond this date the manufacturer no longer guarantees the safety and functionality of the product.

For sterile products, this date is also the guaranteed period of sterility barrier integrity, not merely a reference for appearance or function.

Link to Annex 1

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022 revision), 8.120, requires the final container and outer packaging of sterile products to carry complete traceable information, including the expiry date. The hourglass symbol of ISO 15223-1 is the international standard way of meeting this requirement.

Standard Definition

The date of manufacture symbol uses a factory/building graphic (derived from ISO 7000-2497) to indicate when the device was produced. This is distinct from the expiry date and serves traceability purposes.

Date format conventions:

  • YYYY-MM-DD — specific manufacture date

Regulatory Purpose

The manufacture date enables:

  • Traceability back to production batch and manufacturing conditions
  • Calculation of remaining shelf life from manufacture date when expiry is not absolute
  • Post-market surveillance and field correction (recall) scope identification
  • Verification that storage conditions have been maintained within the expected period
"The date on which the medical device has been manufactured." — ISO 15223-1:2016 § 5.2.2

5.2.2 — Date of Manufacture Symbol: Commentary

Meaning of the symbol

The date of manufacture (Date of Manufacture) symbol is a factory building pictogram and represents the actual production date of the medical device. It is used for traceability, not as a safety limit.

Practical applications for pharmaceutical packaging

  • Batch traceability: the date of manufacture, together with the lot/batch number, ensures that complete production records can be traced
  • Stability calculation: ICH Q1A requires stability studies to be counted from the date of manufacture, so the date of manufacture on the label is the key starting point
  • Recall management: if a batch has a quality problem, the date of manufacture helps define the range of affected batches
  • Cold chain management: products such as freeze-dried formulations must be resold within a specific period after manufacture, and the date of manufacture is the basis for calculating it

Date of manufacture vs. use-by date: how they relate

  • Use-by date = date of manufacture + approved shelf life (shelf life per stability study)
  • When both are shown, the factory symbol (date of manufacture) comes first and the hourglass symbol (use-by date) second
  • If space for marking is limited, give priority to the use-by date (it bears more directly on safe use)

Link to FDA 21 CFR Part 211

FDA 21 CFR 211.68 and 211.130 both require the label to carry enough information to ensure traceability. The date of manufacture is a legally required element of a drug label, and the factory symbol of ISO 15223-1 provides a standardized visual expression for it.

Standard Definition

"Do Not Use After" conveys the same expiration information as the Use By symbol but uses prohibition language. It may appear as:

  • Text only: "DO NOT USE AFTER [DATE]"
  • Combined with the hourglass symbol from § 5.2.1
  • A symbol incorporating a calendar or date graphic with a cross or prohibition circle

Distinction from § 5.2.1

While § 5.2.1 ("Use By") states the last acceptable date, § 5.2.3 ("Do Not Use After") is a stronger prohibition directive. In regulated markets, some regulatory bodies require the prohibition form for specific device categories where use past the date presents significant patient risk.

Placement Requirements

  • Date format follows ISO 8601 conventions
  • Must be legible under the device's intended storage and use conditions

5.2.3 — Do Not Use After This Date: Commentary

Meaning of the symbol

"Do Not Use After" is essentially the same as the use-by date, but the tone is more mandatory. It expresses the time limit as a prohibition rather than a recommendation.

An analogy for the difference in meaning

Use By: like the "best before" date on a food label, a manufacturer's assurance of quality.

Do Not Use After: closer to a regulatory requirement of "do not consume after this date"; it leaves no room for flexible interpretation, and use at the user's own risk is not accepted.

For sterile medical devices (especially high-risk categories such as implantable devices and vascular catheters), the prohibitive expression of 5.2.3 should be preferred.

Application scenarios in pharmaceutical packaging

  • Class III implantable devices (such as prosthetic heart valves and spinal fusion systems): a prohibitive date marking is almost mandatory
  • In vitro diagnostic reagents (IVD): reagent shelf life directly affects diagnostic accuracy, so prohibitive wording is safer
  • Sterilized packaging components: after expiry the sterility barrier may have degraded, so use must be prohibited

Practical reminder

In Taiwan, under the Medical Devices Act and the related labelling rules, imported medical devices must show the expiry date in Chinese or English, and the combined symbol-and-text approach of 5.2.3 (symbol + numeric date) is the most widely accepted format. When TFDA reviews labels, the use of ISO 15223-1 symbols can serve as the technical basis for a declaration of conformity.

5.3.1 — Sterile

Symbol Reference: ISO 7000-1051 group | Table 1, Entry 5.3.1
STERILE
Sterile — Contents are Sterile
ISO 15223-1 § 5.3.1
The word "STERILE" in a defined typographic format (all caps, within a border frame or standalone), indicating that the device and/or its contents have been processed to achieve and maintain a sterile state.

Standard Definition

The STERILE symbol is one of the most critical labels on a medical device or pharmaceutical packaging component. It asserts that the contents have been subjected to a validated sterilization process achieving a Sterility Assurance Level (SAL) of 10-6 or better.

Display Requirements

  • The word "STERILE" shall appear in capital letters
  • May be combined with a sterilization method suffix (see §§ 5.3.3–5.3.5)
  • Font size must ensure legibility on the primary packaging
  • Shall appear on both inner and outer packaging where applicable

SAL and Sterilization Validation

The sterile claim is valid only when:

  • A validated sterilization process (per ISO 11135, ISO 11137, or ISO 17665) has been used
  • The sterile barrier system (per ISO 11607) maintains integrity through the stated shelf life
  • Routine process monitoring and product release testing confirm ongoing process control
"STERILE — the device is sterile. Used alone, STERILE indicates the device has been processed using a sterilization method whose details are not specified." — ISO 15223-1:2016 § 5.3.1

5.3.1 — STERILE Symbol: Commentary

Core concept

The STERILE symbol is one of the most important claims on medical device packaging. It indicates that the product has undergone a validated sterilization process and achieved a sterility assurance level (SAL = 10⁻⁶), that is, a probability of no more than one surviving microorganism per million products.

Pharmaceutical CDMO application scenarios

  • Aseptic fill-finish: the STERILE claim on the final container (vial, ampoule, prefilled syringe) must be supported by complete process validation
  • Sterilized components: for pre-sterilized supply of rubber stoppers, aluminium caps and glass vials, the STERILE marking on the component itself must be backed by the supplier's sterilization validation records
  • Terminally sterilized products: terminally sterilized preparations use the same symbol, but it must be combined with the sterilization-method suffix of 5.3.3–5.3.5

Explanation of the sterility assurance level (SAL)

  • SAL 10⁻⁶: the probability of one surviving microorganism per million products is 0.000001
  • This does not mean "zero microorganisms"; it is a statistical concept
  • ISO 11135 (EO sterilization), ISO 11137 (radiation sterilization) and ISO 17665 (steam sterilization) all use this as the acceptance criterion
  • Overkill method: a design in which D-value × cycles reaches SAL 10⁻⁶ or better

Link to Annex 1 (EU GMP 2022)

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022), Chapter 1, expressly requires the labels of sterile preparations to carry an appropriate declaration of sterile status. The STERILE symbol, combined with the appropriate sterilization-method marking, is a core element in meeting the label integrity requirement of Annex 1, 4.34.

5.3.3–5.3.5 — Sterilization Method Symbols: Commentary

Why indicate the sterilization method?

Different sterilization methods affect materials differently and also determine the need for residuals testing. Showing the sterilization method on the label allows the user to:

  • assess chemical compatibility (especially EO residuals)
  • understand the possible risk of material degradation in the product
  • confirm compliance with the regulatory requirements of the relevant country/region
  • trace the sterilization batch (linking to the sterilization records)

Sterilization methods and the corresponding ISO standards

  • EO (ethylene oxide) (ISO 11135): widely used for heat-sensitive medical devices, such as flexible packaging and electronic components. EO residuals (EO + ECH + EG) must meet the limits of ISO 10993-7
  • Radiation sterilization (ISO 11137): gamma rays (Co-60) or electron beam (E-beam); leaves no chemical residue but may affect the mechanical properties of polymers
  • Steam / autoclave sterilization (ISO 17665): the most commonly used in pharma, suitable for heat-resistant materials (glass, stainless steel, certain polymers). The first choice for terminal sterilization of sterile preparations
  • Aseptic processing: not terminal sterilization; used where the product itself cannot withstand terminal sterilization, and it requires the strictest environmental control (grade A cleanroom)

Integrated use with ISO 11135 and ISO 11137

ISO 11135 (EO sterilization): specifies the development, validation and routine control of the EO sterilization process. A "STERILE EO" claim on the label must be supported by a validation programme conforming to ISO 11135 (including D-value determination and verification of maximum residual levels).

ISO 11137 series (radiation sterilization): ISO 11137-1 specifies the requirements, ISO 11137-2 provides methods for establishing the dose, and ISO 11137-3 gives guidance on dosimetry. A "STERILE R" label requires records of the corresponding dose setting confirmation (VDmax method or SAL method).

Special note on aseptic fill-finish

For drug products manufactured by aseptic fill-finish, the label usually shows only "STERILE" with no method suffix, because aseptic filling is not terminal sterilization. Annex 1 (2022), Chapter 8, sets detailed requirements for aseptic filling, including process simulation (APS/media fill) and environmental monitoring, to ensure the validity of the STERILE claim.

Rationale

  • Residual contamination that cannot be eliminated by resterilization
  • Invalid sterility assurance after reprocessing (SAL not revalidated)

Single Use Only (Use Once)
ISO 15223-1 § 5.3.7
The numeral "2" inside a circle with a diagonal prohibition line — i.e., "not 2 [times]." Indicates the device is intended for use on a single patient during a single procedure only, and shall not be reused.

Single Use Symbol Detail

The official symbol is the figure "2" within a circle, with a line through it (② with prohibition mark), meaning "not to be used a second time." This is distinct from "Do Not Resterilize" — single use addresses reuse on any patient, while Do Not Resterilize addresses the sterilization process specifically.

Single-use labeling carries significant regulatory weight:

  • EU MDR 2017/745 requires single-use devices to clearly bear this symbol
  • FDA guidance on reprocessing of single-use devices (SUDs) imposes stringent requirements on any third-party reprocessor

5.3.6–5.3.7 — Do Not Resterilize and Single Use: Commentary

Distinguishing the core concepts

These two symbols are often confused, but there is a clear difference:

  • Do Not Resterilize (5.3.6): prohibits sterilizing again this device. The focus is on the act of sterilization itself; after use, the device must not be sent back to the autoclave or EO chamber for reprocessing
  • Single Use Only (5.3.7): prohibits reusing this device, whether or not it is resterilized. It is discarded after a single use

Practical applications in pharmaceutical packaging

  • Prefilled syringes (PFS): a typical Single Use Only device. After it is filled with the drug solution, the patient or healthcare professional uses it once and discards it
  • Auto-injectors: mostly single use; some multi-dose pens are reused, but with a fresh needle each time
  • Single-use tubing for aseptic filling: such as peristaltic pump tubing and filling needles, must be marked Single Use Only; reuse poses particulate contamination and bioburden risks
  • Rubber stoppers: single use; even if one comes off during the restoppering process, it should be discarded rather than resterilized and reused

EU MDR and FDA SUD reprocessing requirements

EU MDR 2017/745 Article 17: Member States may allow third parties to reprocess devices originally marked Single Use, but strict equivalent-safety requirements must be met, and the reprocessor must bear the same responsibilities as the manufacturer.

FDA SUD reprocessing: FDA permits reprocessing of some single-use devices (SUDs), but the reprocessor must submit a 510(k) or PMA to FDA, providing safety and effectiveness data equivalent to those of the original device.

For a CDMO, if a device used in a client's product is marked Single Use, the process procedure (SOP) must expressly prohibit any form of reuse, and a disposal record must be established.

Intuitive analogy

Do Not Resterilize: like a disposable scalpel, even if you could clean it, the manufacturer does not allow you to autoclave it and reuse it, because the sharpness of the blade and the integrity of the material can no longer be guaranteed.

Single Use Only: like a disposable injection needle, however it is disinfected, the needle is discarded after use; this is a basic requirement for patient safety and prevention of cross-infection.

Standard Definition

The temperature limitation symbol consists of a thermometer with arrows indicating both upper and lower limits, followed by temperature values:

  • Format: [thermometer symbol] followed by temperature range (e.g., 2°C to 8°C)
  • Upper limit indicated by upward arrow or maximum value
  • Lower limit indicated by downward arrow or minimum value
  • Units are always in degrees Celsius per ISO convention

Common Temperature Ranges in Medical Devices

Transport vs. Storage

Temperature limits may differ between storage and transport conditions. Some labels include both a storage symbol and a separate transport temperature indicator (per ASTM D7386 or ISTA standards for distribution testing).

5.4.1 — Temperature Limit Symbol: Commentary

Meaning of the symbol

The temperature limit symbol (a thermometer with arrows) is the visual core of cold chain management and bears directly on product stability and safety. It is indispensable on the packaging of biologics, vaccines and temperature-sensitive medical devices.

Pharmaceutical CDMO application scenarios

  • Refrigerated biologics (2–8°C): insulin, monoclonal antibodies, recombinant proteins and the like. The temperature symbol on the label must match the temperature conditions approved on the basis of the ICH Q1A stability studies
  • Frozen products (≤ -20°C or ≤ -70°C): mRNA vaccines (such as COVID-19 vaccines) require an ultra-low-temperature cold chain at -70°C, and this condition must be clearly marked on the label
  • Room-temperature drugs (15–25°C): solid oral dosage forms usually carry this range; if the upper limit is slightly higher (up to 30°C), "do not exceed 30°C" must be stated explicitly
  • Components after terminal sterilization: some pre-sterilized rubber stoppers need to be stored at a specific temperature (for example, to avoid high temperatures deforming the stopper and affecting seal integrity)

ICH Q1A and WHO stability conditions compared

  • Long-term conditions (Zone I/II): 25°C ± 2°C / 60% RH ± 5% — corresponds to a label of 15–25°C
  • Long-term conditions (Zone III/IV): 30°C ± 2°C / 65% RH ± 5% (tropical markets)
  • Accelerated conditions: 40°C ± 2°C / 75% RH — used for stability prediction, not as a label temperature
  • Refrigerated conditions: 5°C ± 3°C — the label shows 2–8°C

Handling cold chain excursions

When a product deviates from the labelled temperature during transport or storage, a temperature excursion assessment procedure must be initiated. The assessment is based on the cumulative excursion time (mean kinetic temperature, MKT), accelerated ageing studies in the stability data, and degradation kinetics models of the drug substance/drug product. The temperature symbol of ISO 15223-1 is the starting point of this assessment process and is also key label evidence in complaints and regulatory communication.

5.4.4–5.4.5 — Fragile and Keep Away from Sunlight: Commentary

Fragile symbol (5.4.4)

The broken glass symbol is the fragile marking commonly used in international transport packaging (ISO 780) and has been adopted into the medical device labelling system by ISO 15223-1. It indicates that the product needs special care during handling, transport and stacking.

Application scenarios in pharmaceutical packaging

  • Glass vial/ampoule packaging: although Type I borosilicate glass containers are of excellent quality, they are still fragile. The outer shipping carton should carry the fragile symbol, with appropriate cushioning protection inside
  • Light-sensitive drugs (5.4.5): such as nifedipine (photodegrading to a red impurity), vitamin B₂ and some monoclonal antibodies. Amber glass vials or foil overwrap are needed
  • Diagnostic test strips/reagents: light can denature the reagent and reduce detection sensitivity; the packaging must block light and the label needs the keep-away-from-light symbol
  • Light-activated surface modification of implantable devices: some surface coatings degrade under ultraviolet light and must be stored protected from light until use

ICH Q1B photostability requirements

  • ICH Q1B requires photostability testing of drug products, with an exposure of 1.2 × 10⁶ lux·hr (visible light) and 200 W·hr/m² (UV)
  • If the drug substance or drug product is light-sensitive, a keep-away-from-light symbol must be shown on the label
  • The choice of packaging (clear vs. amber glass) must be supported by photostability data
  • The contribution of secondary packaging (carton/outer box) to the light-protection symbol must be verified in the stability studies

Analogy for light-protective packaging

Just as fine red wine needs to be kept in a cellar rather than on a sunny windowsill, the effect of light on molecular structure is slow but irreversible. For a light-sensitive drug, one overly long exposure to warehouse fluorescent lighting may unknowingly reduce the potency of a whole batch.

5.4.6 — Do Not Use If Package Is Damaged: Commentary

Core concept

The "Do not use if package is damaged" symbol is the user-facing expression of the integrity of the sterile barrier system (SBS). It reminds the user that if package integrity has been compromised, the manufacturer's sterility assurance for the product is void.

Pharmaceutical CDMO application scenarios

  • Nest + tub systems for prefilled syringes (PFS): if the outer TYVEK® pouch has a pinhole or an incomplete seal, the sterility of the whole nest cannot be guaranteed and it must be discarded
  • Visual inspection after vial filling: if an abnormality is found in the crimp integrity check, it must be handled according to the SOP even if the product itself was not exposed
  • End-user inspection: hospital pharmacists or nurses must visually check package integrity before use, and this symbol is exactly the reminder to carry out that step
  • Acceptance after cold chain transport: on receipt, both the temperature record (temperature symbol) and package integrity (this symbol) must be checked

ISO 11607 sterile barrier system requirements

  • ISO 11607-1: specifies requirements for materials, sealing systems and barrier design for the packaging of sterilized medical devices
  • ISO 11607-2: specifies validation requirements for the forming, sealing and assembly processes
  • Seal strength testing: peel strength testing ensures that the seal will not open unexpectedly under normal conditions of use
  • Dye penetration test: a commonly used ISO 11607 integrity test method, verifying that the seal has no micro-leaks

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022), 8.116, requires: "Container closure integrity should be checked at regular intervals using validated methods (container closure integrity testing, CCIT)." This symbol provides the corresponding visual cue at the user end and is the last link in the complete chain from production quality control to end-user behaviour control.

CCIT methods include helium mass spectrometry, electrical capacitance (HVLD, high voltage leak detection) and laser-based headspace analysis, all used to verify the validity of the sterile barrier claim above.

Notes on cross-standard integration

Why is it so important to understand cross-standard links?

The symbols of ISO 15223-1 are "claims", and the related standards (ISO 11135, ISO 11137, ISO 17665 and so on) are the "basis for the claims". In regulatory review (such as FDA 510(k), CE marking technical documentation, or PDMA review), reviewers look not only at whether a symbol is on the label but also at whether complete compliance documentation stands behind the symbol.

CDMO compliance documentation checklist (taking STERILE EO as an example)

  • ISO 11135 sterilization validation report (including bioburden testing and confirmation of the maximum sterilization dose)
  • ISO 10993-7 EO residuals test report (the three residuals EO, ECH and EG)
  • ISO 11607-1/-2 packaging system validation report (seal strength, ageing tests)
  • Stability study report (supporting the expiry date claim)
  • Label design review record (confirmation that the ISO 15223-1 symbols are correct)

TFDA (Taiwan Food and Drug Administration) application notes

Under Taiwan's Medical Devices Act and the Ministry of Health and Welfare's published medical device labelling rules, the labels of imported and domestic medical devices must comply with the symbol provisions of ISO 15223-1 or express the same information by an equivalent means (text). TFDA reviewers are familiar with the ISO 15223-1 symbols, and using this international standard can simplify review communication and speed up the approval process.

Clause 5.5 — Precautionary Symbols

5.5 Precautions — Scope & Purpose

Clause 5.5 covers symbols that warn users of hazards, instruct them to consult documentation, or restrict usage to specific conditions. These symbols must appear wherever labelling regulations or risk management (ISO 14971) require a specific warning to be communicated without relying solely on text.

The fundamental design principle of all precautionary symbols is that they must be self-explanatory to the intended user — a clinician or lab technician — with minimal prior training. ISO 15223-1 specifies that each symbol has an exact meaning that manufacturers may not alter.

"The symbols in this part of ISO 15223 shall not be modified in a manner that changes their meaning." — ISO 15223-1:2016, Clause 4.2

General Requirements for Precautionary Symbols

  • Must be sized to remain legible at the viewing distance relevant to the label format
  • Shall not be placed so close together that they become visually confusing
  • Where accompanied by text, the text shall not contradict the symbol's normative meaning
  • Colour is not mandated by ISO 15223-1, but adjacent regulatory standards (IEC 60601-1) may require specific colours for safety-critical symbols

Why precautionary symbols matter: a pharma CDMO perspective

In a pharma CDMO setting, label warning symbols are the last line of defense. When a clinician picks up a sterile injectable in the ICU, there is no time to read the full instructions for use; one glance at the symbols must convey every critical risk message.

Analogy: the power of road signs

The warning triangle (Caution symbol) is like a "danger ahead" road sign: whatever language the driver speaks, it immediately says "pay attention". ISO 15223-1 aims to give device symbols the same universal readability.

Regulatory links

  • EU MDR 2017/745 Annex I: requires labels to carry all necessary warnings and gives preference to EN ISO 15223-1 symbols
  • FDA 21 CFR Part 801: allows ISO symbols in place of English text, provided the symbols conform to ISO 15223-1
  • ISO 14971 risk management: label symbols are one of the risk control measures and must be included in the residual risk evaluation

CDMO practice: when must the Caution symbol be printed?

Situations where a warning symbol is mandatory

  • A paper IFU (an electronic version may replace it on request)
  • The IFU symbol on the label (it must be clearly visible)
  • Access instructions for the electronic IFU (eIFU)

Regulation (EU) No 207/2012 allows certain device categories to replace the paper IFU entirely with an eIFU, but the IFU symbol on the label must still be retained.

Quick decision: Caution or IFU symbol?

Ask yourself three questions:

  1. Is there enough label space for the full warning text? If not, use a symbol
  2. Does the user need step-by-step guidance to use the device safely? If yes, add the IFU book symbol
  3. Is there a specific hazard (toxicity, infection, electric shock)? If yes, add the Caution triangle

Symbol: Biological Risks / BIOHAZARD

☣

Biological Risks (Biohazard)

ISO 7000-1051

Meaning: Indicates that the item presents a biological hazard — it contains or has been in contact with potentially infectious material (blood, body fluids, pathogens).

Indicates a biological hazard: the item contains, or has been in contact with, potentially infectious material (blood, body fluids, pathogens).

Visual: The universal three-lobed biohazard symbol (Trefoil design), typically rendered in black or orange on contrasting background.

Scope of Application

  • Diagnostic specimens: Blood collection tubes, urine containers, swab kits
  • IVD reagents: Controls and calibrators containing human-derived material
  • Waste containers: Sharps disposal, infectious waste bags
  • Post-use devices: Used syringes, catheters, endoscopes
  • Viral challenge media: Laboratory standards and reference materials

Relationship to ISO 22870 and ISO 15189

Point-of-care testing (POCT) facilities and clinical laboratories operating under ISO 15189 are required to ensure all specimen containers bearing this symbol are handled within BSL-2 containment protocols. The biohazard symbol on an IVD kit label triggers specific laboratory SOP requirements.

CDMO scenarios: guide to using the Biohazard symbol

Analogy: the nuclear plant radiation symbol

Like the radiation symbol (☢), the Biohazard symbol is a globally recognized "stop, special handling required" signal. Even in resource-limited settings, healthcare workers are trained to recognize it.

Points for special attention at a pharma CDMO

  • Autotransfusion devices: systems that return the patient's own blood must carry the Biohazard symbol, because the blood itself is treated as a potential source of infection
  • Diagnostic kits: IVD kits containing positive control material made from human serum must be marked
  • Bacterial filters: after use they become infectious waste, so the packaging should be pre-marked with Biohazard (recommended practice)
  • Blood glucose test strips: strips used for whole-blood testing should be treated as a potential biohazard after use

Avoid overusing the Biohazard symbol

A common mistake is to print the Biohazard symbol on every device that touches the human body (e.g., ordinary bandages, cotton swabs). ISO 15223-1 clearly states that this symbol should be used only where there is a real risk of transmitting infection. Overuse causes alert fatigue and weakens the warning effect of the symbol.

Decision tree: is the Biohazard symbol needed?

  • Does the device contact blood, body fluids or pathogens? If yes, use it
  • Is it an IVD reagent containing human-derived material? If yes, use it
  • Is it an infectious waste container? If yes, use it
  • Is it an ordinary sterilized sterile device? No (it should not be marked)

Clause 5.6 — Identification and Reference Symbols

5.6 Overview — Purpose of ID Symbols

Clause 5.6 covers symbols used to present identification and reference information on medical device labels. These are not warnings — they are data carriers. Every ID symbol is paired with an alphanumeric code or number that uniquely identifies some attribute of the device or its manufacturing history.

The regulatory significance of these symbols has increased dramatically since the introduction of the Unique Device Identification (UDI) systems — EU MDR Article 27, FDA 21 CFR Part 830, and the IMDRF UDI Guidance — which require structured data in machine-readable and human-readable formats on labels.

"The purpose of identification symbols is to enable unambiguous identification of the device or its production context at any point in the supply chain." — ISO 15223-1:2016 interpretive commentary

Key Principle: Symbol Precedes Code

For every Category 5.6 symbol, the symbol appears to the left of or above the corresponding code/number. The symbol tells readers what type of identifier follows; the code itself is the actual identifier. This two-part system is fundamental to UDI label architecture.

UDI framework: identification symbols are the backbone of the modern label

The UDI requirements for modern medical device labels are essentially a regulatory codification of "mandatory use of ISO 15223-1 identification symbols". Understanding these symbols means understanding the labeling language of the UDI system.

Analogy: the product barcode system

Think of supermarket goods: the barcode (GS1 DataBar) contains the manufacturer code, item code and lot number. The ISO 15223-1 identification symbols are the markers beside the label that tell you what each group of digits means: REF says "this is the model number" and LOT says "this is the lot number", just like the "product name" and "expiry date" tags on a supermarket shelf.

The three UDI layers and the corresponding ISO symbols

  • DI (Device Identifier): identified with the REF symbol → model/catalogue number
  • PI (Production Identifier): identified with the LOT and/or SN symbols → lot or serial number
  • AI (Additional Identifier): uses symbols such as QTY and GMDN → quantity, classification code

Global UDI database requirements

  • US GUDID: FDA's Global UDI Database; all devices marketed in the US must be registered
  • EU EUDAMED: the European device database; mandatory under MDR/IVDR
  • Taiwan MDDB: the TFDA medical device database; applies to higher-risk devices

LOT vs SN — When to Use Which

REF / LOT / SN: guide to CDMO label design decisions

CDMO configuration points for the REF symbol

  • Model number structure: the code after REF usually encodes material, size, sterility status and so on (e.g., in REF 4521-ST-20G, ST = Sterile and 20G = 20 gauge)
  • Change management: a device design change (MCN) usually triggers a new REF, which must be reported again to FDA/under the MDR
  • Custom devices: when a CDMO manufactures for a client, it uses the client's REF (the brand owner's model number), not the CDMO's own model number
  • GS1 integration: REF usually corresponds to GS1 Application Identifier (01) — GTIN

LOT symbol: best practices for lot number design

  • Coding logic: include year + month + sequence number, e.g., 26J15-001 (lot 1 of 15 October 2026)
  • Uniqueness: a manufacturer's LOT numbers must be unique over the life of its product
  • Recall trigger: FDA/MDR recalls are made by LOT, so a clearly legible LOT on the label is key to recall efficiency
  • GS1 mapping: LOT corresponds to GS1 AI (10) — Batch/Lot Number
  • Font: FDA 21 CFR 801 recommends a minimum height of 1/16 inch (about 1.6 mm) to ensure legibility

SN symbol: compliance requirements for implantable devices

Under EU MDR Article 27(4), Class III and active implantable devices must include a serial number (SN) on the label and on the UDI carrier. This means:

  • Pacemakers, artificial joints, implantable sensors: SN is mandatory
  • Single-use syringes, test strips: LOT is sufficient
  • The SN must be searchable in EUDAMED (EU) or GUDID (US)

Analogy: LOT vs SN is like "flight number vs seat number"

LOT is like the "flight number": it identifies all devices released in the same lot (all the passengers on the same flight). SN is like the "seat number": it uniquely identifies one specific device (the one passenger in that particular seat). In a recall, knowing the LOT lets you recall the whole lot; knowing the SN lets you pinpoint exactly which patient has that one device.

CDMO application guide for advanced identification symbols

GMDN code: why does a CDMO need to know it?

The GMDN code is the common language of global device procurement and regulatory submissions. Practical impact on a CDMO:

  • EU MDR EUDAMED registration: every UDI-DI must be linked to the correct GMDN code
  • WHO procurement: WHO/UNICEF device procurement specifications use GMDN codes to define products
  • Market research: GMDN codes can be used for competitor analysis and market size estimation
  • Customer requirements: a growing number of hospital purchasing departments require suppliers to list the GMDN code on the COA

IVD symbol: key points for IVDR 2017/746 compliance

  • Mandatory marking: EU IVDR requires the labels of all IVD devices to carry the IVD symbol, regardless of risk class
  • Symbol placement: recommended in the upper right corner of the label or next to the device name, to ensure it is clearly visible
  • FDA IVD: US 21 CFR Part 809 permits use of the IVD symbol but does not require the ISO version
  • Self-testing IVDs: home blood glucose meters, COVID rapid tests and similar products need an additional "For self-testing" statement

Common errors with the QTY symbol

In the European market, where CE marking applies, European Commission enforcement surveys found the following common errors:

  • The outer carton is printed QTY 10, but actually contains 12 (a packaging design change was not matched by a label update)
  • No distinction between "package quantity" and "usable unit quantity": for example, a box containing 5 trays of 10 units each should be labeled QTY 50, not QTY 5
  • In multilingual labels, the QTY symbol was mistranslated as a unit of volume (confusion between quantity and capacity)

Label design: best practices for symbol layout

Symbol grouping principles (the core lesson of Annex A)

The label designs shown in ISO 15223-1 Annex A follow this grouping logic:

  1. Zone 1 — Product identification: brand name, device name, specifications
  2. Zone 2 — Traceability identification: REF + LOT/SN + QTY (core UDI information)
  3. Zone 3 — Date information: date of manufacture, expiry date
  4. Zone 4 — Storage/environment: temperature, humidity, light conditions
  5. Zone 5 — Warning symbols: Caution, Biohazard, Latex, Rx Only
  6. Zone 6 — Regulatory information: CE mark, authorized representative, GS1 barcode/QR code

Analogy: the label is the device's "ID card"

A good label design is like an ID card: a photo on the front (device graphic), a name (device name), an ID number (REF), a date of birth (date of manufacture), an expiry date, and special marks (warning symbols). Everything is in a fixed position, so healthcare workers can find the information they need at first glance.

Priorities when space is limited

When label area is restricted (e.g., needle caps, tubes), the order of priority implied by ISO 15223-1 is:

  1. Safety-related symbols (Caution, Biohazard) — highest priority
  2. Lot identification (LOT) — needed for recalls
  3. Model number (REF) — needed for identification
  4. Expiry date
  5. Other identification information (GMDN, QTY) — may be moved to the outer package

FDA 21 CFR 801 and EU MDR Annex I.23 both allow some information to be placed on the secondary container (secondary packaging) rather than the immediate container, provided the design ensures the information can be seen before use.

Annex B (Informative) — Use of General Prohibition and Negation Symbols

Annex B — Constructing Compound Symbols

Annex B of ISO 15223-1:2016 explains how to construct compound symbols that express "do not" or "not [attribute]" by combining a base symbol with one of two negation overlays:

Negation Method 1: Prohibition Circle (ISO 7000-1641)

A red circle with a red diagonal bar overlaid on the base symbol, signifying "prohibited" or "do not." The circle must fully enclose the base symbol; the diagonal bar runs from upper-left to lower-right at approximately 45°.

Negation Method 2: General Negation Line

A diagonal line without the circle — used when the circular prohibition symbol is too visually complex for small label formats. Less emphatic than the full prohibition circle.

Standard Compound Symbols from Annex B

Annex B Design Rules

  • The prohibition circle colour: red (Pantone 485 C or equivalent)
  • The base symbol inside the circle must remain clearly recognisable — do not distort the base symbol to fit
  • The diagonal bar must cross the base symbol's visual centre
  • Minimum size: the compound symbol must meet the same legibility requirement as the base symbol alone

Negation symbols: the compound symbols CDMOs use most

Do Not Reuse: regulatory implications

The "Do Not Reuse" symbol (usually shown as the numeral "2" crossed out) is the most important compound symbol on sterile single-use devices.

  • FDA position: reuse of a single-use device constitutes adulteration, and the manufacturer cannot guarantee safety after reuse by a third party
  • MDR requirement: EU MDR Annex I.23.4(d) requires single-use devices to be clearly marked, using the ISO 15223-1 symbol
  • Hospital reuse (SUDs reprocessing): the US allows hospitals to commission FDA-registered third-party reprocessors (e.g., Stryker Sustainability Solutions) to reprocess certain "single-use" devices, but this requires a separate 510(k) clearance
  • CDMO obligation: the CDMO responsible for packaging must ensure this symbol is present and correct, even when the device design is the client's responsibility

Not Sterile vs STERILE: consequences of misuse

Placing the "STERILE" symbol on a non-sterile product, or omitting the "Not Sterile" symbol, is a serious labeling error and may lead to:

  • An FDA Warning Letter
  • A device recall (Class I, the most severe)
  • Criminal liability (if patients are injured)
  • Withdrawal of the CE mark

In 2022, the FDA MAUDE database recorded several postoperative infection events caused by "non-sterile device wrongly labelled STERILE".

Analogy: negation symbols are like the "×" mark

The prohibition circle is like the "no entry" traffic sign: a red circle by itself is a universal language for "prohibited", regardless of culture. ISO chose this design precisely because of its universality: healthcare workers worldwide recognize a red circle with a diagonal slash as "prohibited/do not", and the base symbol inside tells them what is prohibited.

Special considerations for the Non-Latex negation symbol

  • ISO 15223-1 does not standardize a "Latex-Free" symbol (it standardizes only "Contains Latex")
  • Where FDA permits, a "Non-Latex" statement may use the Annex B method to construct a negation symbol
  • But FDA recommends that, for patients with severe latex allergy, the text statement "This product contains no natural rubber latex" is clearer and lower risk than a symbol
  • CDMO recommended practice: use both a symbol and a text statement, especially for devices intended for high-risk patient groups (e.g., spina bifida patients)

ISO 15223-1 vs ANSI/AAMI ST93

In addition to ISO 15223-1, the US market also uses ANSI/AAMI ST93 (the US standard for medical device labeling symbols). The two are highly compatible but have differences.

FDA policy on ISO symbols

The 2016 FDA guidance "Use of International Symbol Standard ISO 15223-1 on Medical Device Labels" confirms that manufacturers may use ISO 15223-1 symbols in place of, or in addition to, English text, but they must:

  • Declare the use of ISO 15223-1 on the cover page (510(k)/PMA)
  • Include a symbols glossary (Glossary of Symbols) in the labeling or IFU
  • Ensure users can look up the meaning of each symbol

In an FDA 510(k) submission, a labeling review is required, and the reviewer will check that ISO symbols are used correctly.

DSCSA and EU MDR UDI: CDMO implementation roadmap

US FDA UDI timeline (already in effect)

  • 2013: Class III devices (implantable, life-sustaining) — UDI mandatory
  • 2015: Class II devices — UDI mandatory
  • 2018: Class I and exempt devices — UDI mandatory
  • 2022: deadline for full compliance of all Class I exempt devices
  • Current status: all medical devices on the US market must have a UDI, in both machine-readable (barcode/RFID) and human-readable (ISO symbol) formats

EU MDR UDI timeline (2026 highlights)

  • May 2021: MDR formally entered into application, with UDI requirements phased in
  • 2023: UDI mandatory for Class III devices
  • 2025: UDI mandatory for Class IIa/IIb devices
  • 2027: UDI mandatory for Class I devices (coming soon!)
  • EUDAMED: the device registration module is open; UDI data must be registered before placing on the market

The CDMO's role in UDI compliance

A CDMO is usually not the "manufacturer" in the regulatory sense but a contract manufacturer. However, the CDMO must:

  • Ensure labels (including all ISO symbols) conform to the client's UDI specifications
  • Retain batch records (all process data for each LOT) to support the client's UDI traceability
  • Specify clearly in the QTA (quality technical agreement) who is responsible for checking UDI labels
  • Be responsible for UDI registration if the CDMO acts as the authorized manufacturer

Where DSCSA (Drug Supply Chain Security Act) and UDI intersect

Although the DSCSA (Drug Supply Chain Security Act, 2013) is aimed mainly at drugs, it has particular implications for CDMOs handling drug-device combination products:

  • Prefilled syringes (drug + syringe): subject to both DSCSA and UDI rules
  • Drug side: requires DSCSA serialization and the SNDC (Standardized Numerical Identifier)
  • Device side: requires UDI (LOT/SN/REF symbols)
  • The CDMO must ensure its labeling system can output data that satisfies both systems at the same time

Sterile Injectable Device Label — ISO 15223-1 Compliance Checklist

Use this checklist when reviewing labels for sterile injectable medical devices (prefilled syringes, vials with device components, IV sets) intended for global markets. Each item maps to a specific ISO 15223-1 clause or related regulatory requirement.

Category A — Identification Symbols (5.6) — UDI Required

  • REF symbol followed by device model/catalogue number is present and correct
  • LOT symbol followed by batch code is present — format consistent with QMS batch numbering SOP
  • If implantable or reusable: SN symbol followed by unique serial number is present
  • QTY symbol shows correct count if package contains multiple units
  • If IVD: IVD symbol is present in a prominent location
  • GMDN code is included (mandatory for EUDAMED/EU; recommended for other markets)
  • GS1 DataMatrix or barcode encodes correct GTIN (01), LOT (10), SN (21), Expiry (17)
  • Human-readable text accompanies all machine-readable codes

Category B — Precautionary Symbols (5.5)

  • Caution triangle (ISO 7000-0434) or IFU book symbol is present if hazards or detailed instructions exist
  • Biohazard symbol present if device contacts blood/body fluids or contains human-derived materials
  • LATEX symbol present if any component contains natural rubber latex (FDA mandatory)
  • Non-Pyrogenic symbol present if device is tested non-pyrogenic (IV route required)
  • Rx Only symbol present if device requires physician prescription (US/EU Class II+)

Category C — Sterility Symbols (5.2)

  • STERILE symbol present with correct sterilization method identifier (EO/R/STEAM/ASEPTIC)
  • Single Use / Do Not Reuse symbol present on all single-use devices
  • Do Not Re-sterilize symbol present where re-sterilization by user is prohibited
  • Use-by date (ISO 7000-1424) present in YYYY-MM-DD or YYYY-MM format
  • Date of manufacture (ISO 7000-1570) present if required by destination market

Compliance checklist (continued) and QC points

Category D — Manufacturer / Regulatory (5.4)

  • Manufacturer symbol (factory icon) followed by manufacturer's full name and address
  • If not manufactured in EU: EC REP symbol and authorised representative's name/address
  • If not manufactured in US: US agent contact information
  • CE marking (if applicable) with notified body number

Category E — Environmental / Storage (5.1)

  • Temperature range symbol present if storage temperature is not ambient (2–8°C refrigerated; frozen)
  • Humidity limits symbol if product is moisture-sensitive
  • Fragile / Handle with Care symbol if applicable
  • Keep Dry symbol if moisture protection is critical
  • Keep Away from Light symbol for photosensitive products

Category F — Annex B Negation Symbols

  • Do Not Reuse: prohibition circle correctly overlaid on base symbol; circle is red
  • Not Sterile: if device is sold non-sterile, symbol or text "NON-STERILE" is present
  • Do Not Use if Package Damaged: present on all sterile single-use devices
  • Non-Latex (if claimed): appropriate symbol or text claim present

Common causes of label-related 483s/Warning Letters

  • Critical Label says "STERILE" but the device has not undergone a validated sterilization process
  • Critical LOT number format inconsistent with the batch record (traceability not possible)
  • Critical LATEX symbol missing (mandatory under FDA when NRL is present)
  • Major REF does not match the GUDID/EUDAMED registration
  • Major Expiry date format non-compliant (FDA requires YYYY-MM-DD)
  • Minor Symbol size does not meet legibility requirements
  • Minor Symbols glossary not attached to the IFU

Label verification process

CDMO recommended label verification steps:

  1. Design review: dual review by QA + Regulatory of whether the symbols selected conform to ISO 15223-1
  2. Legibility testing: verify that all symbols are legible under worst-case conditions (smallest font, longest reading distance)
  3. Barcode verification: verify DataMatrix quality to the ISO/IEC 15415 standard (Grade C or above)
  4. User studies: for new symbol combinations, usability testing with representative users is recommended (FDA recommendation)
  5. Multi-market review: confirm that the regulatory differences of each target market have been incorporated into the label design

Case Analysis

Case 1: a LOT symbol error that enlarged a recall

Scenario: At a CDMO producing sterile injection needles, a malfunction of the label printing system caused some batches to be printed with the previous batch's lot number. After an infection event, the recall should have covered only LOT 26A10-005 (about 50,000 units), but because the LOT numbers overlapped, LOT 26A09-005 (about 50,000 units) also had to be recalled, doubling the recall scale.

Root cause: the LOT symbol was correct (LOT was printed), but the number itself was wrong. The Label Print Verification (LPV) system had no automatic check that the lot number matched the batch manufacturing record (BMR).

Corrective actions:

  • Implement automated vision inspection to verify the LOT number
  • Add label sample retention to the batch record
  • Build a LOT number uniqueness database to prevent reuse

Case 2: missing IVD symbol and a failed EUDAMED registration

Scenario: A CDMO client's COVID-19 rapid test kit had completed its IVDR technical documentation and its UDI registration in EUDAMED, but a pre-market audit found that the commercial label artwork lacked the IVD symbol (mandatory under EU IVDR 2017/746).

Impact: the label had to be revised and resubmitted for Notified Body review (because the label is part of the technical documentation), delaying market launch by 3 months.

Lesson: the CDMO's label design review process should include a dedicated "IVDR-specific symbol checklist". The IVD symbol should be built in at the start of design, not discovered missing just before the final audit.

Case 3: the Biohazard symbol dispute

Scenario: A CDMO manufacturing blood glucose test strips was asked by the client to print the Biohazard symbol on the strip box because used strips "may contact blood".

ISO 15223-1 analysis: the ISO standard specifies the Biohazard symbol for devices where there is a real risk of transmitting infection. Unused strips contain no blood and should not be marked Biohazard.

Correct CDMO approach:

  • New strip box: should not carry the Biohazard symbol
  • Waste strip container (for collecting used strips): should carry the Biohazard symbol
  • Add waste handling instructions to the IFU, reminding users that used strips should be treated as a potential biohazard

The CDMO should specify in the QTA who is responsible for reviewing decisions on the use of such symbols.

Key takeaways: the three most important lessons for CDMOs

  1. The LOT symbol is the precision of a recall: the uniqueness and traceability of the LOT number directly determine the scope of a recall. A CDMO's lot number design and label print verification are critical nodes in the quality system.
  2. IVD + IVDR = new obligations: after 2027, all EU IVD products (including Class A general reagents) must carry a full UDI and the IVD symbol. CDMOs serving IVD clients need to plan ahead.
  3. Biohazard ≠ any device that touches blood: correct use (not overuse) of the Biohazard symbol reflects attention to detail in CDMO label compliance. Over-marking causes alert fatigue, while under-marking is a compliance deficiency.
