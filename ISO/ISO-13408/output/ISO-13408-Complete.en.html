Clause 1–3: Scope, Normative References, and Terms & Definitions

Original Text

1 Scope

1.1 This part of ISO 13408 specifies the general requirements for, and offers guidance on, processes, programmes and procedures for development, validation and routine control of the manufacturing process for aseptically-processed health care products.

1.2 This part of ISO 13408 includes requirements and guidance relative to the overall topic of aseptic processing. Specific requirements and guidance on various specialized processes and methods related to filtration, lyophilization, clean-in-place (CIP) technologies, sterilization in place (SIP) and isolator systems are given in other parts of ISO 13408.

"Wherever possible, healthcare products intended to be sterile should be sterilized in their final sealed container (terminal sterilization). When a health care product is intended to be sterile and cannot be terminally sterilized, aseptic processing provides an alternative."

Key Terms and Definitions (Clause 3)

  • Aseptic processing (3.4): Handling of sterile product, containers and/or devices in a controlled environment, in which the air supply, materials, equipment and personnel are regulated to maintain sterility
  • Aseptic processing area (APA) (3.5): Facilities for aseptic processing, consisting of several zones
  • Critical processing zone (3.12): Location within the APA in which product and critical surfaces are exposed to the environment
  • Direct support zone (3.16): Protective area directly surrounding a critical processing zone
  • Indirect support zone (3.24): Location within the APA which protects the direct support zone
  • Isolator (3.26): Enclosure capable of preventing ingress of contaminants by means of physical interior/exterior separation, and capable of being subject to reproducible interior bio-decontamination
  • Unit operation (3.39): Defined chemical or physical step in a manufacturing process
  • Validation (3.40): Documented procedure for obtaining, recording and interpreting the results required to establish that a process will consistently yield product complying with predetermined specifications
  • Worst case (3.41): Set of conditions that represent the greatest challenge to product integrity and safety which will be accepted during routine production

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Aseptic Processing vs. Terminal Sterilization

Terminal Sterilization: The product is sterilized in its final sealed container (e.g. steam, radiation). The SAL can be calculated and the risk is lowest.

Aseptic Processing: When a product cannot withstand terminal sterilization (heat-labile biologics, protein drugs), the components are sterilized beforehand and then assembled and filled in a tightly controlled aseptic environment.

The golden rule of ISO 13408-1: terminal sterilization is always the first choice. Choosing aseptic processing must be supported by sound scientific justification.

Analogy: Preparing a Meal in an Operating Room

Imagine you have to prepare a sterile meal inside an operating room. You cannot reheat it in a microwave (= terminal sterilization is not possible), so you need to: disinfect all the ingredients and utensils beforehand (= component sterilization), work in an environment as tightly controlled as an operating room (= APA), and wear full surgical gowns (= gowning procedure). That is the essence of aseptic processing.

Key Point: Structure of the ISO 13408 Series

ISO 13408 has seven parts. Part 1 is the umbrella standard and the other parts address specific technologies:

  • Part 2: Sterilizing filtration (membrane filtration)
  • Part 3: Lyophilization
  • Part 4: Clean-in-place (CIP)
  • Part 5: Sterilization-in-place (SIP)
  • Part 6: Isolator systems
  • Part 7: Alternative processes for small-volume products

CDMO application: most sterile-product CDMOs deal with Parts 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 at the same time, which means your validation system has to cover the requirements of the whole series.

Clauses 4–5: Quality System Elements & Aseptic Process Definition

Original Text

4 Quality System Elements

4.1.1 A quality management system, appropriate to the nature of the operations, shall be implemented to assure control over all activities affecting aseptic processing. Unless a superseding national, regional, or International Good Manufacturing Practice (e.g. the World Health Organization GMPs) is employed, the quality management system shall be in conformance with the requirements of ISO 9001 and/or ISO 13485.

4.1.2 Documented procedures for each phase of the development, validation, routine monitoring and control of the aseptic process shall be prepared and implemented.

4.2.2 Management shall be responsible for ensuring that there is an adequate number of qualified employees to perform required work and that supervision is provided.

5 Aseptic Process Definition

5.1.1 Aseptic processing is an activity composed of many unit operations that need to be effectively combined to maintain sterility.

5.1.2 A justification for the use of aseptic processing shall be documented. NOTE: The preferred option is terminal sterilization in the final container.

5.1.5 The aseptic process definition shall consider the complete process and give a rationale describing how each element involved in processing contributes to the attainment and maintenance of a sterile product.

5.2 Risk Management

5.2.1.1 A risk management process shall be carried out, applying ISO 14971 and/or ICH Q9. Risks associated with the aseptic process shall be identified, assessed and controlled.

5.2.1.2 The risk management strategy shall take into account the nature of the product and its intended clinical use. Microbiological risk management should follow four stages:

  • a) identification of contamination risks
  • b) assessment of contamination risks
  • c) monitoring and detection of contamination
  • d) prevention of contamination

5.2.2 Each unit operation shall be assessed for risks that might compromise the quality of the product. Factors to be considered shall include: contamination sources; inappropriate aseptic technique; ability of microorganisms to cross a segregation barrier; conditions conducive to microbial proliferation; detection of microbial contamination.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Aseptic Process Definition

The Aseptic Process Definition is the heart of ISO 13408-1. It requires you to list all unit operations, from component preparation, filtration and filling through to final sealing, and to explain how each step maintains sterility assurance.

In practice, this is equivalent to the Contamination Control Strategy (CCS) required by EU GMP Annex 1: a living document that records how your aseptic process controls every contamination risk by design.

Key Point: Link to EU GMP Annex 1 (2022)

The 2022 revision of EU GMP Annex 1 makes a CCS mandatory, and the "Aseptic Process Definition + Risk Management" in ISO 13408-1 is the framework on which a CCS is built.

ISO 13408-1 → 5.2 the four steps of risk management map fully onto the CCS elements of Annex 1: identify → evaluate → monitor → prevent. The two reinforce each other and together form the core argument for sterility assurance.

As a CDMO, when you take on a new customer's biologic filling project, the first core document to establish is the Aseptic Process Definition (APD). Clause 6: Manufacturing Environment

Original Text

6.3 Layout — Zone Classification

6.3.2.1 The critical processing zone shall be segregated and operated in a way that ISO 14644-1:1999, Class 5 conditions are maintained under normal operational conditions.

Examples of activities in critical processing zones: aseptic assembly of filling equipment; aseptic connections; aseptic compounding and mixing; filling of product, containers and stoppers; staging and conveying of sterilized primary packaging materials; aseptic filling, stoppering, transfer of open or partially stoppered vials.

6.3.3 For a critical processing zone, a direct support zone with at least an ISO 14644-1:1999, Class 7 environment shall be provided.

In the case of an isolator or similar separative device, a risk assessment shall determine the necessary surrounding environmental class. Typically an ISO Class 8 is used.

6.3.4 Indirect support zones shall correspond to ISO 14644-1:1999, Class 8 (typically).

6.5 HVAC System

6.5.2.1 Within the APA, an HEPA-filtered air supply shall maintain a positive pressure relative to surrounding areas of lower grade under all operational conditions.

For the critical processing zone, a unidirectional airflow of sufficient velocity to provide adequate protection shall operate other than in isolators.

6.5.3.1 Within the critical processing zone and direct support zone, an effectively flushing airflow pattern shall be specified. Effectiveness shall be demonstrated, verified and documented as demonstrated by air visualization studies.

6.8 Environmental and Personnel Monitoring

6.8.1.5 The critical processing zone shall be monitored during each operational shift.

6.8.4.1 Gloved fingerprints of personnel present in the direct support zone and/or critical processing zone shall be monitored daily. At defined intervals samples from the gowns shall also be taken.

6.8.6.1 Alert and action levels: For the critical processing zone, each microorganism detected shall be investigated. Alert levels and action levels shall be reviewed at defined, regular intervals.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Concentric Layers of Protection

Think of the APA as the concentric lines of defense of a military base:
• Innermost layer (Class 5/Grade A) = the core command center; anyone entering needs the highest security clearance
• Middle layer (Class 7/Grade B) = a security buffer zone that controls entry and provides logistical support
• Outer layer (Class 8/Grade C-D) = the outer perimeter, with general but still controlled access
Each layer uses differential pressure (positive pressure) as an "air barrier" to keep external contamination out.

Key Compliance Point: Environmental Monitoring Program

Mandatory environmental monitoring requirements in ISO 13408-1:

  • Frequency: Grade A (every batch/every shift), Grade B (every shift), Grade C/D (periodic)
  • Personnel monitoring: fingerprints (finger dabs) must be monitored daily, which is stricter than the current practice of many companies
  • Trend analysis: do not look only at individual excursions; a trend report must be established
  • Microbial identification: any microorganism recovered in Grade A must be identified to genus/species level

Difference from the FDA aseptic processing guidance: the FDA requires continuous (real-time) particle monitoring in Grade A. The language of ISO 13408 is more principle-based, but in practice both point to the same strict standard.

Practical Application: HVAC Validation

Air visualization (smoke test / airflow visualization study) is a mandatory requirement of ISO 13408-1, but it is also the item most often questioned in audits. The point is not just "having done it", but:

  • It must be performed under worst-case conditions, including the highest personnel load, equipment in operation, and door openings/interventions
  • It must be recorded on video, and the video must clearly show that the airflow in the Grade A area really protects the product exposure points
  • It must be repeated after any change in equipment configuration

  1. Why does ISO 13408-1 require the direct support zone to be Class 7 (Grade B)? Would Class 8 be acceptable? What additional controls would be needed?
  2. When a single colony is found at a Grade A environmental monitoring location, what does ISO 13408-1 require you to do? How does this differ from handling an action level excursion?

Core Concept: Comparison of the Three APA Zones

Special case of isolators: when an isolator is used, its surrounding environment can be reduced to Class 8 (Grade C/D), because the physical barrier replaces the environmental control of a traditional Grade B. This is exactly where the commercial value of isolators lies.

Clauses 7–9: Equipment, Personnel, and Manufacture of the Product

Original Text

7 Equipment

7.1.1 All equipment and components that contact the product or are used within an APA shall be qualified. Qualification shall demonstrate that the equipment is suitable for its intended use and capable of being cleaned and sterilized.

7.1.2 Equipment qualification shall include, at minimum: installation qualification (IQ); operational qualification (OQ); performance qualification (PQ). Any alteration of the equipment or the intended use of the equipment shall be evaluated for its potential impact on the qualification.

7.2.1 A preventive maintenance programme shall be established for all equipment used for aseptic processing in the APA. The equipment shall not be a source of contamination.

8 Personnel

8.1.1 Personnel entering the APA shall have appropriate education and training and shall demonstrate competence in aseptic techniques.

8.2.1 Personnel who regularly work in the APA shall be qualified on aseptic techniques and procedures. Training shall include theoretical and practical aspects.

8.3.2 The gowning procedure shall be validated. Its effectiveness shall be demonstrated by monitoring of the gowned personnel entering the APA. A valid gowning qualification for the aseptic processing area (APA) qualification (APAQ) shall be maintained for each individual.

8.4.1 Personnel with any illness that might adversely affect product quality (e.g. respiratory conditions) shall be excluded from working in the aseptic processing area.

9 Manufacture of the Product

9.1.1 The sterility of all product and/or components entering the critical processing zone shall be assured prior to their introduction.

9.2.1 The maximum duration of each individual phase of the manufacturing process (from the preparation to the completion of processing) shall be defined, validated and justified. The duration limits shall be based on the results of studies to demonstrate satisfactory bioburden control.

9.4.1 Cleaning and disinfection procedures shall be established, validated and documented for each area of the APA. The programme shall include the use of disinfectants of different modes of action on a rotating basis. The programme shall include sporicidal agents.

9.5.1 All equipment and components that come into contact with the product shall be cleaned and sterilized before use. Validated cleaning and sterilization procedures shall be followed.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: IQ/OQ/PQ Qualification System

ISO 13408-1 explicitly requires that equipment used in aseptic processing complete the three stages of qualification:

  • IQ (Installation Qualification): the equipment is installed per specification, documentation is complete, and parts are traceable
  • OQ (Operational Qualification): the equipment operates within the predetermined ranges (e.g. differential pressure, temperature) under worst-case conditions
  • PQ (Performance Qualification): under actual product conditions, the equipment consistently produces results that meet specifications

Key: any equipment change must be reassessed for its impact on qualification. This is where many companies fail to comply strictly.

Key Point: Personnel Qualification (APAQ)

ISO 13408-1 requires every person entering the APA to maintain a valid APAQ (APA Qualification), including:

  • Gowning qualification: several consecutive successful microbiological samplings
  • Aseptic technique assessment (theory + hands-on)
  • Periodic requalification (usually annually)
  • Before first entry into Grade A, the person must pass an APS observation

This is one of the strengthened requirements in the 2022 revision of EU GMP Annex 1 and a common FDA 483 observation.

Process Duration Control

Maximum process duration = result of the bioburden control study

Key time points to be validated:
1. Maximum hold time from compounding to filtration
   → based on bioburden growth studies (typically 0-24 h at room temperature)

2. Maximum time from filtration to completion of filling
   → based on validation of sterility maintenance of the whole filling system

3. Maximum exposure time of partially stoppered product in Grade A
   → directly affects the duration of the APS design

4. Maximum sterile hold time of equipment after CIP/SIP
   → determines the flexibility of the production schedule

Every time limit needs a scientific basis and cannot be set arbitrarily.

Analogy: Disinfectant Rotation

Clause 9.4 of ISO 13408-1 requires that disinfectants be "rotated, using different mechanisms of action" and include a sporicidal agent. This is like an antibiotic strategy in a hospital: long-term use of a single disinfectant breeds resistant organisms (e.g. chlorhexidine-resistant Gram-negative bacteria). Rotation ensures that different types of microorganisms are all targeted, and the sporicidal agent (e.g. vaporized hydrogen peroxide) deals with the hardest-to-kill spores.

Clause 10: Process Simulation (Aseptic Process Simulation / Media Fill)

Original Text

10.1 General

10.1.1 A process simulation programme shall be established for each aseptic process. Process simulation shall be used to evaluate the ability of the aseptic process to produce a sterile product.

10.1.2 Process simulation shall simulate the actual process as closely as possible, including simulating worst-case conditions or interventions. A justification for the conditions chosen as worst-case shall be documented.

10.3 Simulation Procedures

10.3.1 The process simulation shall be designed to ensure that the simulated process is representative of the actual process, including:

  • a) type and number of operator interventions
  • b) worst-case personnel and environmental conditions
  • c) duration of the entire process
  • d) all critical steps of the aseptic process
  • e) all types of containers, sizes and/or fill volumes used in production

10.3.4 The minimum number of units to be processed in the simulation shall be defined and justified, taking into account the statistical validity of the result. This number shall typically be not less than 3 000 units per run.

10.5 Initial Performance Qualification

10.5.1 The process simulation shall be performed a minimum of three consecutive runs as part of initial performance qualification. Positive results shall not be obtained in any run.

10.6 Periodic Performance Requalification

10.6.1 Periodic performance requalification shall be conducted at a frequency defined in the process simulation programme. This frequency shall be no less than twice per year for each shift.

10.6.2 The acceptance criterion for periodic process simulation shall be no contaminated unit in any individual run (zero contamination).

10.7 Repeat of Initial Performance Qualification

10.7.1 A repeat of the initial performance qualification shall be performed following: significant changes to the aseptic manufacturing process; significant changes to the facility, HVAC or equipment; significant changes in personnel; extended shutdown of the manufacturing facility.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Purpose of Process Simulation (APS/Media Fill)

Process simulation (APS), commonly called media fill: a microbial growth medium (e.g. soybean casein digest medium, SCDM) is used in place of the actual product and filled under fully simulated production conditions, and the filled units are then incubated and examined for microbial growth.

This is currently the only feasible way to evaluate the ability of the entire aseptic process to maintain sterility. It cannot calculate an SAL (unlike terminal sterilization), but it is an "integrated test": it verifies the combined effect of the environment, personnel, equipment and procedures.

Analogy: Fire Drill

Process simulation is like a fire drill: you do not need a real fire (= a batch that is actually contaminated), but through the drill (= media fill) you can assess your emergency response capability (= aseptic processing capability). If the drill reveals problems (= media fill failure), you must find the root cause and correct it, then requalify, before you can continue real production.

APS Design Elements and Acceptance Criteria

Initial qualification (Initial PQ):
  - At least 3 consecutive successful runs
  - Each run ≥ 3,000 units
  - Zero contamination in each run (0 positive units)

Periodic requalification:
  - Frequency: at least 2 times per year per shift
  - Acceptance criterion: zero contamination in each run

Actions after a failure:
  - Any positive → stop production immediately
  - Open a deviation investigation
  - Decide on the disposition of the affected batches
  - Repeat the initial qualification (≥3 runs)

Worst case must include:
  ✓ Longest production duration
  ✓ Maximum number of interventions
  ✓ Maximum number of operators
  ✓ Simulation of all planned shifts
  ✓ Representative container formats

Key Point: Regulatory Consequences of an APS Failure

An APS failure (any positive unit) is one of the most serious events in aseptic manufacturing:

  • EU GMP: all batches produced during the same process period must be placed under investigation, and a recall may be required
  • FDA: may lead to a Warning Letter and require a CAPA plan
  • CDMO impact: may affect delivery timelines for all customers' products, with huge commercial losses

Therefore the APS program must be designed to fully simulate worst-case conditions, while ensuring that personnel fully understand the importance of aseptic technique.

Practical Application: When Must the Initial PQ Be Repeated?

ISO 13408-1 explicitly requires repeating the initial qualification in the following situations:

  • Major modification of filling equipment (e.g. adding a new intervention point)
  • Significant change to the cleanroom HVAC system (e.g. replacing a fan)
  • Restart of the facility after a prolonged shutdown
  • A large number of new employees joining the aseptic operations team
  • Requalification after a failure of a periodic run

For a CDMO, when a customer's product is transferred to your site, an initial APS (3 runs) is usually required, even if the product already has APS records at the original site.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Limitations of Sterility Testing

The sterility test is a necessary condition for final product release, but ISO 13408-1 clearly recognizes its fundamental limitations:

  • A sterility test can only test a small number of samples (usually below 0.1% of the batch size), so statistically it cannot assure the sterility of the whole batch
  • If 0.1% of the units in a batch are contaminated, the probability that the sterility test detects it is only about 10%
  • The test itself may produce false positives (laboratory contamination) and false negatives (contamination level too low)

Conclusion: sterility assurance must be built on process control (through APS and environmental monitoring), not on end-product testing. This is the core of aseptic manufacturing philosophy.

Key Point: Investigating False-Positive Sterility Tests

Clause 11.2 of ISO 13408-1 requires that positive results be investigated to determine whether they are true positives or false positives (laboratory contamination). This is a strict requirement:

  • A written false-positive investigation procedure is required
  • Any false-positive claim must have sound scientific justification (it cannot be claimed simply because "no cause could be found")
  • EU GMP and the FDA take a very strict position: a false positive must be extremely hard to accept and needs the support of retesting and microbial identification

Analogy: Seat Belt vs. Airbag

Aseptic process controls (APS, EM, gowning procedures) are like seat belts and road design: they actively prevent accidents. The sterility test is like an airbag: it only protects after the accident has already happened. A good aseptic manufacturer relies on the first line of defense, not on the last safety net of testing.

Practical Application: ISO 13408-1 in CDMO Audits

During a customer audit or a regulatory inspection, the key points examined when assessing ISO 13408-1 compliance are:

  1. Is there a written APD (Aseptic Process Definition)? Is it reviewed periodically?
  2. Does the APS program adequately simulate worst-case conditions? What were the results of the last 3 runs?
  3. Are the APAQ records complete? What is the qualification status of each Grade A/B operator?
  4. Is the disinfectant rotation program documented? Does it include a sporicidal agent?
  5. Is EM trend analysis performed regularly? Is there a CAPA for any alert/action level excursions?

Part 1 Summary

  • Umbrella status: ISO 13408-1 is the foundation of the whole series and establishes the Aseptic Process Definition (APD) framework; the subsequent parts (2-7) build on it to cover specific technologies

Clause 1–4: Scope, Definitions, and Quality System

Original Text

1 Scope

This document specifies requirements for sterilizing filtration as part of aseptic processing of health care products conducted in accordance with ISO 13408-1. It also offers guidance to filter users concerning general requirements for set-up, validation and routine operation of a sterilizing filtration process.

This document is not applicable to removal of viruses. Sterilizing filtration is not applicable to fluids that intentionally contain particles larger than the pore size of the filter (e.g. bacterial whole-cell vaccines).

Introduction — Key Distinctions

"During terminal sterilization the kinetics of inactivation follows a mathematical order and allow calculation of a sterility assurance level (SAL). Removal of organisms from a fluid by filtration does not follow such mathematical order and so the use of the term 'sterility assurance level' is not appropriate for product sterilized by filtration."

Key Definitions

  • Sterilizing filtration (3.11): Removal of viable microorganisms from fluids by passage through a filter under specified process conditions resulting in a sterile filtrate
  • Bacterial challenge test (3.1): Technical operation to evaluate the capability of a filter to retain organisms from liquid bacterial suspension under defined conditions
  • Filter integrity test (3.7): Non-destructive physical test that can be correlated to the bacterial retention capability of a filter assembly
  • Extractable (3.4): Substance that can be released from a filter using extraction solvents or conditions expected to be at least as aggressive as normal use conditions
  • Leachable (3.8): Substance that can be released from a filter or filter assembly during normal use conditions

4.3 Procurement of Filters

4.3.2 There shall be a written agreement between the filter user and filter manufacturer that the filter manufacturer will notify the filter user of any changes in the filter manufacturing conditions with potential to affect the defined fluid and process parameters.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Nature of Sterilizing Filtration and the Limits of SAL

The kill kinetics of terminal sterilization (e.g. steam) follow a logarithmic curve, so an SAL (e.g. 10⁻⁶) can be calculated by extrapolation. Filtration, however, is physical retention, not killing.

A filter membrane cannot guarantee 100% retention (especially of organisms close to the pore size), and it fails completely if the membrane is damaged. Therefore:

  • Sterilizing filtration cannot express its reliability as an SAL value
  • Reliability must be demonstrated through filter validation (bacterial challenge test) + integrity testing (every batch)
  • Maintaining sterility throughout the entire system downstream of the filter is equally critical

Key Point: Supplier Change Notification Obligation

Clause 4.3.2 requires the filter supplier to notify the user of any manufacturing process change that may affect performance. This is why you must:

  • Sign a Quality Agreement with the filter supplier
  • List in the agreement the types of change that trigger notification (membrane material, pore size, manufacturing site, etc.)
  • Establish a supplier change assessment procedure to determine whether revalidation is needed

This requirement has played an important role in several drug recalls, in which a supplier quietly changed the filter material and unexpected leachables appeared in the product.

Analogy: A Filter Is Like a Coffee Filter Paper

A 0.2 µm filter is like an ultra-precise coffee filter paper: it can hold back bacteria (the coffee grounds), but if the paper has a hole (integrity failure), all the grounds pass through. So confirming the integrity of the paper (the integrity test) matters more than the act of filtering itself. And you need to run the challenge test with the liquid actually being filtered (the coffee), not with plain water.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Brevundimonas diminuta?

Brevundimonas diminuta (formerly Pseudomonas diminuta) is the standard challenge organism for 0.2 µm filter validation, because:

  • It is extremely small (about 0.3-0.4 µm), one of the smallest known non-spore-forming Gram-negative bacteria
  • Culture conditions affect cell size (smaller under starvation conditions), which allows a "worst-case" challenge to be produced
  • Specific culture conditions can produce a challenging cell size (ASTM F838-15 method)

Critical warning: if the bioburden of your product contains organisms smaller than B. diminuta (such as certain Mycoplasma), you must change the challenge organism!

Filtration Validation Programme

The four main components of filter validation:

1. Microbial retention validation
   Challenge organism: B. diminuta (0.2µm) or A. laidlawii (0.1µm)
   Challenge level: ≥1×10⁷ CFU/cm²
   Filter lots: ≥3 lots, 3 consecutive successes
   Conditions: worst-case process conditions (upper limits of differential pressure, temperature, flow rate)

2. Integrity test correlation
   - Establish the correlation between bacterial retention and physical integrity
   - Set the integrity test specification for production filters (e.g. bubble point / forward flow limits)

3. Filter-fluid compatibility
   - Chemical compatibility (filter material is not dissolved)
   - Leachables: under production conditions
   - Extractables: under extreme conditions
   - Adsorption: loss of product components

4. Filtration system sterilization validation
   - Sterilization by SIP (sterilization-in-place) or autoclave
   - Effect of the sterilization cycle on filter performance

PUPSIT — Pre-Use Post-Sterilization Integrity Test

PUPSIT (Pre-Use Post-Sterilization Integrity Test) is a mandatory requirement of EU GMP Annex 1 (2022), and ISO 13408-2 also explicitly supports it (8.4.2).

The purpose of PUPSIT: to confirm that the filter was not damaged during the sterilization process (e.g. SIP). It guards against the mechanical damage to the membrane that steam sterilization can cause.

The challenge: the PUPSIT operation itself introduces a contamination risk (wetting with test fluid and connecting instruments are required). A validated PUPSIT procedure is therefore needed to ensure the test does not compromise downstream sterility.

Prerequisite for not performing PUPSIT: it must be supported by a risk assessment, with alternative control measures explained (and whether the regulator accepts them). The FDA generally expects PUPSIT, and omitting it requires strong scientific justification.

Analogy: Checking the Seat Belt Before You Leave

PUPSIT is like checking that the seat belt is intact before every trip (rather than only after an accident). Filter sterilization (SIP or autoclave) is like pulling the seat belt tight: this action itself may damage some components. Confirming the belt is fine before departure (PUPSIT) is much better than discovering at the destination that it broke long ago (post-use integrity test failure), because by then the whole batch has already been filled.

Practical Application: Special Challenges of Biologics

Special challenges in filtration validation for biologics such as monoclonal antibodies (mAbs):

  • High viscosity: high-concentration mAbs (>100 mg/mL) are viscous, causing a high differential pressure across the filter and longer filtration times
  • Adsorption: proteins may adsorb onto PVDF or PES membranes, causing loss of concentration or loss of Fc effector function
  • Antimicrobial properties: some antimicrobial peptide drugs kill B. diminuta, invalidating the challenge test and requiring the use of a "surrogate fluid"
  • Particles: high-concentration protein aggregates may block the filter and affect filter capacity

When a CDMO takes on a new biologic customer, the filter compatibility study is usually one of the most complex and time-consuming validation activities, and it needs to start early in process development.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Integrity Test Failure = Batch Rejection?

The provision in Clause 11.2 is very important: if the post-use integrity test fails, the batch must in principle be rejected.

Exceptions (hard to accept):
You must be able to "demonstrate that the failure was not caused by inadequate microbial retention of the filter". In practice this is almost impossible, because you cannot use a non-destructive method after the fact to test whether microorganisms passed through.

In practice, an integrity test failure is a sufficient condition for batch rejection. This also illustrates the commercial value of PUPSIT: finding a problem before filling avoids discovering it only after the whole batch has been filled.

Practical Application: Redundant Filter Design Strategy

Common filter design strategies in high-risk applications (e.g. biologics, high-value batches):

  • Series (redundant): two 0.2 µm filters in series, one of which is a redundant backup. If the integrity test of the primary filter passes, testing the redundant filter is not required. If the primary filter fails, the status of the redundant filter can be checked, but the decision strategy must be defined in advance in the validation.
  • Bioburden reduction filter: a 0.22 µm bioburden reduction filter is placed upstream of the sterilizing filter to reduce the challenge to the final filter and extend filter capacity. This "prefilter" does not need the same validation requirements as the sterilizing filter.

  • Inherent limitation: sterilizing filtration does not use an SAL; its reliability depends on validation (bacterial challenge test) + integrity testing of every batch, not on theoretical calculation
  • Standard challenge organisms: B. diminuta (ATCC 19146) for 0.2 µm, A. laidlawii for 0.1 µm Mycoplasma filtration, minimum challenge 10⁷ CFU/cm², 3 filter lots and 3 consecutive successes required

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Physics of Lyophilization and Sterility Assurance

Lyophilization (Freeze-Drying): a three-step process:

  1. Freezing: the liquid product is frozen to a solid (-40°C to -60°C)
  2. Primary Drying: vacuum + heating make the ice sublime directly to water vapor (without passing through the liquid state) → removes 95%+ of the water
  3. Secondary Drying: the temperature is raised to remove bound water and reach the target moisture content (usually <1%)

What is special about ISO 13408-3: it does not address the physicochemical properties of the product (that is the job of CMC); it focuses on sterility assurance, ensuring that the lyophilization process does not compromise sterility.

Analogy: Freeze-Dried Space Food

Lyophilization is like making the freeze-dried food that astronauts eat: after freezing, the food is vacuum-dried, so it keeps its shape but is extremely light. Freeze-drying drugs works on the same principle: protein drugs are unstable as liquids (they degrade at room temperature), but once lyophilized into a solid powder they can be stored at room temperature for years.
The difference is that drug lyophilization takes place in an aseptic environment, and the partially stoppered vials sit "open" inside the lyophilizer, which means the entire lyophilizer chamber must be an aseptic space of Grade A quality.

Critical Time Limits: The Most Important Hold Times

Clause 6.6 of ISO 13408-3 lists four critical time limits, each of which must be supported by scientific validation:

  • Maximum time from filling to entering the lyophilizer: prevents bioburden growth in the intermediate product
  • Maximum time from end of lyophilization to unloading: prevents the accumulation of contamination inside the lyophilizer, especially since after secondary drying the moisture is low but spores may still be present
  • Maximum time from SIP to use: the sterile hold time of the lyophilizer, which usually requires positive-pressure inert gas to maintain
  • Maximum use time after sterilization of tools: the sterile shelf life of auxiliary tools such as trays and forceps

The setting of these time limits must be based on bioburden studies or a theoretical rationale, and cannot be arbitrary.

Core Concept: Aseptic Challenges of Lyophilizer Loading

Lyophilizer loading is one of the highest-risk interventions in the entire aseptic process:

  • Hundreds or thousands of partially stoppered vials must be moved from the filling line to the lyophilizer, manually or automatically
  • The entire transfer path must maintain a Grade A (ISO Class 5) environment
  • Modern solution: an automatic loading system (ALU) + isolator, which can complete loading in an enclosed Grade A pathway and avoid personnel intervention

   Typical specification: < 0.003 mbar/min (depending on equipment specification)

   - Ultimate vacuum

Regulatory Debate on Lyophilizer Campaign Use

Clause 7.5.3.2 of ISO 13408-3 allows SIP only once per campaign (rather than every batch) "in defined circumstances". But this concept is debated from a regulatory standpoint:

  • EU GMP preference: SIP for every batch is the safest practice
  • Conditions under which a campaign is acceptable (must be validated): the same product, continuous production, the lyophilizer chamber is not opened between batches, and there is documented evidence that sterility inside the lyophilizer is maintained for longer than the entire campaign
  • CDMO recommendation: unless there is a strong commercial reason, perform SIP for every batch to avoid being challenged during regulatory review

Analogy: A Lyophilizer Is Like a Giant Pressure Cooker (Operating in Reverse)

An ordinary pressure cooker cooks under pressure (>1 atm); a lyophilizer dries under reduced pressure (<1 mbar vacuum). But they have one thing in common: both must have an intact seal (otherwise one explodes and the other becomes contaminated). The leak test confirms that "the seal of this giant pot is intact": a tiny leak point under vacuum will draw in outside ambient air (containing microorganisms) and contaminate the entire chamber and batch.

When a CDMO offers lyophilization services, differences in technical capability often show up in:

  • Equipment scale: lyophilizer shelf area (usually 10-100 m² for commercial scale)

  • Degree of automation: a fully automatic loading/unloading system reduces the risk of personnel interventions in APS
  • Lyophilization process development capability: using a Lyostar (research scale) to optimize transfer to commercial specifications
  • Scale-up validation: transferring a lyophilization process from a small research unit to commercial scale is the most critical and time-consuming activity

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Four Elements of CIP (TACT)

Effective CIP depends on four complementary elements:

  • T (Temperature): high temperature accelerates chemical reactions and enhances detergent efficacy (usually 60-80°C)
  • A (Action, mechanical): turbulence generated by flow rate and spray force physically remove residues
  • C (Concentration): the chemical cleaning power of the detergent (usually NaOH 1-2%, HNO₃ 1%, etc.)
  • T (Time): detergent contact time, allowing the chemical reaction to take place fully

These four elements can compensate for each other: raising the temperature can shorten the time, and increasing the flow rate can lower the concentration. But validation must confirm that the chosen combination of parameters is effective.

Analogy: An Industrial Dishwasher

CIP is like a giant industrial dishwasher: it cleans in place (the pipes do not have to be disassembled and carried to a wash sink), using hot water + detergent + spray pressure to clean the internal surfaces of the whole piping system. A dead leg is like the gaps between stacked plates: the detergent cannot reach them, so the soil stays there. A good CIP system design ensures that every corner can be "hit" by the detergent.

Key Concept: Acceptable Residue Limits (Acceptance Limits)

ISO 13408-4 requires setting "residues without significant patient safety risk", which in practice is usually calculated by the following methods:

  • 10 ppm rule: carryover of the previous product into the next product must not exceed 10 ppm (a traditional method, gradually being phased out)
  • 0.1% therapeutic dose rule: the residue of the previous product must not exceed 0.1% of its minimum therapeutic dose (toxicology-based)
  • HBELs (Health-Based Exposure Limits): limits calculated from the PDE (Permitted Daily Exposure) or ADE, the modern approach required by the 2014 EMA guideline

For a CDMO, the HBEL assessment is now the foundation document of a customer's cleaning validation program and usually requires evaluation by a toxicologist.

Framework for Calculating Cleaning Validation Limits

Calculation of residue limits for cleaning validation:

Method 1: Minimum therapeutic dose method (traditional)
Residue limit = (minimum therapeutic dose × safety factor) /
            maximum batch size × minimum daily dose

Method 2: PDE/ADE method (modern, HBEL-based)
Residue limit (mg/equipment surface) =
    ADE (mg/day) × minimum daily dose /
    maximum batch size of the next product

Sampling methods for cleaning validation:
1. Swab sampling
   - Direct contact method, most representative
   - Used for accessible surfaces

2. Rinse sampling
   - Sampling the final rinse water/solvent
   - Covers a larger area
   - Lower sensitivity

3. Visual inspection
   - Supplementary method, confirms no visible residue
   - Usually a necessary but not sufficient condition

Practical Application: CIP Challenges for a CDMO with Shared Multi-Product Equipment

The biggest CIP challenge for a CDMO is shared multi-product equipment. Every product changeover requires:

  1. Identifying the pairings of the previous product and the next product (matrix approach)
  2. Determining the worst-case product combination to be validated
  3. HBEL assessment (especially for residues of highly potent drugs, HPAPI)
  4. Releasing the equipment for the next product only after cleaning validation is complete

For highly toxic drugs that cannot be effectively cleaned off (e.g. oncology drugs), dedicated equipment may be needed. This is a CapEx question that a CDMO must assess in advance when taking on such customers.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Worst-Case Design for CIP Validation

CIP validation must be performed under worst-case conditions. "Worst case" usually includes:

  • The hardest-to-remove residue: usually the product with the highest concentration or highest viscosity in the validation matrix
  • The worst-to-clean location: dead legs, areas not reached by spray nozzles, weld seams
  • Lowest detergent concentration (lower limit): verifies that cleaning is still effective at the low end of the approved range
  • Shortest contact time (lower limit): verifies that cleaning is still effective within the shortest specified time
  • Lowest temperature (lower limit): verifies that cleaning is still effective at the low end of the temperature specification

Logic: if validation passes under worst-case conditions, it will certainly pass under normal conditions. The reverse does not hold.

The Three-Repetition Requirement for CIP Validation

Clause 6.4 of ISO 13408-4 requires a minimum of three validation runs. This resembles the three-run requirement for APS: three consistent successful results show that the process is reproducible. Note, however:

  • The three runs must be under the same (or worse) conditions
  • The three runs must use the same sampling locations (including the worst-to-clean locations)
  • Every run must meet the predefined residue limit

If the first run passes but the second or third fails, the entire validation fails, and you must find the cause, optimize the CIP procedure and revalidate.

Analogy: Restaurant Dishwashing Standards

It is like a high-end restaurant that requires, after tableware is washed, an ATP luminometer (a rapid detector of bacteria and food residue) reading below a set value before the items can be reused. That is the concept of CIP "acceptable residue limits". For a CDMO, CIP requires not only physical cleanliness (no visible residue), but also chemical residues low enough not to affect the next product, and a microbial load at an acceptable level.

1 Scope

1.1 This part of ISO 13408 specifies the general requirements for sterilization in place (SIP) applied to product contact surfaces of equipment used in the manufacture of sterile health care products by aseptic processing.   • SIP (3.6): Method of sterilization of the internal surfaces of parts of the equipment or an entire process system in situ, without disassembly, using appropriate sterilizing agents. NOTE: In ISO 13408-5, "SIP" includes not only steam in place, but all kinds of sterilization used in situ.

  • d) physical and/or chemical characteristics of sterilizing agent(s)

  • h) maintenance of sterility after the completion of the process

Tutorial Commentary

Core concept: the fundamental difference between SIP and filtration — where SAL applies

The biggest difference between SIP and filtration: SIP is a true sterilization process, so SAL can be calculated!

The microbial kill kinetics of steam sterilization follow a logarithmic curve (D value, Z value) and can be extrapolated to an SAL of 10⁻⁶ (a one-in-a-million probability of survival). This is completely different from filtration (physical retention, for which SAL cannot be calculated).

The goal of SIP validation: to confirm that across the entire equipment internal surface (including the hardest-to-reach locations), the sterilant contact conditions are sufficient to achieve the target SAL (usually 10⁻⁶).

Analogy: pressure-cooking the pipework

SIP is like using steam to "disinfect the whole piping system from the inside" without taking the equipment apart. Picture a complex plumbing system (formulation vessel, piping, valves, filter housings): an ordinary disinfectant can clean the surfaces, but SIP uses saturated steam above 121°C so that every corner reaches a temperature that kills spores. It is like a pressure cooker, which heats from the inside rather than from the outside.

Key concept: maintaining sterility after SIP (Post-SIP Sterility Maintenance)

ISO 13408-5, 5.1.1(h) specifically stresses that after SIP is complete, the sterile state of the equipment must be maintained until use. This is usually achieved by:

  • Keeping the piping filled with positive-pressure inert gas (sterile nitrogen)
  • Closing all valves open to the environment
  • Setting a maximum hold time from SIP to use (supported by validation)

If any event that could compromise sterility occurs between SIP and use (such as an instrument failure or a loose pipe connection), you must reassess whether SIP needs to be repeated.

Clauses 6–9: Validation — Heat Distribution, Penetration, and Biological Indicators

Original Text

Annex A — Specific Guidance on Steam SIP

(Annex A provides detailed guidance on Steam SIP, the most common SIP method)

A.1 General: Steam SIP is based on the same principle as steam sterilization in an autoclave. The main differences are:

  • Steam is directed to the equipment to be sterilized via the existing pipework and connections
  • Non-condensable gases present in the system (air) must be removed to achieve saturated steam conditions
  • Temperature measurements at multiple positions (including worst-case positions such as dead legs, lowest points, and steam traps) are used to demonstrate that sterilizing conditions are maintained throughout

A.2 Heat Distribution Study: A heat distribution study shall be performed to demonstrate that the sterilizing temperature is uniformly achieved and maintained throughout the equipment during the sterilization phase. This is typically done by placing calibrated temperature probes at defined positions including worst-case positions.

A.3 Biological Indicator Study: Biological indicators (BIs) shall be placed at the worst-case positions to demonstrate sterilization efficacy. The use of Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores (D-value ≥ 1.5 min at 121°C) is appropriate for steam SIP. The acceptance criterion is that all BIs at all positions shall be negative after the SIP process.

A.4 Process Cycle Development: The SIP cycle shall be developed to ensure that all product contact surfaces achieve the target F₀ value (equivalent time at 121°C with z = 10°C). The target F₀ value shall be established considering the bioburden and the required SAL.

6 Routine Monitoring

Each SIP cycle shall be monitored and documented, including: temperature at defined monitoring points throughout the cycle; duration of sterilization phase; correlation of monitoring data with validated cycle parameters.

Tutorial Commentary

F₀ calculation and SIP validation

F₀ (equivalent sterilization time at 121°C) calculation:

F₀ = ∫L dt

where L = 10^((T-121)/z)
T = actual temperature (°C)
z = 10°C (typical z value for steam sterilization)

Examples:
- 121°C for 15 min → F₀ = 15 min
- 125°C for 15 min → F₀ = 15 × 10^(4/10) ≈ 38 min
- 118°C for 15 min → F₀ = 15 × 10^(-3/10) ≈ 7.5 min

Typical F₀ targets for SIP validation:
- Biologics systems (low bioburden): F₀ ≥ 15 min
- High-risk locations (dead legs): confirm that F₀ is not below the target value

Note: F₀ is a cumulative calculation over the entire SIP process
It is not only the hold time; the heat-up and cool-down phases also contribute

Core concept: use of biological indicators (BIs)

Geobacillus stearothermophilus is the standard BI for steam sterilization:

  • D₁₂₁ value ≥ 1.5 min (ISO requirement); commercial BIs typically have D₁₂₁ = 1.5-2.5 min
  • Contains 10⁶ spores; theoretically all are killed under SAL = 10⁻⁶ conditions (12 log reduction)
  • After validation, BIs must be incubated (55°C, 7 days) to confirm no growth

BI strategy in SIP validation: place BIs at the most difficult-to-sterilize locations (dead legs, lowest points of horizontal pipe runs). If all BIs at the worst-case locations are negative, then all other locations in the system have in theory also met the target.

Hazards of dead legs and design rules

Dead legs are the most common cause of failure in SIP validation. Typical rules:

  • Industry guidance: dead leg length should not exceed 2 times the connection diameter (the 2D rule)
  • Dead legs longer than 2D need a special design (such as a self-draining valve) or a separate sterilization procedure
  • Mounting points for temperature probes and pressure sensors are all potential dead legs
  • The design of sampling valves must take into account the dead leg behind the valve seat

When purchasing new equipment, identify and eliminate dead legs at the DQ (design qualification) stage; this costs far less than retrofitting later.

Part 5 Summary

  • SIP vs filtration: SIP is a true sterilization process for which SAL can be calculated (target 10⁻⁶); filtration is physical retention for which SAL cannot be calculated

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: space station vs cleanroom

An isolator is like a space station: the interior is completely separated from the harsh environment of outer space (a physical barrier), so a high degree of control can be maintained inside even when the outside is relatively poor. RABS is like a cleanroom with a secured entry door: it has restrictions, but it is not a fully enclosed space station. To "enter" the space station you must go through a complex airlock procedure (similar to the RTP transfer port of an isolator); you cannot simply open the door and walk in.

VHP decontamination validation requirements

Vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VHP/HPV) is the standard bio-decontamination method for isolators. Its validation must include:

  • Biological indicator (BI) challenge: G. stearothermophilus spores (10⁶ CFU) placed at the hardest-to-reach locations inside the isolator (worst case)
  • VHP concentration monitoring: confirm that the VHP concentration is evenly distributed throughout the chamber
  • Desorption and residue assessment: after VHP decontamination, confirm that hydrogen peroxide residues fall to an acceptable level (for both product and operator safety)
  • Material compatibility: confirm that commonly used filters, tubing and container materials can withstand the VHP cycle

Amendment 1 specifically adds a "personnel exposure limit" requirement, reflecting the emphasis on operator safety.

Practical application: the business logic of isolators at a CDMO

An isolator has a higher CapEx than a conventional Grade A/B cleanroom, but a lower OpEx, because:

  • Simplified environmental monitoring: the EM requirements for the Grade C environment around an isolator are much lighter than for Grade B
  • Simplified gowning: operators do not need full Grade A sterile gowning (Grade C attire is enough)
  • Media fill frequency may be reduced: some regulators (EU GMP in particular) recognize that isolators carry a lower risk of contamination from personnel, and may allow a lower APS frequency (with a sound justification)
  • Lower risk of product cross-contamination: the fully enclosed environment helps a CDMO accept a wider range of active drugs (HPAPI, ADC)

ISO 13408-7 (Part 7) — Alternative Processes for Medical Devices and Combination Products

Original Text

1 Scope

This part of ISO 13408 specifies requirements and provides guidance on alternative approaches to process simulations for the qualification of the aseptic processing of medical devices and combination products that cannot be terminally sterilized and where the process simulation approach according to ISO 13408-1 cannot be applied.

This part describes how risk assessment can be used during the development of an aseptic process to design a process simulation study for medical devices and combination products in those cases where a straightforward substitution of media for product during aseptic processing is not feasible or would not simulate the actual aseptic process.

Key Definitions

  • Surrogate product (3.4): Item designed to represent product in process simulations and which is comparable to the actual product
  • Extrinsic contamination (3.1): Ingress of material of external origin during the manufacturing process. NOTE: The focus is biological agents (bacteria, mould, yeast)

10.3 Simulation Procedures — Development of Process Simulation Strategy

1. A process simulation strategy shall be documented for a process that cannot be validated using a conventional process simulation approach.

2. If the process simulation approach outlined in ISO 13408-1 is not practicable, a rationale shall be documented including evidence that consideration was given to:

  • use of sterile liquid media as a substitute for product during process simulation, or
  • direct media contact at the end of the process into the sterile barrier system prior to final closure

3. The entire aseptic process definition shall be included in the process simulation strategy. If the aseptic process is divided into sub-processes, the process simulation for each sub-process in total shall include all steps in the aseptic process.

10.3 c) Process Simulation Throughout the Product Lifecycle

1. The initial process simulation approach shall be established during development. The first process simulation shall be performed in advance of the production of the first-in-human clinical products.

3. For commercial production, a process simulation study shall be designed and performed as part of process validation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core concept: why Part 7 exists

The conventional media fill of ISO 13408-1 assumes that liquid growth medium can be substituted for the product during filling. This does not work for many medical devices and combination products, for example:

  • Prefilled syringe components: aseptic assembly of syringes, plungers and needles
  • Drug + device combination products: drug-eluting stents, drug-coated balloons
  • Solid medical devices: sterile orthopedic implants, heart valves
  • Biologics (such as cell therapy): the product itself is biological material and cannot be replaced by growth medium

ISO 13408-7 provides a framework for how to use risk assessment to design an alternative process simulation strategy.

Key requirement: all steps must be covered

One of the core principles of ISO 13408-7: even when the process is divided into several sub-processes that are simulated separately, the overall strategy must cover all aseptic processing steps.

This is the key requirement that prevents "gaps": a step cannot be skipped because it is "difficult to simulate". If directly substituting growth medium is not feasible, another way must be found to validate that step (for example a combination of environmental monitoring, personnel monitoring and equipment qualification).

Framework for designing an alternative process simulation strategy

ISO 13408-7 process simulation strategy decision flow:

Step 1: Can liquid growth medium directly replace the product?
  Yes → perform a media fill per ISO 13408-1
  No → go to Step 2

Step 2: Can growth medium be injected into the container/sterile barrier
         at the end of the process (before final closure)?
  Yes → use the "end-of-process medium injection" method
  No → go to Step 3

Step 3: Identify all high-risk steps
  For each step:
  - Determine the type of contamination risk (personnel contact, environmental exposure, equipment contact)
  - Select the most suitable simulation/validation method:
    * Surrogate simulation
    * Enhanced environmental monitoring
    * Documented observation of personnel behavior
    * Localized media fill of specific steps

Step 4: Ensure that all steps are covered
  → Document the entire strategy fully
  → Regulators will pay particular attention to "gaps" during review

Analogy: the alternative to a drill

A conventional media fill is like a fire drill (directly substituting the product). But if you are building a real aircraft rather than a liquid, you cannot swap the aircraft for water to run the test. ISO 13408-7 is like "flight simulator training": you design a variety of scenarios (the hardest operating steps, emergency interventions, worst-case environmental conditions) and use the closest possible approximation to reality (surrogates or step-by-step validation) to demonstrate the sterility assurance capability of the whole process.

Practical application: challenges for combination product CDMOs

ISO 13408-7 is particularly important for the following types of business:

  • Prefilled syringe (PFS) assembly: steps such as needle insertion and plunger placement may need an alternative simulation strategy
  • Drug-coated devices: aseptic handling after the coating process, where it may not be possible to inject growth medium directly
  • Cell/gene therapy products: the product itself is biological material, and once it is replaced by growth medium the whole "simulation" no longer represents the real process
  • Implantable drug delivery systems: such as aseptic assembly of micropumps

When a CDMO takes on this type of business, it needs to define the process simulation strategy early in CMC development and communicate proactively with regulators (FDA, EMA) (Pre-Sub Meeting / Scientific Advice) to confirm that the strategy will be accepted.
