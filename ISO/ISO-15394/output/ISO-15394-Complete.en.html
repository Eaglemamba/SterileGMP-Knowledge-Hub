I

Introduction — Why Standardized Barcodes on Shipping Labels?

EDI integration, global traceability, and multi-industry automation

Introduction

The use of electronic data interchange (EDI) in association with the physical transport and handling of packages — and when traceability is appropriate, such as that described in ISO 9000 — requires a clear and unique identifier linking the electronic data and the transport unit.

Bar code-marked transport labels are in widespread use in global industries. Several different standards exist, each designed to meet the requirements of the specific industry sector. For effective and economic use within and between industry sectors, one common multi-industry standard is a necessity.

A bar code-marked transport label is designed to facilitate the automation of shipping and handling of administrative operations. The bar code information on the transport label may be used as a key to access the appropriate database that contains detailed information about the transport unit, including information transmitted using EDI.

Two-dimensional symbols may be included to assist in moving large amounts of shipping label or EDI data from sender to recipient and to assist the transportation carrier automated sortation and tracking systems.

This document incorporates the technology, data structure and conformance standards of ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 31 with the user requirements for shipping labels into a single application standard.

Related standards:

  • ISO 22742 — Product packaging (complementary to ISO 15394)
  • ISO 17365 — RF tags on shipping/transport units (same TC 122)

Why standardize shipping barcodes?

The core problem and the solution

Problem: In the global supply chain, each industry (pharma, food, automotive) uses its own label format. When goods arrive at a warehouse, scanners cannot read the labels automatically and the data has to be re-keyed by hand.

Solution: ISO 15394 provides a unified multi-industry framework that makes the barcode label a bridge between the physical goods and the electronic data (EDI).

What pharma staff need to know

ISO 15394 is the label standard for the logistics level (shipping cartons/pallets);

ISO 22742 is the label standard for the product level (single packs/saleable packaging).

The two are complementary: the serialization requirements of the DSCSA and the EU FMD involve both levels.

Analogy: the barcode label is the supply chain's "ID card"

Every transport unit (pallet, shipping carton) needs an "ID card" (a unique identifier). When the EDI system is linked to the logistics system, scanning this ID card retrieves all the electronic data without manual entry. This is exactly the core function of the SSCC (Serial Shipping Container Code).

Pharmaceutical shipping labels usually work at three levels:

Analysis of the normative references

The status of the GS1 General Specifications

The GS1 General Specifications are the core document of the GS1 organization (responsible for systems such as SSCC, GTIN and GS1-128), and ISO 15394 cites it directly as a normative basis. A pharmaceutical company must become a GS1 member before it can obtain a legitimate GS1 Company Prefix, and only then generate valid SSCCs and GTINs.

Code 39 vs Code 128 vs GS1-128

Code 39: older; supports upper-case alphanumeric characters only, and is used with the ANSI DI (Data Identifier).

Code 128: the modern version; supports the full ASCII character set and can be used with GS1 AIs or ANSI DIs.

GS1-128: a subset of Code 128 that uses only GS1 Application Identifiers; it is the mainstream choice for pharmaceutical shipping labels.

Key definitions referenced throughout the standard:

Quick terminology comparison

SSCC vs GTIN vs SGTIN: how the three differ

The SSCC identifies a "loaded container"; the GTIN identifies a "product type"; the SGTIN = GTIN + serial number, used for item-level tracking.

HRI vs barcode: "read by people vs read by machines"

Barcode/2D symbol: read by machines (scanners, warehouse systems) to transmit data.

HRI (human-readable interpretation): the digits/text printed next to the barcode so that operators can key the data in manually if the scanner fails.

ISO 15394 requires HRI to be printed next to linear barcodes (mandatory); for 2D symbols it is optional.

Why the quiet zone matters

The quiet zone is the blank area on the left and right of a barcode. If it is insufficient, the scanner may misread the boundary and the scan fails. GS1-128 specifies a minimum quiet zone width of 10X (X = minimum module width). When verifying the print quality of pharmaceutical labels, measuring the quiet zone is a mandatory item in GS1 barcode verification.

Clause 4 — Concepts

Two-layer approach · Transport unit hierarchy · Unique transport unit identifier · Label formats

4.1 Principles — Two-Layer Approach

The purpose of a bar code label is to facilitate the automatic exchange of data among all members within a channel of distribution, i.e., supplier, carrier, purchaser and other intermediaries.

Where a bar code label is used in conjunction with electronic databases and/or EDI systems, the amount of data may be significantly reduced and may consist of only one piece of data: the unique identifier for the transport unit.

Trading partners have different information requirements. Some information may be common to two or more trading partners, while other information may be specific to a single trading partner. Information becomes available at different times:

  • Product-specific information at the point of manufacture or packaging
  • Order processing information at the time of processing the order
  • Transport information at the time of shipment

4.2 Transport Unit Hierarchy

4.2.1 Transport Package — A package intended for transportation and handling of one or more articles, smaller packages, or bulk material.

4.2.2 Unit Load — One or more transport packages held together by means such as pallet, slip sheet, strapping, interlocking, glue, shrink wrap or net wrap.

4.2.3 Transport Unit — Both unit loads and transport packages are referred to as transport units in this document.

4.3 Unique Transport Unit Identifier

One unique transport unit identifier shall be assigned and applied to each transport unit prior to shipment. The identifier ("licence plate") is the key providing access to information stored in computer files and may be transmitted electronically.

The identifier may be used by all trading partners to retrieve information about the transport unit or about the status of its physical movement along the supply chain. It enables systems to track and trace individual transport units.

4.4 Label Formats

4.4.1 Base Label — Includes the minimum set of data that fulfils requirements of all trading partners when data is exchanged electronically. A unique transport unit identifier shall be, and a "Ship to" name and address should be, included. Additional recommended data: Ship from, key to carrier's database, key to customer's database.

4.4.2 Extended Label — Used when base label data does not satisfy all trading partner requirements. Organized in three segments:

  • Carrier segment: Key to carrier's database + shipment ID, delivery instructions
  • Customer segment: Key to customer's database + customer part number
  • Supplier segment: Product identification, batch number, dimensions

Clause 5 — Data Content

SSCC · GTIN · GS1 Application Identifiers · Data representation · Structured data files

5.1 Data Representation

5.1.1 Data in Linear Bar Code Symbols — Three permissible combinations:

  1. GS1 Application Identifiers (AIs) per ISO/IEC 15418 — only in GS1-128 (subset of Code 128)
  2. ASC MH10 Data Identifiers (DIs) per ISO/IEC 15418 — with Code 39 (ISO/IEC 16388)
  3. ASC MH10 Data Identifiers (DIs) per ISO/IEC 15418 — with Code 128 (ISO/IEC 15417)

5.1.2 Data in Two-Dimensional Symbols — May be used as mutually agreed by trading partners. Data syntax shall conform to ISO/IEC 15434.

5.1.3 Data in Human-Readable Form — HRI of linear bar code should be provided adjacent to the symbol. Some data may be in human-readable form only.

5.2 Data Elements

5.2.1 Unique Transport Unit Identifier

Shall be encoded in a linear bar code, preceded by the appropriate GS1 AI or ANSI DI. Structure defined in ISO/IEC 15459-1. It:

  • Starts with issuing agency code (IAC)
  • Is unique — no issuer re-issues a number until sufficient time has passed
  • Contains only numeric and upper-case alphabetic characters
  • Does not contain more than 20 characters (including the AI or DI)

The unique transport unit identifier shall be either:

  • The SSCC using AI "00" in GS1-128, or
  • The unique transport unit identifier using ANSI MH10.8.2 Category 10 Data Identifiers for Licence Plates (J–999J) in Code 39 or Code 128

5.2.2 Ship To — Address of delivery party. Up to 5 lines, max 35 alphanumeric characters each, or a location number in bar code.

5.2.3 Ship From — Address for returns. Same format as Ship To. Shall be located in the left, upper-most area of the label.

5.2.4 Key to Carrier's Database — Mutually agreed with carrier. May include: carrier tracking number, carrier code for shipment/transport unit.

5.2.5 Key to Customer's Database — Mutually agreed with customer. May include: purchase order number, part number, KANBAN/pull signal number, shipment ID.

5.2.6 Other Data Elements — As much additional data as required may be included in the extended label.

GS1 Application Identifier quick reference

5.3 Concatenating Data Fields in Linear Bar Code Symbols

5.3.1 Using GS1 Application Identifiers (AI) — When several AIs and their data are concatenated into one GS1-128 symbol, each variable-length field shall be followed by the FNC1 (Function 1) character, unless it is the last field encoded in the symbol. The FNC1 character is transmitted as GS (ASCII 29) by the decoder.

5.3.2 Using ANSI MH10.8.2 Data Identifiers (DI) — When DIs are concatenated into one Code 39 or Code 128 symbol, each field shall be followed by a plus symbol "+", unless it is the last field.

5.4 Structured Data Files

5.4.1 General — Structured data files (documentation, EDI messages, delivery notes, quality certificates, insurance certificates) may be included in high-capacity 2D symbols. Shall comply with ISO/IEC 15434 syntax or GS1 General Specifications.

5.4.2 Shipping and Receiving Data — All linear bar code data may be combined into one single 2D symbol to facilitate more efficient data capture. May also incorporate additional data not represented in linear bar codes.

5.4.3 Supporting Documentation — Bill of lading, manifest, packing slip, customs data, or EDI message formats may be encoded in an item-attendant 2D symbol. Structure shall conform to ISO/IEC 15434.

5.4.4 Carrier Sorting and Tracking — Sortation data includes routing data, location data, and other tracking support data. Structure shall conform to ISO/IEC 15434.

5.5 Data Area Identification

Data areas (whether bar code or human-readable) shall be identified with the corresponding data area title. The title may include the relevant AI or DI. Examples from the standard:

(10)TW-2601A[FNC1]
(17)271231
(21)SN0000001[FNC1]

The red [FNC1] marks the separator for variable-length fields. Fixed-length fields (such as (01), 14 digits, and (17), 6 digits) need no FNC1 after them.

The role of the FNC1 separator

In GS1-128, AIs have either a fixed length or a variable length:

  • Fixed length (such as (01) = 14 digits, (17) = 6 digits): the scanner knows how many digits to read and stops, so FNC1 is not needed.
  • Variable length (such as (10) lot number, up to 20 characters, and (21) serial number, up to 20 characters): the scanner cannot tell where the field ends, so an FNC1 must be added at the end of the field (unless it is the last field in the whole barcode).

When transmitted to the computer, FNC1 becomes the ASCII GS character (decimal 29).

(01)07622101234560(10)B2601A(17)280630(21)SN1234567

What does each AI represent, and which ones are variable-length requiring FNC1 separation?

Identify the meaning of each AI, and indicate which are variable-length fields (requiring FNC1).

Q3 — SSCC Structure

Your QA team asks: "What is the maximum number of characters in an SSCC, and which GS1 Application Identifier is used?" Also, can an SSCC contain lowercase letters?

QA asks: how many digits can an SSCC have, and which AI does it use? Can an SSCC contain lowercase letters?

Show Answer

SSCC uses AI (00) and is always exactly 18 digits (numeric only). The full encoded string including AI is 20 characters: "(00)" + 18 digits.

Per Clause 5.2.1, the unique transport unit identifier shall contain only numeric and upper-case alphabetic characters; no lowercase letters are permitted.

Q4 — Base vs Extended Label

A logistics partner insists that only the SSCC and Ship To address need to be on your pallet label. Your regulatory team says lot number and expiry date must also be visible. Who is correct under ISO 15394, and what type of label format resolves this?

The logistics partner says the pallet label needs only the SSCC + the consignee address; the regulatory team says the lot number and expiry date must also be shown. Under ISO 15394, who is right, and which label format resolves it?

Show Answer

Both are correct in their context. ISO 15394 Clause 4.4.1 states the base label requires only SSCC + Ship To, which is adequate when full EDI data exchange is established. However, Clause 4.4.2 specifies the extended label for when EDI is insufficient or trading partners require additional visible data.

Pharmaceutical regulations (DSCSA, EU FMD) mandate lot/expiry visibility on the physical label, requiring the extended label format with AI(10) and AI(17) in the supplier segment.

Q5 — Normative References

Your manufacturing site is debating whether to use PDF417 or DataMatrix for encoding a complete Certificate of Analysis in a 2D symbol on the shipping label. Which ISO standard governs the data syntax inside either of these 2D symbols, and which standard specifies the PDF417 symbology itself?

You are discussing whether to use PDF417 or DataMatrix to encode the full certificate of analysis (CoA) on a shipping label. Which ISO standard specifies the data syntax for 2D symbols, and which specifies the PDF417 symbol itself?

Show Answer

Data syntax inside 2D symbols is governed by ISO/IEC 15434 (Syntax for high-capacity ADC media); this applies to both PDF417 and DataMatrix.

The PDF417 symbology specification is ISO/IEC 15438:2015; the DataMatrix symbology specification is ISO/IEC 16022. Per Clause 5.4.3, supporting documentation encoded in 2D symbols on transport labels shall conform to ISO/IEC 15434.

Annex A — Guidelines for Using Linear Bar Codes

Annex A: Linear Bar Code Specifications

A.1 Introduction — Three Options

ISO 15394 defines three data-encoding options for linear bar codes on shipping and transport labels:

  • Option a) — GS1 Application Identifiers (AIs) with GS1-128 symbology
  • Option b) — ANSI MH10.8.2 Data Identifiers with Code 39 symbology
  • Option c) — ANSI MH10.8.2 Data Identifiers with Code 128 symbology

Organizations may choose to support a single option or multiple options. Where multiple options are present in a scanning system, symbology identifiers (per ISO/IEC 15424) shall be used by host systems to distinguish them.

A.2 Symbology Identifiers

The symbology identifier is a prefix transmitted by the decoder but not encoded in the symbol:

  • ]C1 — GS1-128 (FNC1 in first position after start code)
  • ]A0 — Code 39 with Data Identifiers
  • ]C0 — Code 128 with Data Identifiers

For single-option environments: decoders supporting symbology identifiers shall validate the appropriate identifier. Decoders that do not support identifiers cannot automatically distinguish option a) from option c).

A.4 Approved Symbologies

Linear bar code symbologies shall be one of:

  • Code 39 — in accordance with ISO/IEC 16388
  • Code 128 — in accordance with ISO/IEC 15417

NOTE: GS1-128 is a subset of Code 128.

A.5 Symbol Height

Minimum bar height of a linear symbol: 12.7 mm, and should be at least 15% of the total symbol length including quiet zones.

A.6 X-Dimension (Narrow Element Dimension)

  • Minimum X-dimension: 0.25 mm (shall not be less)
  • Code 39 and Code 128: recommended range 0.25 mm – 0.43 mm
  • GS1-128 general: recommended range 0.25 mm – 0.81 mm
  • GS1-128 SSCC: recommended range 0.50 mm – 0.81 mm (Serial Shipping Container Code)

If fewer characters are required, a larger X-dimension may be used provided print quality per A.11 and label width per Table 2 are met. Symbols at 0.25–0.33 mm require special care.

A.7 Wide-to-Narrow Ratio (Code 39)

Should be 3.0:1; measured ratio shall be between 2.4:1 and 3.2:1.

A.8 Quiet Zones

Leading and trailing quiet zones:

  • Should be ≥6.4 mm
  • Where X-dimension >0.64 mm: quiet zones shall be ≥10× the X-dimension

Printer registration parameters shall be considered to ensure minimum quiet zones are maintained.

A.9 Orientation

  • Flat/slightly curved surfaces: bars vertical — picket fence orientation (preferred)
  • Tightly curved surfaces (tubes, rods, cylinders): bars perpendicular to longitudinal axis — ladder orientation
  • Horizontal (ladder) orientation on flat surfaces: by trading partner agreement only

A.10 Placement

No more than two linear symbols shall appear side by side on a label. If two symbols are placed side by side, they shall not share the same horizontal scan path to prevent scanning interference.

A.11 Print Quality

ISO/IEC 15416 determines print quality. Grade format: grade/aperture/wavelength.

Minimum linear bar code quality: 1.5/10/660, meaning:

  • Overall symbol grade ≥1.5 (C) at point of production
  • Measurement aperture: 0.250 mm diameter (reference #10)
  • Light source: 660 nm ±10 nm

Environmental effects (shipping, handling, moisture) can degrade symbols. Labellers are not responsible for damage after leaving their facilities. Unattended scanning may require higher print quality grades — agree with trading partners.

SSCC Special Requirement

The Serial Shipping Container Code (SSCC) is encoded in GS1-128, and its minimum X-dimension is raised to 0.50 mm (instead of the general 0.25 mm). This is because pallet barcodes must be scanned from a greater distance or in harsher environments.

X-dimension vs scanning distance

The X-dimension (narrow element width) directly affects scanning distance and data capacity:

Quiet Zone 10× Rule

When the X-dimension is >0.64 mm, the quiet zone changes from a fixed 6.4 mm to a dynamic calculation:

Quiet zone ≥ 10 × X-dimension

Example: X-dim = 0.81 mm → quiet zone ≥ 8.1 mm (each side). This rule gives high-speed scanners enough "breathing room" to find the symbol boundary.

Symbology Identifier Analogy

Imagine a barcode scanner that can read English, French and Spanish. Once it has read the data, how does it know which language it is? The answer is the prefix code (]C1 = English/GS1; ]A0 = French/Code 39; ]C0 = Spanish/Code 128). Only when the system receives the prefix can it correctly parse the AI or DI data structure that follows.

Interpreting print quality grade 1.5/10/660

  • 1.5 — the minimum overall grade (grade C; the maximum is 4.0/A); this is the basic threshold
  • 10 — measurement aperture 0.250 mm (reference number 10), corresponding to an 8 mil barcode
  • 660 — light source wavelength 660 nm (red light), matching most laser scanners
  • For unattended automatic scanning, a higher grade is recommended (such as 2.0/10/660 or above)

  • Print quality: Minimum 2.5/10/660
  • Macro PDF417: May be used (for concatenation of large data sets >1,200 characters)
  • Symbol skew: Shall not exceed ±5°
  • Placement: Clear of any folds or creases in the document

B.4 Carrier Sortation and Tracking

Three symbologies are capable of high-speed scanning for carrier applications:

  • MaxiCode (ISO/IEC 16023) — Mode 2 or 3 recommended
  • PDF417 (ISO/IEC 15438)
  • QR Code (ISO/IEC 18004)

B.4.3 MaxiCode Specifications

  • Mode: Mode 2 (numeric postal code) or Mode 3 (alphanumeric postal code)
  • Fixed size: ~28.14 mm × 26.91 mm (not scalable)
  • Error correction: Fixed standard level per ISO/IEC 16023
  • Quiet zone: 1 mm all sides
  • Print quality: Minimum 2.5/10/W (W = broadband light source)
  • Placement: In the carrier segment of the label; labels on top of transport units
  • Concatenation: Two Structured Append symbols maximum; symbols printed side by side

B.4.4 QR Code for Carrier Sortation and Tracking

  • Model: QR Code Model 2 (concatenation structure shall NOT be used)
  • Error correction: Level M, Q, or H
  • Module dimension: 0.85 mm – 1.5 mm (larger than shipping labels — for high-speed scanning)
  • Quiet zone: 4 modules minimum on all sides

DataMatrix DPM applications and the GS1 Digital Link trend

DataMatrix ECC200 in pharmaceutical DPM

DPM (Direct Part Marking) — marking directly on the part/container itself, without relying on a paper label:

  • Suitable for: glass ampoules, metal valve components, metal housings of medical devices
  • Minimum module size: 0.381 mm (ISO 15394 annex)
  • Damage recovery: ECC200 can still be read after 25% area damage (highest ECC level)
  • Scanner requirement: generally a 2D imaging scanner is required; a laser scanner cannot be used
  • Pharmaceutical GS1 application: AI (01) GTIN + AI (10) Lot + AI (17) Expiry combined in a single DataMatrix

GS1 Digital Link QR Code — trend analysis

GS1 Digital Link is the next-generation barcode architecture, allowing one QR Code to serve both:

  • B2B: scan → traditional GS1 data (GTIN, lot number, expiry date)
  • B2C: scan → web page URL (product information, recall notices, anti-counterfeiting checks)

Example format: https://id.gs1.org/01/09521234543213/10/ABC123/17/261231

Pharmaceutical significance: one QR Code can meet both the ISO 15394 tracking needs and patient information needs; GS1 Digital Link is expected to gradually replace GS1-128 after 2027.

Logic for estimating PDF417 capacity

PDF417 capacity is determined jointly by four variables:

  • X-dimension ↑ → symbol width ↑, capacity ↓
  • Number of data columns ↑ → width ↑, capacity ↑ (up to 18 columns, recommended ≤12)
  • Symbol height ↑ → capacity ↑ (maximum 61 mm)
  • Error correction level ↑ → capacity ↓ (more redundant data)

Practical example (X = 0.25 mm, 12 columns, height 50 mm): approximately 601 characters (including error correction Level 5)

Scanner field-of-view limit: if the equipment's maximum field of view is 76 mm, the symbol width must not exceed this value, and a "tall and narrow" symbol layout must be used instead.

MaxiCode analogy — the courier company's secret weapon

MaxiCode is a symbol developed by UPS for high-speed conveyors. It has a fixed size (about 2.8 cm × 2.7 cm) and a hexagonal honeycomb structure, and scanners can read it from any angle. It is like the ETC sensor at a highway toll station: no need to aim, and it can still be read at high speed. Modes 2/3 decode the postal code first, so sorting can be completed even if the symbol is damaged.

Macro PDF417 — concatenation for large data

When a single PDF417 symbol cannot hold all the data, use Macro PDF417:

  • Suitable for: supporting document applications (B.3); when data exceeds >1,200 characters, plan for concatenation
  • Shipping/receiving applications (B.2) prohibit Macro PDF417
  • The decoder must support symbology identifier ]L1 (indicating escape characters and sequence characters are included)
  • If scanning is interrupted by another symbol, the concatenation sequence is broken and the result is unpredictable

Clause 6 — Data Carriers

6. Data Carriers — Selection and Requirements

6.1 General

The primary data carrier on a shipping, transport, and receiving label shall be a GS1-128 bar code symbol. This is the mandatory baseline for all labels conforming to ISO 15394.

In addition to the primary GS1-128 carrier, supplementary 2D symbols may be included on the label to carry additional data or to serve specific applications such as carrier sorting or supporting documentation.

6.2 GS1-128 as Primary Data Carrier

GS1-128 shall encode all mandatory data elements using GS1 Application Identifiers (AIs). The symbol shall comply with:

  • GS1 General Specifications for GS1-128 encoding rules
  • ISO/IEC 15417 (Code 128 specification)
  • X-dimension and print quality requirements per Annex A

6.3 Supplementary 2D Symbols

Where supplementary 2D symbols are used, the applicable symbology and its technical requirements shall be as specified in Annex B:

  • PDF417 — for additional shipping data or supporting documentation
  • DataMatrix ECC200 — for specific applications (particularly DPM)
  • QR Code Model 2 — for shipping, receiving, or digital link applications
  • MaxiCode — for carrier sortation and tracking (high-speed environments)

6.4 Selection Criteria

Selection of the appropriate supplementary 2D symbol depends on:

  • Data volume: Total characters to be encoded
  • Scanning environment: Hand-held vs. automated tunnel scanners vs. high-speed sortation
  • Label area available: Label dimensions constraining symbol footprint
  • Trading partner requirements: Carrier, customer, or regulatory mandates
  • Printing technology: Thermal transfer, inkjet, or direct part marking

Why GS1-128 is the primary carrier

  • Universality: GS1-128 is recognized by all GS1 member organizations and trading partners worldwide
  • AI structure: the Application Identifier (AI) prefix gives each data element clear semantics (for example, AI 01 = GTIN; AI 17 = expiry date)
  • FNC1 marker: the FNC1 character unique to GS1-128 marks AI boundaries and avoids confusion between data elements
  • Mature infrastructure: ERP, WMS and TMS systems all have built-in GS1-128 decoding support

Primary barcode vs supplementary symbol analogy

GS1-128 is like a passport (universally accepted, with a fixed format and fields); DataMatrix/QR Code is like the visa sticker inside the passport (supplementary information for a specific country). Customs checks the passport, and a specific lane checks the visa. Likewise, the WMS scans GS1-128, and the pharmacy traceability system scans DataMatrix.

Interference risk when multiple symbols coexist

When a label carries several barcodes, note the following:

  • No more than 2 linear barcodes side by side, and not on the same horizontal scan path
  • 2D symbols must be located in the designated area of the label (customer zone or carrier zone)
  • All symbols must keep their own quiet zones, which must not intrude on one another
  • While a Macro PDF417 concatenation is being read, scanning another symbol will interrupt the entire concatenation sequence
Clause 7 — Label Design

7. Label Design — Mandatory Requirements

7.1 Mandatory Data Zones

A label conforming to ISO 15394 shall contain the following mandatory zones:

  • Ship To Zone: Destination address, postal code, GS1-128 SSCC or routing bar code
  • Carrier Zone: Carrier-specific data; MaxiCode symbol (if using carrier sortation)
  • Customer/Product Zone: GTIN (AI 01), Lot/Batch (AI 10), Expiry Date (AI 17), Quantity (AI 37) encoded in GS1-128; optional QR Code
  • Human Readable Information (HRI): Plain text of encoded AI data, placed directly adjacent to the corresponding bar code symbol

7.2 Minimum Label Sizes

Label dimensions shall be sufficient to accommodate all mandatory symbols with required quiet zones. For labels using all mandatory data elements:

  • Minimum label width: 102 mm (for standard label configuration)
  • Minimum label height: determined by symbol heights and HRI text
  • SSCC label (pallet): typically 102 mm × 152 mm or larger

7.3 X-Dimension and Scanning Distance

The X-dimension shall be selected based on the expected scanning distance in the application environment. Key principles:

  • Larger X-dimension → readable at greater distance, but fewer characters per unit area
  • Smaller X-dimension → higher data density, but requires closer scanning
  • For automated systems: agree X-dimension with scanner equipment capabilities

7.4 Human Readable Interpretation (HRI)

HRI text requirements:

  • Shall be placed immediately above or below the corresponding bar code symbol
  • Shall display the AI number in parentheses followed by the data: e.g., (01) 09521234543213
  • Minimum font size: sufficient to be read by human operators without magnification
  • Application Identifiers are shown in parentheses in HRI but are NOT encoded with parentheses in the bar code

7.5 Bearer Bars

Bearer bars are horizontal bars running the full width of a linear bar code symbol. They help:

  • Maintain consistent scanning path across the full symbol
  • Protect symbol from top/bottom edge damage
  • Improve reading performance on corrugated substrates

Bearer bars are recommended for linear symbols where the bar code edges may be subject to physical damage or scanning path interference.

7.6 Color and Contrast

Bar code symbols shall meet minimum contrast requirements:

  • Dark bars on light background (black on white is optimal)
  • Minimum print contrast signal (PCS): per ISO/IEC 15416 measurement
  • Colored substrates require verification that the contrast meets minimum grade at 660 nm wavelength
  • Kraft corrugated (brown): may not meet quality requirements — verify with actual scanning tests
  • Red ink on white background: typically unreadable at 660 nm (red light absorbed by red ink)

Label Design Practice

HRI format rules — AI parentheses

Display rules for AIs in the HRI (human-readable information):

  • HRI display: (01) 09521234543213 (10) ABC123 (17) 261231
  • Barcode encoding: no parentheses; the AIs are embedded directly; a variable-length AI is followed by an FNC1 separator
  • FNC1 is not visible in GS1-128, but it tells the decoder that this AI has ended
  • The parentheses are only a display convention of the HRI and are not part of the data

Key pitfalls with colour and contrast

Common design errors and their consequences:

  • Red ink: a 660 nm red-light scanner "cannot see" a red barcode, because the red light is reflected by the red ink (same colour) → the barcode is completely unreadable
  • Dark background: a black barcode printed on a dark blue background → insufficient PCS → fails the quality grade
  • Kraft paper: a brown background gives poor contrast for barcodes; a white label substrate is recommended
  • Glossy materials: specular reflection may overexpose the scanner and reduce the decoding success rate

Bearer Bars analogy

Bearer bars are the "guardrails" of a barcode. Think of the lane guardrails on a highway bridge: their main function is to keep vehicles from veering off, while also strengthening the bridge structure. Bearer bars keep the scanner's laser beam on the correct horizontal path as it crosses the whole barcode, so it does not "fall off" the edge early. This is especially important on wavy corrugated cartons.

Label area planning formula

When designing a label, estimate the minimum required width as follows:

  • GS1-128 width = (number of data characters × 11 + 35) × X-dimension + 2 × quiet zone
  • Quiet zone = max(6.4 mm, 10 × X-dimension) (when X >0.64 mm)
  • Practical label width: usually take a standard size of 102 mm or 152 mm to ensure enough space

Height allocation (top to bottom): Ship To text zone → barcode zone (≥12.7 mm) → HRI text zone → spacing → next segment

Clause 8 — Label Placement

8. Label Placement — Packaging Hierarchy

8.1 Primary Packaging

For primary packaging (innermost package in direct contact with product), label placement shall ensure:

  • Labels are positioned to be accessible for scanning without removing from secondary packaging, where possible
  • Labels shall not obscure any safety warnings or mandatory regulatory information
  • For small primary packages (vials, ampoules, syringes): labels wrap around the container; bar code area should be on the flat-facing surface

8.2 Secondary Packaging (Cartons / Cases)

Labels shall be applied to secondary packaging in accordance with trading partner agreements. Requirements:

  • Labels should be applied to the side of the package most likely to face outward during transit
  • Labels shall be placed so that they are fully visible when the package is in normal shipping orientation
  • Labels shall not be placed across package edges, seams, or closures where deformation may occur
  • Minimum placement: one label on one face, per trading partner requirements; multiple faces may be required

8.3 Tertiary Packaging (Pallets)

Pallet (transport unit) labeling shall ensure 4-side visibility:

  • Labels shall be applied to all four sides of the pallet load OR at minimum two adjacent sides if agreed by trading partners
  • Labels shall be placed at a height of 400 mm – 800 mm from the floor (optimal scanning height for forklift-mounted scanners)
  • Labels shall be applied adjacent to, but not crossing, the corner of the pallet
  • SSCC (Serial Shipping Container Code) is mandatory for each pallet unit

8.4 Placement — Avoidance Rules

Labels shall NOT be placed:

  • Across corners, edges, or seams where they may wrinkle or fold
  • On surfaces subject to condensation, abrasion, or chemical exposure that would degrade readability
  • Where adhesive failure due to surface characteristics (porous, oily, textured) would cause label loss
  • In locations blocked by strapping, shrink wrap seams, or other packaging materials

8.5 Environmental and Physical Considerations

Labelers shall consider environmental effects during the entire distribution chain:

  • Temperature extremes (cold chain products): adhesive performance at low temperature
  • Moisture and humidity: label face stock and adhesive water resistance
  • UV exposure for outdoor storage: ink fading and substrate degradation
  • Physical abrasion from neighboring packages in transit

Labelers are not responsible for damage to labels incurred during shipping after leaving the supplier's facility. Every effort should be made to protect and place labels to minimize transit damage.

Annex C — Designing compliant labels using a building block approach

Building Block Label Design: From SSCC Foundation to DSCSA Serialization Extension | p37–p43

C.1–C.2 Label Definition and the Building Block Concept

Label means a printed area applied to a package that carries text and/or bar code data (linear, composite or 2D symbols). It can be printed separately and applied at different stages to assemble a complete label.

A building block is the basic standard unit of a label format. Each block or sub-block may contain:

  • Text or graphics
  • Bar code symbols (2D or linear, with human readable interpretation)
  • Blank space

Blocks can be produced individually or combined with other blocks, supporting a flexible "print when the data becomes available" workflow. Blocks should be arranged by vertical stacking.

C.3–C.4 LPB Text Height & Segments

Text height is defined in Lines-Per-Block (LPB), with 9 sizes (1–8 and 10 LPB); the label printer selects the actual character height according to equipment capability. The standard defines three information segments, stacked from top to bottom:

  1. Carrier segment — at the top
  2. Consignee segment
  3. Shipper segment — at the bottom

C.5–C.6 Label Size and Format Requirements

Label size is not fixed; it is determined by the label printer according to trading partner needs, equipment capability and the size of the shipping package.

Block dimension requirements (summary of Table C.1)

Each block can be divided into up to 4 sub-blocks, separated by vertical dividing lines. A text block or sub-block must not contain a bar code symbol; bar codes should be placed in a separate bar code symbol block.

* Character height includes ascenders, descenders and leading. The height-to-width ratio must not exceed 2:1 (measured on the character "M").

Building Block Design and Its Application in Pharma CDMOs

Analogy: building block label = LEGO bricks

Imagine a shipping label as LEGO bricks of different colors stacked vertically: the top layer is the SSCC block for courier scanning, the middle is the GTIN + Lot + Expiry block for the customer, and the bottom layer is the shipper information block. Each layer can be printed and replaced independently without affecting the others.

This is especially useful for a CDMO: the serial number is usually only determined at the final packaging step, so with a building block design the GTIN/Lot block can be applied early and the serial number block added last to complete a DSCSA-compliant label.

LPB Selection Principles — CDMO Practice

  • Title text: fixed at 6 LPB, uppercase, left aligned, maximum 2 lines
  • Primary data (e.g., Lot No., Exp Date): 3–4 LPB recommended
  • Secondary data (e.g., PO#, weight): 5–6 LPB
  • Bar code HRI (human readable interpretation): typically 5–7 LPB, placed below the bar code

The LPB sizes used on the same label must be visibly different; 8 LPB must be clearly smaller than 7 LPB.

  • Labels on all four sides (readable through 360°): suitable for automated storage (ASRS) or high-speed conveyor scanning
  • At least two adjacent sides (at 90°): ensures a forklift approaching from any angle can scan the label
  • Label bottom edge from the floor: 400–800 mm (avoids interference from forklift tines and floor contamination)
  • Top edge from the top of the pallet: ≤ 100 mm (for reading by a high-position scanner)

Labelling Challenges for Small Containers (Vial / Syringe / Ampule)

ISO 15394 mainly addresses the shipping unit level. For small single-use containers such as vials and syringes, refer to ISO 15223-1 (medical device symbols), the GS1 Healthcare Guideline for DataMatrix, and the UDI requirements of each market.

Analogy: label position = car park entrance sign

A height-restriction sign in a car park must be placed far enough before the entrance for drivers to see it, and at eye level. Bar code label placement follows the same logic: put it where the scanner can "see" it, free of obstruction (shrink wrap / overlapping labels), at a height suited to the scanner's focal distance.

Key Points for a CDMO Shipping Labelling SOP

  • Cartons: main label at the lower right corner + at least 1 side label; confirm the bar code read rate before shrink wrapping
  • Pallets: 1 SSCC label on each of 2 adjacent sides; height 400–600 mm
  • Cold chain containers: label material must withstand -80°C; consider the effect of moisture condensation on the bar code when dry ice sublimates
  • In-process control (IPC): in-line scan verification (in-process check) with a certified scanner; rejection rate < 0.1%

Annex G — The impact of systems confronted with multiple symbologies and formats

Multiple Symbology Environments: Auto-Discrimination, Migration Strategies and Equipment Upgrades | p64–p67

G.1 Overview — The Multiple Symbology Problem in Open Systems

In an open supply chain, a scanner may encounter three combinations (see 5.1.1):

  • Option (a)：GS1 Application Identifiers (AI) + GS1-128
  • Option (b)：ANSI MH10.8.2 Data Identifiers (DI) + Code 39
  • Option (c)：ANSI MH10.8.2 DI + Code 128

The Symbology Identifier (ISO/IEC 15424) is a prefix that the decoder adds before the data; it is not encoded in the bar code itself and is output automatically by the decoder hardware.

G.2 Single-Option Environment

When only one symbology is scanned, the host system should verify the symbology identifier and filter out non-conforming data streams. Users of option (b) should disable the other symbologies in the decoder to prevent misreads.

G.3 Multiple-Option Environment

A system supporting 2–3 options must fully implement the Symbology Identifier capability. The combination of "symbology identifier + DI or AI" provides reliable data entry and automatic discrimination between the options.

G.4 Migration Choices

Feasible migration paths:

  1. Code 39 + DI → GS1-128 (AI)
  2. Code 39 + DI → Code 128 + DI
  3. Code 128 + DI → GS1-128 (AI)

All migrations require a fairly long parallel operation period and agreement among the supplier, carrier and customer.

G.4.3 Equipment Requirements

  • Printers: must be able to correctly generate symbols in the new format (including control characters such as FNC1)
  • Decoders: configured to read both old and new formats and to transmit the symbology identifier
  • Older decoders that do not support symbology identifiers should be upgraded or replaced

G.5 Migration Management Recommendations

G.5.1 Industry authorities (such as GS1 Taiwan, AIAG) should first survey the current equipment, plan the upgrade path, coordinate the three parties, and set a phase-out date for the old format.

G.5.2 Label printers should verify the mapping software (AI/DI format differences, e.g., date format), fully support FNC1, and carry out print quality testing before go-live.

G.5.3 Label scanners should ensure the decoders comply with ISO/IEC 15424, implement AI and DI format validation software, and complete the data format conversion logic (date and unit differences between AI and DI formats).

Multiple Symbologies — Practical Analysis for CDMOs

Analogy: symbology identifier = language tag

Imagine the bar code data as a voice message, and the symbology identifier as a tag the system automatically adds at the front saying "this is a Chinese message" or "this is an English message". When the host system (IT system) hears "]C1" it knows the data is GS1-128 and parses it with AI; when it hears "]A0" it knows it is Code 39 + DI and parses it with DI. Without this tag, the system cannot tell which set of rules to use for interpretation.

Design Principles for GS1 + Non-GS1 on the Same Label

When a CDMO supplies both pharma customers (who need GS1 AI) and automotive customers (who need DI), two bar codes can be printed on the same label:

  • Left side: GS1-128 (containing GTIN + Lot + Exp, AI format)
  • Right side: Code 39 (containing Part No. + Lot, DI format)
  • The host system discriminates automatically through the symbology identifier and routes each to the corresponding business logic
  • Both share the same Lot data, ensuring consistent traceability

Recommended Steps for a CDMO Migration Plan

  1. Inventory the existing scanning equipment (does it support symbology identifier output?)
  2. Confirm the parallel operation period with customers (typically 6–12 months)
  3. Update the ERP/WMS mapping logic (AI ↔ DI field mapping)
  4. Upgrade printer firmware and test FNC1 character generation
  5. Qualify (IQ/OQ/PQ) the new labelling system
  6. Notify QA to update the SOPs and the Label Specification

The AI vs. DI Format Difference Trap

During migration, the change of date format from a 2-digit year to a 4-digit year is the most common source of mapping errors.

Q1 — Staged Labelling with Building Blocks

Your CDMO is contracted to manufacture an injectable product. The product batch identification (GTIN + Lot + Exp) can be confirmed at packaging, but the serial number cannot be assigned until the customer's MAH applies for it in the European EMVS system. Under the ISO 15394 building block design, how should the staged printing and labelling of this label be planned? Explain which blocks should be printed at which stage, and what the GMP documentation requirements are.

Click to view the answer

Stage 1 (at packaging): print and apply the "product block" — a DataMatrix bar code block containing AI 01 (GTIN), AI 10 (Lot No.) and AI 17 (Expiry Date) + a human readable text block. This block must not be covered by later blocks (packaging label module).

Stage 2 (after the MAH assigns the serial number): print the "serial block" — containing AI 21 (Serial Number), made as a separate label block and applied over a reserved blank area or as an overlay.

GMP documentation requirements: the BPR must record: (1) the print date, time and operator for each block; (2) the source of the serial number (through which system the MAH assigned it); (3) layout confirmation before overlabelling (including photo records or electronic verification records); (4) the quantity of rejected labels and destruction records. This meets EU GMP Annex 16 and FDA 21 CFR 211.68.

Q2 — Decoder Settings in a Multiple Symbology Environment

Your warehouse receives three types of shipping labels: (1) pharma suppliers use GS1-128 + AI; (2) chemical raw material suppliers use Code 39 + DI "J"; (3) medical device suppliers use Code 128 + DI. The WMS often confuses (a) and (c) when scanning bar codes, causing data parsing errors. Following the recommendations in Annex G, explain how to configure the decoders and the WMS software to solve this problem.

Click to view the answer

Root problem: GS1-128 (option a) and Code 128 + DI (option c) both use the Code 128 physical symbol, and without a symbology identifier they cannot be distinguished automatically.

Decoder settings: confirm that the decoder supports ISO/IEC 15424 Symbology Identifier output. GS1-128 outputs ]C1, Code 128 + DI outputs ]C0, and Code 39 + DI outputs ]A0. If the decoder does not support this function, upgrade the firmware or replace the equipment.

WMS software: read the symbology identifier (a 2–3 character prefix) at the front of the scanned data, and route to different parsing logic according to ]C1 / ]C0 / ]A0:

  • ]C1 → GS1 AI parser (GTIN, Lot, Exp, Serial)
  • ]C0 → ANSI DI parser (Part No., Lot, Qty)
  • ]A0 → DI "J" parser (Licence Plate)

Q3 — Conflict Between LPB Text Height and Bar Code Blocks

A label designer is designing a 100 mm × 150 mm pharmaceutical shipper carton label. The customer requires the following on the same label: GTIN, Lot No., Expiry Date (text), a DataMatrix bar code, a GS1-128 bar code (SSCC), and Ship-from / Ship-to addresses. The designer wants to place the text and the DataMatrix in the same block, but you notice this does not comply with the standard. Identify the problem and propose a correct layout consistent with ISO 15394 Annex C.

Click to view the answer

The problem (Annex C.6.3): a text building block or text sub-block must not contain a bar code symbol. The DataMatrix and the text must be placed in separate blocks.

Correct layout (top to bottom):

  1. Carrier Segment (top): double-height block (51 mm) — GS1-128 SSCC bar code + HRI
  2. Consignee Segment: standard block (25 mm) — Ship-to address text, 2 sub-blocks (name + address)
  3. Shipper Segment: standard block (25 mm) — Ship-from address text
  4. Product Data block (bar code): standard block — DataMatrix (containing GTIN + Lot + Exp + Serial)
  5. Product Data block (text): standard block — human readable text for GTIN / Lot / Exp, each in a separate sub-block, with 6 LPB titles + 3 LPB data

Q4 — Material and Placement of Cold Chain Pallet Labels

You need to design pallet shipping labels for a batch of -80°C ultra-low temperature lyophilized active pharmaceutical ingredient (API), packed in dry ice shippers and wrapped in transparent plastic film. The label must remain readable throughout the cold chain transport (from -80°C until thawing on arrival at 20°C). Following Annex F and GMP practice, explain the material selection, label placement and in-process verification requirements.

Click to view the answer

Material selection: the label stock must be "cryogenic-grade" synthetic paper or film (polyester / polypropylene); ordinary direct thermal paper must not be used (the adhesive fails at low temperature). The adhesive must be a cold-resistant acrylic adhesive, tested to at least -80°C. Thermal transfer or laser printing is recommended for the bar code rather than direct thermal (DT) printing.

Label placement (Annex F Figure F.1b): one label on each of two adjacent sides of the pallet (positions 4A + 4B). Label position: bottom edge 400–800 mm from the floor, avoiding the dry ice sublimation zone (within 100 mm of the bottom). After wrapping with transparent plastic film, confirm that the bar code can still be scanned directly through the film (not obscured by moisture or condensation). If the outer film obstructs scanning, consider adding a transparent protective cover over the label or using a label pocket.

In-process verification: scan verification in the low temperature environment should be performed after packing is complete and before frozen storage, confirming a bar code read rate ≥ 99.9% (using a certified ISO verifier). Scan again after thawing (at receiving inspection by the consignee) to confirm the cold chain process has not damaged the bar code. Both scan results are recorded in the BPR.

Q5 — A "Universal Label" Design for Global Multi-Market Shipments

Your CDMO's customer (the MAH) plans to export the same batch of drug product to the US (DSCSA), the EU (EU FMD) and China (NMPA traceability code) at the same time. Under the ISO 15394 + GS1 framework, explain how to design one label that meets the requirements of all three markets, identify which elements are common and which are market specific, and how to handle in practice the coexistence of the "China 20-digit traceability code" and GS1 AIs.

Click to view the answer

Common elements (shared by all three markets):

  • DataMatrix with AI 01 (GTIN) + AI 10 (Lot) + AI 17 (Exp) + AI 21 (Serial) — meets the DSCSA and EU FMD four elements, and is also the data source for the China traceability code
  • GS1-128 (SSCC) — pallet / shipper carton traceability
  • Human readable text: Product Name, Lot No., Exp Date (common)

Market-specific elements:

  • EU FMD: NCC (National Code Container) = anti-tampering device integrity marker, requires a physical label
  • China: the NMPA 20-digit traceability code requires an additional DataMatrix bar code (or QR Code), encoding the China traceability code with AI 91 or AI 240, or printing the NMPA code in an additional block
  • HRI language for each market: English (US / EU), Chinese (China)

Practical solution: print two DataMatrix codes on the label: (1) a GS1 DataMatrix (with the 4 standard AIs); (2) a China NMPA DataMatrix (AI 91 + 20-digit code). Using the ISO 15394 building block design, place the NMPA block beside or below the GS1 block, with the heading "CN Traceability Code". For electronic traceability: GS1 EPCIS uploads to EMVS (EU) and VRS (US); the NMPA platform receives separately uploaded data in the Chinese format.
