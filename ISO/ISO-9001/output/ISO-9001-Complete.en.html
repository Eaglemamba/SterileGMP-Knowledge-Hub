The committee responsible for this document is Technical Committee ISO/TC 176, Quality management and quality assurance, Subcommittee SC 2, Quality systems.

Version History & Context

Three Core Changes in the 2015 Version

  • Annex SL common structure: shares the high-level structure with ISO 14001, ISO 45001 and others, making it easier to integrate management systems
  • Risk-based thinking: moves from reactive "preventive action" to proactively identifying and managing risks and opportunities
  • Context of the organization: requires the organization to systematically understand internal and external issues and interested parties

Analogy: From "Fire Drills" to "Risk Forecasting"

The 2008 version was like training the fire brigade only after each fire (preventive action). The 2015 version expects an organization to act like an insurance actuary: map the risks in advance, assess likelihood and impact, and install fire suppression in advance where the risk is high.

Introduction 0.1 — General

The QMS as a Strategic Decision

0.1 General

The adoption of a quality management system is a strategic decision for an organization that can help to improve its overall performance and provide a sound basis for sustainable development initiatives.

The potential benefits to an organization of implementing a quality management system based on this International Standard are:

  1. the ability to consistently provide products and services that meet customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements;
  2. facilitating opportunities to enhance customer satisfaction;
  3. addressing risks and opportunities associated with its context and objectives;
  4. the ability to demonstrate conformity to specified quality management system requirements.

This International Standard employs the process approach, which incorporates the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle and risk-based thinking.

In this International Standard, the following verbal forms are used:

  • "shall" — indicates a requirement
  • "should" — indicates a recommendation
  • "may" — indicates a permission
  • "can" — indicates a possibility or a capability

Key Points

The QMS Is a Strategic Tool, Not an Administrative Burden

The standard states up front that adopting a QMS is a strategic decision. This means the QMS is not just paperwork to pass a customer audit, but a management framework that helps the organization achieve its business objectives.

For a pharmaceutical CDMO, this means ISO 9001 certification is a market signal to prospective clients that "we can supply consistently and reliably."

"shall" vs "should": Audit Focus

The most important wording distinction in an audit:

  • shall = mandatory requirement; the auditor will always check it
  • should = recommendation; implementation is optional

Treating a should as a shall, or overlooking a shall requirement, are common causes of nonconformities.

Analogy: Where ISO 9001 Sits in the Pharmaceutical Industry

ISO 9001 is the "foundation" of quality management. ISO 13485 (medical devices) and ISO 15378 (primary packaging materials for medicinal products) are specialized buildings erected on the 9001 foundation. Mastering 9001 means mastering the common language of the whole ISO quality family.

Introduction 0.2 — Quality Management Principles

The Quality Management Principles

0.2 Quality Management Principles

This International Standard is based on the quality management principles described in ISO 9000. The descriptions include a statement of each principle, a rationale of why the principle is important for the organization, some examples of benefits associated with the principle and examples of typical actions to improve the organization's performance when applying the principle.

The quality management principles are:

  • Customer focus
  • Leadership
  • Engagement of people
  • Process approach
  • Improvement
  • Evidence-based decision making
  • Relationship management

Note: The standard lists 7 principles here; ISO 9000:2015 details all 7. An eighth principle — risk-based thinking — is embedded throughout but is treated as a concept rather than a standalone principle in 9001:2015.

How the Principles Map to a Pharmaceutical CDMO

Analogy: Principles Are the Compass, Clauses Are the Map

The seven principles are the "philosophical foundation" of ISO 9001, while the specific clauses (Clauses 4-10) are the map that puts them into practice. Understanding the principles lets you make judgments consistent with the intent of the standard when there is no explicit rule.

Introduction 0.3 — Process Approach & PDCA Cycle

Process Approach and the PDCA Cycle: Risk-Based Thinking

0.3.1 General — Process Approach

This International Standard promotes the adoption of a process approach when developing, implementing and improving the effectiveness of a quality management system, to enhance customer satisfaction by meeting customer requirements.

Understanding and managing interrelated processes as a system contributes to the organization's effectiveness and efficiency in achieving its intended results. The process approach involves the systematic definition and management of processes, and their interactions, so as to achieve the intended results in accordance with the quality policy and strategic direction of the organization.

The application of the process approach in a quality management system enables:

  1. understanding and consistency in meeting requirements;
  2. the consideration of processes in terms of added value;
  3. the achievement of effective process performance;
  4. improvement of processes based on evaluation of data and information.

0.3.2 Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) Cycle

The PDCA cycle can be applied to all processes and to the quality management system as a whole. Clauses 4 to 10 can be grouped in relation to the PDCA cycle:

  • Plan: establish objectives and processes (Clauses 4, 5, 6, 7)
  • Do: implement what was planned (Clause 8)
  • Check: monitor and measure processes against policies, objectives, requirements (Clause 9)
  • Act: take actions to improve performance (Clause 10)

0.3.3 Risk-Based Thinking

Risk-based thinking is essential for achieving an effective quality management system. The concept of risk-based thinking has been implicit in previous editions of this International Standard including, for example, carrying out preventive action to eliminate potential nonconformities.

To conform to the requirements of this International Standard, an organization needs to plan and implement actions to address risks and opportunities. Addressing both risks and opportunities establishes a basis for increasing the effectiveness of the quality management system, achieving improved results and preventing negative effects.

Opportunities can arise as a result of a situation favourable to achieving an intended result, for example, a set of circumstances that allow the organization to attract customers, develop new products and services, reduce waste or improve productivity.

PDCA Applied in Pharmaceuticals

P — Plan (Cl. 4-7)
ICH Q8 design space, process development, quality objective setting, SOP creation, resource planning
D — Do (Cl. 8)
Commercial manufacturing batches, aseptic filling operations, supplier management, confirmation of customer requirements
C — Check (Cl. 9)
QC release testing, OOS/OOT investigations, environmental monitoring, internal audits
A — Act (Cl. 10)
CAPA implementation, management review decisions, process improvement, elimination of root causes of deviations

The Process Unit (Turtle Diagram) Concept

The ISO 9001 process approach expects every core process to answer the following:

  • Inputs: What does this process need?
  • Outputs: What does this process produce?
  • Resources: People, equipment, environment
  • Methods: SOPs, guidelines, techniques
  • KPIs: How do we measure whether this process is effective?

This is exactly the framework of the "Turtle Diagram", which is commonly used to describe processes in a pharmaceutical QMS.

Risk-Based Thinking vs the Old Preventive Action

2008 version: 8.5.3 required the organization to establish a documented "preventive action" procedure, which often became a formality.

2015 version: the standalone "preventive action" clause was removed, and risk-based thinking now runs throughout the entire standard. The organization must identify risks and decide how to address them already at the planning stage (6.1).

Pharmaceutical application: this is entirely consistent with the philosophy of ICH Q9 quality risk management (tools such as FMEA and HACCP).

Analogy: From "Fix It After the Accident" to "Build Safeguards First"

The 2008 preventive action was like installing guardrails only after a crash at an intersection. The 2015 risk-based thinking is like carrying out a risk assessment while designing the road: which intersections are accident-prone, and should they get traffic lights or a roundabout? Design first, then open to traffic.

Introduction 0.4 — Relationship with Other Management System Standards

Relationship with Other Standards: Annex SL

0.4 Relationship with Other Standards

This International Standard applies the framework developed by ISO to improve alignment among its International Standards for management systems (sometimes referred to as Annex SL — the High Level Structure).

This International Standard enables an organization to use the process approach, coupled with the PDCA cycle and risk-based thinking, to align or integrate its quality management system with the requirements of other management system standards.

This International Standard relates to ISO 9000 and ISO 9004 as follows:

  • ISO 9000:2015 provides essential background for the proper understanding and implementation of this International Standard — vocabulary and fundamental concepts
  • ISO 9004 provides guidance for organizations that choose to progress beyond the requirements of this International Standard — Managing for the sustained success of an organization

Sector-specific quality management system standards based on the requirements of this International Standard have been developed for a number of sectors. Some of these standards specify additional quality management system requirements, while others are limited to providing guidance.

The Meaning of Annex SL and Standards Integration

What Is Annex SL (the High-Level Structure)?

To keep different management system standards from "speaking different languages", ISO designed the Annex SL common framework: 10 identical high-level clause numbers that the standards share:

  • Clause 4: Context of the organization
  • Clause 5: Leadership
  • Clause 6: Planning
  • Clause 7: Support
  • Clause 8: Operation
  • Clause 9: Performance evaluation
  • Clause 10: Improvement

Standards that adopt Annex SL include ISO 9001, ISO 14001 (environment), ISO 45001 (occupational health and safety), ISO 27001 (information security) and others.

Opportunities for an Integrated QMS in Pharmaceuticals

Because of the common Annex SL structure, a site that holds ISO 9001 + ISO 14001 + ISO 45001 can merge them into an integrated management system (IMS), reducing duplicated documents and audit burden.

CDMO scenario: customer audits, ISO 9001 and cGMP can be managed together under a similar documentation framework, saving maintenance cost.

Analogy: Annex SL = the USB-C Connector Standard

In the past, each management standard was like a different manufacturer's charging connector: different shapes, not interchangeable. Annex SL is ISO's "USB-C standard": all management system standards plug into the same structural socket, which makes integration much easier.

Clauses 1–3: Scope, Normative References & Terms

Scope, Normative References, Terms and Definitions

Clause 1 — Scope

This International Standard specifies requirements for a quality management system when an organization:

  1. needs to demonstrate its ability to consistently provide products and services that meet customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements, and
  2. aims to enhance customer satisfaction through the effective application of the system, including processes for improvement of the system and the assurance of conformity to customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements.
All the requirements of this International Standard are generic and are intended to be applicable to any organization, regardless of its type or size, or the products and services it provides.

NOTE 1: The terms "product" or "service" only apply to products and services intended for, or required by, a customer.
NOTE 2: Statutory and regulatory requirements can be expressed as legal requirements.

Clause 2 — Normative References

The following documents are normatively referenced in this document and are indispensable for its application:

  • ISO 9000:2015, Quality management systems — Fundamentals and vocabulary

This is the only normative reference. All other related standards (ISO 9004, etc.) are informative only.

Clause 3 — Terms and Definitions

For the purposes of this document, the terms and definitions given in ISO 9000:2015 apply.

Scope and Applicability Explained

"All requirements applicable": the Applicability Principle

ISO 9001 requires the organization to apply all clauses, but allows it to record in Clause 4.3 (QMS scope) which clauses are not applicable, with justification.

Common scenarios where a clause is not applicable:

  • Pure service organization → 8.3 Design and development may not apply
  • Manufacturer that does no design → 8.3 does not apply, but the justification must be recorded

Important: non-applicability must not affect the organization's ability to "ensure conformity of products and customer satisfaction."

Scope Considerations for a Pharmaceutical CDMO

For a CDMO, all clauses usually apply, because:

  • It does design and development (customized process / method development) → 8.3 applies
  • It uses external providers (raw materials / analytical services) → 8.4 applies
  • It deals directly with brand-name pharmaceutical customers → all customer satisfaction clauses apply

Analogy: 9000 Is the Dictionary, 9001 Is the Regulation

ISO 9000 is like a medical dictionary (it tells you what each term means). ISO 9001 is like the Pharmaceutical Affairs Act (it tells you what you must do). The two must be used together, but only 9001 can be used for certification.

Clause 4 — Context of the Organization

Context of the Organization: Internal and External Issues, Interested Parties, QMS Scope and Processes

4.1 Understanding the Organization and Its Context

The organization shall determine external and internal issues that are relevant to its purpose and its strategic direction and that affect its ability to achieve the intended result(s) of its quality management system.

The organization shall monitor and review information about these external and internal issues.

NOTE 2: Understanding the external context can be facilitated by considering issues arising from legal, technological, competitive, market, cultural, social and economic environments, whether international, national, regional or local.
NOTE 3: Understanding the internal context can be facilitated by considering issues related to values, culture, knowledge and performance of the organization.

4.2 Understanding the Needs and Expectations of Interested Parties

The organization shall determine:

  1. the interested parties that are relevant to the quality management system;
  2. the requirements of these interested parties that are relevant to the quality management system.

The organization shall monitor and review information about these interested parties and their relevant requirements.

4.3 Determining the Scope of the QMS

The organization shall determine the boundaries and applicability of the quality management system to establish its scope. When determining this scope, the organization shall consider:

  1. the external and internal issues referred to in 4.1;
  2. the requirements of relevant interested parties referred to in 4.2;
  3. the products and services of the organization.
The scope of the organization's quality management system shall be available and be maintained as documented information. The scope shall state the types of products and services covered, and provide justification for any requirement of this International Standard that the organization determines is not applicable.

4.4 Quality Management System and Its Processes

The organization shall establish, implement, maintain and continually improve a quality management system, including the processes needed and their interactions.

The organization shall determine the processes needed and shall:

  1. determine the inputs required and the outputs expected from these processes;
  2. determine the sequence and interaction of these processes;
  3. determine and apply the criteria and methods (including monitoring, measurements and related performance indicators) needed to ensure the effective operation and control of these processes;
  4. determine the resources needed for these processes and ensure their availability;
  5. assign the responsibilities and authorities for these processes;
  6. address the risks and opportunities as determined in accordance with the requirements of 6.1;
  7. evaluate these processes and implement any changes needed to ensure that these processes achieve their intended results;
  8. improve the processes and the quality management system.

4.4.2: To the extent necessary, the organization shall (a) maintain documented information to support the operation of its processes; (b) retain documented information to have confidence that the processes are being carried out as planned.

Tools for Analyzing Organizational Context

4.2: Identifying Interested Parties

Main interested parties of a pharmaceutical CDMO:

  • Customers (brand-name pharmaceutical companies): product specifications, technology transfer requirements, audit expectations
  • Regulatory authorities (FDA / EMA / TFDA): GMP compliance requirements
  • Employees: safe environment, fair treatment, career development
  • Suppliers: long-term cooperation terms, quality agreements
  • Investors / shareholders: financial performance, regulatory risk management
  • Patients (end users): product safety and efficacy

4.4: The Importance of a Process Inventory

4.4.1 requires the organization to determine all the processes needed for the QMS. Practical suggestions:

  1. List the core business processes (e.g., order intake → technical review → manufacturing → QC → release → shipment)
  2. Add support processes (HR, purchasing, equipment maintenance, document management)
  3. Add management processes (strategic planning, management review, internal audit)
  4. Assign a process owner to each process

This process inventory is the backbone of the QMS architecture and is also documented information that auditors always check.

Analogy: QMS Scope = the Coverage of an Insurance Policy

The QMS scope in 4.3 is like the "coverage" of an insurance policy: you must state clearly which addresses, products and activities are covered. Anything outside the scope is not insured, and likewise not covered by the ISO certificate. A CDMO with multiple sites and product lines needs a written justification for each scope decision.

Clause 5 — Leadership

Leadership: Top Management's 10 Commitments, Quality Policy, Organizational Roles

5.1.1 General — Leadership and Commitment

Top management shall demonstrate leadership and commitment with respect to the quality management system by:

  1. taking accountability for the effectiveness of the quality management system;
  2. ensuring that the quality policy and quality objectives are established for the quality management system and are compatible with the context and strategic direction of the organization;
  3. ensuring the integration of the quality management system requirements into the organization's business processes;
  4. promoting the use of the process approach and risk-based thinking;
  5. ensuring that the resources needed for the quality management system are available;
  6. communicating the importance of effective quality management and of conforming to the quality management system requirements;
  7. ensuring that the quality management system achieves its intended results;
  8. engaging, directing and supporting persons to contribute to the effectiveness of the quality management system;
  9. promoting improvement;
  10. supporting other relevant management roles to demonstrate their leadership as it applies to their areas of responsibility.

5.1.2 Customer Focus

Top management shall demonstrate leadership and commitment with respect to customer focus by ensuring that:

  1. customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements are determined, understood and consistently met;
  2. the risks and opportunities that can affect conformity of products and services and the ability to enhance customer satisfaction are determined and addressed;
  3. the focus on enhancing customer satisfaction is maintained.

5.2 Policy

5.2.1 Establishing the Quality Policy

Top management shall establish, implement and maintain a quality policy that:

  1. is appropriate to the purpose and context of the organization and supports its strategic direction;
  2. provides a framework for setting quality objectives;
  3. includes a commitment to satisfy applicable requirements;
  4. includes a commitment to continual improvement of the quality management system.

5.2.2 Communicating the Quality Policy

The quality policy shall:

  1. be available and be maintained as documented information;
  2. be communicated, understood and applied within the organization;
  3. be available to relevant interested parties, as appropriate.

5.3 Organizational Roles, Responsibilities and Authorities

Top management shall ensure that the responsibilities and authorities for relevant roles are assigned, communicated and understood within the organization.

Top management shall assign the responsibility and authority for:

  1. ensuring that the quality management system conforms to the requirements of this International Standard;
  2. ensuring that the processes are delivering their intended outputs;
  3. reporting on the performance of the quality management system and on opportunities for improvement, in particular to top management;
  4. ensuring the promotion of customer focus throughout the organization;
  5. ensuring that the integrity of the quality management system is maintained when changes to the quality management system are planned and implemented.

Leadership: More Than Signing Documents

5.1: The 10 Actions of Top Management (Audit Focus)

What auditors look for is not whether top management has signed off the quality policy, but evidence of substantive action:

  • Management review meeting minutes (did top management chair them personally, or delegate?)
  • Documents linking quality objectives to company strategy
  • Records of quality topics discussed at board / senior management meetings
  • Approval records for resource decisions (staff, equipment)
  • Records of communication to employees (all-hands meetings, emails, internal announcements)

5.1.2 Customer Focus: the Pharmaceutical Link to CQAs

For a pharmaceutical CDMO, "customer requirements" include:

  • Product CQAs (critical quality attributes), such as purity, assay and sterility
  • Regulatory requirements: FDA / EMA / ICH requirements
  • Contractual requirements: delivery time, packaging specifications, stability data
  • Implicit needs: the level of service the customer expects but has not stated

Top management must ensure the organization has a mechanism to continuously track and meet these requirements, rather than relying only on verbal communication from the sales department.

5.3: A Major Change in the 2015 Version: No Management Representative (MR) Requirement

ISO 9001:2008 required the appointment of a Management Representative.

ISO 9001:2015 removed this requirement. The reason: to avoid quality being "outsourced to a single QA manager", and to stress that quality is the responsibility of the whole organization and of top management.

In practice many organizations still assign these functions to a quality manager, but the standard no longer mandates a single "management representative" title.

The Six Requirements for the Quality Policy (Summary of 5.2)

  1. Is appropriate to the purpose and context of the organization
  2. Is aligned with the strategic direction
  3. Provides a framework for setting quality objectives
  4. Includes a commitment to satisfy applicable requirements
  5. Includes a commitment to continual improvement
  6. Is available as documented information and is communicated

Common audit finding: the quality policy is posted on the wall, but employees do not know its content when asked. This violates the requirement that it be "communicated, understood and applied."

Analogy: Top Management Is the "Thermometer of Quality Culture"

The temperature of an organization's quality culture is set by the behavior of top management. If the CEO says in a meeting, "This shipment does not meet specification, but the customer is in a hurry, so ship it", that one sentence defeats the whole quality system. Conversely, when top management actively takes part in CAPA discussions, quality awareness naturally spreads downward.

Clause 6 — Planning

Planning: Risks and Opportunities, Quality Objectives, Planning of Changes

6.1 Actions to Address Risks and Opportunities

6.1.1

When planning for the quality management system, the organization shall consider the issues referred to in 4.1 and the requirements referred to in 4.2 and determine the risks and opportunities that need to be addressed to:

  1. give assurance that the quality management system can achieve its intended result(s);
  2. enhance desirable effects;
  3. prevent, or reduce, undesired effects;
  4. achieve improvement.

6.1.2

The organization shall plan:

  1. actions to address these risks and opportunities;
  2. how to: (1) integrate and implement the actions into its quality management system processes; (2) evaluate the effectiveness of these actions.
Actions taken to address risks and opportunities shall be proportionate to the potential impact on the conformity of products and services.

NOTE 1: Options to address risks can include avoiding risk, taking risk in order to pursue an opportunity, eliminating the risk source, changing the likelihood or consequences, sharing the risk, or retaining risk by informed decision.

NOTE 2: Opportunities can lead to the adoption of new practices, launching new products, opening new markets, addressing new customers, building partnerships, using new technology and other desirable possibilities.

6.2 Quality Objectives and Planning to Achieve Them

6.2.1

The organization shall establish quality objectives at relevant functions, levels and processes. The quality objectives shall:

  1. be consistent with the quality policy;
  2. be measurable;
  3. take into account applicable requirements;
  4. be relevant to conformity of products and services and to enhancement of customer satisfaction;
  5. be monitored;
  6. be communicated;
  7. be updated as appropriate.

The organization shall maintain documented information on the quality objectives.

6.2.2 — Planning to Achieve Quality Objectives

When planning how to achieve its quality objectives, the organization shall determine:

  1. what will be done;
  2. what resources will be required;
  3. who will be responsible;
  4. when it will be completed;
  5. how the results will be evaluated.

6.3 Planning of Changes

When the organization determines the need for changes to the quality management system, the changes shall be carried out in a planned manner. The organization shall consider:

  1. the purpose of the changes and their potential consequences;
  2. the integrity of the quality management system;
  3. the availability of resources;
  4. the allocation or reallocation of responsibilities and authorities.

Planning Explained: From PESTLE to Action

6.2: Quality Objectives = SMART Objectives

The standard requires quality objectives to be "measurable", which maps directly onto the SMART principle:

  • Specific: state clearly what is to be achieved
  • Measurable: has a quantitative indicator (e.g., batch yield ≥ 98%)
  • Achievable: realistic, with resources to support it
  • Relevant: related to the quality policy and customer needs
  • Time-bound: has a clear deadline

Common quality objectives for a pharmaceutical CDMO: OOS rate, number of customer complaints, on-time CAPA closure rate, number of audit nonconformities.

6.3: Change Control

6.3 requires changes to the QMS to be carried out "in a planned manner". This maps directly onto the pharmaceutical change control system:

  • Any change to SOPs, equipment, processes or sites
  • The impact on product quality, customer requirements and regulatory compliance must be assessed
  • There must be an approval process and impact assessment records
  • For major changes: notification to the regulatory authority or the customer may be required

Analogy: Risk Management = What the Pharmaceutical Industry Already Does

For pharmaceutical professionals, "risks and opportunities" in 6.1 are nothing new: this is what ICH Q9 does. ISO 9001 risk management is broader than ICH Q9 and extends to business risks (supplier disruption, changes in market demand), but the core logic is identical: identify → assess → address → monitor.

Clause 7 — Support

Support: Resources, Competence, Awareness, Communication, Documented Information

7.1 Resources

7.1.1 General

The organization shall determine and provide the resources needed for the establishment, implementation, maintenance and continual improvement of the quality management system. The organization shall consider:

  1. the capabilities of, and constraints on, existing internal resources;
  2. what needs to be obtained from external providers.

7.1.2 People

The organization shall determine and provide the persons necessary for the effective implementation of its quality management system and for the operation and control of its processes.

7.1.3 Infrastructure

The organization shall determine, provide and maintain the infrastructure necessary for the operation of its processes. NOTE: Infrastructure can include: (a) buildings and associated utilities; (b) equipment, including hardware and software; (c) transportation resources; (d) information and communication technology.

7.1.4 Environment for the Operation of Processes

The organization shall determine, provide and maintain the environment necessary for the operation of its processes. NOTE: A suitable environment can be a combination of human and physical factors, such as: (a) social (e.g. non-discriminatory, calm, non-confrontational); (b) psychological (e.g. stress-reducing, burnout prevention, emotionally protective); (c) physical (e.g. temperature, heat, humidity, light, airflow, hygiene, noise).

7.1.5 Monitoring and Measuring Resources

7.1.5.1 General: The organization shall determine and provide the resources needed to ensure valid and reliable results when monitoring or measuring is used to verify the conformity of products and services. The resources provided shall: (a) be suitable for the specific type of monitoring and measurement activities being undertaken; (b) be maintained to ensure their continuing fitness for their purpose.

7.1.5.2 Measurement Traceability: When measurement traceability is a requirement, measuring equipment shall be:

  1. calibrated or verified, or both, at specified intervals, or prior to use, against measurement standards traceable to international or national measurement standards;
  2. identified in order to determine their status;
  3. safeguarded from adjustments, damage or deterioration that would invalidate the calibration status and subsequent measurement results.

7.1.6 Organizational Knowledge

The organization shall determine the knowledge necessary for the operation of its processes and to achieve conformity of products and services. This knowledge shall be maintained and made available to the extent necessary.

When addressing changing needs and trends, the organization shall consider its current knowledge and determine how to acquire or access any necessary additional knowledge and required updates.

NOTE 1: Organizational knowledge is knowledge specific to the organization; it is generally gained by experience. It is information that is used and shared to achieve the organization's objectives.

NOTE 2: Organizational knowledge can be based on: (a) internal sources (e.g. intellectual property; knowledge gained from experience; lessons learned from failures and successful projects; capturing and sharing undocumented knowledge and experience; the results of improvements in processes, products and services); (b) external sources (e.g. standards; academia; conferences; gathering knowledge from customers or external providers).

7.2 Competence

The organization shall:

  1. determine the necessary competence of person(s) doing work under its control that affects the performance and effectiveness of the quality management system;
  2. ensure that these persons are competent on the basis of appropriate education, training, or experience;
  3. where applicable, take actions to acquire the necessary competence, and evaluate the effectiveness of the actions taken;
  4. retain appropriate documented information as evidence of competence.

7.3 Awareness

The organization shall ensure that persons doing work under the organization's control are aware of:

  1. the quality policy;
  2. relevant quality objectives;
  3. their contribution to the effectiveness of the quality management system, including the benefits of improved performance;
  4. the implications of not conforming with the quality management system requirements.

7.4 Communication

The organization shall determine the internal and external communications relevant to the quality management system, including:

  1. on what it will communicate;
  2. when to communicate;
  3. with whom to communicate;
  4. how to communicate;
  5. who communicates.

7.5 Documented Information

7.5.1 General

The organization's quality management system shall include: (a) documented information required by this International Standard; (b) documented information determined by the organization as being necessary for the effectiveness of the quality management system.

7.5.2 Creating and Updating

When creating and updating documented information, the organization shall ensure appropriate:

  1. identification and description (e.g. a title, date, author, or reference number);
  2. format (e.g. language, software version, graphics) and media (e.g. paper, electronic);
  3. review and approval for suitability and adequacy.

7.5.3.1: Documented information required by the quality management system shall be controlled to ensure: (a) it is available and suitable for use, where and when it is needed; (b) it is adequately protected (e.g. from loss of confidentiality, improper use, or loss of integrity).

7.5.3.2: For the control of documented information, the organization shall address the following activities, as applicable:

  1. distribution, access, retrieval and use;
  2. storage and preservation, including preservation of legibility;
  3. control of changes (e.g. version control);
  4. retention and disposition.
Documented information retained as evidence of conformity shall be protected from unintended alterations.

The Support System Explained

7.1.5: Metrological Traceability

Measurement management is one of the cores of GMP in a pharmaceutical plant:

  • Every measuring device must be calibrated periodically (calibration interval + reference standards traceable to NIST/BIPM)
  • Calibration records must be retained (documented information)
  • When a device is found to be out of order → the validity of past measurement results must be assessed
  • Equipment inventory + calibration labels = baseline compliance requirement

7.1.6: Organizational Knowledge, the Most Underrated Clause in Pharmaceuticals

This is a new clause in ISO 9001:2015 and one of the requirements most easily overlooked in the pharmaceutical industry.

Common knowledge-loss scenarios:

  • A senior QP (Qualified Person) leaves → years of regulatory judgment experience walk out the door
  • Process development staff move on → the tacit knowledge behind formulation optimization cannot be found
  • Old equipment is scrapped → operating tips and troubleshooting methods were never documented
  • After a customer's technology transfer is completed → the context and decision rationale of the original research are lost

Countermeasures: knowledge management plan, technical documentation, mentoring, handover procedures for departing staff, lessons-learned database

7.2: The Four-Step Competence Management Process

  1. Determine: what competence is needed for which positions? (job descriptions, competence matrix)
  2. Ensure: obtain the needed competence through training, recruitment or outsourcing
  3. Maintain: continuing education, annual refresher training, competence assessment
  4. Retain evidence: training records, test scores, OJT forms (documented information)

Note: education/qualifications ≠ competence. Competence must be demonstrated through assessment, not judged by certificates alone.

7.5: The Significance of the Unified Term "Documented Information"

ISO 9001:2008 used three different terms:

  • Documents: SOPs, standards, procedures
  • Quality Manual: mandatory
  • Records: evidence of activities

ISO 9001:2015 unified these into "Documented Information":

  • "Maintain documented information" = the former "documents" (kept up to date at all times)
  • "Retain documented information" = the former "records" (unalterable evidence)

A pharmaceutical QMS documentation system needs its terminology updated accordingly, but the substantive requirements have not changed significantly. The quality manual is no longer mandatory, but is still recommended as a description of the QMS architecture.

Practical Considerations for a Pharmaceutical CDMO

How would your site answer the following Clause 7 audit questions?

  • Cl.7.1.6: When a senior employee leaves, how do you ensure critical technical knowledge is not lost? Do you have a lessons-learned database?
  • Cl.7.2: Do training records consist only of attendance sign-in sheets, or are there competence assessment records?
  • Cl.7.3: Do operators know how their work affects customers and patients?
  • Cl.7.5.3: Does the electronic document system have version control? Do paper records prevent unauthorized changes?

Analogy: Organizational Knowledge = the Company's "Hidden Assets"

The value of "20 years of process optimization knowledge" does not appear on the financial statements, yet it is often a CDMO's most important competitive advantage. 7.1.6 requires the organization to manage such hidden assets. Just as you back up your computer data regularly, knowledge also needs to be "backed up" into documents and systems, rather than left in the heads of a few people.

Operational Planning and Control

Clause 8.1 — The execution layer: translating risk/opportunity plans into controlled processes

8.1 Operational Planning and Control

The organization shall plan, implement and control the processes needed to meet the requirements for the provision of products and services, and to implement the actions determined in Clause 6, by:

  1. determining the requirements for the products and services;
  2. establishing criteria for:
    1. the processes;
    2. the acceptance of products and services;
  3. determining the resources needed to achieve conformity to the product and service requirements;
  4. implementing control of the processes in accordance with the criteria;
  5. determining, maintaining and retaining documented information to the extent necessary:
    1. to have confidence that the processes have been carried out as planned;
    2. to demonstrate the conformity of products and services to their requirements.

The output of this planning shall be suitable for the organization's operations.

The organization shall control planned changes and review the consequences of unintended changes, taking action to mitigate any adverse effects, as necessary.

The organization shall ensure that outsourced processes are controlled (see 8.4).

Commentary: Operational Planning Is the Execution Layer of Clause 6 Planning

Core logic: plan, execute, document

8.1 is the "execution engine" of ISO 9001. Clause 6 decides what needs to be done (the risk/opportunity plan); 8.1 specifies how to turn those plans into controlled process operations.

  • Criteria: process acceptance criteria, equivalent to a pharmaceutical plant's process specifications and in-process control limits
  • Documented information: batch records and process procedures; pharmaceutical plants already go far beyond this requirement
  • Control of planned changes: a formal Change Control system is a must in GMP, whereas ISO 9001 only requires that the consequences be "controlled and reviewed"

Analogy: construction drawings for a building

Clause 6 is the architect's design concept (deciding what to build); 8.1 is the construction drawings plus the quality management plan (telling the workers the standard and acceptance conditions for every step). Without drawings, even hardworking builders can build the wrong thing.

Pharmaceutical perspective: control of outsourced processes

"Outsourced processes shall be controlled" (see 8.4): in a pharmaceutical CDMO, every outsourced testing, sterilization or packaging step falls under this. ISO 9001 requires "control"; GMP goes further and requires a formal Technical Agreement and supplier audits.

Communication with customers shall include:

  2. handling enquiries, contracts or orders, including changes;
  3. obtaining customer feedback relating to products and services, including customer complaints;
  4. handling or controlling customer property;
  5. establishing specific requirements for contingency actions, when relevant.

8.2.2 Determining Requirements

The organization shall ensure that:

  1. the requirements for the products and services are defined, including:
    1. any applicable statutory and regulatory requirements;
    2. those considered necessary by the organization;
  2. the organization can meet the claims for the products and services it offers.

The organization shall ensure that it has the ability to meet the requirements before committing to supply, to include:

  1. requirements specified by the customer, including delivery and post-delivery activities;
  2. requirements not stated by the customer, but necessary for the specified or intended use;
  3. requirements specified by the organization;
  4. statutory and regulatory requirements;
  5. contract or order requirements differing from those previously expressed.

Commentary: Contract Review, a Core Capability of a CDMO

The 5 aspects of customer communication in 8.2.1

  • (1) Providing product information: specifications, CoA, SDS
  • (2) Handling enquiries, contracts and orders: including change management
  • (3) Customer feedback and complaints: ISO requires collecting them; GMP requires CAPA
  • (4) Handling customer property: customer-supplied materials, tooling and IP
  • (5) Contingency action requirements: plans for responding to supply chain disruption

Analogy: confirming an order at a restaurant

8.2.3 contract review is like a chef confirming a special order before accepting it: are the ingredients available (resources), can the chef cook it (capability), does the guest have allergies (regulatory/implied requirements)? Confirm first, then commit, so that nothing goes wrong when the dish is served.

Contract review (8.2.3) in a pharmaceutical CDMO

Before accepting a new client's project, a CDMO's contract review must cover:

  • Regulatory market requirements (FDA, EMA or PMDA: which market?)
  • Technical feasibility (dosage form, process capability, sterility grade)
  • Implied requirements (e.g. a sterile product brings the Annex 1 requirements with it, even if the customer does not specify them)
  • Content of the Technical Agreement (Quality Agreement)

This is exactly what 8.2.2b means by "the organization can meet the claims for the products and services it offers".

Design and Development

Clauses 8.3.1–8.3.6 — Planning, inputs, controls (review/verification/validation), outputs, changes

8.3.1–8.3.2 General & Planning

The organization shall establish, implement and maintain a design and development process appropriate to ensure the subsequent provision of products and services.

In determining the stages and controls, the organization shall consider:

  • nature, duration and complexity of activities;
  • required process stages, reviews, verification and validation activities;
  • responsibilities and authorities;
  • internal and external resource needs;
  • involvement of customers and users;
  • documented information needed to demonstrate requirements have been met.

8.3.3 Design and Development Inputs

The organization shall determine requirements essential for the specific types of products and services, considering:

  1. functional and performance requirements;
  2. information derived from previous similar activities;
  3. statutory and regulatory requirements;
  4. standards or codes of practice;
  5. potential consequences of failure.

Inputs shall be adequate, complete and unambiguous. Conflicting inputs shall be resolved.

8.3.4 Design and Development Controls

Controls shall ensure that:

  1. the results to be achieved are defined;
  2. reviews are conducted to evaluate the ability to meet requirements;
  3. verification activities ensure outputs meet input requirements;
  4. validation activities ensure products/services meet requirements for specified application or intended use;
  5. necessary actions are taken on problems determined;
  6. documented information of these activities is retained.
NOTE: Design and development reviews, verification and validation have distinct purposes. They can be conducted separately or in any combination.

8.3.5 Design and Development Outputs

Outputs shall:

  1. meet the input requirements;
  2. be adequate for subsequent provision processes;
  3. include or reference monitoring/measuring requirements and acceptance criteria;
  4. specify characteristics essential for intended purpose and safe provision.

8.3.6 Design and Development Changes

The organization shall identify, review and control changes to ensure no adverse impact on conformity. Retained documented information shall include:

  1. design and development changes;
  2. results of reviews;
  3. authorization of changes;
  4. actions taken to prevent adverse impacts.

Commentary: How Design and Development Maps to Pharmaceuticals

The fundamental difference between Review, Verification and Validation

The NOTE in ISO 9001 stresses that the three have different purposes, which matters especially in a pharmaceutical environment:

  • Review: evaluating whether the design results can "meet the requirements", as in a Design Review Meeting; pharmaceutical plants run the equivalent as the formulation design review under ICH Q8
  • Verification: confirming that the "outputs meet the input specifications", e.g. confirming that the formulation design meets the specifications in the Target Product Profile (TPP)
  • Validation: confirming that the product can achieve its intended use under "actual conditions of use": in pharmaceutical plants this means process validation, cleaning validation and sterilization validation

Analogy: designing an aircraft

Review: engineers meet to review the design drawings and confirm that the wingspan calculations are correct.
Verification: a model is tested in a wind tunnel to confirm that the aerodynamic data meet the design specifications.
Validation: flight tests on the real aircraft confirm that it can fly safely in real skies.

None of the three steps can be skipped: passing the wind tunnel does not mean the real aircraft will fly.

How design and development maps to pharmaceutical plants

  • 8.3 Design and development → ICH Q8 pharmaceutical development
  • Design inputs (8.3.3) → Target Product Profile (TPP), regulatory requirements, early-stage study data
  • Design outputs (8.3.5) → batch manufacturing record (BMR), QC methods, specifications
  • Design changes (8.3.6) → post-approval changes after technology transfer (change management)
  • Design and development to production: Technology Transfer = moving from R&D to commercial production

Linking ICH Q8 Quality by Design (QbD) with 8.3

ICH Q8 promotes a systematic understanding of the product and process from the earliest stage of design, and establishing a Design Space. This is the advanced version of the design controls in ISO 9001 8.3:

  • CQA (critical quality attributes) = design inputs
  • CPP (critical process parameters) = part of the design outputs
  • Design Space = the validated operating range

Control of Externally Provided Processes, Products and Services

Clauses 8.4.1–8.4.3 — Three scenarios, risk-proportionate controls, six communication categories

8.4.1 General

The organization shall ensure that externally provided processes, products and services conform to requirements.

Controls shall be determined when:

  1. products and services from external providers are intended for incorporation into the organization's own products and services;
  2. products and services are provided directly to the customer by external providers on behalf of the organization;
  3. a process, or part of a process, is provided by an external provider as a result of a decision by the organization (outsourcing).

The organization shall determine and apply criteria for the evaluation, selection, monitoring of performance, and re-evaluation of external providers, based on their ability to provide in accordance with requirements. Documented information of these activities and any necessary actions shall be retained.

8.4.2 Type and Extent of Control

The organization shall ensure externally provided processes, products and services do not adversely affect ability to consistently deliver conforming products/services. The organization shall:

  1. ensure externally provided processes remain within the control of its QMS;
  2. define both the controls for the external provider and those for the resulting output;
  3. take into consideration:
    1. the potential impact on the organization's ability to consistently meet requirements;
    2. the effectiveness of the controls applied by the external provider;
  4. determine verification or other activities necessary to ensure requirements are met.

8.4.3 Information for External Providers

The organization shall communicate to external providers its requirements for:

  1. the processes, products and services to be provided;
  2. the approval of: products and services; methods, processes and equipment; the release of products and services;
  3. competence, including any required qualification of persons;
  4. the external providers' interactions with the organization;
  5. control and monitoring of the external providers' performance to be applied by the organization;
  6. verification or validation activities that the organization, or its customer, intends to perform at the external providers' premises.

Commentary: Supplier Control, ISO 9001 vs. GMP

The three situations in 8.4.1

  • Situation A (incorporated into your own products): purchasing API, excipients and packaging materials, the typical purchasing scenario in a pharmaceutical plant
  • Situation B (delivered directly to the customer on your behalf): a distributor ships in your place; less common in pharmaceutical plants but found in contract research
  • Situation C (outsourced processes): outsourced sterilization, testing or packaging, which is the core business model of a CDMO

The difference in depth of supplier control between ISO 9001 and GMP

ISO 9001 requires "evaluation, selection, monitoring and re-evaluation" but does not prescribe how. GMP standards go further:

  • ICH Q10: requires written supplier quality agreements (Technical/Quality Agreement)
  • ISO 15378 (primary packaging materials for medicinal products): supplier audit programme and periodic evaluation reports
  • FDA QS Regulation (21 CFR 820): requires a formal Approved Supplier List and audit records
  • EU GMP Annex 8: sampling and testing of starting materials, i.e. confirmation before they arrive on site

Analogy: a restaurant's ingredient supplier management

A Michelin-starred restaurant does not just look at a supplier's price quote. It visits the farm in person to confirm the growing methods (on-site audit), asks for pesticide test reports (CoA) and builds a long-term relationship (Approved Supplier). ISO 9001 says "manage your suppliers well"; GMP tells you "exactly how to manage them".

The six categories of communication to external providers in 8.4.3

  1. The specifications of the process, product or service to be provided
  2. Items requiring approval (products, methods, equipment, release)
  3. Competence requirements for personnel
  4. The external providers' interactions with the organization
  5. How the organization will control and monitor the external providers' performance
  6. Verification or validation activities that the organization or its customer intends to carry out at the external provider's premises

Point 6 is especially important: a customer performing a quality audit at the CDMO site, or the CDMO performing a vendor audit at a raw material supplier, both fall within the scope of this clause.

Commentary: How Production Control Maps in Depth to Pharmaceutical GMP

8.5.1f, "special process" validation: a core ISO 9001 concept

The most important clause: when the output of a process "cannot be verified by subsequent monitoring or measurement", the process must be validated and periodically revalidated.

This is the theoretical basis for pharmaceutical validation. You cannot sterility-test every vial of an injectable (the test is destructive), so the entire aseptic process must be validated in advance:

  • Sterilization validation: autoclave (steam), dry heat and sterilizing filtration
  • Media fill (process simulation): simulation of the aseptic filling process
  • Cleaning validation: control of residues and cross-contamination in shared-line (multi-product) manufacturing
  • Computerized system validation (CSV): automated processes that cannot be confirmed by eye

Analogy: baking a cake vs. canning

A baked cake can be cut open to check that it is done (subsequent measurement can verify it). But after canning sterilization you cannot open every can to test whether the contents are still safe, so the process must be validated in advance: confirming that, under a given combination of temperature, pressure and time, every location reaches a sufficient lethality (F₀ value). This is the essence of "special process" validation.

What 8.5.2 traceability means in a pharmaceutical plant

ISO 9001 requires unique identification and traceability "where necessary". Pharmaceutical GMP mandates complete batch traceability:

  • Batch/lot number system
  • Raw materials traceable to the CoA and supplier
  • Production equipment, personnel and environmental data
  • In the event of a recall, all affected batches can be traced quickly (batch record review)

21 CFR Part 211 requires batch records to be retained for 1 year after the expiration date, or at least 3 years, which far exceeds the minimum ISO 9001 requirement.

8.5.5 Post-delivery activities: the pharmacovigilance equivalent in pharmaceutical plants

ISO 9001 requires consideration of "potential undesired consequences, the lifetime of the products and services, and statutory and regulatory requirements". The pharmaceutical equivalents are:

  • Pharmacovigilance: post-marketing monitoring of adverse reactions
  • Field Safety Corrective Action (FSCA): recalls and safety notices
  • Ongoing stability programme: confirming quality throughout the shelf life
  • Annual Product Review (APR/PQR): required by EU GMP Ch.1

8.5.6 Change control: the pharmaceutical Change Control system

ISO 9001 requires records of the results of change reviews, the persons authorizing the change and any necessary actions. Pharmaceutical GMP goes further:

  • Classification (minor/major/critical change)
  • Risk assessment
  • Cross-functional review (QA, production, regulatory, engineering)
  • Regulatory filing assessment (whether a Variation/PAS/CBE is needed)
  • Effectiveness check

8.6 Release of Products and Services

The release of products and services shall not proceed until the planned arrangements have been satisfactorily completed, unless otherwise approved by a relevant authority and, as applicable, by the customer.

The organization shall ensure that outputs that do not conform to their requirements are identified and controlled to prevent their unintended use or delivery. The organization shall deal with nonconforming outputs in one or more of the following ways:

  1. correction;
  2. segregation, containment, return or suspension of provision of products and services;
  3. informing the customer;
  4. obtaining authorization for acceptance under concession.

Conformity to the requirements shall be verified when nonconforming outputs are corrected.

  2. describes the actions taken;

  4. identifies the authority deciding the action.

Commentary: Release Control and Nonconforming Outputs, Stricter Requirements in Pharmaceutical Plants

8.6 Product release: the responsibility of the QP (Qualified Person)

ISO 9001 requires that planned arrangements be completed satisfactorily before release, and that the person authorizing release be recorded. Pharmaceutical plants operate a stricter system:

  • EU GMP: every batch must be certified and released by a QP (Qualified Person), who carries legal responsibility
  • FDA: the Quality Unit is responsible for batch review and release (21 CFR 211.192)
  • Pre-release requirements: complete batch record review, deviation assessment, OOS investigation results and stability data

Where ISO 9001 speaks of "traceability to the person authorizing release", in a pharmaceutical plant the QP carries personal legal responsibility to the authorities, which is a completely different level.

Stricter nonconforming product management in pharmaceutical plants

  • OOS (Out-of-Specification): must be investigated according to the FDA OOS guidance; it cannot simply be accepted by concession
  • Rework: requires a formal Rework Protocol, and the material must pass the full set of QC tests again
  • Destruction records: destruction of nonconforming product requires two-person witnessing and a record
  • Deviation system: ISO 9001 only requires documented records, whereas pharmaceutical plants require deviation investigation, root cause analysis (RCA) and CAPA

Analogy: an aircraft instrument panel

Patients will never fill in a survey for the CDMO, but patient safety is the ultimate purpose of the CDMO's quality work. The patient-centric quality culture promoted by ICH Q10 embodies exactly this idea.

Internal Audit

Clauses 9.2.1–9.2.2 — Audit programme, objectivity, reporting, corrective action, documented information

9.2.1 Internal Audit Requirements

The organization shall conduct internal audits at planned intervals to provide information on whether the QMS:

  1. conforms to:
    1. the organization's own requirements for its QMS;
    2. the requirements of this International Standard;
  2. is effectively implemented and maintained.

9.2.2 Audit Programme

The organization shall:

  1. plan, establish, implement and maintain an audit programme including the frequency, methods, responsibilities, planning requirements and reporting, taking into consideration the importance of the processes, changes affecting the organization, and the results of previous audits;
  2. define the audit criteria and scope for each audit;
  3. select auditors and conduct audits to ensure objectivity and impartiality;
  4. ensure that the results are reported to relevant management;
  5. take appropriate correction and corrective actions without undue delay;
  6. retain documented information as evidence of the audit programme and audit results.

NOTE: See ISO 19011 for guidance on auditing management systems.

Commentary: Internal Audit, Everyday Training for Regulatory Inspections

Risk-based design of the audit programme

9.2.2a requires the audit frequency to take into account "the importance of the processes concerned, changes affecting the organization, and the results of previous audits". This is the basic logic of a risk-based audit programme.

Pharmaceutical practice: high-risk processes such as aseptic filling, sterilization and OSD tablet compression are audited more frequently than warehousing or administrative departments. Additional audits should be scheduled after new processes are introduced or equipment is changed.

Objectivity and Impartiality

ISO 9001 requires auditors to be independent of the area being audited: auditors should not audit their own work.

Pharmaceutical practice: cross-audits are common (Line A personnel audit Line B), or QA leads the audit with production staff taking part in the technical aspects. External (third-party) audits provide even greater objectivity.

Analogy: scheduled aircraft maintenance checks

Airlines do not wait for an aircraft to develop a problem before inspecting it; they carry out A-checks, C-checks and D-checks at set flight-hour intervals. Internal audit is the "scheduled maintenance" of the QMS: not closing the stable door after the horse has bolted, but systematically finding the early signs of problems.

Internal audit = everyday training for regulatory inspections

For a pharmaceutical plant, internal audit has a dual value:

  • QMS maintenance: finding compliance gaps and identifying opportunities for improvement
  • Inspection readiness: auditing yourself to the standard of an FDA/EMA/MOHW inspection, familiarizing staff with inspection scenarios and finding documentation gaps

A good pharmaceutical QA department treats internal audit reports as simulated inspection observations and resolves the issues in a targeted way before the real inspection.

Management Review

Clauses 9.3.1–9.3.3 — General, eight input categories, three output decisions

9.3.1 General

Top management shall review the organization's QMS, at planned intervals, to ensure its continuing suitability, adequacy, effectiveness and alignment with the strategic direction of the organization.

9.3.2 Management Review Inputs

The management review shall be planned and carried out taking into consideration:

  1. the status of actions from previous management reviews;
  2. changes in external and internal issues relevant to the QMS;
  3. information on the performance and effectiveness of the QMS, including trends in:
    1. customer satisfaction and feedback from relevant interested parties;
    2. the extent to which quality objectives have been met;
    3. process performance and conformity of products and services;
    4. nonconformities and corrective actions;
    5. monitoring and measurement results;
    6. audit results;
    7. the performance of external providers;
  4. the adequacy of resources;
  5. the effectiveness of actions taken to address risks and opportunities;
  6. opportunities for improvement.

9.3.3 Management Review Outputs

The outputs shall include decisions and actions related to:

  1. opportunities for improvement;
  2. any need for changes to the QMS;
  3. resource needs.

The organization shall retain documented information as evidence of the results of management reviews.

Commentary: Management Review, Top Management Responsibility in Practice

Three key words in management review

  • Suitability: is the QMS still suited to the organization's purpose and context? (as the business changes in scale)
  • Adequacy: are the QMS's resources and structure sufficient? (people, equipment, systems)
  • Effectiveness: is the QMS really operating and delivering results? (not just having documents, but having effect)

Analogy: the annual board meeting

Management review is like a company's annual board meeting: senior executives do not look at day-to-day detail but at overall trends: which goals were achieved last year, what major problems arose, what resources are needed next year. The input list in 9.3.2 is the "quality version of the board agenda".

"Status of actions from previous management reviews": tracking to closure

9.3.2a requires a review of the implementation status of the previous review's decisions. This is the "closed loop" principle of quality management: make a decision (output), carry it out, track the result, and confirm closure at the next management review.

Without this step, management review degenerates into an "annual routine reporting meeting" whose decisions are never actually implemented.

10.1

Improvement: General

Clause 10.1 — Three categories of improvement opportunities

10.1 General

The organization shall determine and select opportunities for improvement and implement any necessary actions to meet customer requirements and enhance customer satisfaction.

These shall include:

  1. improving products and services to meet requirements as well as to address future needs and expectations;
  2. correcting, preventing or reducing undesired effects;
  3. improving the performance and effectiveness of the QMS.

NOTE: Examples of improvement can include correction, corrective action, continual improvement, breakthrough change, innovation and re-organization.

Commentary: The Full Spectrum of Improvement

Three levels of improvement

  • Product/service improvement: improving formulation, process and packaging, usually corresponding to technical development projects
  • Problem prevention and correction: this is the core of 10.2 CAPA
  • QMS performance improvement: process optimization, digitalization and improved training programmes

The improvement spectrum in the NOTE

From incremental to breakthrough: correction → corrective action → continual improvement → breakthrough change → innovation → re-organization

Pharmaceutical improvement activities usually focus on the first three; "breakthrough change" requires a regulatory filing.

10.2

Nonconformity and Corrective Action

Clauses 10.2.1–10.2.2 — Six-step CAPA process, documented information requirements

10.2.1 Nonconformity and Corrective Action

When a nonconformity occurs, including any arising from complaints, the organization shall:

  1. react to the nonconformity and, as applicable:
    1. take action to control and correct it;
    2. deal with the consequences;
  2. evaluate the need for action to eliminate the cause(s) of the nonconformity, in order that it does not recur or occur elsewhere, by:
    1. reviewing and analysing the nonconformity;
    2. determining the causes of the nonconformity;
    3. determining if similar nonconformities exist, or could potentially occur;
  3. implement any action needed;
  4. review the effectiveness of any corrective action taken;
  5. update risks and opportunities determined during planning, if necessary;
  6. make changes to the QMS, if necessary.

Corrective actions shall be appropriate to the effects of the nonconformities encountered.

10.2.2 Documented Information

The organization shall retain documented information as evidence of:

  1. the nature of the nonconformities and any subsequent actions taken;
  2. the results of any corrective action.

Commentary: CAPA, the Most Critical System in FDA/EMA Inspections

Analogy: a doctor treating a patient

Correction: giving a fever reducer to bring the temperature down (treating the symptom).
Corrective Action: drawing blood to find the source of infection and prescribing antibiotics to cure it (eliminating the root cause).

If you only reduce the fever without finding the cause, the fever will return next week. If you only do Correction without Corrective Action, the same problem will recur.

The central role of CAPA in FDA/EMA inspections

For pharmaceutical plants, the CAPA system is one of the systems that regulators focus on most:

  • Common FDA Warning Letter deficiencies: inadequate root cause investigation, effectiveness checks not performed, CAPAs overdue and not closed
  • EMA GMP inspection focus: does CAPA address recurring deviations? Does senior management receive CAPA trend reports?
  • ICH Q10 requirement: the CAPA system is one of the four key elements of the pharmaceutical quality system

The six steps of 10.2.1 mapped to the pharmaceutical CAPA process

  1. React (10.2.1a): immediate control: quarantine, stop the line, notify the customer
  2. Evaluate root cause (10.2.1b): root cause analysis (RCA): 5 Whys, Fishbone, FMEA
  3. Implement action (10.2.1c): carry out the CAPA: revise SOPs, training, equipment improvements
  4. Review effectiveness (10.2.1d): Effectiveness Check: confirm after 30/60/90 days that the problem has not recurred
  5. Update risk assessment (10.2.1e): go back and revise the FMEA or risk register
  6. Update the QMS (10.2.1f): if the documentation system or procedures need to change

Correction vs. Corrective Action: the most important distinction

In ISO 9001, 10.2.1a is Correction and 10.2.1b-f is Corrective Action. Both are needed; neither can be omitted.

10.3

Continual Improvement

Clause 10.3 — Suitability, adequacy, effectiveness; feeds from analysis and management review

10.3 Continual Improvement

The organization shall continually improve the suitability, adequacy and effectiveness of the quality management system.

The organization shall consider the results of analysis and evaluation, and the outputs from management review, to determine if there are needs or opportunities that shall be addressed as part of continual improvement.

Commentary: Continual Improvement, the Heart of ISO 9001

The meaning of "continual": not just "improved once"

In continual improvement, "continual" (recurring, cyclical) is used rather than "continuous" (uninterrupted), meaning that improvement is a repeating cycle rather than a one-off activity.

Drivers: 9.1.3 analysis and evaluation results (data-driven) + 9.3.3 management review outputs (top management decisions) → identify improvement opportunities → implement improvements → evaluate again.

Analogy: the PDCA spiral

Continual improvement is not going around a circle and returning to the starting point, but climbing a spiral staircase: each turn takes you one level higher than the last. When each PDCA cycle ends, the standard has been raised and the next cycle starts from a higher point.

Continual improvement mechanisms in pharmaceutical plants

  • APR/PQR (Annual Product Review): systematically identifying opportunities to improve products and processes
  • Lean / Six Sigma: improving process efficiency (reducing waste, lowering variability)
  • Knowledge management (ICH Q10): accumulating process understanding and continually reducing uncertainty in the design space
  • Benchmarking: comparing against industry best practice to set improvement targets

Understanding the purpose of the comparison table

ISO 9001 is a general foundational framework for quality management, applicable to organizations of any industry and any size. Pharmaceutical GMP layers industry-specific mandatory requirements on top of this foundation, reflecting the special responsibility medicines carry for patient safety. Understanding these two layers helps pharmaceutical staff recognize that "what we do goes far beyond what ISO 9001 requires".

Key Takeaways

Clauses 8–10 in context: the operational heart of ISO 9001

Structure Summary: Clauses 8–10

Clause 8 (Operation) is the largest and most operationally detailed clause — it covers the complete production value chain from customer requirements through to nonconforming output control. Key themes:

  • 8.3: Review / Verification / Validation are distinct — each has a unique purpose and cannot substitute for each other
  • 8.4: Supplier control must be risk-proportionate — the higher the impact, the deeper the control
  • 8.5.1f: "Special processes" (where output cannot be verified by subsequent monitoring) must be validated — this is the theoretical basis for sterilization validation and media fill in pharma
  • 8.7: Four disposal actions for nonconforming outputs — correction, segregation/suspension, informing customer, concession

Clause 9 (Performance Evaluation) closes the loop: measure → analyze → audit → management review. All feeds into the improvement cycle.

Clause 10 (Improvement): The critical distinction is Correction (fix the symptom immediately) vs. Corrective Action (eliminate the root cause permanently). Both are needed. CAPA effectiveness verification (10.2.1d) is what separates a real CAPA system from paperwork.

Core learning points for pharmaceutical staff

ISO 9001 is the floor; GMP is the ceiling

For pharmaceutical practitioners, the ISO 9001 requirements are in most cases the minimum version of what you are already doing. Understanding the logical framework of ISO 9001 helps you to:

  • Explain your quality system to people outside the pharmaceutical industry
  • Assess the quality maturity of new suppliers (they may hold only ISO 9001 certification)
  • Prepare for an ISO 9001 audit and understand where the additional GMP requirements come from

Three "must-remember" key concepts

  1. Review ≠ Verification ≠ Validation: three different purposes that cannot substitute for one another (8.3.4)
  2. Correction ≠ Corrective Action: one eliminates the symptom, the other eliminates the root cause (10.2.1)
  3. Special processes need validation: because they cannot be verified by subsequent measurement; this is the basis for sterilization and aseptic filling validation (8.5.1f)

The perspective of a CDMO Operations Director

The whole cycle described in Clauses 8-10 (plan and execute → evaluate performance → improve continually) is the DNA of Operational Excellence. ISO 9001 provides the framework, and GMP fills in the specific requirements of pharmaceutical plants. As a leader on the COO track, you should lift this framework from a "compliance tool" to a "competitive advantage": a well-run QMS does not just pass inspections, it genuinely reduces deviations, improves OEE and shortens release cycle times, all of which directly affect the CDMO's competitiveness and customer confidence.

Structure and Terminology

Annex A (Informative) — Clarification of new structure, terminology and concepts

A.1 Structure and Terminology

The clause structure (i.e. clause sequence) and some of the terminology of this edition of this International Standard, in comparison with the previous edition (ISO 9001:2008), have been changed to improve alignment with other management systems standards.

There is no requirement in this International Standard for its structure and terminology to be applied to the documented information of an organization's quality management system.

The structure of clauses is intended to provide a coherent presentation of requirements, rather than a model for documenting an organization's policies, objectives and processes.

There is no requirement for the terms used by an organization to be replaced by the terms used in this International Standard to specify quality management system requirements. Organizations can choose to use terms which suit their operations (e.g. using "records", "documentation" or "protocols" rather than "documented information"; or "supplier", "partner" or "vendor" rather than "external provider").

Table A.1 — Major Terminology Differences: ISO 9001:2008 → 2015

Commentary: What the Terminology Changes Mean

Why did ISO 9001 change its terminology?

The biggest driver behind the 2015 edition was the adoption of the High Level Structure (HLS). ISO decided that all management system standards (QMS, EMS, OHSMS and so on) would share the same clause framework, so that companies can easily integrate several management systems. Harmonizing terminology is the first step.

Analogy: standardizing the power plug

Plug specifications used to differ from country to country, and you needed an adapter every time you travelled abroad. The HLS is the "universal plug" of ISO management systems: whether you run ISO 9001 (quality), ISO 14001 (environment) or ISO 45001 (safety) at the same time, the clause numbers and terminology are aligned, so you only need to maintain one system.

Pharmaceutical practice: terminology mapping

ISO 9001:2015 states explicitly that an organization does not have to rename "SOP" in its internal documents as "documented information", nor rename "supplier" as "external provider". This is a great relief for pharmaceutical plants that have already built a large documentation system.

Practical approach: simply explain in the terminology mapping table of the QMS manual how the company's terms correspond to the ISO 2015 terms. Auditors accept this approach.

The impact of "the Quality Manual is no longer required"

The 2008 edition required a Quality Manual. The 2015 edition removed this requirement in favour of flexible "documented information". However, in GMP-regulated industries (pharmaceuticals, medical devices), regulators (FDA, EMA) usually still expect to see some form of quality manual or equivalent document as an overview of the system.

A.2

Products and Services

Why "services" is now explicitly included alongside "products"

A.2 Products and Services

ISO 9001:2008 used the term "product" to include all output categories. This edition uses "products and services". "Products and services" include all output categories (hardware, services, software and processed materials).

The specific inclusion of "services" is intended to highlight the differences between products and services in the application of some requirements. The characteristic of services is that at least part of the output is realized at the interface with the customer. This means, for example, that conformity to requirements cannot necessarily be confirmed before service delivery.

In most cases, products and services are used together. Most outputs that organizations provide to customers, or are supplied to them by external providers, include both products and services. For example, a tangible or intangible product can have some associated service or a service can have some associated tangible or intangible product.

Commentary: What Explicitly Including Services Means

The core difference: the "real-time" nature of services

A manufacturer produces a finished product that can be inspected and confirmed before delivery. The hallmark of a service (or a service component) is that at least part of the output is completed at the moment of interaction with the customer, so it cannot be fully inspected beforehand.

For example: a CRO (contract research organization) providing clinical trial management services. You cannot fully verify all service quality "before delivery", because the service itself is the process.

The pharmaceutical CDMO perspective

Your company (a CDMO) provides both "products" (manufactured medicinal products) and "services" (process development, technology transfer, regulatory support). The ISO 9001:2015 framework covers both more clearly, helping a CDMO build a more complete QMS.

Practical impact on pharmaceutical plants

  • Outsourced testing services (external laboratories) = purchase of services, brought under 8.4 control
  • Outsourced sterilization (EO sterilization facility) = service + process outsourcing, requiring a supplier audit
  • The CDMO's own manufacturing services = the Quality Agreement is the key document
A.3

Understanding Needs & Expectations of Interested Parties

Scope limitation — not all interested parties require QMS coverage

A.3 Understanding the Needs and Expectations of Interested Parties

Subclause 4.2 specifies requirements for the organization to determine the interested parties that are relevant to the quality management system and the requirements of those interested parties.

However, 4.2 does not imply extension of quality management system requirements beyond the scope of this International Standard. As stated in the scope, this International Standard is applicable where an organization needs to demonstrate its ability to consistently provide products and services that meet customer and applicable statutory and regulatory requirements, and aims to enhance customer satisfaction.

There is no requirement in this International Standard for the organization to consider interested parties where it has decided that those parties are not relevant to its quality management system. It is for the organization to decide if a particular requirement of a relevant interested party is relevant to its quality management system.

Commentary: Defining the Scope of Interested Parties

What are "Interested Parties"?

Individuals or groups that affect, or are affected by, the organization. In a pharmaceutical CDMO, typical interested parties include:

  • Customers (brand-name and generic pharmaceutical companies)
  • Regulators (FDA, EMA, PMDA, TFDA)
  • Employees
  • Raw material suppliers
  • Shareholders / investors
  • The community (environmental impact, noise and so on)

Important limit: not every interested party needs to be included in the QMS

The standard states explicitly that the organization decides for itself which interested parties are relevant to the QMS. For example, residents' concerns about noise may fall within the scope of the EMS (environmental management) rather than the QMS. Do not over-expand the boundary of the QMS, or the system will become bloated and hard to maintain.

Practical principle

Once the interested parties are identified, you only need to bring under control those requirements that "directly affect product/service quality, or that impose regulatory/contractual requirements on the QMS". Investors care about financial returns, which the QMS does not need to address, but regulators' cGMP requirements must absolutely be included.

A.4

Risk-Based Thinking

The most significant philosophical shift in ISO 9001:2015 — replaces the preventive action clause

A.4 Risk-Based Thinking

The concept of risk-based thinking has been implicit in previous editions of this International Standard, e.g. through requirements for planning, review and improvement. This International Standard specifies requirements for the organization to understand its context (see 4.1) and determine risks as a basis for planning (see 6.1). This represents the application of risk-based thinking to planning and implementing quality management system processes (see 4.4).

One of the key purposes of a quality management system is to act as a preventive tool. Consequently, this International Standard does not have a separate clause or subclause on preventive action. The concept of preventive action is expressed through the use of risk-based thinking in formulating quality management system requirements.

The risk-based thinking applied in this International Standard has enabled some reduction in prescriptive requirements and their replacement by performance-based requirements. There is greater flexibility than in ISO 9001:2008 in the requirements for processes, documented information and organizational responsibilities.

Although 6.1 specifies that the organization shall plan actions to address risks, there is no requirement for formal methods for risk management or a documented risk management process. Organizations can decide whether or not to develop a more extensive risk management methodology than is required by this International Standard, e.g. through the application of other guidance or standards.

Not all the processes of a quality management system represent the same level of risk in terms of the organization's ability to meet its objectives, and the effects of uncertainty are not the same for all organizations. Under the requirements of 6.1, the organization is responsible for its application of risk-based thinking and the actions it takes to address risk, including whether or not to retain documented information as evidence of its determination of risks.

Commentary: The Most Critical Philosophical Shift

The core shift: from "do you have a procedure?" to "have you thought about the risks?"

ISO 9001:2008 had a separate "Preventive Action" clause (8.5.3) requiring the organization to establish a preventive action procedure. The 2015 edition removed this clause and instead requires risk-based thinking throughout the entire QMS.

This means it is no longer enough to say "we have a preventive action SOP"; you must be able to show that "when planning and carrying out every process, we have systematically considered the risks".

Analogy: risk awareness when driving vs. risk management in racing

What ISO 9001 requires is "basic risk awareness when driving": slowing down for a bend, taking care in the rain. ICH Q9 requires "racing-driver-level systematic risk assessment": the curvature of every bend, tyre wear rate and braking distance are all analysed quantitatively.

Pharmaceutical plants carry both sets of requirements: ISO 9001 is the baseline, and ICH Q9 is the height you actually have to reach.

Practical application in CDMO filling operations

  • Planning stage: assess the complexity risk of a new customer's product (toxicity, low dose, high potency) → determine facility requirements
  • Process design: FMEA of the critical steps in aseptic filling (per ICH Q9)
  • Supplier management: classify suppliers by risk level and decide the audit frequency
  • Documentation: ISO 9001 does not require you to keep records of all risk assessments, but FDA cGMP usually expects to see them

Key differences between ISO 9001 and ICH Q9 / ISO 14971

Conclusion: the "risk-based thinking" of ISO 9001 is the minimum threshold; in practice, pharmaceutical plants must follow ICH Q9 to carry out a more rigorous QRM.

A.5

Applicability

"Exclusions" is gone — replaced by scope-based applicability determination

A.5 Applicability

This International Standard does not refer to "exclusions" in relation to the applicability of its requirements to the organization's quality management system. However, an organization can review the applicability of requirements due to the size or complexity of the organization, the management model it adopts, the range of the organization's activities and the nature of the risks and opportunities it encounters.

The requirements for applicability are addressed in 4.3, which defines conditions under which an organization can decide that a requirement cannot be applied to any of the processes within the scope of its quality management system. The organization can only decide that a requirement is not applicable if its decision will not result in failure to achieve conformity of products and services.

Only Clause 8 requirements can be determined not applicable when they do not apply to the organization's operations.

Commentary: The Abolition of the "Exclusions" Clause

The problem with the old "Exclusions"

ISO 9001:2008 allowed organizations to declare certain Clause 7 (Product Realization) requirements "not applicable" and to justify this in the quality manual. In practice many organizations abused this, "excluding" every clause that was inconvenient to implement.

The logic of the new edition: only Clause 8 requirements can be declared not applicable

The 2015 edition changed this: a Clause 8 requirement can be declared not applicable only when there is simply no corresponding activity within the scope of your business. Condition: the decision of non-applicability must not result in a failure to achieve conformity of products and services.

For example: a pure software company with no physical "design and development outputs" that need physical verification may declare some sub-clauses of 8.3 not applicable. A pharmaceutical plant, however, can almost never reasonably exclude any Clause 8 requirement.

A.6–8

Documented Information / Organizational Knowledge / External Provision

Documented Information · Organizational Knowledge · Control of External Provision

A.6 Documented Information

As part of the alignment with other management system standards, a common clause on "documented information" has been adopted without significant change or addition (see 7.5).

Where ISO 9001:2008 used specific terminology such as "document" or "documented procedures", "quality manual" or "quality plan", this edition defines requirements to "maintain documented information".

Where ISO 9001:2008 used the term "records" to denote documents needed to provide evidence of conformity with requirements, this is now expressed as a requirement to "retain documented information". The organization is responsible for determining what documented information needs to be retained, the period of time for which it is to be retained and the media to be used for its retention.

A requirement to "maintain" documented information does not exclude the possibility that the organization might also need to "retain" that same documented information for a particular purpose, e.g. to retain previous versions of it.

Where this International Standard refers to "information" rather than "documented information" (e.g. in 4.1), there is no requirement that this information is to be documented.

A.7 Organizational Knowledge

In 7.1.6, this International Standard addresses the need to determine and manage the knowledge maintained by the organization, to ensure the operation of its processes and that it can achieve conformity of products and services.

Requirements regarding organizational knowledge were introduced for the purpose of:

  1. Safeguarding the organization from loss of knowledge, e.g.:
    • through staff turnover
    • failure to capture and share information
  2. Encouraging the organization to acquire knowledge, e.g.:
    • learning from experience
    • mentoring
    • benchmarking

A.8 Control of Externally Provided Processes, Products and Services

All forms of externally provided processes, products and services are addressed in 8.4, e.g. whether through:

  1. purchasing from a supplier;
  2. an arrangement with an associate company;
  3. outsourcing processes to an external provider.

Outsourcing always has the essential characteristic of a service, since it will have at least one activity necessarily performed at the interface between the provider and the organization.

The controls required for external provision can vary widely depending on the nature of the processes, products and services. The organization can apply risk-based thinking to determine the type and extent of controls appropriate to particular external providers and externally provided processes, products and services.

Commentary: What the Three New Requirements Mean for Pharmaceuticals

A.6: "Maintain" vs. "Retain" documented information

This is the term pair most easily confused in the 2015 edition. The way to understand it:

  • Maintain: continuously valid, living documents such as SOPs, the quality manual and process descriptions. Corresponds to "Documents" in the old edition.
  • Retain: completed records kept as evidence, such as batch records, test reports and audit records. Corresponds to "Records" in the old edition.

A single document can be required to be both "maintained" (keeping the current version in effect) and "retained" (archiving old versions).

A.7: Organizational knowledge, the Achilles heel of pharmaceutical plants

Organizational Knowledge is an entirely new requirement in the 2015 edition (with no corresponding clause in the old edition). It is especially critical for a pharmaceutical CDMO:

  • Senior engineers or QA staff retire or leave → loss of critical process knowledge
  • An equipment supplier stops providing support → a break in maintenance knowledge
  • Technology transfer of a customer's product → knowledge transfer needs systematic records

Practical tools: process knowledge repositories, Technology Transfer Packages, handover SOPs, mentoring programmes and Lessons Learned databases.

The common theme of the three A clauses

A.6 (flexible documentation), A.7 (knowledge management) and A.8 (control of supply risk) all reflect the core spirit of the 2015 edition: fewer prescriptive rules and more room for the organization's own judgement, but you must be able to explain the basis for that judgement. For a mature pharmaceutical QMS this is a relaxation; for a weak QMS it is a bigger challenge.

Who is ISO/TC 176?

ISO Technical Committee 176 (Quality management and quality assurance) is responsible for developing the entire ISO 9000 family and related companion standards. All the standards listed in this annex are products of the same committee, consistent with and complementing one another.

Comparison

QMS Standards Comparison: ISO 9001 vs. ISO 13485 vs. ISO 15378

Positioning of the Three Major QMS Standards: A Must-Have Perspective for the Pharmaceutical Packaging Industry

Why compare these three standards?

ISO 15378 (GMP quality management systems for primary packaging materials for medicinal products) is a specialist standard based on ISO 9001 with a GMP layer added. Understanding how the three are positioned helps a pharmaceutical CDMO decide which standard framework to use in which situations, and how to integrate multiple standards.

ISO 13485 vs. ISO 9001: why is it not based on the 2015 edition?

ISO 13485:2016 is still built on the framework of ISO 9001:2008, not the 2015 edition. The reason: regulators in the medical device industry (FDA, BSI and others) consider the flexible direction of ISO 9001:2015 (removing the mandatory continual improvement requirement and the quality manual requirement) unsuitable for the high-risk medical device sector. ISO 13485 therefore retains the stricter mandatory requirements, and may consider aligning with the HLS structure only in a future edition.

Strategy for integrating the three standards

For manufacturers working across several fields (for example, making both primary packaging materials and supplying medical devices), the keys to integrating the three standards are:

  • Use the HLS structure of ISO 9001 as the backbone
  • Layer on the mandatory requirements of ISO 13485 within the applicable scope
  • Layer on the GMP requirements of ISO 15378 within the scope of the packaging business
  • Avoid maintaining three separate documentation systems for different customer groups: integrate as far as possible

Commentary: A Guide to the Bibliography

Recommended reading order for building a pharmaceutical QMS

If you are in pharmaceutical QA, the recommended priority order from this bibliography is:

  1. ISO 9000 (not in the bibliography, but foundational): first build the terminology and concepts
  2. ISO 9004 ([1]): see what a more mature QMS looks like; suited to the system optimization stage
  3. ISO 19011 ([18]): practical guidance for building internal audit capability
  4. ISO 10012 ([10]): measurement management, a supplement to the GMP calibration system
  5. ISO 10015 ([13]): design of a GMP training system
  6. ISO 31000 ([19]): if you want to build a formal risk management framework

ISO 31000: a framework for formal risk management

Although ISO 9001 itself does not require a formal risk management methodology, the annex bibliography includes ISO 31000 (risk management principles and guidelines). This signals that ISO 9001 recognizes that organizations that need to can adopt ISO 31000 as the basis for building a more complete risk management system.

In the pharmaceutical industry, ISO 31000 + ICH Q9 + specific FMEA tools make up a complete QRM system.

ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group

One of the last two URLs in the bibliography is the website of the ISO 9001 Auditing Practices Group (APG). This group is run jointly by ISO/TC 176 and the IAF (International Accreditation Forum) and provides free audit technical documents and answers to frequently asked questions, a valuable free resource for preparing for an ISO 9001 certification audit.

Section Summary

Key Points Review of ISO 9001:2015 Annex A & B

Key Takeaways — Annex A

  • A.1 Terminology changes: organizations are not forced to change their internal terminology, but need to understand the terminology mapping (especially the maintain vs. retain distinction for documented information)
  • A.2 Products and services: the "real-time" nature of services requires upfront quality control, because they cannot be fully inspected afterwards
  • A.3 Interested parties: the organization decides for itself which interested parties are relevant to the QMS and need not expand the scope indefinitely
  • A.4 Risk-based thinking: replaces the separate preventive action clause; ISO 9001 does not require a formal risk management methodology (an important contrast with ICH Q9 / ISO 14971)
  • A.5 Applicability: the term "exclusions" is abolished; only Clause 8 requirements can be declared not applicable, and this must not affect product conformity
  • A.6 Documented information: maintain (living documents) vs. retain (records) is the core concept
  • A.7 Organizational knowledge: a new requirement in the 2015 edition, protecting critical process knowledge from being lost with staff turnover
  • A.8 Control of external provision: control intensity is decided on a risk basis, covering three forms: purchasing, partnering and outsourcing

Key Takeaways — Annex B & Bibliography

Key messages of Annex B

  • ISO/TC 176 maintains a complete ecosystem of supporting standards: ISO 9001 is the core, and the other standards provide specific implementation guidance
  • Table B.1 is a navigation tool for quickly locating companion resources
  • This annex does not include industry-specific QMS standards (such as ISO 13485 and ISO 15378), which do not fall directly under TC 176's remit

Core conclusions of the three-standard comparison

  • ISO 9001: flexible, driven by risk-based thinking, a general framework suitable for any organization
  • ISO 13485: stricter, continual improvement not mandatory, ISO 14971 risk management mandatory, medical-device oriented
  • ISO 15378: ISO 9001 + GMP layer, specific to primary packaging materials for filling, most directly relevant to pharmaceutical customers

A final thinking framework

The core message of ISO 9001 Annex A is: the 2015 edition shifts from "compliance orientation" to "performance orientation". The question is no longer "do you have this procedure?" but "have you identified the risks and taken effective action?" This is exactly the spirit of ICH Q10: a living, effective quality system, rather than a pile of beautiful documents.
