1.1 Purpose

The purpose of the ISPE Good Practice Guide: Process Gases is to document accepted good processes and procedures within pharmaceutical manufacturing applications. The Guide promotes science- and risk-based methods for the design, construction, commissioning, and qualification processes for gas systems used to support production. The Guide is intended to align with ICH Q9 and ASTM E2500-07.

1.2 Scope

This Guide considers those gases that come into direct contact with the biopharmaceutical and pharmaceutical manufacturing process streams; these include: nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide, and compressed air.

Medicinal or medical gases, breathing air, and steam are outside the scope of this Guide.

Process streams include bodily contact surfaces of invasive medical devices and fluid paths of medical devices that are used for intravenous solution, blood, or other critical applications to administer life saving or sustaining fluids.

The Guide focuses on defining cost effective engineering approaches and practices used to deliver process gas systems for a manufacturing facility in a timely manner that will meet its intended purpose.

The Guide is neither a standard nor a GMP. It is not intended to replace governing laws, codes, standards, or regulations that apply to facilities of this type.

This Guide is not intended to address any aspect of process/product validation.

1.3 Benefits

This Guide describes the fundamentals of process gas systems used within the GMP workplace environment and provides:

  • guidance on accepted industry practices related to gas systems
  • the life science engineering community with a common language and understanding of gas systems
  • a common resource for engineering information related to gas systems
  • quality professionals with an understanding of which system parameters are important to product quality and patient safety
  • information on how to avoid increasing facility installation and operational costs
  • regulatory professionals with a scientific risk-based approach to demonstrate compliance

The Guide aims to clarify gas system issues critical to product quality for the production of biopharmaceutical and pharmaceutical drug substances and drug products.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: What Is a "Process Gas"?

The key to the definition of a Process Gas is "direct contact": these gases come into direct physical contact with the product itself or with product-contacting surfaces. This is why they require GxP control.

The five core gases:

  • Nitrogen (N₂): inert blanketing, tank blanketing, oxygen-free transfer
  • Oxygen (O₂): cell culture sparging, oxidation reactions
  • Argon (Ar): inert protection (more inert than N₂)
  • Carbon Dioxide (CO₂): pH control, cell culture
  • Compressed Air: instrument control, pneumatic equipment, drying

Explicitly excluded: medical gases (such as breathing air) and steam, which are governed by other regulations.

Analogy: Food Contact in a Restaurant Kitchen

Imagine a restaurant: the utensils used in the kitchen (knives, cutting boards, containers) must meet food safety standards because they touch the food directly. The delivery vehicles outside the restaurant, although also important, do not need to meet the same food safety rules.

Process gases are like kitchen utensils: because they come into direct contact with the "product" (the drug), they must be under GMP control. Non-process gases (such as facility HVAC air) are like the delivery vehicles: they have other safety standards but fall outside the same GMP framework.

Key Point: The GxP Trigger

To decide whether a gas needs GxP control, ask one question: "Does it contact the product or a product-contacting surface?"

If the answer is yes, the gas is a process gas and requires design qualification, IQ/OQ/PQ, continuous monitoring and deviation handling procedures.

If the answer is no (for example, a plant utility gas), it falls under GEP (Good Engineering Practice), with lighter controls.

This guide is aligned with ICH Q9 (Quality Risk Management) and ASTM E2500-07 (verification framework), which is the mainstream direction in industry today.

Practice Questions

  2. If N₂ is used to blanket an empty tank, but the tank will only be filled with drug solution later, is the N₂ a process gas at that point?
1.5 Key Concepts: The Five Key Concepts

Original Text

This Guide aims to promote a science- and risk-based approach to provide an effective basis for the planning, design, and execution of gas system projects.

Five key concepts are applied throughout this Guide:

1. Product and Process Understanding

An understanding of the supported process is fundamental to determining system requirements. Product and process understanding is the basis for making science- and risk-based decisions to ensure that the system is fit for its intended use. Efforts to ensure fitness for intended use should focus on those aspects that are critical to patient safety and product quality. These critical aspects should be identified, specified, and verified.

Systems within the scope of this Guide support the manufacture and preservation of materials used in many applications including but not limited to clinical trials, commercial production of API and final products. For some manufacturing systems, process requirements depend on a thorough understanding of product characteristics, identification of Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) and related Critical Process Parameters (CPPs), which enable process requirements to be accurately defined.

Specification of requirements should be focused on critical aspects. The extent and detail of requirement specification should be commensurate with associated risk, complexity, and novelty of the system.

2. Life Cycle Approach within a Quality Management System

Adopting a system life cycle approach entails defining activities in a systematic way from system conception to retirement. This enables the optimization of the management control process. The life cycle should form a fundamental part of the organization's Quality Management System (QMS), which should be maintained up to date as new ways of working are developed.

As experience is gained in system use, the QMS should enable continuous process and system improvements based on periodic review and evaluation, operational and performance data, and root-cause analysis of failures. Identified improvements and corrective actions should follow change management.

3. Scaleable Life Cycle Activities

Life cycle activities should be scaled according to:

  • system impact on patient safety, product quality, and data integrity (risk assessment)
  • system complexity and novelty (architecture and categorization of system components)
  • outcome of supplier assessment (supplier capability)
  • Compliance and inspection risk

Potential business risk also may influence the scaling of life cycle activities. The strategy should be clearly defined in a plan and follow established and approved policies and procedures.

4. Science-Based Quality Risk Management

Quality Risk Management is a systematic process for the assessment, control, communication, and review of risks. Application of Quality Risk Management enables effort to be focused on critical aspects of a system in a controlled and justified manner.

Quality Risk Management should be based on clear process understanding and potential impact on patient safety, product quality. Qualitative or quantitative techniques may be used to identify and manage risks. Controls are developed to reduce risks to an acceptable level. Implemented controls are monitored during operation to ensure ongoing effectiveness.

5. Utilizing Supplier Involvement

Regulated organizations should seek to maximize supplier involvement throughout the system life cycle in order to utilize knowledge, experience, and documentation, subject to satisfactory supplier assessment.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Logical Structure of the Five Concepts

These five concepts are not an isolated list but a progressive design philosophy:

  • Concept 1: understand the product and process first, then define the system requirements
  • Concept 2: bring the system under life cycle management (not "built, therefore done")
  • Concept 3: decide how much verification work to do (scale) according to the level of risk
  • Concept 4: use QRM to focus on what really matters
  • Concept 5: draw on the gas supplier's expertise instead of working in isolation

This logic is fully aligned with ISPE Baseline Vol. 5 C&Q and ICH Q9.

Analogy: Building a New Home

Imagine you are building a new house (the gas system):

  • Concept 1: first confirm how many people will live in the house and what special needs they have (process understanding)
  • Concept 2: think about the maintenance plan for the next 20 years, not just opening day (life cycle)
  • Concept 3: the bedroom door needs a lock, but not bank-vault security (scale by risk)
  • Concept 4: assess which area is most likely to go wrong and concentrate resources there (QRM)
  • Concept 5: listen to the advice of the architect and the electricians and plumbers (supplier involvement)

Key Point: Extending CQA and CPP

Traditionally, CQA/CPP describe the attributes of a drug product. This guide extends the concept to gas systems: treat the gas as an "excipient", so that the output quality of the gas (such as purity and moisture content) can be defined as CQAs, and the process parameters that control gas quality are the CPPs.

This extension is very important: it lets the gas system fit directly into the QbD (Quality by Design) framework and gives the risk assessment a clear anchor.

Practical Application: Scaleable QRM in a CDMO

In a CDMO, the same gas line may serve different products for several clients at once. The concept of scaleability is especially important here:

  • N₂ line serving biologics (cell culture): residual O₂ is a CQA and must be tightly controlled
  • N₂ line serving solid oral dosage form packaging: purity requirements may be lower
  • Within the same CDMO, the depth of gas system verification can reasonably differ between facility areas

1.5.1 Key Terms and Terminology

Original Text

This Guide uses the term "User Requirements" according to the ISPE Good Practice Guide: Applied Risk Management for Commissioning and Qualification. Organizations may alternatively use the term User Requirement Specifications or Process User Requirements for the same document type.

This Guide uses Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) and Critical Process Parameters (CPPs) according to the definition given below (from ICH Q8), which states that "CQAs are generally associated with the drug substance, excipients, intermediates (in process materials) and drug product." In this Guide we have applied them to the system output (gas) considering it as an excipient; therefore having direct product contact or becoming part of the final product formulation.

Companies may adapt the terminology to suit their practices, with the actual CQAs and CPPs defined based on the application and considering company practices and standards.

Critical Aspects

Are typically functions, features, abilities, and performance characteristics necessary for the manufacturing process and systems to ensure consistent product quality and patient safety. They should be identified and documented based on scientific product and process understanding. (ASTM E2500-07)

Critical Process Parameter (CPP)

A process parameter whose variability has an impact on the critical quality attribute and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces the desired quality. (ICH Q8 (R2) II 4.0)

Critical Quality Attribute (CQA)

A physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality. Note: CQAs are generally associated with the drug substance, excipients, intermediates (in process materials) and drug product. (ICH Q8 (R2) II 2.2)

GxP Compliance

Meeting all applicable pharmaceutical and associated life-science regulatory requirements. (Note: as the Guide is focused on production facilities Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) is also used.)

Quality Management System (QMS)

Management system to direct and control an organization with regard to quality (e.g., ISO). (This is equivalent to Quality System as defined in ICH Q9.)

Subject Matter Expert (SME)

Those individuals with specific expertise in a particular area or field. SME responsibilities include planning and defining verification strategies, defining acceptance criteria, selection of appropriate test methods, execution of verification tests, and reviewing results. (ASTM E2500-07)

System Owner

The person ultimately responsible for the system performance. This person may be the head of the functional unit or department using that system, or maintaining the system although the role should be based on specific knowledge of the process rather than position in the organization. The system owner is responsible for ensuring that the system and its operation is in compliance and fit for intended use with applicable Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) throughout its useful life.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point: The Key Principle for the System Owner

The guide sets out a very important definition for the System Owner: "The role should be based on subject matter expertise in the process, not on seniority in the organization."

This means that an engineer who truly understands the compressed air system is better suited to be System Owner than a nominal department head. In practice, a common problem in CDMOs is a System Owner who is named but does not know the system, and such an arrangement creates serious risk in an audit.

Analogy: Gas as an Excipient

Think of N₂ as the water for injection (WFI) in a liquid formulation: WFI is an excipient, and its quality directly affects the final product. When N₂ blankets the drug solution in a bioreactor, it plays a similar role: its purity (O₂ content, hydrocarbon content) can directly affect the product's quality attributes.

This is why ISPE extends the CQA/CPP concepts to gases: to give engineers and QA a common language and framework.

  1. In your CDMO, who should be the "System Owner" of the compressed air system? Engineering? Operations? QA? Explain your reasons.
  2. Is the hydrocarbon content of N₂ a CQA in cell culture applications? If the client's product is a small-molecule API rather than a biologic, would the answer be different?

ISPE

2.1 Introduction

This section describes the development of the User Requirements for a process gas system and provides guidance on how to determine the system Critical Aspects, Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) and Critical Process Parameters (CPPs) within the design space of the process serviced.

This chapter follows the principles of the science- and risk-based approaches described in ICH Q9 and ASTM E2500-07. The application of risk management and testing is extensively described within the ISPE Guide: Science and Risk-Based Approach for the Delivery of Facilities, Systems, and Equipment, which should be referenced in conjunction with this Guide.

The User Requirements Specification (URS) is a foundation document which assists in the use of a Quality by Design (QbD) approach and considers the CQAs, CPPs, and critical aspects. This process allows the designer the freedom to optimize the design during the design and implementation of a new gas system or revisions to existing systems, allowing the project effort to be focused.

For existing systems that are being modified, existing User Requirements will need modification to suit the proposed change, or a user requirement for the change can be produced.

2.2 Process Requirements

Both existing and potential process requirements should be understood before defining user requirements.

The user requirements should describe what the system is intended to provide and how it may be used, i.e., the requirements of the product and process it serves. Examples of requirements listed in this section include:

  • intended product per system (e.g., single versus multi-product functionality)
  • process design constraints
  • cleaning/sanitization plans per system
  • material handling and integration within the system
  • intended functionality of system components

This Guide bases requirements on product and process needs and not on available equipment specifications or capabilities.

"Example: The dewpoint requirements for process air should be based on needs for processing; non-condensing process air in the coldest processing environment or on adiabatic cooling on expansion, uses of process air in a process drying step, etc. In this example, -40°C should not automatically be chosen as a CPP simply based on the availability of equipment and its capability to produce process air at this temperature."

Requirements related to pressure and/or flow:

  • gas required to transfer product
  • Clean in Place (CIP) air for assisting system drainage/drying requirements (typically a business attribute unless pressure and/or flow are required to drive a reaction)
  • system instrumentation and alarms (quality alarm vs. business alarm — e.g., high moisture content = quality alarm; high compressor oil temperature = business issue)

Where supply pressure (and the related flow) is critical for the equipment fed from the gas system it is common industry practice to provide a low pressure alarm on the equipment fed — this is considered the most robust approach, as it is impractical to monitor the pressure in an entire distribution system.

Requirements related to hydrocarbons (oil content):

  • hydrocarbons may have a toxic impact in cell culture processes
  • hydrocarbons in oxygen lines may pose a safety risk (ASTM G88-05)
  • A typical system requirement is ≤ 25 ppm or "non-detectable."

Requirements related to particulate or microbial:

  • Typically the specification provided is the same as the "at rest" environmental conditions of the room into which the gas is to be introduced.

Requirements related to moisture:

  • based on potential needs for adiabatic expansion or dewpoint of exposed areas
  • The specification for dewpoint should not automatically require -40°C unless the process requires it
  • to minimize the potential for promoting corrosion

Requirements related to quality and purity:

  • pharmacopeial requirements (e.g., USP, EP, JP)
  • Purity requirements should be driven by product and process needs (as defined in regulatory filings)
  • In most cases medical grade gases are not required. Medical gases are only for direct use on people or animals (i.e., "breathing air")

Other requirements:

  • applicable codes and standards for safety
  • traceability for measuring instruments to National Standards
  • relevant organizational standards

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The URS Is the Starting Point of Design, Not the End

In gas system design, the User Requirements Specification (URS) plays the role of a "constitution":

  • It defines "what the system needs to do" (the What), not "how to do it" (the How)
  • It leaves the designer room to optimize and does not prescribe the solution in advance
  • It is the starting point for the later DS (Design Specification), i.e. URS → DS → CS
  • When an existing system is modified, the URS also needs to be updated accordingly

The guide stresses in particular that the URS should be based on product and process requirements, not on equipment capability. This is the core expression of the QbD spirit.

3.1.1 Compressed Gases

Gases under pressure are commonly referred to as compressed gases. A compressed gas means: A gas or mixture of gases having, in a container, an absolute pressure exceeding 40 psi at 70°F (21.1°C); or a gas or mixture of gases having, in a container, an absolute pressure exceeding 104 psi at 130°F (54.4°C) regardless of the pressure at 70°F (21.1°C); or a liquid having a vapor pressure exceeding 40 psi at 100°F (37.8°C) as determined by ASTM D-323-72.

Compressed gases may be divided into two categories depending upon their inherent physical state under pressure and their range of boiling points:

  • non-liquefied gases
  • liquefied gases

Non-Liquefied Gases
Non-liquefied gases are elements, or a combination of elements, that have boiling points lower than -130°F (-90°C). Examples include nitrogen, oxygen, and argon. When specific elements become liquefied at extremely low temperatures they are referred to as cryogenic liquids.

Liquefied Gases
Liquefied gases are those elements or compounds that become liquid in their containers at ambient temperatures and at specified pressures from 25 psig (172 kPa) to 1500 psig (10,340 kPa). These gases have boiling points in the range of -130°F (-90°C) to 68°F (20°C).

Carbon dioxide is an example of a liquefied gas that also has unique physical characteristics in that it may exist as a gas, liquid, or solid simultaneously at its triple point temperature of -69.9°F (-56.6°C) and at a pressure of 60.4 psig (416 kPa).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Compressed gas: Any gas in a container with an absolute pressure exceeding 40 psi at 21°C is a compressed gas. This definition determines the category of storage, transport and regulatory requirements.

Non-liquefied gas vs liquefied gas:

  • Non-liquefied gases (e.g., N₂, O₂, Ar) have very low boiling points (below -90°C) and remain gaseous in containers at ambient temperature.
  • Liquefied gases (e.g., CO₂, NH₃) have boiling points close to ambient temperature and exist as liquids in pressurized containers.

Cryogenic liquid: A non-liquefied gas that has been liquefied at extremely low temperature, such as liquid nitrogen (LN₂) and liquid oxygen (LOX). This is the main purchasing form for plants that use gas in large quantities.

Analogy

Think of compressed gases as bottled drinks. Liquefied gases are like mineral water (a liquid in the bottle that evaporates only when opened); non-liquefied gases are like baking powder (inherently gaseous, forced into a cylinder at high pressure). CO₂ is special: like dry ice, it can exist as solid, liquid and gas at the same time (the triple point), and sublimes directly when released.

Key Notes

The triple point of CO₂ (-56.6°C, 416 kPa) is very important in pharmaceutical supercritical fluid extraction (SFE). Above its critical temperature (31°C) and critical pressure (7.38 MPa), CO₂ becomes a supercritical fluid that combines the diffusivity of a gas with the solvating power of a liquid, and it is widely used to extract and purify natural products.

3.2.2 Process Gas Sources and Manufacturing Considerations

Nitrogen, oxygen, and argon are naturally occurring elements. The earth's atmosphere is composed of approximately 78.09% nitrogen; 20.95% oxygen; and 0.93% argon by volume at sea level. Oxygen supports combustion. Nitrogen and argon are considered to be chemically inert.

Nitrogen, oxygen, and argon are most commonly manufactured by fractional distillation of liquefied air. A manufacturing facility that processes liquefied air is commonly referred to as an air-separation unit (ASU). Other manufacturing methods include electrolysis, molecular sieve separation, and chemical reactions.

The purity of the processed gases by the distillation method typically meets or exceeds minimum purities stated in the current published pharmacopoeial monographs, see Table 3.2.

*Nitrogen purity includes rare gases

Liquefied atmospheric gases may be delivered to users and cylinder packaging operations in specialized vacuum-insulated trailers. Cylinder packaging operations process the gases into returnable liquid containers and high pressure cylinders of various sizes.

Carbon dioxide occurs naturally in the atmosphere in small quantities, typically between 150 to 300 ppm. Carbon dioxide feedstock is pre-purified from a variety of sources, including chemical refining processes, ammonia production, ethyl alcohol production, fermentation processes, and coal-powered electrical plant combustion.

Carbon dioxide purity levels are typically greater than 99.5% and meet or exceed those required by the current pharmacopoeial monographs. Liquid carbon dioxide is normally delivered in specialized insulated trailers.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Air-Separation Unit (ASU): The main industrial method of producing nitrogen and oxygen. Air is liquefied and fractionally distilled using differences in boiling point (N₂: -195.8°C vs O₂: -183.0°C), yielding high-purity N₂, O₂ and Ar at the same time.

Quality code: Gas purity is often expressed as a code; for example, "4.8" means 99.998% (the digit before the decimal point is the number of nines, and the digit after it is the final digit). When purchasing, always confirm that the purity grade meets the process requirement.

Special source of CO₂: CO₂ is mostly a by-product of chemical production (e.g., ammonia plant off-gas), so its impurity profile differs from that of air-separation products and its CoA needs particular scrutiny at purchase.

Key Notes

Note the data in Table 3.2: nitrogen from typical cryogenic distillation has a purity of 99.998%, far above the 99.0% (USP) or 99.5% (EP) required by the pharmacopoeias. This means commercially supplied gas usually exceeds the compendial specification, but a CDMO still needs to specify the purity grade explicitly in the URS (user requirements specification) and obtain a CoA or CoC as the basis for releasing each batch.

Because CO₂ comes from many sources (fermentation, combustion, chemical processes), its trace impurities (e.g., benzene, HCN) need particular control, especially when it is used for pH control in bioprocesses.

Analogy

An air-separation unit is like an "air refinery": it takes the mixed soup that is air and, through precise temperature control, separates the components layer by layer according to boiling point, much as a distillation column separates alcohol from water. The purest N₂ is drawn from the coldest top of the column, O₂ from a slightly warmer position, and Ar sits in between.

3.3 Process Gases: Typical Applications

Original Text ISPE

The biopharmaceutical and life science industries use compressed gases in a wide range of applications including:

  • pneumatic systems
  • chemical synthesis
  • controlled atmospheres and blanketing
  • cell cultivation
  • chilling/freezing
  • material extraction

Nitrogen is most commonly used as an inert gas for the conditioning of pharmaceutical products in storage and transportation. In specific applications, argon or carbon dioxide may be used instead of nitrogen. These gases (nitrogen, argon, and carbon dioxide) are also used during the manufacture of APIs and in the production of finished pharmaceutical products.

Gases such as oxygen, compressed air, and carbon dioxide are often used for cultivation of mammalian cells and for fermentation.

In fermentation and cell culturing processes, to improve productivity, cell growth intensity and viability, modern aeration concepts with enriched oxygen up to 100% in the inlet air may be applied. The technical implementation is usually done by mixing of gaseous oxygen and air from the usual pressure line by means of special devices, followed by sterilizing gas filtration in front of the bioreactor.

Filtration of oxygen is a significant safety concern: it leads to accelerated oxidation and corrosion of plastics as well as spontaneous combustion. Because thinner filter components such as non-woven support and drainage layers are much more vulnerable to oxidation, it is recommended to use filters that contain more oxidation resistant materials (e.g., polyaramide nonwoven layers) instead of the relatively fast degradable and flammable polypropylene non-wovens found in typical air sterilizing PTFE membrane filters.

Beside degradation effects, oxygen flow builds up static charges on filter media surfaces that can lead to spontaneous discharging with the risk of ignition of degraded organic traces and flammable filter components, followed by combustion and fire generation inside pipes and filter housings.

Blanketing with an inert gas, such as nitrogen, is commonly used in chemical processes, primarily to minimize the risk of fire, dust explosions, and other explosions. It is also used to prevent undesired reactions with atmospheric oxygen. Blanketing is a method for constantly maintaining a protective layer of gas on top of APIs or pharmaceutical solution. The inert gas replaces humid air or explosive vapor of solvents in the headspace of contained batches.

Nitrogen is also used as a blanket in bio-reactors for oxygen replacement for anaerobic fermentation processes. On Water for Injection (WFI) or Purified Water, nitrogen blanketing may be used to reduce changes in conductivity associated with dissolved carbon dioxide.

Oxygen is commonly used to enrich air and to enhance oxidation. In biological processes, it can also be used to increase the rate of the process, e.g., fermentation or in the cultivation of mammalian cells.

Carbon dioxide is used with incubators, in the production of biopharmaceuticals and in the cultivation of mammalian cells.

The storage of biological materials at very low temperatures is known as cryo-preservation. Cryo-preservation is used to ensure the survival of living cells and organisms. The cells can be stored either submerged in liquid nitrogen or in contact with the cold vapor phase of nitrogen.

Supercritical Fluid Extraction (SFE) refers to a process in which a Supercritical Fluid (SF) is used as a solvent. The most widely used SF gas is carbon dioxide (CO₂).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Blanketing/Inerting: Maintaining a layer of inert gas above a liquid surface to prevent oxidation, explosion and moisture ingress. Commonly applied to API storage tanks and the headspace of WFI storage tanks.

Purging: Displacing the air in a line or vessel with inert gas to ensure no residual oxygen remains; often used to pre-condition lines before filling.

Cryo-preservation: Cells are immersed in liquid nitrogen (-196°C) or exposed to its cold vapor phase to maintain long-term viability; this is a key operation for CGT (cell and gene therapy) products.

Supercritical fluid extraction (SFE): Above its critical point (31°C, 73.8 bar), CO₂ has both gas-like and liquid-like properties, with strong solvating power and no organic solvent residue, making it a green technology for pharmaceutical extraction and purification.

Key Notes: Special Risk in Oxygen Filtration

This is a safety blind spot that many engineers overlook: when the oxygen concentration rises to 100%, standard PTFE + polypropylene (PP) nonwoven filter media face the following:

  • Accelerated oxidative degradation: PP fibers become brittle quickly in pure oxygen
  • Static charge accumulation: Friction from the gas flow generates static electricity, which may ignite organic residues
  • Spontaneous ignition risk: A fire may start inside the filter

Therefore, high-concentration oxygen filtration must use special filter media containing polyaramide nonwoven, and may require special approval from the regulatory authority.

Analogy

Nitrogen blanketing is like vacuum-sealing a food storage bag and then filling it with inert gas; supermarket "modified atmosphere packaging" works on exactly this principle to stop food oxidizing and spoiling. API storage tanks in pharmaceutical plants are the same: N₂ replaces the headspace air to prevent degradation of the drug substance.

Cell cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen is like pressing a "pause button" on living cells: at -196°C metabolism almost stops, and the cells can regain viability after thawing. It is the lifeline of CGT products such as CAR-T and stem cells.

CDMO Application

Decision framework for selecting gas by operation:

  • Aseptic filling headspace inerting → N₂ (most common, low cost, inert)
  • Oxidation protection for highly sensitive products (e.g., proteins) → Ar (heavier than air, so it blankets better)
  • pH control in mammalian cell culture → CO₂ (reacts with the bicarbonate buffer to regulate pH)
  • Oxygenation to improve fermentation efficiency → O₂ (oxygen-enriched aeration, but special oxygen-compatible filters must be used)
  • Power for pneumatic equipment (pharmacopoeial grade not required) → Compressed air (ISO 8573 compliance is sufficient)

3.4 Applicable Regulations, Standards, and Codes

Original Text ISPE

Gas suppliers can describe the quality of gases as either "industrial" or "pharmaceutical." Gas should be purchased according to an agreed specification and delivered with a Certificate of Conformity (CoC) or a Certificate of Analysis (CoA), depending on the criticality of the gas.

Gas production normally follows Good Engineering Practices (GEPs). Gas is not a medicinal product and does not need to be produced following Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs).

3.4.1 Process Aids

ICH Q7 "GMP Guide for Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients," Chapter 20 Glossary, defines process aids as materials, excluding solvents, used as an aid in the manufacture of an intermediate or API that do not themselves participate in a chemical or biological reaction (e.g., filter aid, activated carbon, etc).

ICH Q7, Chapter 7 (Materials Management) states:

"Materials should be purchased against an agreed specification, from a supplier or suppliers approved by the quality unit(s)."
"If the supplier of a critical material is not the manufacturer of that material, the name and address of that manufacturer should be known by the intermediate and/or API manufacturer."

When used in API production, inert process gases (e.g., nitrogen) would typically fall into the category of "process aid" rather than raw material. When used in production of finished pharmaceuticals, nitrogen would typically be defined as an inactive ingredient.

ICH Q7 (chapter 7.3) also suggests that at least one test to verify the identity of each batch of material should be conducted, with the exception of processing aids where a supplier's Certificate of Analysis can be used in place of performing other tests, provided that the manufacturer has a system in place to evaluate suppliers.

3.4.2 Components

As defined in US FDA Title 21 CFR Part 210.3, a component means any ingredient intended for use in the manufacture of a drug product, including those that may not appear in such drug product; and an inactive ingredient means any component other than an active ingredient.

Because of the special containers and characteristics, gases can be difficult to analyze on site, and the sec. 211.84 (d)(2) suggests that in lieu of testing for identity and conformity, a report of analysis may be accepted from the supplier of a component, provided that at least one specific identity test is conducted on such component by the manufacturer, and provided that the manufacturer establishes the reliability of the supplier's analyses through appropriate validation of the supplier's test results at appropriate intervals.

3.4.3 Gas Related Regulations

The FDA's quality system-based inspections have a risk-management approach and focus on operating systems. The Compliance Program Guidance Manual 7356.002 says that the materials system includes measures and activities to control finished products, components, including water or gases, that are incorporated into the product. Areas to be covered during inspection include:

  • identification and inventory of components
  • at least one specific identity test conducted on each lot of each component
  • testing or validation of the supplier's test results for components
  • water and process gas supply, design, maintenance, validation, and operation

The PIC/S Aide-Memoire — Inspection of Utilities (PI 009-3) provides guidance for GMP inspectors including useful checklists for HVAC, water, steam, and gas systems.

Other applicable regulations, standards, and codes include:

  • USP/NF – Gas Monographs: Medical Air, Carbon Dioxide, Nitrogen, Oxygen

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Difference between CoC and CoA:

  • CoA (certificate of analysis): Contains actual test data (e.g., purity, dew point, oil content); suitable for high-criticality gases (in direct contact with the product).
  • CoC (certificate of conformance): The supplier declares conformance to the agreed specification, without necessarily including measured data; suitable for low-criticality gases (e.g., air for pneumatic equipment).

Processing aid vs inactive ingredient vs component:

  • N₂ used for inerting in API manufacture → defined by ICH Q7 as a processing aid
  • N₂ used in the headspace of the final dosage form → defined by 21 CFR as a component/inactive ingredient
  • The legal classification differs, and so do the GMP control requirements!

Key Notes: Inspection Focus

When FDA investigators inspect gas systems under Compliance Program 7356.002, they pay particular attention to:

  • Whether every batch of gas has at least one identity test
  • Whether the reliability of the supplier's CoA is verified periodically (supplier qualification)
  • Whether the gas distribution system has design documents, maintenance records and validation documents
  • If a CoA is used in place of in-house testing, whether a complete supplier evaluation system is in place

All of these should be reflected in the CDMO's supplier management procedure (SOP) and component control procedure.

Analogy

The regulatory classification of gases is like managing restaurant supplies. A processing aid (N₂ in API manufacture) is like the cleaning agent used in the kitchen: it does not go into the food, but it must be properly certified. A component/inactive ingredient (N₂ in the filling headspace) is like the protective gas used to package food: it goes directly into the final product, so it must meet food-grade specifications and come with a detailed analytical report.

3.5 Safe Handling and Storage of Gases

Original Text ISPE

3.5.1 Source Gas Distribution and Storage

Argon, nitrogen, and oxygen are typically supplied to the customer by one or more of the following means:

  • on-site liquid storage vessel
  • transportable liquid containers
  • high-pressure cylinders and manifolds
  • gas pipeline
  • gas generation systems

Storage Vessels
Storage vessels for liquid argon, nitrogen, and oxygen are commercially available in capacities ranging from 350 to 13,000 US gallons (1,325 to 49,210 liters). The storage vessels may be vertical, spherical, or horizontal. Liquid storage vessels have three basic components:

  1. inner pressure vessel
  2. outer vessel
  3. insulation

Inner Pressure Vessel
Usually made of stainless steel or other materials with favorable strength at cryogenic temperatures. Typically designed to sustain a maximum allowable working pressure of 250 psig (1,724 kPa). Equipped with dual pressure relief valves and rupture disks to protect against over-pressurization.

Rupture disks are thin metal membranes designed to rupture at a prescribed pressure above the pressure relief valve setting. Unlike pressure relief valves, rupture disks, once activated, do not close — the pressure and entire contents of the tank may be released.

Outer Vessel
Made of carbon steel or stainless steel. Retains the insulation and a vacuum around the inner pressure vessel. Equipped with a vacuum rupture disk to protect the outer vessel from pressurized leaks into the insulation space.

Insulation
The space between the inner and outer vessel contains several inches of insulating material maintained in a vacuum to reduce heat transfer and reduce the boil-off of stored liquid.

Vaporizing Systems
Most user applications use gases in their gaseous state. The stored liquid is converted to gas through the use of a vaporizing system, which may utilize heat from ambient air, hot water, or steam.

Frost or ice will frequently build up on the fins of the ambient air vaporizer system during gas use. Typically, icing should not exceed three-quarters of the vaporizer's length. Excessive ice build-up can reduce the vaporizer efficiency.

Carbon Dioxide Storage Tank Installations
Carbon dioxide is normally maintained in its liquid phase by an integral refrigeration system and pressure control system. Bulk carbon dioxide storage tanks are available in capacities up to approximately 50 tons.

Typically, gas suppliers are responsible for the bulk gas storage tanks and related controls. Customer responsibility normally begins after the vaporizing system and at the outlet of the gas supply valve or regulator.

3.5.2 Transportable Liquid Containers

Small quantities of liquid gases may be supplied in portable or transportable containers. Liquid containers are usually double-walled and vacuum insulated, and normally set to a specified gas service pressure with safety relief devices.

General rules for handling and storing liquid containers include:

  • Rough handling or subjecting containers to shocks, drops, or impact should be avoided.
  • Containers should be moved using carts and equipment specifically designed for the purpose.
  • Containers should be kept vertical and upright.
  • Containers should be stored in a well-ventilated area, preferably out-of-doors.
  • Regulators, hoses, and equipment should have been designed to withstand the gas service pressures.
  • Containers and connections should be properly labeled and tagged.
  • Gas/liquid supply valves should be opened slowly.
  • During periods of heavy gas use, the outer surface of the container may frost. Reduce withdrawal rate; do not apply heat to the container.
  • Connections should not be fitted forcibly.
  • Adaptors should not be used to circumvent gas connection standards.
  • Containers should not be lifted by protection rings or top-works.

3.5.3 Transportable Gas Cylinders

Small quantities of compressed gas or gas mixtures may be supplied in high pressure cylinders in a variety of sizes up to 330 ft³ (10 m³). Cylinders may be of steel or aluminum construction and should be equipped with a valve, gas-specific outlet connection, and a pressure relief device. Once activated, pressure relief devices do not reset and the cylinder contents will be lost.

High pressure cylinder handling and storage recommendations include:

  • Reference should be made to Material Safety Data Sheets (MSDSs) for important gas product safety information.
  • Cylinders should be moved using handcarts and equipment specifically designed for the purpose.
  • Cylinders should be protected from being knocked or damaged.
  • Cylinders should be secured in place when in use.
  • Cylinder caps should be kept in place when not in use.
  • "Full" cylinders should be segregated from "empty" cylinders.
  • Cylinders should be segregated by hazard class.
  • Flow/pressure regulating devices should be used to withdraw cylinder contents.
  • Cylinder valves should be closed tightly when a cylinder is not in use.
  • Cylinder markings, labels, and tags should be kept complete and unmodified.
  • Cylinders should not be lifted by valves or protection caps.
  • Cylinders should not be used as rollers for moving equipment.
  • Cylinders should not be stored near heaters, open flames, or sparks.
  • Cylinders should not be placed where they may become part of an electric circuit.
  • When welding, an arc should not be struck against a cylinder.

3.5.4 Gas Safety Considerations

Oxygen-Deficient Atmospheres
The primary hazard of the inert gases argon, nitrogen, and carbon dioxide is their ability to displace oxygen in the air and create an oxygen-deficient environment. An oxygen-deficient atmosphere is defined as oxygen concentration in air of less than 19.5%.

An oxygen-deficient atmosphere is a serious physiological hazard. Depending on the degree of deficiency, effects on personnel can vary from physiological changes to illness or death by asphyxiation. Exposure can also cause sudden unconsciousness, preventing personnel from helping or protecting themselves.

Where the potential exists for an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, oxygen-level monitors should be installed to alert personnel before entering the controlled area.

Compression fittings and connections in gas distribution systems should be minimized to reduce potential sources of gas leaks. Pressure relief devices should be piped outside a facility to a point of safe discharge.

Oxygen-Enriched Atmospheres
An oxygen-enriched atmosphere is defined as oxygen concentration in air greater than 23.5%. An enriched oxygen atmosphere can cause materials that will burn in air to do so more vigorously. Hydrocarbons and oils may react violently and explosively when in contact with oxygen or oxygen-enriched atmospheres.

Liquid nitrogen and liquid argon storage vessels, and liquid piping systems, should have appropriate insulation. Without appropriate insulation, ambient air may condense on extremely cold surfaces forming liquid air. Liquid air can contain up to 50% oxygen; evaporation can increase the oxygen concentration to over 80%.

Cryogenic Liquid and Cold Temperature Hazards
The safe use of liquid argon, nitrogen, and oxygen requires knowledge of how materials change their behavior at cryogenic temperatures. Three common strength characteristics altered by low temperatures are:

  1. Ductility: the ability of a material to permanently change its shape or stretch without fracturing. Carbon steel loses ductility at low temperatures.
  2. Tensile Strength: the resistance of a material to tearing. The tensile strength of many metals increases at low temperatures.
  3. Impact Strength: the ability to resist impact without shattering. Rubber and plastic are reduced so much at low temperatures that they can shatter like glass.

Some stainless steels, copper, nickel alloys, and aluminum do not lose significant strength at cryogenic temperatures. Materials used in gaseous service may not be suitable for handling or storing the liquid phase.

Training and Safety Programs
General safety recommendations for handling liquid gas products include:

  • Liquid nitrogen and liquid argon should be handled only by personnel instructed in the properties of the material and in the proper procedures for handling it.
  • Piping in which liquid nitrogen or liquid argon could be trapped between two valves should be equipped with pressure relief valves piped to properly designed vents.
  • Liquid nitrogen or liquid argon should be stored in open and well-ventilated areas.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Basis for selecting the gas supply method:

  • On-site bulk liquid tank: First choice for high-volume, continuous operations; lowest cost, but requires on-site facilities and periodic refilling by the supplier.
  • Portable liquid container (Dewar): For medium-volume use; flexible to move, suitable for laboratories or multiple points of use.
  • High-pressure cylinder: For small-volume use and a wide variety of gases, but with high management cost (tracking, cylinder returns, inventory).
  • On-site gas generation (PSA/membrane separation): Suitable for large N₂ consumption; payback period about 2-3 years, and it eliminates supply chain risk.

Key Notes: Safe Design of Storage Tanks

The dual-protection design of cryogenic liquid tanks is essential:

  • Pressure relief valve: Opens automatically on overpressure and closes automatically once the pressure falls; reusable.
  • Rupture disk: Activates at a higher pressure as the last line of defense, but once it bursts the entire tank contents are released and it cannot be reset.

The difference between these two must be verified during FAT/SAT, and the vent line of the rupture disk must be routed to a safe outdoor location; pressure must never be released indoors.

Key Notes: The Lethal Danger of Oxygen-Deficient Atmospheres

N₂, Ar and CO₂ are all colorless, odorless inert gases, so the human body cannot sense their concentration! When the O₂ concentration in the environment is:

  • Below 19.5%: Defined by regulation as oxygen-deficient; an alarm is required
  • 16%: Dizziness, impaired judgment
  • 12%: Loss of consciousness
  • Below 6%: Death within minutes

What makes this especially dangerous is that a person may collapse suddenly without any warning and be unable to self-rescue. Oxygen monitoring during confined space entry is the safety baseline of a GMP facility.

Analogy

The three-layer structure of a cryogenic tank (inner vessel + vacuum layer + outer shell) is like a giant "stainless steel thermos": the vacuum insulation layer prevents heat conduction, so -196°C liquid nitrogen can be stored for a long time in an ambient environment. If the vacuum layer is lost (outer vessel leak), warm outside air transfers heat in rapidly, causing fast evaporation of the liquid (boil-off) and a sharp pressure rise, which is exactly what the rupture disk is designed for.

When LOX leaks and contacts cold piping, moisture in the air condenses into "liquid air" in which the O₂ concentration can reach 50-80%, which creates an oxygen-enriched, explosive environment around the piping and is extremely dangerous.

CDMO Application: Gas Source Decision

Key factors a CDMO considers when selecting a gas supply method:

  • Consumption: Daily use < 50 Nm³ → cylinders; 50-500 Nm³ → liquid containers; > 500 Nm³ → bulk liquid tank or on-site generation
  • Purity requirement: Product contact → pharmacopoeial grade with CoA; pneumatic equipment → ISO 8573 industrial grade
  • Supply continuity: Production line running 24/7 → bulk liquid tank + automatic refill monitoring system
  • Regulatory compliance: Gas distribution systems in GMP areas require DQ/IQ/OQ/PQ validation documents

Original Text ISPE

3.6.1 Gas Specifications

Source gas specifications should be user-defined. Where a gas is used as an excipient, process aid, or as a component in drug manufacturing process, users should evaluate the potential impact the gas might have on the finished product. To evaluate the impact, a risk assessment is recommended.

Mitigating risk may be accomplished through one or more of the following actions:

  • source gas specifications
  • detailed work instructions
  • training
  • system engineering controls
  • equipment maintenance programs
  • change-control procedures

There are financial benefits gained from using one of the "standard" specifications from a supplier — the requirements should be evaluated against these.

Source Gas Purity
Fractional distillation and other source gas manufacturing processes produce high purity liquefied gas products. End-users are encouraged to consider the following in developing their gas product specifications.

Purity classification is usually expressed as a quality code (e.g., 4.8). In the quality code notation, the number before the point represents the number of nines and the last number indicates the last decimal (i.e., 4.8 = 99.998%, and 6.0 = 99.9999%).

There are two methods for determining the purity of a gas:

  1. direct measurement using specifically designed analytical instruments
  2. measuring minor components of interest and subtracting from 100%

Both the measurement uncertainty characteristics of instruments and the analytical method used can affect the accuracy of purity measurements. Users should identify minor components of concern in a source gas specification. Minor components of concern should be determined by the specific gas application and relevant CQAs (identified through a process risk assessment).

Water Content in Source Gases
Water content in the source gases is usually very low and inherent to the source gas manufacturing processes.

Purging of gas delivery hoses during product transfer at user sites should maintain low-level moisture specifications. New or re-commissioned gas distribution systems should be thoroughly dried and their installation qualified. Prior to utilizing the gas from such systems, users should verify acceptable moisture content levels.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Meaning of dew point: The dew point is the temperature at which water vapor in the gas begins to condense. The lower the dew point, the drier the gas. A dew point of -65°C indicates extremely low moisture content, ensuring that the gas does not carry moisture into the piping or product-contact surfaces.

Key parameters of a gas specification in the URS (per ISO 8573):

  • Solid particles: Class 1-9 by particle size
  • Water (dew point): Class 1 (-70°C) to Class 9 (+10°C)
  • Oil content: Class 0 (<0.01 mg/m³) to Class 4 (>5 mg/m³)

Minor components of concern: Each process has different impurities of concern: CO affects cell viability; THC affects product safety; H₂O affects stability; O₂ (in N₂) affects oxidation-sensitive products.

Framework for Writing a URS Gas Specification

Process gas URS specification items (using N₂ for aseptic filling as an example):

1. Purity: ≥ 99.998% (Grade 4.8)
2. Dew point: ≤ -65°C (at delivery point)
3. Oil content: ≤ 0.01 mg/m³ (ISO 8573 Class 0)
4. Particulates: ISO 8573 Class 1
   (≥ 0.1 μm → ≤ 20,000 /m³)
5. O₂ residual: ≤ 10 ppm (for oxidation-sensitive products)
6. CO residual: ≤ 1 ppm
7. Microorganisms: ≤ 1 CFU/m³ (optional, depending on contact risk)

Documentation requirements:
- CoA for every batch (including measured values)
- Periodic supplier audit records
- Distribution system validation documents (IQ/OQ/PQ)

Key Notes: Risk Assessment Drives the Specification

A gas specification should not be "one size fits all"; it must be tailored based on the results of a risk assessment. Factors to consider:

  • Degree of contact: Direct product contact vs indirect contact vs no contact (utility systems)
  • Product CQAs: Oxidation sensitivity, risk of particulate contamination, risk of microbial contamination
  • Type of operation: Headspace inerting (trace O₂ allowed) vs purging vs pneumatic drive (no contact)
  • Regulatory requirements: USP/EP pharmacopoeial specifications are the minimum threshold, and a CQA-driven specification may be stricter

ISPE explicitly recommends: using the supplier's "standard specifications" can reduce cost, but it must be evaluated whether they meet the user's CQA needs.

Analogy

Setting a gas specification is like choosing the water source for infant formula. Ordinary tap water (industrial-grade gas) can wash a car, mineral water (pharmacopoeial grade) can be drunk, but infant formula needs sterile water that meets a specific purity specification (a URS-tailored specification). Over-specifying wastes money, while under-specifying creates a quality risk; the balance point is decided by risk assessment.

A gas distribution system kept under positive pressure is like the vascular system: blood (gas) flows under positive pressure so that outside contaminants (air) cannot seep back in. Once the pressure is lost (a line leak), contamination can get in.

4.1 Design of Generation of Compressed Air Systems

Original Text

For process applications, compressed air systems should include:

  • a supply side (generation): including compressors and air treatment
  • a demand side: including distribution and storage systems, connecting the gas to the process application

The supply side and the demand side of compressed air systems should be integrated and should be considered as a complete system to maximize overall efficiency and ensure the required air quality is delivered at the specified flow rates and pressures required.

This section of the Guide focuses on the supply (generation) side of the compressed air system. The supply side of the system should continuously provide clean, dry air at the correct pressure, quality, and flow.

The design of the supply side of compressed air systems should consider:

  • compressors
  • filtration
  • air receivers
  • dryers
  • system control

Possible improvements to a system's operating efficiency should also be detailed, to help improve sustainability and to reduce operating costs.

A single generation system may be used for both instrument (or facility) air and process air; however, additional criteria may need to be met in order to achieve quality requirements for process air. Entire systems can be designed to meet the additional criteria for process air. Alternatively, further treatment to meet these additional criteria can be given only to air in a process branch.

4.1.1 Arrangement of Supply Side Components

Air discharged from a compressor at pressure will usually be saturated and contain particulate (usually with a particle size of under 10 µm, i.e., at sizes below the typical size limit of an inlet air filter). If the compressor is a lubricated compressor, the discharge air can also contain oil as a vapor or particulate. Typically, oil content in the discharge of a lubricated compressor is around 3 ppm. Oil removal filters (coalescing type) should be included in the sequence if lubricated compressors are used.

When using a desiccant type dryer, the typical arrangement of equipment is:

  1. compressor
  2. general purpose filter (If placed after a lubricated compressor, this filter will need coalescing properties.)
  3. optional "wet" receiver for control storage (The wet receiver provides purge air when a desiccant tower regenerates and prevents an artificial pressure drop in the compressor.)
  4. desiccant dryer
  5. particulate filter (Desiccant beds can move during use, generating dust which can escape from the desiccant tower.)
  6. dry air receiver (This receiver is responsible for controlling the system pressure during periods where there is a high demand from a distribution system. This receiver provides "capacitance" or stored energy to control pressure fluctuations.)
  7. optional system pressure controller (This device is responsible for controlling the pressure in a system and can optimize the capacitance in a dry air receiver.)

Redundancy of filters or dryer with transfer valves may be necessary to allow maintenance to be performed safely while a system is kept operational.

"Monitoring differential pressure across a filter for pressure build-up or spikes may not be appropriate, as a collapsed or ruptured element could yield a very low to no pressure differential, and coalescing filters may not show an increase in differential pressure at the end of their useful life."

Filter effectiveness is dependent on maintenance and conditions. Higher temperatures reduce effective filtration limits. Manufacturers usually rate a filter's efficiency at 70°F (or 20°C). In addition, higher oil carryover will load a filter more rapidly.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Supply side: Includes the compressors and air treatment equipment, which "generate" clean, dry compressed air.

Demand side: Includes the piping and receivers, which deliver the air to the process point of use (POU).

Instrument air: Drives pneumatic valves and instruments; the quality requirements are relatively relaxed.

Process air: Contacts or is close to the product (for example blow-molding, mixing, autoclave actuation); the quality requirements are strict, and usually call for a lower dew point and tighter microbial control.

Analogy

Think of a compressed air system as the water supply of a cafe: the "supply side" is the waterworks plus the water purification equipment, and the "demand side" is the piping that runs from the plant to every tap. You can run the whole site on one purification setup, or you can fit extra filters only at the bar (the point with high quality needs). Pharmaceutical sites usually take the latter approach, adding extra treatment on the process branch to reduce overall investment.

Key Notes

Differential pressure monitoring must not be the only indicator of filter failure. A damaged or collapsed filter element can show a differential pressure close to zero, which is mistaken for "clear flow" while air is actually leaking around the element. A scheduled replacement program (preventive maintenance) is therefore more reliable than watching the gauge alone.

High oil loading and high temperature both accelerate element failure, so facilities staff should build these into a correction factor for the maintenance frequency.

4.2-4.3 Equipment Selection & Compressors

Original Text

4.2 Equipment Selection

Equipment selection should start with a review of user requirements. Typically, a specific volume flow rate or mass flow and pressure are required by a process.

4.3 Compressors

There are two basic types of compressors:

  1. positive-displacement
  2. dynamic

In positive-displacement type compressors, a given quantity of air is squeezed or mechanically reduced to create pressure. The volumetric flow rate is directly proportional to the size and speed of the compressor.

Dynamic compressors induce velocity using impellers and then convert the energy of the air motion into pressure using scrolls or diffusers.

4.3.1 Positive Displacement — Types

Positive displacement compressors include:

  • reciprocating or piston type compressors
  • oil free rotary scroll compressors
  • oil (or lubricant) injected rotary screw or rotary vane compressors
  • oil (or lubricant) free rotary screw compressors

4.3.2 Large Water Cooled Piston Type Compressors (50–500 hp / 37–400 kW)

Double-acting reciprocating compressors use both sides of the piston for air compression. These compressors may be single- or multi-stage, depending on discharge pressure and size. They can be made for pressures from 50 psig, but are primarily used for high pressure (600 psig/40 barg) applications, such as plastic blow molding. They may rarely be used for general process air pressures in pharmaceutical applications (i.e., 80 to 150 psig/5.5 to 10 barg). Large piston compressors usually require large foundations to minimize vibration and expensive wearable components, but remain among the most efficient compressors at approximately 19 to 20 bhp/100 cfm at 100 psig. Generally, piston type compressors have been replaced by rotary screw compressors.

4.3.3 Small Air Cooled Piston and Scroll Type Compressors (to 20 hp/15 kW)

The air cooled piston type compressors come in splash lubrication and pressure lubricated as well as oil-less designs. Scroll compressors are oil free by design, although their wearing parts may discharge some carbon fines, which can be removed with filtration. Laboratories and small pharmaceutical manufacturing may use a small air cooled piston type compressor or rotary scroll.

4.3.4 Rotary Screw or Rotary Vane – Lubricant or Oil Injected Compressors (5–600 hp)

The most common type of oil injected rotary compressor is the single stage helical-twin, screw-type (also known as rotary screw or helical-lobe). Rotary screw compressors usually have a lower initial cost, more compact size, and lower weight than large piston compressors. They are also considered relatively simple to maintain.

Oil injected rotary compressors typically use cleaner and longer life synthetic lubricants, including:

  • diesters
  • polyglycols
  • polyalphaolefins
  • polyol esters
  • silicon-based lubricants

The lubricant has three purposes: lubrication, sealing, and cooling. Discharge air normally contains 2 to 5 ppm of entrained lubricant; therefore, coalescing filters should be used. Other considerations include the cost of lubricant, separation elements, oil-water separation systems for condensate traps.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Positive displacement compressors: Mechanically "squeeze" a fixed volume of air; output flow is proportional to speed and pressure is stable. Types include reciprocating, screw, and rotary vane.

Dynamic compressors: Use a high-speed impeller to accelerate the gas, then convert the velocity energy into pressure energy through a diffuser; the typical example is the centrifugal compressor. Suited to high flow rates and inherently oil-free.

Analogy

A positive displacement compressor is like a syringe: you push the plunger and a fixed volume of air is compressed. A dynamic compressor is like an electric fan: it accelerates the air by rotation and then slows it down to raise the pressure. The syringe type gives stable pressure but its flow is limited by mechanical size; the fan type is especially suited to large gas volumes.

Key Notes: Risks of Lubricated Compressors

The outlet air of a lubricated screw compressor contains 2-5 ppm of lubricating oil. This is acceptable for general industrial use, but in a pharmaceutical plant it is an unacceptable source of contamination if the air is used in product-contact areas.

Even with a coalescing filter fitted, a residual risk of oil mist remains, and once the filter fails the oil goes straight into the process. This is why oil-free compressors have become the industry standard for pharmaceutical compressed air.

4.3.5-4.3.7 Oil-Free & Dynamic Compressors

Original Text

4.3.5 Lubricant-Free Rotary Screw Compressors (25–1250 hp / 18–900 kW)

"Lubricant free" refers to no lubricant in the compression area or passages. Lubricant may still be used to lubricate gearing, but it is kept separate from the air compression section by seals. Two types are available:

  1. dry
  2. water-injected

In the dry-type of lubricant-free rotary screw compressor, there is no injected fluid to remove the heat of compression. The lack of a sealing fluid also typically requires higher rotation speeds, timing gears, and a high temperature coating.

Single-stage lubricant-free rotary screw compressors can operate up to 50 psig. Two stages of compression may also be used, with an intercooler between the stages and an aftercooler after the second stage. Two-stage lubricant-free rotary screw compressors can achieve up to 150 psig.

In water-injected lubricant-free rotary screw compressors, similar timing gear construction is used, but water is injected into the compression chamber to act as a seal and to remove the heat of compression. This allows pressures in the 100 to 150 psig range to be achieved in a single stage. Typically, water lubricated compressors are below 100 kW. Consideration should be given to the potential risk of microbial contamination in water lubricated systems.

4.3.6 Dynamic Compressors

Dynamic compressors raise the pressure of air or gas by producing energy as air motion and converting it to pressure. Dynamic compressors include centrifugal and axial types and normally produce lubricant free air.

4.3.7 Centrifugal Air Compressors (200–20,000 hp / 150–15,000 kW)

Centrifugal air compressors may:

  • have two to four stages for pressures in the 100 to 150 psig range
  • range from around 300 to more than 100,000 cfm, but generally occur in the 1,200 to 5,000 cfm range

These compressors can provide lubricant-free air delivery, as there is no lubricant in the compression chambers. Lubrication for speed increasing gears and the hydrodynamic shaft bearings is separated from the compression chambers using shaft seals. Bearings have no moving parts as the pinion (high speed shaft) rides on a thin film of oil; therefore, built in shaft vibration monitoring is normally used to record operational trends and to protect the equipment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dry oil-free screw: The compression chamber contains no liquid at all; precision coatings and high-speed timing gears keep the rotors synchronized, and heat is managed by multi-stage coolers. Expensive, but gives the purest air quality.

Water-injected: Water replaces lubricating oil as the sealing and cooling medium. The benefit is that a higher pressure can be reached in a single stage, but water quality and microbial risk must be managed.

Centrifugal: Suited to very high flow rates and inherently oil-free (the bearing oil is isolated from the compression chamber); suitable for large plants that need a large volume of process compressed air.

Key Notes: Microbial Risk of Water-Injected Compressors

A water-injected compressor is "oil-free", but the water itself is a growth medium for microorganisms. If the water circulation system is not tightly controlled, the compressed air may become microbially contaminated, which is a serious CQA risk for a pharmaceutical plant.

Required: regular replacement of the circulating water, microbial monitoring, and making sure the downstream filter design covers this risk.

Analogy

A centrifugal compressor is like a plant's large boiler house: big, expensive, and once installed it supplies a large amount of energy steadily. It suits large pharmaceutical plants or CDMO campuses with several buildings needing high-flow air supply. A small multi-product CDMO is better served by the more flexible oil-free screw type, which can be started and stopped on demand.

Practical Application

CDMO scenario: If your site supplies several aseptic filling lines at once (isolator pressurization, stopper vibratory bowls, pneumatic valves), the compressed air falls into the product-contact auxiliary gas category. An oil-free dry screw compressor plus terminal filtration downstream is the combination that best meets current regulatory expectations, and it forms part of the contamination control strategy (CCS) emphasized in EU GMP Annex 1 (2022).

4.4 Compressed Air System Controls

Original Text

Compressed air system controls usually have two purposes:

  1. to meet the requirements of the demand side of a compressed air system
  2. to monitor selected parameters, providing alarms and interlocks to protect the equipment from premature failure

Controls can affect efficiency, life, and reliability of compressed air systems.

The simplest system is to use inlet valve throttling to regulate the compressor's capacity. Throttling the intake valve all the way to 0% capacity will still have system power consumption circa 70% of the full load power. Also, compressor operation can become unstable; typically the operating range is 50 to 60% of the maximum capacity.

The system discharge can be throttled – this is not an efficient process and is rarely used.

System speed control may be used. This approach works for all compressor types, and improves efficiency. The operating range will vary depending on the system type; the cost of the variable speed drive can be significant for larger units.

The simplest form of control is stop start; improved efficiency can be from unloading the compressor, letting it idle, usually with a time limit on the idle run time.

More complex control systems can monitor system pressure and vary the compressor speed to maintain a constant pressure, monitoring usage to optimize the start and run times.

During system design the control options should be carefully investigated to select a system that will provide the optimum life cycle cost.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Inlet valve throttling: The simplest way to modulate capacity, but inefficient: even when closed down to 0% output it still draws about 70% of full-load power.

Variable speed drive (VSD): Adjusts motor speed to actual demand; the most efficient option and the mainstream control method for modern large systems. The initial cost is high, but the long-term power savings are significant.

Unload-idle: Lets the compressor run idle (delivering no air) during demand troughs, which causes less wear on the equipment than frequent starts and stops.

Key Notes

The choice of control system directly affects the facility's annual energy bill. Pharmaceutical compressed air systems usually run 24/7, and at sites with widely fluctuating demand (such as a multi-product CDMO) the electricity saved by a VSD often pays back the equipment cost difference within 2-3 years.

Original Text

Treating compressed air to remove contaminants such as dirt (viable and non-viable particulate), lubricant, and water, may use equipment such as:

  • compressor aftercoolers
  • filters
  • separators
  • dryers
  • air receivers
  • traps
  • automatic drains

A commonly recognized standard is ISO 8573-1:2010 which identifies three classes of contaminants:

  1. particulate
  2. water
  3. oil/oil vapor

ISO 8573-1:2010 does not differentiate viable and non-viable particulates. Therefore, when using ISO 8573-1:2010 to define purity class of compressed air, further definition may be required in the User Requirements.

Measurements relate to standard conditions of 20°C, 1 bar absolute pressure.

4.5.1 Air Inlet Filters

Air inlet filters protect compressors from atmospheric airborne particles. Air inlet filters do not determine final air quality. Provided the air quality will not compromise the compressor reliability, it may be more cost effective to treat the air downstream of the compressor.

4.5.2 Intercooler and Aftercoolers

Intercoolers and aftercoolers are heat exchangers designed to remove heat of compression, to improve system efficiency. Moisture separators following an intercooler or aftercooler separate liquids entrained in the air or gas.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

ISO 8573-1:2010 is the international standard for compressed air quality. It defines seven classes (Class 0-6) for three types of contaminant:

  • Particulate: classified by particle size and concentration
  • Water: expressed as pressure dew point (PDP)
  • Oil & vapor: expressed in mg/m³

Important limitation: ISO 8573-1 does not distinguish between viable (microbial) and non-viable particles. A pharmaceutical plant must define microbial limits separately in the URS (user requirements specification).

Key Notes: Quality Targets for Pharmaceutical Plants

Pharmaceutical process air usually targets ISO Class 1-2 (oil content ≤ 0.01–0.1 mg/m³), combined with a microbial specification (for example <1 CFU/m³, or sterile after a 0.2 µm sterilizing-grade filter).

Class 0 means "user-defined and more stringent than Class 1", and applies where the highest quality is required (for example compressed air in direct contact with sterile product).

Analogy

The ISO 8573 quality classes are like drinking water standards: Class 6 is industrial cooling water (good enough to use), Class 1 is bottled mineral water, and Class 0 is water-for-injection grade. Which class your process needs depends on how much the air contacts the product: the closer to the product, the stricter the requirement.

Practical Application

Typical design for a CDMO aseptic filling plant:

  • Compressed air for isolator pressurization → ISO Class 1 + microbial 0 CFU (terminal 0.2 µm sterilizing-grade filter)
  • Stopper vibratory bowl drive air → ISO Class 2 + microbial control
  • Pneumatic valve actuation → ISO Class 3-4 (instrument air)
  • General equipment cooling → ISO Class 4-5
4.6 Dryers

Original Text

Once air leaves an aftercooler and moisture separator, its moisture content should have been reduced significantly; however, it may still be saturated. The temperature at which this moisture condenses is called the dewpoint. The dewpoint may be measured at line pressure or at atmospheric pressure.

Dryers are usually rated on pressure dewpoint. Dryer design should anticipate worst case operating condition based on the maximum allowable dewpoint. Types of dryers include:

  1. regenerative-desiccant dryer
  2. heat-of-compression dryer
  3. refrigerant dryer

4.6.1 Regenerative-Desiccant Dryers

Regenerative-desiccant dryers normally use a porous desiccant that adsorbs moisture. Desiccants include: silica gel, activated alumina, molecular sieves.

Normally, the desiccant resides in a twin tower. Compressed air to be dried flows through one tower, while the desiccant in the other is being regenerated. There are three types:

  1. Heatless dryer: requires about 15% of the dryer's capacity to be used for purge air, but normally has lowest initial cost.
  2. Heated dryer: reduces purge to 7 or 8%, but may cost more than a heatless dryer.
  3. Blower purge dryer: can reduce purge to 0 to 2%, but may cost more than a heated dryer.

Regenerative-desiccant-type dryers are commonly used in pharmaceutical manufacturing applications for process air, as they have a dewpoint rating more than 70°F (21°C) lower than refrigerant dryers (-40°F / -40°C dewpoint at standard inlet conditions).

4.6.2 Heat-of-Compression Dryers

Heat-of-compression dryers are regenerative desiccant dryers that use the heat generated during compression to achieve desiccant regeneration. They represent an efficient means for drying to −40°F (−40°C) dewpoint using either no or only small amounts of purge air for regeneration. Both require hot air from lubricant free rotary or centrifugal compressors.

Single-Vessel: A rotating desiccant drum directs air into two separate air streams. Where load is variable, so is the dewpoint. Heat-of-compression dryers are unlikely to provide −40°F (−40°C) constant dewpoint.

Twin-Tower: This dryer can be designed for constant dewpoint with varying loads. It has low to no purge air and repeatable dewpoint.

4.6.3 Refrigerant Dryers

Refrigerant dryers have low initial and operating costs, but are not frequently used in pharmaceutical applications for process air, as they usually require a much lower dewpoint. Refrigerated dryers provide a dewpoint in the 38°F to 50°F (3°C to 10°C) range.

Where process air system generation is combined with instrument air systems, it would not be unusual to use a refrigerated air dryer for the main generation system and a smaller desiccant dryer for the process air branch requirements.

Key Concepts

Dew point: The temperature at which water vapor in the air begins to condense into liquid water. The lower the dew point, the drier the air.

Pressure dew point (PDP): The dew point measured at the working pressure of the piping; it is the standard specification parameter for compressed air systems.

Analogy

A refrigerant dryer is like a dehumidifier box in a refrigerator: it makes the air a little drier, but it stops working in winter (low ambient temperature). A desiccant dryer is like a silica gel pack: it keeps absorbing moisture at any temperature and can be regenerated by heating and reused. Pharmaceutical plants need a stable, very low dew point all year round, which is why desiccant dryers are the first choice.

A heatless desiccant dryer consumes 15% of the dried air to purge and regenerate the other tower. For the compressed air system, this 15% is simply wasted energy.

Externally heated types reduce this to 7-8% and blower-purge types to 0-2%, but equipment cost rises step by step. Selection should be based on a life cycle cost (LCC) analysis, not just the initial equipment quote.

The dew point of a heat-of-compression single-vessel dryer drifts with load, so it is unsuitable for GMP processes that need a stable dew point. Twin-tower types should be preferred to obtain a stable dew point.

4.7 Compressed Air Filters

Original Text

Compressed air filters downstream of the air compressor are normally required for the removal of contaminants. Types of filter available include:

  • particulate filters to remove solid particles
  • coalescing filters to remove hydrocarbon droplets, moisture droplets, and particulates
  • adsorbent filters to remove hydrocarbon vapors and other aromatics (odors and tastes)

A particulate filter may be recommended after a desiccant-type dryer to remove desiccant dust. A coalescing-type filter may be recommended before a desiccant-type dryer to prevent fouling of the desiccant bed if a lubricant injected compressor is used upstream.

Filter grades of 0.45 µm or 0.2 µm may be employed at POUs (Point of Use).

When reviewing filters for their applicability, particle size removal ratings (e.g., 0.2 µm) and the filter efficiency rating (e.g., 99.97% efficient) should be reviewed. These ratings are normally based on defined inlet conditions, usually 70°F (21°C) at 100 psig.

A typical sequence of filters for a system is:

  1. Particulate Filter: typically 1 µm to 3 µm for removing rust, pipe scale, metal oxides, and desiccant particles
  2. High Temperature Particulate Filter: Temperature spikes can occur when using a heated desiccant dryer. Similar ratings to standard particulates apply, but can withstand higher operating temperatures to 350°F to 450°F (176.7°C to 232.2°C).
  3. Coalescing Filters: Liquid droplet removal capacity is typically 0.001 ppm by weight and aerosol size rated at 0.01 µm.
  4. Final Filter: A 0.2 µm liquid rated cartridge filter (down to 0.003 µm absolute rated for particles in gases) may be used as a final filter to obtain "sterile grade" compressed air. These filter cartridges are typically sanitary style to minimize the risk of bypass and incorporate a hydrophobic non-volatile membrane as the filter media.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Three main types of filter:

  • Particulate filter: Removes solid particles (rust, pipe scale, desiccant dust), usually rated 1-3 µm.
  • Coalescing filter: Causes fine oil or water mist droplets to coalesce into larger droplets that drain away. Coalescing efficiency can reach 0.01 µm liquid aerosol.
  • Adsorbent/activated carbon filter: Removes gaseous hydrocarbons such as oil vapor, usually with activated carbon as the medium.

Point-of-use (POU) filter: A 0.2 µm hydrophobic membrane filter used for final sterilizing-grade compressed air, preventing microbial penetration.

Analogy

The compressed air filtration chain is like a city water supply: first a coarse screen stops large particles, then a settling tank removes suspended matter, then activated carbon removes odors, and finally a 0.2 µm ultrafiltration membrane ensures the water is safe to drink. Every layer has its job, none can be omitted, and the order cannot be wrong.

Key Notes

A heated desiccant dryer produces a brief temperature peak (heat spike) at the moment of tower changeover. An ordinary coalescing filter may fail at this temperature, so a high-temperature particulate filter (rated for 176-232°C) must be used at this position.

Design points for the final 0.2 µm sterilizing-grade filter: use a sanitary housing to prevent bypass; the membrane material must be hydrophobic (non-wetting) so that compressed air flows through freely and water is not retained, which would cause an excessive pressure drop.

Practical Application

Typical compressed air filtration arrangement for a CDMO aseptic filling line (from upstream to point of use):

  1. Compressor outlet → coalescing pre-filter (removes oil mist, protects the desiccant dryer)
  2. Desiccant dryer outlet → 1-3 µm particulate filter (removes desiccant dust)
  3. Distribution network inlet → activated carbon adsorption filter (removes residual hydrocarbon vapor)
  4. Point of use (POU) → 0.2 µm hydrophobic sterilizing-grade filter (sterile air entering the filling area)

4.8 Air Receivers

Original Text

Receivers can be used to provide surge capacity to meet peak process needs and minimize changes in system pressure during periods of peak demand.

Compressed air systems require a receiver. When demand peaks are short and limited (usually less than 60 to 90 seconds), a large air receiver may allow a smaller air compressor to be specified. Additional benefits include the:

  • improved system reliability, as the load cycles on the compressor are reduced
  • increased efficiency, as the likelihood of another compressor starting is minimized

A desiccant dryer's purge can be the temporary push to peak demand. Given the cyclical nature of purge, it creates a temporary artificial demand on the compressor for a brief period but can be invisible to the compressor controls with a wet air receiver (i.e., an air receiver placed before a purge type desiccant dryer).

A receiver placed downstream of air treatment equipment can be used to manage pressure variation in a distribution system.

"A rule of thumb used for sizing a receiver is to use a unit with a capacity of 6 to 10 times the compressor peak capacity (free air delivered/second)."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Wet receiver: Located between the compressor and the dryer, it buffers the instantaneous pressure drop caused by dryer regeneration purge, so the compressor does not "see" the purge demand and avoids frequent starting.

Dry receiver: Located downstream of the dryer, it acts as a buffer of stored energy that stabilizes pressure fluctuations in the piping network and meets short-term peak demand.

Capacitance: The source uses this physics analogy to describe the receiver's "buffering energy", much as a capacitor smooths voltage fluctuations in a circuit.

  or upgrade to a larger compressor capacity

Key Notes

Air receivers are pressure vessels regulated by national laws (in the US the ASME Boiler & Pressure Vessel Code, in Europe the PED). They must be inspected regularly (pressure test, visual inspection) and included in the facility maintenance program.

Condensate accumulates at the bottom of the receiver (because the air cools while stored), so a suitable drain mechanism (traps / drains) must be installed; otherwise water will enter the downstream system.

  1. Why does the regeneration purge of a desiccant dryer create an "artificial instantaneous demand peak" for the compressor? How does a wet receiver solve this?
  2. When was the last statutory pressure vessel inspection of your plant's air receivers? Are complete records available?
4.9 Traps and Drains

Original Text

Traps or drains allow the removal of condensate from the compressed air system to protect the equipment being supplied by the system from the effects of a non-compressible fluid. Automatic condensate traps are used to conserve energy by preventing the loss of air through open petcocks and valves.

Methods to drain condensate include:

  1. mechanical traps
  2. solenoid actuated drain valves
  3. zero air-loss traps with reservoirs
  4. manual

4.9.1 Mechanical Traps

These are usually float-type mechanical traps with inverted buckets as a secondary design feature. Float-type traps are designed to open only as increasing condensate raises the water level and consequently, a float. Before the water is fully evacuated, the float reseals the trap, to prevent waste of air.

These traps can easily be blocked by buildup of particulate in the condensate. The inspection and maintenance of these traps should be incorporated into a maintenance program.

4.9.2 Solenoid Actuated Drain Valves

Solenoid-actuated drain valves are electrically operated and employ a timed sequence for opening and closing. Concerns include:

  • The open interval duration may be inadequate to evacuate all condensate.
  • The closed interval may be too long creating a back up of condensate into the compression stages.

The settings on these units should be revised depending on seasonal variation in outside air supplied to a system.

4.9.3 Zero-Air-Loss Traps with Reservoirs

Zero-air-loss traps can improve efficiency of a compressed air system. Types include:

  1. solenoid actuated with level sensor
  2. pneumatically actuated air cylinder, which opens a ball valve through a linkage to expel the condensate in the reservoir to the low-level point

Zero-air-loss traps combine the efficiency of no air loss and reduced maintenance with larger orifices, typically preventing the build-up of particulate in the trap. Strainers are recommended for all traps to prevent the likelihood of sediment blocking the passage of condensate.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Condensate: Liquid water (possibly containing oil) that separates out when compressed air cools. It must be drained regularly; otherwise it enters equipment and causes corrosion, microbial growth, and even damage to pneumatic components (liquids are incompressible).

Zero air-loss trap: The most efficient drainage method. Condensate is first collected in a small storage chamber and the valve opens to discharge only when the liquid level is reached, so no compressed air escapes with it.

Analogy

A float trap is like a toilet cistern float: when the water level is high the valve opens to drain, and when the water has drained the float drops and closes the valve. The problem is that if debris builds up in the float mechanism it can jam and fail to operate. A timed solenoid drain is like a timer-controlled plant waterer: it opens and closes on schedule whether or not there is water, so in summer (high air moisture content) it may not be enough, and in winter it may waste a lot of air.

Key Notes

The timing settings of a solenoid drain valve must be adjusted seasonally. In Taiwan's humid summers, the moisture content of the air is 3-5 times that of winter, and condensate generation increases sharply. A fixed opening interval can lead to condensate overflow, or to a large amount of compressed air being vented for nothing. It is advisable to include this in the quarterly facility maintenance checks.

Oil-water mixed condensate is industrial wastewater and cannot be discharged directly into the drain. It must be treated by an oil-water separation system, which is an environmental regulatory requirement and one of the inspection focus points.

Practice Questions

  1. If your plant uses solenoid drain valves, how should a verification procedure be designed to make sure condensate is effectively removed under different seasonal conditions?
  2. A strainer is recommended upstream of every trap. What problem does this solve? How does the maintenance frequency change compared with not fitting a strainer?
4.10 Designing for Redundancy (N+1 Strategy)

Original Text

The design of a compressed air system should consider the implications of loss of supply to the process. The N+1 redundancy strategy — where N is the number of compressors required to meet the demand, plus one standby unit — is a common approach to ensure continuity of supply.

Key redundancy design considerations include:

  • Standby compressors should be capable of being brought online automatically or with minimal manual intervention in the event of a primary compressor failure.
  • Redundancy of filters or dryer with transfer valves may be necessary to allow maintenance to be performed safely while a system is kept operational.
  • Control systems should be configured to monitor system pressure and automatically switch to standby equipment when the primary unit fails or drops below a set pressure threshold.
  • Planned maintenance of one compressor should be possible without interrupting the supply to the process (i.e., the remaining compressors can carry the full process load).

The level of redundancy should be proportional to the criticality of the application. For critical pharmaceutical manufacturing processes where loss of compressed air supply could lead to batch loss, equipment damage, or patient safety risk, a higher level of redundancy is justified.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

N+1 redundancy strategy: N compressors are sufficient to meet normal demand, with 1 additional standby unit. If any one unit fails or is under maintenance, the remaining units can still meet the full process demand.

Similar logic also applies to dryers (twin parallel sets with changeover valves) and main filters (parallel bypass design).

Analogy

N+1 is like the twin-engine design of an aircraft: normally each engine delivers half the power, and if one fails the other can keep the aircraft flying alone. The same applies to pharmaceutical compressed air: you cannot save money by buying a single large machine, because if it fails the whole process stops, and the cost of the lost batches is often higher than the cost of the redundant equipment.

Formulas and Calculations: ROI Analysis of Redundancy Investment

Standby compressor cost: $150,000–300,000 USD (typical)

Batch loss caused by compressed air failure:
  Aseptic filling batches × loss risk (average per batch)
  = e.g.: 2 batches × $500,000/batch = $1,000,000

Annualized failure probability (no redundancy):
  Assume MTBF = 5 years → 20%/year failure probability

Expected annual loss = $1,000,000 × 20% = $200,000/year

Payback period ≈ $250,000 / $200,000 ≈ 1.25 years

Conclusion: a standby compressor usually pays back within 1–2 years

Key Notes: The Redundancy Logic for a CDMO

For a CDMO, the impact of a compressed air failure is not just one batch, but also:

  • Isolator loses pressure → sterile environment is immediately compromised → the whole line needs requalification
  • Stopper vibratory bowl stops → filling line is interrupted → some filled batches may have to be rejected
  • Pneumatic doors/valves cannot operate → personnel cannot enter or leave the production area safely
  • Loss of customer relationships → the CDMO's reputation and contract renewals

These knock-on effects make the business case for N+1 redundancy very clear: it is not only a technical issue, but the core safeguard of the CDMO's business continuity.

Practical Application

Recommended action: draw up a "failure mode map" for your plant's compressed air system:

  1. List all critical equipment that depends on compressed air (isolators, filling machines, pneumatic valves, etc.)
  2. Assess the impact on each item of losing compressed air (batch loss, safety risk, compliance risk)
  3. For the highest-risk nodes, confirm whether there is sufficient backup (N+1, emergency nitrogen backup, receiver buffer time)
  4. Record the rationale for the redundancy design in change control

Practice Questions

  1. Is your plant's compressed air system currently N+1 or N? If N, has a compressor failure ever caused a production interruption?
  2. The "automatic changeover" function of a standby compressor needs periodic verification (for example PM tests). Are your verification frequency and test procedure documented?

Chapter Summary

  • System architecture: A compressed air system consists of the supply side (compressors + treatment equipment) and the demand side (piping + POU), and must be designed as a whole system rather than considering each part independently.
  • Oil-free compressors: The industry standard for pharmaceutical process air. The outlet of a lubricated compressor contains 2-5 ppm of oil, and even with a coalescing filter a residual risk remains, so it is unsuitable for product-contact applications.
  • Dryer selection: A regenerative desiccant dryer can reach a pressure dew point below −40°C and is the first choice for pharmaceutical process air; a refrigerant dryer reaches only +3 to +10°C and is suitable for instrument air.
  • Filtration chain: From 1-3 µm coarse filtration to the final 0.2 µm hydrophobic sterilizing-grade filter, every layer has an irreplaceable role; a high-temperature filter must be used at the location of temperature spikes.
  • Receiver function: The wet receiver buffers dryer purge demand and the dry receiver stabilizes piping pressure; size the receiver at 6-10 times the compressor peak capacity as a reference.
  • Condensate management: Zero air-loss traps are the most efficient; solenoid types need seasonal setting adjustments; oil-water condensate must go through oil-water separation before discharge.
  • N+1 redundancy: For a CDMO, the knock-on losses of a compressed air failure (rejected batches, isolator pressure loss, customer trust) usually far exceed the investment in redundant equipment; redundancy is the core safeguard of business continuity.

5.1 Concepts

Original Text

The initial design of distribution systems for process gases should match the available gas quality and supply method to the end user specifications. This forms the basis for the design, and should provide detail on the:

  • level of clean-up required for a gas prior to use
  • the pressure control and management of the supply and delivery systems

Delivery of supplies of a gas at the required specification should be consistent. The capabilities of both internal (e.g., compressed air or nitrogen) and external (e.g., bottled or bulk gases) supplies should be considered.

A supplier audit may be required for external suppliers. A Certificate of Analysis (CoA) should ensure that a gas is of the required specification. Equipment used to provide the information for the CoA should be appropriately maintained and calibrated.

Supplies of internally manufactured gases (e.g., compressed air or nitrogen) should be considered in the same way as external supplies, including:

  • assessment of quality of supply
  • management and reporting of quality of supply
  • measurement and reporting of expected contaminants (e.g., oil carryover from a compressed air system)

5.1.1 Risk Assessment

The design process should include a risk assessment to ensure that the proposed design is fit for purpose. The assessment should consider potential systems failures and ensure that the controls in place are adequate to mitigate risks to product quality or patient safety.

Gas suppliers may have technical support staff that can assist during the risk assessment and system design phase of a project.

Depending on the pharmaceutical application, the environment in which the gas lines are run, and the quality of gas supplied, some aspects of industrial gas supply may not be required. These requirements should be considered during a risk assessment, e.g.:

  • Gases distributed for pharmaceutical applications are usually dry and of a high quality and may not require distribution systems to be drainable; pipe line fall, drop legs, and drain traps may not be needed.
  • Where a distribution system is clean, with a clean gas being distributed, having gas point take-offs at the top of a pipeline may not be required.

A risk assessment should also consider:

  • How operations are made aware that there is sufficient connected capacity to undertake a batch or process (e.g., use of monitoring systems and back-up supplies for critical processes)
  • Whether automatic changeover for finite supply applications (bulk or cylinders) is required, or if a suitable low pressure/low level warning system is sufficient
  • For flammable, toxic, or corrosive gases: control of discharge from regulators and lines during cylinder changeover, line disconnection, or in the event of line or regulator failure
  • Effects on staff from exposure to a gas in general operation and during potential failure modes (e.g., oxygen exclusion, flammability, static build-up, chemical reaction)
  • High concentration of all gases (including inert) should be considered to present a risk to personnel

Document all steps as part of the design risk assessment.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Distribution System Design: Design is not simply choosing a pipe and connecting it to the equipment. The starting point is the end user specification: how pure the gas must be, how much flow and what pressure your process needs. The design works backward from there.

CoA (Certificate of Analysis): The quality assurance document for each batch or cylinder of purchased gas. ISPE stresses that a purchased-gas supplier is equivalent to a raw material supplier: it requires a supplier audit, CoA review and equipment calibration records. FDA inspectors always check this.

The blind spot of in-house generated gases: Many CDMOs treat compressed air as a "utility" rather than a "raw material", but ISPE is explicit that in-house gases (compressed air, nitrogen) need exactly the same quality management as purchased gases, including contaminant reporting (such as oil carry-over).

Analogy

Think of a process gas distribution system as the oxygen piping in a hospital. A hospital would never run a piece of plastic tubing to the oxygen outlet in an operating room. It needs material verification, pressure calculations, backup systems, and backflow protection at every connection. Your plant's gas distribution system is the same: patient safety (= product quality) depends on the rigor of this design.

Key Notes

Risk assessment is the central logic running through all of Chapter 5. ISPE does not prescribe that you must build a ring main or install a given number of filters. It requires you to use risk assessment to decide which industrial-standard practices can be waived in a GMP environment and which must be kept. This "risk-based flexibility" is the spirit of modern regulation, and it is also the most important documentation tool you have in engineering design.

Practice Questions

  1. Does your site have a CoA system for in-house compressed air? If not, what regulatory risk does that represent?
  2. Why does ISPE say pharmaceutical gas systems generally do not need a designed pipe line fall (drainage slope)? How does this differ from industrial compressed air systems?
5.1.2 Basic System Design Concepts

Original Text

Process take-off requirements should be understood to allow sizing of a distribution system. The rate of gas usage and pressure required at critical points in the process should be quantified. These critical points should include periods of peak take-off of gas. The distribution system should be sized to maintain an adequate supply at critical points.

Averages should not be used, even where there is variable take-off of gas. (Experienced designers may use a system utilization factor.)

Basic system design concepts include:

  • one way distribution system
  • high demand use points
  • ring main
  • splitting systems
  • use of a non-return valve/receiver to split systems
  • line sizing
  • routing

5.1.2.1 One Way Distribution System

The simplest distribution system design comprises a single pipeline feeding each POU (point of use). For a small number of widely spaced delivery points, a one way distribution system from a gas source may represent the lowest initial cost at installation. The pipeline should be sized to accommodate peak usage.

Where the highest gas consumption POUs are positioned nearest the gas supply, it may be possible to reduce the size of the pipeline to POUs further along the system. Intermediate receiving vessels may be used to reduce the effects of usage variability or supply line size. They may be placed in the main branch or on critical POUs and used where the:

  • highest gas consumption POUs cannot be sited nearest the gas supply
  • size of pipeline to provide peak loads is excessive
  • demand varies significantly

Intermediate receiving vessels can buffer the output to specific POUs (e.g., nitrogen inertion of large API reactor systems). Consideration should be given to providing non-return valves at intermediate receiving vessels to ensure that the main pipeline is not back fed if the main pipeline pressure dips below the receiver pressure at high demand periods.

5.1.2.2 Ring Main

A ring main comprises two feed sources. The ring main concept is considered a more robust distribution design when compared to a one way design, as:

  • The system will self balance due to the two feed sources of the ring main allowing line size to be optimized. Points at the extreme of the ring may be susceptible to pressure drops but these will be reduced.
  • A considered design and placement of isolation valves will allow the line to be serviced, modified, or repaired without shutting down the complete ring and associated POUs.
  • The ring main itself provides a level of receiver capacity which may be sufficient to buffer high demand, reducing the need for intermediate receiving vessels.
  • The system allows addition of multiple gas sources (e.g., multiple air compressors) in different locations throughout the site, enhancing the redundancy of the system.

A ring main system can provide a cost effective solution relative to a one way distribution line, particularly where cost analysis includes allowances for foreseeable future growth.

5.1.2.3 High Demand Points of Use

Where there are high demand POUs, it may be more economic to provide gas from a local receiving vessel using a smaller distribution system pipeline size at a higher supply pressure. Consideration should be given to over-sizing the distribution pipe locally to the POU to provide a "local receiver."

Consideration should be given to providing non-return valves at intermediate receiving vessels to ensure that the main pipeline is not back fed particularly where several buffering receivers are attached to one supply. Pressure and flow regulation should be considered in the distribution system design.

5.1.2.4 Splitting Systems

Requirements of relevant GMP codes (e.g., system design and documentation, validation processes, ongoing maintenance, calibration quality monitoring, and formal monitoring of the system output quality) add to the installation and operating costs of GMP gas distribution systems.

Where end-user specifications allow, it may be feasible to split a system into non-GMP and GMP sub-systems. This may be particularly beneficial in locations where there is a large site gas distribution system, with only a limited part of the system used for GMP purposes. A non-GMP system can be used to feed a GMP system, providing that the GMP system is adequately monitored and controlled.

The risk assessment associated with a split system should consider how the integrity, purity, and quality of the GMP take-off points is maintained under all scenarios. Redundancy in filtration and back flow prevention may be required.

5.1.2.5 Sizing of Pipes

Pipe size can be based on either a calculated design maximum use or the total connected load multiplied by a diversity factor. Typical figures used are 6–9 m/s (18–27 ft/sec) velocity, or 3–5 psi (0.3 to 0.4 bar) allowable pressure drop, depending on conditions. Filter pressure drops should be considered in calculations.

The greater the pressure drop, the greater the minimum system supply pressure needs to be. As pressure increases, the energy requirement for the compressor (or cost of bulk gas) increases following a square law — so small differences have a significant effect on energy consumption.

5.1.2.6 Routing

The siting of pipes should facilitate regular inspection and servicing of valves. Annual checks for leaks may be conducted as part of GEP using techniques such as ultrasonic leak detection. Statutory periodic inspections may be required for high pressure equipment in some geographical regions.

Where a process gas has a dewpoint near the local environmental limits, pipes should be insulated or sited where they do not cause internal or external condensation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Design Philosophy of Ring Main vs. One-Way

One-Way Distribution: Like a single-lane road: gas has only one path from start to end. The benefit is simple piping and low cost; the drawback is that pressure at the end use points fluctuates with distance and demand, and maintenance on any pipe section affects all downstream points of use.

Ring Main: Like a city ring road: gas can enter any outlet from the left or the right at the same time, and pressure balances itself automatically. This is the first choice for large pharmaceutical production lines, especially when future expansion is planned.

Analogy

A ring main is like the Taipei MRT circular line. If one station has a problem, passengers can go around the other half of the loop. A one-way system, by contrast, is like a road with only one lane: once it is blocked, every vehicle is stuck. For a CDMO running 24-hour continuous production, the value of the property "no single-point failure stops the whole line" is the justification for the cost of a ring main design.

Pipe Sizing Reference

Two methods of pipe sizing:

Method 1: Maximum velocity method
  Typical velocity limit: 6–9 m/s (18–27 ft/sec)
  Above this velocity → sharp pressure drop + noise + accelerated fitting wear

Method 2: Allowable pressure drop method
  Allowable pressure drop: 3–5 psi (0.3–0.4 bar) overall
  Greater pressure drop → supply pressure must be raised
  Energy use follows a square law:
  Energy ∝ P²
  → a 10% increase in supply pressure raises energy use by about 21%

Notes:
  Filter pressure drop (clean + dirty) must be included in the calculation
  Design on peak load, not average load

Key Note: The Risk of Feeding a Non-GMP System into a GMP System

The "system segregation" described in 5.1.2.4 is what many large pharmaceutical plants actually do: the plant's existing large industrial compressed air system supplies the whole site, and a "policing filter set" and a non-return valve are installed before the GMP area to separate the GMP section.

The key to this design is that the quality of the GMP section must remain under control even when the quality of the industrial section deteriorates. This means the GMP section needs its own monitoring, its own records and sufficient redundant filtration.

CDMO Scenario

In the multi-client environment of a CDMO, a ring main design is especially important. Different clients' processes may use very different amounts of nitrogen or compressed air on the same day. The self-balancing property of a ring main prevents a high-demand process from disturbing pressure stability in other areas. In addition, placing enough isolation valves on the ring main allows engineers to maintain any pipe section without stopping production across the whole site. This is the "maintainability" design element FDA inspectors pay attention to.

Practice Questions

  1. If your site plans to add a sterile filling line in five years, should you choose a ring main or one-way distribution when designing the gas distribution system now? What is your reasoning?
  2. Why must pipe sizing use peak flow rather than average flow? Explain with a real production scenario.
  3. What does the "square-law relationship between pressure drop and pressure" mean in practice for day-to-day running costs?
5.2 Non-Return or Check Valves

Original Text

There are several types of non-return valves. For process gases, the spring loaded wafer check valve, using an elastomeric seal for the wafer, allows a gas tight shut off and rapid action whichever way up the valve is mounted.

Non-return valves should be suitable for both the gas supply side and for likely contaminants from the process side.

For GMP systems, cross contamination can be considered a critical risk. Risk assessments performed during the design of a process gas system should investigate potential consequences of reverse flow under normal and system failure modes. Where a potential risk is identified, backflow prevention and non-return valves may be required.

Non-return valves should be fitted as closely as possible to POUs to reduce potential contamination in the supply line. Where there is a potential for cross contamination, cleaning and servicing of non-return valves should ensure that non-return valves remain functional. Redundancy may be required for high risk critical processes.

Where the function of a non-return valve is considered critical (as a process or a safety requirement), the systems design should facilitate testing of the non-return valve. This may be achieved either by making removal of the non-return valve easy, or by the installation of test ports on either side of the non-return valve and isolating valves in the line either side of the non-return valve.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Non-Return Valve / Check Valve: A valve that allows gas to flow in only one direction. For sterile products, this is the last physical barrier preventing product contaminants from flowing back up the gas line into the supply system.

Spring-Loaded Wafer Check Valve: The type of valve ISPE recommends for process gases. Its features: the elastomeric seal ensures gas tightness, the spring ensures fast closure, and the design does not rely on gravity, so it can be installed at any angle. These three features are very practical in complex piping layouts.

Test Ports: Installed on both sides of the non-return valve so maintenance staff can verify its function without removing it. This is a necessary design for high-risk processes: if your non-return valve is a "critical function", it must be testable.

Key Note: Real-World Backflow Contamination Scenarios

Imagine a lyophilizer being filled with nitrogen for headspace protection. If a downstream valve fails, product vapor (containing organics or microorganisms) could flow back along the nitrogen line into the whole gas supply system and contaminate all later batches' headspace gas. This is exactly why ISPE requires non-return valves to be installed "as close to the POU as possible": the shorter the distance the backflow can travel, the smaller the potential area of contamination.

FDA 21 CFR Part 211 has explicit requirements for preventing contamination from pharmaceutical utilities, and the design and verification records of non-return valves are always checked during inspections.

Analogy

A non-return valve is like the P-trap under a bathroom drain: it lets water go in only one direction and keeps sewer gas from flowing back into the bathroom. In a pharmaceutical gas system, what it stops is not odor but microorganisms or organic contaminants that could endanger drug quality. The logic of "needing test ports" is also intuitive: if the P-trap in your home were a safety-critical item, you would want to test whether it still works without taking the pipe apart.

CDMO Inspection Perspective

What FDA inspectors focus on regarding non-return valves in gas systems:

  • Installation location: Is it right next to the POU, rather than on a remote main?
  • Material compatibility: Is the valve body and seal material compatible with the gas and with possible process-side contaminants?
  • Verification records: Is the function of the non-return valve tested regularly? Are the test method, frequency and acceptance criteria documented in an SOP?
  • Redundancy: For high-risk processes (such as direct product-contact gas), is there a double non-return valve or other backup?

Practice Questions

  1. Under what circumstances is a single non-return valve not enough, so that redundancy is needed?
  2. What risk arises when the non-return valve is installed on the main rather than near the POU?
5.3 Pressure Reducing Valves

Original Text

Pressure reducing valves should be selected so that the normal operating range is in the mid-point of the valve capabilities. Operating near the extremes of the valve capabilities may give less consistent results.

Materials of construction should be selected based on the properties of the gas being supplied and anticipated processes, e.g.:

  1. To extend the operating life of the valve where highly corrosive gases (e.g., hydrogen chloride gas) or processes occur, purging tees may be used to allow the pressure reducing valve to be purged after use with a suitable purging gas. For example, for hydrogen chloride gas, the purging gas could be dry nitrogen.
  2. For sterile operations where the gas may come into direct contact with the product (e.g., purging/headspace gas in a freeze drying process), the materials of construction and finish can be critical.

5.3.1 Two-Stage Serial Reduction

Two-stage serial reduction uses two valves in series to reduce or eliminate extreme variations between the inflow pressure and the final reduced outlet pressure. Two stage reduction is recommended when:

  • Initial pressures are 200 psi (14 bar) or greater, or
  • The desired pressure reduction ratio is greater than 4:1 (e.g., from 200 psi to 50 psi / 14 to 3.5 bar), or
  • The inflow pressure fluctuates greatly

The advantage is that neither valve is subjected to extreme pressure differentials, thus prolonging the life of the valves and delivering more precise pressure regulation.

5.3.2 Parallel Connection

Connecting two or more smaller size pressure reducing valves in parallel to serve a single supply main may be used where there is a wide variation in demand at the reduced pressure. Parallel installations offer:

  • Increased capacity where needed
  • Improved valve performance for widely variable demands
  • The ability to service individual valves without requiring a system to be shut down

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Pressure Reducing Valve (PRV): A valve that reduces high supply pressure to the working pressure the process needs. Selection principle: normal operating pressure should fall in the mid-range of the valve's capability, not near its maximum or minimum, just as a car engine is most efficient and lasts longest at 60%–80% of maximum rpm.

Two-Stage Serial Reduction: Used when inlet pressure is ≥ 200 psi, or the reduction ratio is > 4:1. The logic is to take the large pressure difference in two steps so that each valve's differential stays in a reasonable range, giving higher accuracy and longer valve life.

Parallel Connection: Several small PRVs connected in parallel, used at supply points where demand varies widely. The benefit is that the valves can be maintained in rotation without shutting down, an important design consideration for continuous production in a GMP environment.

Key Note: Material Requirements for Direct Product-Contact Gas

When gas directly contacts the drug product (for example nitrogen headspace filling of a lyophilizer), the PRV's material and surface finish become part of a critical quality attribute (CQA). Considerations then include 316L stainless steel, electropolished internal surfaces, oil-free assembly, and material compatibility documentation. ISPE states that these are "critical" for sterile operations; in regulatory terms, this means a documented basis for the verification is required.

Reduction Ratio Criteria

Deciding whether two-stage serial reduction is needed:

Condition 1: Inlet pressure ≥ 200 psi (14 bar)
Condition 2: Reduction ratio > 4:1
        e.g. 200 psi → 50 psi = 4:1 (borderline)
           200 psi → 40 psi = 5:1 (two stages needed)
Condition 3: Large inlet pressure fluctuation

If any one condition is met → two-stage serial reduction is recommended

Two-stage design principles:
  Stage 1: Reduce from inlet pressure to an intermediate pressure
  Stage 2: Reduce from the intermediate pressure to the final working pressure
  Preferably each stage has a ratio ≤ 4:1

Practice Questions

  1. Your site's cylinder gas supply (typically 2000 psi) is reduced to the process pressure (for example 100 psi). How many reduction stages are needed, and why?
  2. Why is a parallel PRV design particularly suited to a CDMO environment? Think about it from the standpoint of continuous production.
5.4 Mixing Valves

Original Text

A mixture of gases may be purchased pre-mixed and supplied through a dedicated system, or gases may be mixed at the POU using a mixing valve arrangement.

Gases may be mixed using a mass flow controller on each gas line. The mass flow controller is controlled by a flow sensor and feeds the gases into a static mixer.

Gas mixing systems can be accurate to ±1%.

The setpoints can be adjusted manually or automatically. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Gas Mixing / Blending System: Mixes two or more gases in real time at the point of use. Common applications: CO₂/N₂ mixing (for pH control in bioreactors), O₂/N₂ mixing (for cell culture), and N₂/CO₂ (for incubator environment control).

Mass Flow Controller (MFC): An instrument that precisely controls the mass flow of each gas, unaffected by changes in pressure and temperature (because it measures mass, not volume). Two MFCs plus a static mixer make up a basic gas mixing system. Accuracy can reach ±1%.

Pre-mixed vs. on-site mixing: Pre-mixed gas is convenient, but the ratio may vary from cylinder to cylinder each time you change one. On-site MFC mixing is highly accurate and can be adjusted in real time, and suits processes whose ratio changes frequently.

Key Note: Verifying the Mixing Ratio

In a GMP environment, the gas mixing ratio itself may be a critical process parameter (CPP). For example, the CO₂ concentration in a cell culture controls pH, which directly affects cell growth and product quality. The MFC calibration records, the performance qualification of the static mixer, and periodic analysis of the mixed gas therefore all need to be part of the process validation program. An accuracy of ±1% sounds good, but you need data to support that claim under your specific conditions.

Analogy

An MFC mixing system is like a high-precision cocktail machine: each "ingredient" (gas) has precise flow control, and the mixing ratio can be programmed and recorded. A pre-mixed gas cylinder is like buying a ready-made cocktail kit: convenient, but you have to trust the supplier's CoA for the ratio in each kit. In a GMP process that needs tight control, which would you choose? The answer depends on your CPP design and risk assessment.

5.5 Filtration

Original Text

5.5.1 Gas Filtration for Sterile Applications

Pharmaceutical gas distribution systems are usually protected with filters rated 1 µm or smaller at the generation source. Where sterile gas or sterile venting is required, this is usually provided by a 0.2 µm rated hydrophobic sterilizing grade filter at the points of delivery or use.

Sterile gas/vent filters can either be pre-sterilized by autoclaving and aseptically installed, or sterilized in situ by Steaming in Place (SIP). For single use bioreactors and other disposable systems, filters can be sterilized using gamma irradiation. Gas sterilizing filters usually incorporate 0.2 µm rated microporous membranes made of hydrophobic polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) or hydrophobic polyvinylidenefluoride (PVDF). Filters with PTFE membranes are not compatible with gamma irradiation.

Some 0.2 µm rated membrane filters are also rated for removal of airborne viruses down to 20 nm (0.020 µm) and airborne particles as fine as 0.003 µm. This is possible because filters are typically rated for particle/microbial size removal from liquids, whereas additional removal mechanisms occur in gases that enhance removal of particles finer than the liquid pore size rating.

Pharmaceutical grade gas sterilizing grade filters are normally provided with a CoA from the manufacturer. The manufacturer may perform non-destructive (integrity) testing on filters prior to shipment. Retention of bacteria under liquid challenge conditions is considered the most stringent qualification for critical gas sterilizing and venting applications.

Depending on user risk assessments and intended use, filters may be integrity tested:

  • prior to use
  • after use
  • at a predetermined interval
  • after events that are considered stress conditions for the filter (e.g., sterilization, high temperature air flow)

Critical use filters can be installed with POU integrity test ports that allow integrity testing in situ.

5.5.2 Filter Integrity Testing

Three methods for integrity testing of sterilizing gas filters are the:

  1. Bubble Point Pressure Test
  2. Forward Flow (Diffusion) Test
  3. Water Intrusion Test

5.5.2.1 Bubble Point Pressure Test

The Bubble Point Pressure test determines the installation integrity of a wetted filter by determining the gas pressure necessary to force the wetting liquid from the largest flow pathways or defects in the filter. For hydrophobic filters, the wetting liquid is usually a 70% IPA/30% water solution.

In a manual test, a length of tubing is connected to the downstream port of the filter assembly and the other end immersed in liquid. Bulk flow of gas is indicated by rapid bubbling. The pressure at which rapid bubbling is observed is recorded as the "Bubble Point Pressure." This procedure is not applicable to sterilized filters prior to their use, but may be utilized prior to sterilization or after use.

Automated instruments perform a Bubble Point Pressure test from the upstream side of the wetted filter — automated tests can be performed on sterilized filters before use. A pressure reading lower than the manufacturer's minimum recommended limit is an indication of integrity failure.

Bubble Point Pressure testing is less suited to high area cartridges and multi-cartridge assemblies due to the subjectivity of the endpoint detection.

5.5.2.2 Forward Flow (Diffusion) Test

The Forward Flow or "Diffusion" test determines the integrity of a wetted filter by quantitatively measuring gas flow at a gas test pressure set marginally below the minimum Bubble Point Pressure (typically at around 80%). The measured gas flow rate is empirically correlated to microbial retention.

Automated instruments perform a Forward Flow test from the upstream side of the filter — can be performed on sterilized filters before use without compromising downstream sterility. A Forward Flow rate higher than the manufacturer's maximum allowable specification is an indication of integrity test failure.

The Forward Flow or "Diffusion" test is a quantitative measurement suitable for high area cartridges and multi-cartridge assemblies. However, it is also limited by the requirement to wet the filter with a low surface tension liquid prior to testing and to dry it before returning it to service.

5.5.2.3 Water Intrusion Test

In the Water Intrusion Test, the integrity of a clean and dry hydrophobic membrane filter is determined by flooding the filter assembly with water, applying air overpressure to force water into the largest flow paths, or defects in the filter. Water flow through the filter is measured by pressure decay or by direct volumetric flow.

A flow rate higher than the manufacturer's maximum allowable limit is an indication of integrity test failure.

Water intrusion testing avoids the use of alcohol and does not contaminate the downstream of integral filters, making it particularly suitable for testing of sterile hydrophobic membrane filters in situ. However, it is limited by: the requirement to introduce and drain water from the upstream, higher false failure rates due to wet or contaminated filters, and sensitivity to temperature variation. It is not well suited for very small filter assemblies.

In operation, sterilizing gas filters are usually integrity tested before sterilization or after use by the Forward Flow (Diffusion) or Bubble Point Pressure tests. When testing filters in situ after sterilization, a variation of the Water Intrusion Test using an automated test instrument may be used.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Layered Structure of Filtration

A process gas filtration system is designed in layers. No single filter solves every problem; each stage takes on a different filtration task:

  • Source protection (≤1 µm): Installed at the gas generation source (such as the compressor outlet) to remove large particles and protect downstream equipment
  • Final point-of-use filtration (0.2 µm, sterilizing grade): The last line of defense for product-contact gas. This is what "last line of defense" means: it is the primary barrier ensuring the gas is sterile
  • PTFE vs. PVDF membranes: Both are hydrophobic materials, but PTFE is not compatible with gamma sterilization, so single-use systems need PVDF

Key Note: The "Unexpected Capability" of 0.2 µm Filters

A liquid-rated pore size of 0.2 µm can remove particles as small as 0.003 µm in the gas phase, and even viruses (20 nm = 0.020 µm). The reason is that particle removal mechanisms in gas (impaction, interception, diffusion, electrostatic attraction) are more varied than in liquid, so filtration efficiency is far higher than the liquid-phase rating would suggest. This is important safety-margin knowledge for pharmaceutical gas design.

5.6 Filters in Compressed Air Systems

Original Text

The concepts described in this section can be applied to other process gas systems, depending on the risks associated with the gas supply source and the CQAs for a gas.

A suite of filters used in a commercial application may comprise the following filters:

5.6.1 Compressor or Source Gas Pre-Filter

Reduction of particulate on the gas feed can use lower cost filter media and installation methods than in-line filtration systems. This is therefore a cost effective filter and helps to prolong equipment life.

5.6.2 Compressor Discharge

It is considered good practice to install a moisture separator or coalescing filter and a general purpose filter on the discharge from the compressor. This should help to reduce the moisture and particulate build up in the receiver. Coalescing filters remove moisture droplets condensed by the compressor after cooler.

5.6.3 Pre-dryer Filtration

A general purpose filter will normally be used to remove particulate down to 1 µm and to help keep the drying media clean. The general purpose filter is usually used in conjunction with a high efficiency oil/hydrocarbon removal filter, to remove any atmospheric contamination and particulate down to 0.01 µm.

5.6.4 Post Dryer Filtration

After the dryer, a similar suite of filters will be used to prevent any particulate migration from the dryer into the system. For oil-lubricated compressors, the high efficiency oil/hydrocarbon coalescing filter is used here as a means of particulate control (oil-less compressors do not require coalescing filters).

After a gas storage system (receiver), filter sets are usually provided as parallel systems. This allows for replacement of a filter while the system is kept operational.

5.6.5 Post Receiver Filters

A final filter is normally used before the distribution system to ensure that the gas entering the distribution system meets required specifications. For sterile applications this may be a 0.2 µm rated membrane filter, which will need to be sterilized. For oral solid dose applications, a 0.45 µm or 1 µm rated filter may be suitable.

5.6.6 Alternative Post Dryer Filter Arrangement

For small systems, where there is a very low risk that system operation and maintenance could affect gas quality, POU filtration may be considered unnecessary. A robust post dryer filter arrangement may be considered as an alternative:

  • The post dryer filter set may be arranged as a "double" set of gas filters, with a second set in series acting as a "policing" filter set. The gas quality is checked in normal operation on the pre-filter set; if a gas fails a test, the second set of filters should ensure that the gas will be re-filtered prior to entering the system. During system maintenance the final set of filters are used to replace the pre-filter set and new filters are installed in the final location, minimizing operating cost.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Five-Layer Architecture of Compressed Air Filtration

ISPE describes a complete "defense-in-depth filtration" system in which each layer has a specific purpose:

  • Inlet pre-filtration: Low-cost removal of large particles to protect the compressor itself
  • Compressor outlet: Removes water droplets formed by compression condensation (coalescing filter) + general particulates
  • Pre-dryer filtration: Protects the desiccant from oil and particle contamination (0.01 µm oil aerosol removal)
  • Post-dryer filtration: Prevents desiccant particles from migrating into the distribution system
  • After the receiver / before entering distribution: Final particle-size gatekeeper (0.2 µm for sterile)

Analogy

The multi-layer filtration of compressed air is like municipal tap water purification: reservoir (coarse filtration/sedimentation) → rapid sand filter (removes suspended solids) → activated carbon (removes organics/taste) → chlorine disinfection → household water filter (final point of use). Each step targets a specific contaminant, and none is redundant. In a pharmaceutical plant, the 0.2 µm POU filter is that "household water filter": it protects the last stretch of the path that directly contacts the drug product.

Key Note: The GMP Significance of Parallel Filter Banks

The "parallel design" of the filters after the receiver is an important GMP operational flexibility: one bank operates on line while the other can be changed without shutting down. This solves a common CDMO pain point: changing filters usually requires scheduled downtime, which disrupts batch production plans. A parallel design turns a filter change into "routine maintenance that does not stop the batch". For filter replacement records, this kind of "on-line change" design also needs a corresponding SOP and a post-change verification procedure.

Practical Application: The Value of a "Policing Filter" Design for a CDMO

The "double series (policing filter)" concept in 5.6.6 is particularly suited to small and mid-sized CDMOs or laboratory production lines:

  • When the main filter fails, the policing filter takes over automatically and the product is unaffected
  • Rotation at maintenance: the old policing filter becomes the main filter, and a new filter is installed as the new policing filter (zero downtime)
  • Saves the cost of installing POU filters (in some cases individual POU filtration can be eliminated)

The precondition is that risk assessment confirms the system is clean enough and documentation is complete. You must be able to explain the logic clearly during an inspection.

5.7 Material Selection

Original Text

Factors which should be considered when selecting materials of construction for a distribution system include:

  • potential impact on the desired gas quality attributes
  • local regulations
  • robustness
  • installation time
  • local custom and practice
  • insurer's requirements
  • cost

Materials used for a process gas distribution system and associated equipment should be resistant to adverse effects of the gas being transported. They should also comply with the necessary national and local regulations for a gas.

The effects of the environment on the external surfaces of the distribution system (e.g., cleaning and maintenance) should be considered. For example, copper may not be resistant to cleaning materials and may develop a surface coating (by oxidation) which may shed particles, so copper is usually not used within a production area; however, copper may be acceptable outside a production area.

5.7.1 Moisture Ingress

Materials used for distribution systems should provide a robust vapor barrier to prevent an increase in the moisture levels of a gas. Vapor ingress can be a significant issue for flexible POU pipes. Careful selection of fittings is also necessary where moisture ingress may affect critical attributes, e.g., pneumatic push-in fittings can lead to moisture ingress.

5.7.2 Particulate Generation

Once any loose material in a process gas distribution system has been blown out, particulate generation is not usually an issue in relation to a gas, even for carbon steel. Steel does not normally corrode if the Relative Humidity (RH) is below 35%. At the low Pressure Dewpoints (PDPs) typically used in pharmaceutical systems, carbon or galvanized steel would not normally be a source of particulate.

Where the installation is exposed in a classified area, the risk of particulate shedding from the external surface of the pipe work should be considered.

5.7.3 Ease of Assembly and Modification

Ease of assembly together with cost/installation time/robustness in use have a major impact and may give different results depending on the application. Copper pipe may be the material of choice for a distribution system, whereas stainless tube with compression fittings may be used locally for a specific piece of equipment.

5.7.4 Availability of Fittings

"Push fit" fittings are not usually specified as sanitary, so require careful consideration before use. Suppliers should have a readily accessible source of fittings to ensure that a system can be maintained over its entire lifetime. The use of "special" fittings during construction may become costly to maintain.

5.7.5 Pipe Work Support Requirements

Some materials used for pipe work can require a higher number of supports, which can increase installation costs. Additional supports also may be required where a system is subject to rapid changes in pressure or extremes of temperature. Where ball valves are used, these can induce movement in pipe work and additional supports may be required.

5.7.6 Optional Materials

Material selection is dependent on the risks the interaction between the gas and materials of construction presents to the end user. Decision processes should be documented and checked.

5.7.6.1 Stainless Steel

Stainless steel is usually used in production areas:

  • ASTM 304 grade stainless steel may be adequate for non-sterile manufacturing areas, but there may be a risk of corrosion depending on the cleaning and sanitizing materials used.
  • For sterile operations, ASTM 316 grade or equivalent is normally used, as the cleaning and sanitizing materials used are generally more corrosive.

Sanitary jointing methods are not usually required, as there is slight risk of microbial growth within a dry system. Orbital welding may be specified to ensure consistent reliable welds, with a purge gas used to prevent oxide formation inside pipe work.

It may be cost effective to specify lower grade stainless steel (e.g., ASTM 304) for distribution systems, with a higher grade used in production areas where it will be subject to more corrosive cleaning agents.

5.7.6.2 Medical Grade Copper

Medical gas grade copper (e.g., ASTM B 280, ISO 7396-1, BS EN13348) may be used in major installations (100 ft / 30.5 m of pipe or more). Material should be specified as clean and degreased, purged during fabrication, and jointed without flux using "hard" or silver solder.

Copper pipe may be used for a distribution system, with a break at a service outlet panel or service void, with the final part of the pipe work in stainless steel within a production area. Copper pipe may not be recommended for use with nitrogen, as moisture in the gas may cause internal corrosion.

5.7.6.3 Plastics

Modern thermoplastic (e.g., high density polyethylene, HDPE) can be used for the clean distribution of gases. Thick walled HDPE pipe is available, suited to compressed gases up to 160 psi (11 bar). Disadvantages of plastic pipe work include:

  • Systems may require additional supports
  • Additional expansion requirements over metal systems
  • Ultra violet stability for external applications should be checked
  • Plastic material cannot be used for a flammable or oxidizing gas
  • The plastic may not present such a high quality vapor barrier as metals

In some regions, OSHA regulates against using brittle plastics for compressed air distribution systems.

5.7.6.4 Aluminum

Proprietary systems using aluminum pipe work may be limited in size and connected using plastic push-in connectors. These systems should not be used for a flammable or oxidizing gas.

5.7.6.5 Carbon Steel

Where a gas is kept at a low Relative Humidity (RH) (below 35%), risk of internal corrosion is minimal; therefore, steel is an option for use in a distribution system. If used, galvanized material is usually selected to provide a corrosion resistant external finish.

Jointing has to be specified carefully, to ensure minimal risk of generating loose material or contaminating inside a pipe with hydrocarbon, e.g., welding with a purge gas, or screwed joints made without lubricant/sealant, using non-shedding material such as PTFE tape. For these reasons as well as the general appearance, carbon steel is not generally used.

Testing and Cleaning

Materials specified for use in a processed gas system should be specified to be free of particulates and any hydrocarbon. They should be shipped and stored on site wrapped and sealed. Before use on site, system fittings and components should be inspected to ensure that the internal finishes are as specified and not marked or damaged. A GMP protocol should be used to ensure the system is kept clean during construction, e.g.:

  • no opening left open
  • only clean tools used
  • tools marked for their purpose

Key Note: Limits on Using Copper Pipe in a GxP Environment

Copper pipe is the traditional material for medical gas systems, but in a pharmaceutical GMP environment it has two important limitations:

  1. Not suitable for product areas: Copper oxidizes in the presence of chlorine-containing or strongly alkaline cleaning agents, and the oxide can flake off as particulate contamination. Production areas are cleaned frequently with stronger agents, so copper pipe usually stops at the service corridor and does not enter the production area.

ISPE specifically raises this warning: especially for high-purity nitrogen systems with a low dew point requirement, copper pipe is not a suitable material.

Original Text ISPE

Control and monitoring systems may act as a risk mitigation tool in process gas systems.

Where appropriate in this chapter, a distinction is made between process gas systems and compressed air systems based upon differences in ownership and responsibility; compressed air systems are usually owned by an organization, whereas process gas storage/generation systems are usually rented from a specialist supplier.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Control and Monitoring Systems: The core position of this chapter is that the monitoring system is itself a risk mitigation tool. In other words, even if gas quality fluctuates occasionally, as long as the deviation is detected in real time and triggers a response (automatic valve closure, an alarm, start-up of backup equipment), non-conforming gas can be prevented from reaching the process.

Why the difference in ownership matters: A compressed air system is owned by the site, so instrument calibration, maintenance, and documentation are all managed within the on-site GMP system. Process gases (N₂, CO₂, Ar) are usually leased from a gas supplier. Analogy

This ownership distinction is like a building's own power system (owned and managed by the site) versus a rented backup generator (maintained by the vendor, but you still need to know its fuel level and fault signals). Both must be connected to your central control room to give a complete picture of utility safety.

Key Notes

When process gas is supplied by an external supplier, the site may need to duplicate the monitoring instruments (duplicate monitoring instruments), so that: (1) the gas supplier uses its own standard instruments; and (2) the site uses instruments whose calibration it manages itself, ensuring the data meet GMP documentation requirements. The two sets of instruments run in parallel and do not depend on each other.

6.2 Control Systems

Original Text ISPE

There are three categories of system which are controlled:

  • Compressed Air Systems
  • Cylinder Systems
  • On Site Gas Generation and Bulk Storage Systems

6.2.1 Compressed Air Systems

The compressors normally have a specific proprietary control system. This should control and monitor the compressor, shutting it down in the event of an alarm or sensor failure in order to protect the equipment. Usually compressor control is based on maintaining a minimum pressure at its discharge – on a basic system through shutting down or starting up the compressor, on a more sophisticated system through regulating the compressor output.

There is usually provision for interconnecting these control systems or linking them through a "supervisory" controller that will manage multiple compressors, usually rotating the primary duty unit to ensure equal use of each system.

The dryers within a system typically come with a supplier's proprietary control system. The control systems typically operate based on a timer to control the sequence of operation between the two dryer vessels or the changeover between vessels is controlled by the discharge pressure dewpoint. The latter system is considered more energy efficient.

In the event of a system failure, the alarm from a dryer pressure dewpoint monitor may be used to automatically close the valve downstream of the dryer, as this would prevent putting potentially wet air into a distribution system – where a sudden loss of supply would create problems, this signal could also initiate a standby dryer.

The system status and any system alarms can be fed through a Building Management System (BMS) to facilitate remote monitoring, and management of any alarms.

6.2.1.1 Cylinder Systems

There are two basic types of cylinder systems:

  1. Manual Changeover
  2. Automatic Changeover

Manual Changeover: These require the standby cylinder to be manually selected when the duty cylinder runs on low pressure. These basic systems do not provide capability for remote monitoring, though a low cylinder pressure switch may be provided for connection to either a local alarm, an alarm in the area in which the gas is used, or to a BMS.

Automatic Changeover: The system automatically changes over from a duty to a standby cylinder, and will give an alarm signal to notify the system owner that the empty cylinder needs to be changed. This may be connected to a local alarm, an alarm in the area in which the gas is used, or to a BMS.

6.2.2 On Site Generation Systems and Bulk Storage Systems

These systems are typically supplied and maintained by a specialist supplier; with their own control and monitoring systems.

The design and operation of these systems should be understood by the user, and alarm signals from the system made available for connection to a monitoring system, as required. Users may duplicate monitoring instruments for connection to their monitoring system to allow the gas system supplier to provide a standard package. This also has the advantage that the user retains management of the calibration of the devices.

The integration and monitoring of process gas systems should be considered during the design phase to ensure that the optimum solution is specified.

Where there are defined Lower Explosive Limits (LELs) for gas concentrations, monitoring systems and local safety alarms may be required by local regulations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Differences in control logic across the three types of system:

  • Compressed air systems: Fully owned by the site; everything from the compressor to the dryer to the piping must be brought into the GMP system. The control logic centers on "maintaining a minimum outlet pressure".
  • Cylinder systems: The simplest, with manual or automatic changeover. The management focus is to ensure that a standby cylinder is in place and that a low-pressure alarm is passed to the on-duty personnel or the BMS.
  • On-site generation / large bulk tanks: The most complex; usually monitored remotely by the gas company, and the site must integrate its alarm signals into its own BMS or DCS.

Dewpoint-Controlled vs Timer-Based Switching

There are two switching logics for twin-tower dryers:

  • Timer-based switching: Switches at a fixed time. It is simple but ignores the actual drying performance, so it may switch too early (wasting energy) or too late (dewpoint deteriorates).
  • Dewpoint-controlled switching: Detects the outlet dewpoint and switches only when actually needed, which is more energy-efficient and more stable. ISPE explicitly states that this approach is preferable.

Key safety logic: if the dewpoint monitor raises an alarm, the downstream valve should close automatically to prevent moist air from entering the piping.

Analogy

Changing cylinders manually is like a manual fuel station: you have to check for yourself that the level is low before swapping. An automatic changeover system is like a modern car fuel tank: a light comes on when the level is low, and the system has already switched to the backup bank before the old one runs out, so supply is uninterrupted. For a pharmaceutical continuous process, automatic changeover is a basic requirement; otherwise an interruption in gas supply can directly cause batch failure.

Key Notes - LEL (Lower Explosive Limit)

Lower Explosive Limit (LEL): A flammable gas (such as hydrogen) can explode only once it reaches a certain concentration in air, and the LEL is the lowest concentration that can ignite. For example, the LEL of hydrogen is about 4% (v/v).

N₂ and CO₂ are not flammable themselves, but at high concentrations they create an asphyxiation (oxygen deficiency) hazard, so an oxygen deficiency alarm (O₂ deficiency alarm) is needed rather than an LEL alarm. This is a common misunderstanding in CDMO safety management: the correct type of safety monitoring must be chosen according to the properties of the gas.

CDMO Application

In a CDMO aseptic filling facility, nitrogen blanketing is used to prevent oxidation of the formulation. If the N₂ cylinders use manual changeover and are not replaced in time, the N₂ supply is interrupted, air may enter the filling chamber, the O₂ concentration rises, and the product undergoes oxidative degradation. This is an OOS event that directly affects batch quality and requires a full deviation investigation.

  2. What differences are there in GMP documentation between a dewpoint-controlled dryer and a timer-based dryer? Which is easier to demonstrate compliance for?
6.3.1 Operational Parameter Monitoring

Original Text ISPE

Pressure is often categorized as a CPP (Critical Process Parameter) for a process gas system, usually introducing a concomitant calibration and monitoring regime.

The gas supply system may be controlled by the pressure, either in the system receiver or in the entry point to the distribution system.

The distribution system should be sized so that end users receive the necessary flow and associated inlet pressure, based on design usage diversity or calculated pressure drop under peak usage.

"This Guide proposes that the pressure is usually an engineering parameter and not a CPP."

The basis for this is:

  • Any piece of equipment supplied by the gas system that has a requirement for a specific flow/pressure will have an inlet pressure switch that monitors and alarms for that use; this is a critical location/instrument.
  • The system pressure control provides a general indication, but does not monitor at each POU, or consider any usage, or effect at an individual POU.

It may be appropriate to review specific installations to determine if that rationale is appropriate.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Pressure: a CPP or an engineering parameter? This is an important point raised by ISPE. Traditionally, many sites list the main supply header pressure as a CPP, backed by a strict calibration program. ISPE, however, takes the view that the header pressure is only a "general direction" indicator, and what really matters is the inlet pressure at each point of use (POU).

Logical inference: A normal header pressure does not mean that the pressure at the farthest POU is sufficient (because of pressure drop). The real key is therefore to install a pressure switch at the equipment inlet to directly monitor the gas pressure available to the equipment.

Formula and Calculation

Pressure distribution design logic:

Header design pressure = pressure required at the farthest POU
             + pipe pressure drop (calculated from flow and pipe diameter)
             + safety margin

Pressure drop at peak usage must be calculated for the maximum simultaneous usage
(diversity factor)

Analogy

Header pressure is like the water pressure in a city's main water pipe: the water authority knows the main pressure is normal, but whether the pressure in the top-floor bathroom of your home is sufficient depends on your own supply design. Process gas is the same: what matters is the pressure at the inlet of each piece of equipment, not the system-level header pressure.

Key Notes

This ISPE recommendation has real cost implications: downgrading the header pressure to an "engineering parameter" can reduce the calibration burden and documentation requirements. But this must be stated explicitly in the URS (user requirements specification), and the full risk assessment rationale must be recorded in the DQ/IQ/OQ documents. If a regulatory authority asks during an inspection, the site must be able to explain clearly why the header pressure is not treated as a CPP.

  2. During peak usage (multiple pieces of equipment drawing gas at the same time), how do you confirm that the header pressure will not affect the farthest equipment because of excessive pressure drop?
6.3.2 Quality Monitoring

Original Text ISPE

Quality monitoring is required at POUs located in controlled room environments. Regulations require that the particulate and microbiological quality of the compressed gas be equal to or better than that of the air in the environment into which the gas is introduced (at rest). (See Eudralex Volume 4 Annex-1, 2004; 21 CFR Part 210.)

Process gas systems may be operated at very low dewpoints or negative dewpoints, in accordance with user requirements. A properly operated process gas system with low moisture should not support the growth or survival of microorganisms.

The quantity of water necessary to support microbiological growth is usually expressed as water activity (aw). For gases, water activity is related to relative humidity (RH = aw × 100%) and also dewpoint which may be used to express moisture in a compressed gas. Molds and yeasts do not grow where the aw is less than 0.6 and bacteria do not grow where the aw is less than 0.85 (Scott et al., 2001).

Process gas systems are not normally designed to be sterilized. Control of microorganisms is usually accomplished at the POUs by using 0.2 μm sterilizing grade filters.

Low moisture environments may be used to preserve pharmaceutical products but may not prevent survival of microorganisms:

  1. Desiccation has been a long recognized method for short term preservation of microbiological cultures. Desiccated microorganisms have a variable death rate and a sizable population would have to be introduced into a system to be detectable downstream.
  2. Bacteria may also survive through the production of endospores. Endospores are tough, dormant non-reproductive structures produced by bacteria with the primary function to ensure survival through periods of environmental stress. Endospores may persist indefinitely in a dormant state awaiting conditions favorable for sporulation or return to vegetative growth.

Where the user requirements are particularly demanding, the requirement to ensure that a process gas system facilitates cleaning activities may be included, and the design specified accordingly (e.g., facilitate complete drainage).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Quality Equivalence Principle: When process gas enters the cleanroom, its particulate and microbial quality must be equal to or better than the at-rest classification of the cleanroom it enters. For example, when operating in an ISO 5 / EU Grade A environment, the N₂ delivered must also meet ISO 5. This is an explicit requirement of Annex 1 and 21 CFR Part 210.

Water Activity (aw): Indicates the amount of "free water" in a system that is available to microorganisms. aw = 1 is pure water, and below aw < 0.6 almost no organism can survive. Dry gas (low dewpoint) has an aw close to 0 and is in theory unfavorable to microbial growth, but this does not mean it is sterile!

Key Notes - The Threat of Endospores

Endospores: This is the key to understanding the microbial risk of dry gas. Bacteria such as Bacillus form endospores under harsh conditions, and these can survive for decades under extreme dryness, high temperature, and radiation.

In other words, dry gas can inhibit microbial growth but cannot kill endospores. Once the gas reaches an environment with moisture and nutrients (such as the product), the endospores may revive. This is why product-contact gas must use a 0.2 µm sterilizing-grade filter and cannot rely on a low dewpoint alone to ensure microbial safety.

Analogy

Endospores are like plant seeds: in a desert (dry gas) they stay dormant, and as soon as they meet water (moisture in the product) they germinate and grow. A dry pipe is like a desert: the seeds do not grow there, but they have not disappeared. The 0.2 µm filter is the real barrier that "stops the seeds".

Water Activity Thresholds for Microbial Growth

aw < 0.60  →  molds and yeasts cannot grow (Molds & Yeasts)
aw < 0.85  →  most bacteria cannot grow (Most Bacteria)
aw < 0.91  →  most pathogenic bacteria cannot grow

Conversion:
RH (%) = aw × 100
aw = 0.6 is approximately RH 60%
Very low dewpoint (e.g. -40°C dp) → RH <1% → aw << 0.01

CDMO Application

In the N₂ blanketing design of an aseptic filling line, a 0.2 µm sterilizing filter should be installed at the final point of use (POU) and integrity tested periodically. The microbial monitoring sampling point should be located upstream of the filter (pre-filter), because sampling downstream of the filter may introduce a contamination risk through the act of sampling itself (exposing the sterilized flow path). This corresponds directly to the sampling strategy discussion in ISPE 6.3.2.

6.3.2.1 Test Method Considerations

Original Text ISPE

Test method(s) normally depend on the types of microorganisms that are desired to be detected. Factors that should be considered include the:

  • drug product(s) manufactured within the facility
  • type of process gas
  • regulatory controls
  • manufacturing processes

The drug product manufacturing associated risks should be considered. Parenteral drug products are manufactured to be sterile; therefore, the process gas should be rendered sterile prior to use in the manufacturing process. Non-sterile drug products may be manufactured using process gases that are not rendered sterile; therefore, the introduction of objectionable microorganisms should be considered.

A risk assessment should be used to develop a rationale for the sample point strategy.

Process gas systems may be designed and controlled using combinations of inline and POU filters. Monitoring strategies for inline and POU filter sample locations should be considered. Monitoring may occur upstream or downstream of a filter, or duplex filters may be required.

For example, in the case of gas supply to a sterile operation (e.g., nitrogen blanketing during aseptic filling processes), the process gas sample should be taken before the final sterile filter. As this gas is 0.2 µm filtered for particulate, the process gas quality should be the same pre- and post- the sterile final filter.

To take a clean compressed air sample after the sterile filter requires the sampling site to follow the same level of sterilization as the unit operation. Sampling after a final filter poses a potential risk to the unit operation each time a sample is taken.

Design of sample points should avoid piping common to other utilities such as high purity waters and pure steam.

A sample should represent the quality at a POU; the location used should be demonstrated to represent the quality at the POU.

The type of process gas should be considered. Process air and O₂ should be monitored for aerobic microorganisms. Reduced oxygen content gases such as N₂, CO₂, and Ar should be monitored for microorganisms capable of anaerobic growth.

The size of a sample can affect the monitoring method chosen. Sample size may be based on the sample size required for room classification (in accordance with ISO-14644). A sample size of 0.7 m³ would be required for ISO 5 environments.

Process gas distribution systems are not environmentally classified.

A gas generation and distribution process is a closed environment, with minimal risk of contamination from an external source. Contaminants in pipe work are most likely to come from the process gas or the pipe work; therefore, a sample taken from the longest pipe run is the most likely to find issues resulting from this cause of contamination.

Inclusion of 0.2 μm sterilizing grade filters at a POU can ensure that process gas quality at a POU is equal to or better than that of the air in the environment into which the gas is introduced.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Sampling Location Strategy: ISPE's key recommendation is to place the sampling point "upstream" of the final sterile filter (before the final sterile filter). There are two reasons:

  • The gas quality upstream of the filter = the quality downstream of the filter (because the 0.2 µm filter retains all particles and microorganisms)
  • Sampling downstream of the filter opens the sterilized flow path every time, introducing a risk of external contamination. This is a recurring process risk and is not acceptable.

Longest Pipe Run: Contamination in a closed piping system mainly comes from inside the pipework (piping material, weld joints, retained liquid), so the end of the longest pipe run is the most likely place for problems to accumulate and is the most challenging sampling point.

Aerobic vs Anaerobic Microbial Monitoring

This is an easily overlooked detail:

  • Compressed air, O₂ supply: The environment contains oxygen, so monitor for aerobic microorganisms
  • N₂, CO₂, Ar supply: A low-oxygen environment, so monitor for anaerobic microorganisms
  • ISPE specifically notes that if no aerobic organisms are confirmed during performance testing, routine monitoring may look only for anaerobes (low-oxygen gas systems). This is a science-based way to reduce the monitoring burden, but it needs documented support.

Key Notes - Sample Volume

ISO 14644-1 specifies a particle sampling volume of 0.7 m³ for an ISO 5 environment. A system whose process gas quality must reach ISO 5 should be sampled with the same volume to ensure statistical confidence.

Note: ISO 8573-7 (the microbial standard for compressed air) recommends 1 m³, but this is not widely adopted in industry practice. For routine monitoring, a smaller volume (such as 1 cubic foot) is also accepted, but the initial validation testing should use a larger volume.

Analogy

Sampling after the final filter is like this: a medical staff member has just finished sterilizing surgical instruments, then opens the package to "check" whether they are sterile; every opening of a package is a chance for contamination. The correct approach is to confirm quality before sterilization and to periodically integrity test the filter itself, rather than repeatedly sampling during use.

CDMO Application - OOS Event Handling

Suppose your N₂ microbial monitoring detects anaerobes above the Action Limit at the longest pipe run sampling point. The CDMO's standard response process is:

  1. Immediate isolation: Stop using gas from that pipe run and assess the batch impact
  2. Root cause investigation: Check filter integrity, pipe dead legs, and recent maintenance records
  3. Batch impact assessment: Ask "Did any product contact this gas?" and "Was the filter in place and intact?"
  4. Corrective action: Sanitize/clean the pipe run, replace the filter, and resample to confirm
  5. Documentation: A complete deviation report and CAPA, with reporting to the customer and regulatory authorities where necessary

6.3.2.2 Microbial Monitoring Methods

Original Text ISPE

Methods existing for monitoring environments may be extended to monitor process gases. The recovery medium for the microorganisms should include the growth media used in the environmental monitoring program for room air, surfaces, and personnel (PDA Technical Report No. 13), as a minimum. The medium should include the demonstrated ability to support the growth of a variety of bacteria and fungi.

The sample device should be sanitizable or sterilizable prior to collection of each microbiological sample. Validation or performance qualification of the method should be considered prior to implementation.

Impaction Methods

Slit to Agar Sampler: This method uses a revolving agar plate located at a specified distance from a slit orifice to impinge the sample air flow onto an agar surface. This device can sample a large volume of gas and can provide a time versus concentration relationship. The equipment is large, requires 150 mm agar plates, and is difficult to sanitize.

Impact Sampler: Pulls a stream of air through a metal plate containing many small and evenly spaced holes onto an agar surface. These samplers for process gases are relatively large and difficult to sanitize; however, the entire sample flow path and impaction plate can be steam sterilized. These units have the advantage of sampling a cubic meter of gas in less than ten minutes.

Centrifugal Sampler: These samplers use a fan to pull a cylinder shaped cone of air into the sample head and particles or microorganisms are deposited by centrifugal force onto an agar strip. The sample head can be steam sterilized and the units are light in weight. Special agar strips are required. Special adapters for sampling process gases are available.

Filtration

Filtration is a simple to use and low cost method for monitoring microorganisms in process gases, relative to methods requiring instrumentation. When numerous sample points should be monitored on a regular basis, it may be less costly to stock multiple filter holders than multiple sample instruments.

The method consists of a type of filter holder (plastic or stainless steel), sterile filters, and a calibrated flow meter. The appropriate volume of gas is filtered through the filter and the filter is aseptically transferred to an agar medium for growth and enumeration. The membrane usually has a nominal pore size rating of 0.45 μm.

Membrane Filter Materials

Membrane filters for environmental monitoring may be made of:

  • cellulose nitrate
  • cellulose acetate
  • mixed cellulose esters
  • Polyethersulfone (PES)
  • hydrophilic PVDF
  • gelatin
  • nylon

Users should verify that the filter does not inhibit the recovery of indicator microorganisms. A cubic meter sample may be collected in a twelve minute time period. Gelatin membrane filters are not suitable for compressed gas monitoring.

Liquid Impingement

Liquid impingement methods use a liquid to trap and collect microorganisms. The stream of gas is directed through an orifice at high velocity into the liquid. Microorganisms are forced into the liquid and removed from the gas stream.

Disadvantages may include a glass construction resulting in a fragile device. Improper sample handling may result in false positive data. The method should be qualified to demonstrate that it does not destroy vegetative microorganisms.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Notes

Gelatin membrane filters are not suitable for compressed gas: Gelatin becomes brittle or even ruptures under a dry, high-pressure gas flow, causing sampling failure or false negatives, so this is a material selection error that must be avoided. When validating the sampling method, you should explicitly confirm that the filter material used does not inhibit the target microorganisms (recovery validation).

0.45 µm vs 0.2 µm: Environmental microbial monitoring uses 0.45 µm (which retains bacteria) rather than 0.2 µm, because 0.2 µm has high flow resistance, giving a low sampling flow rate and an overly long sampling time.

Analogy

The four methods are like four ways of fishing: impaction (casting a big net) is highly efficient but the equipment is bulky; centrifugal (a fishing bucket) is light and flexible; filtration (a fishing net) is cheap and can be deployed in quantity; liquid impingement (a fish-tank filter) can capture the most fragile fish (vegetative cells), but the glass equipment breaks easily. Which one to choose depends on the type of "fish" you want to catch and the number of sampling points.

CDMO Application

For a CDMO, the filtration method is usually the most practical: multiple customer products, multiple sampling points, and a need for flexible deployment. But for N₂ sampling in an aseptic filling facility, a steam-sterilizable impaction sampler may be needed, because the sampling itself must not contaminate the system, the equipment must be fully sterilizable, and the sampling operation must be performed in a RABS or isolator environment.

  1. You need to monitor 10 N₂ points of use in the plant at the same time, with a limited budget. Which sampling method would you choose, and why?
  2. In sampling method validation, what is the purpose of the "recovery" test? How would you design this test?
6.3.2.3 Particulate Monitoring

Original Text ISPE

There are several methods and a variety of instrumentation available for detecting, sizing, and quantifying particulates in compressed gases.

Methods include laser particle counting, condensation nucleus counting coupled with scanning mobility particle sizing, differential mobility analysis, and membrane capture. The application of these methods is covered in ISO-8573-4:2001.

The laser particle counter method is commonly used for process gases, due to the moderate sample rate, relatively low cost, and low maintenance requirements. Care should be taken to avoid including moisture as droplets in particle counts and to ensure that the sample hose connecting the particle counter and the sample point does not contribute to the particle count. The tubing should be made of a smooth non-shedding material and as short as possible to avoid particle fallout. The connection to the sample port should avoid introducing exterior air or generation of particles.

Monitoring Process (high/low pressure)

Process gas systems usually supply gas at a flow or pressure that is too high for a particle counting instrument. The flow to the instrument should be restricted to prevent damage to the particle counter, as high pressure may damage the sensor. This is usually accomplished by using a device that supplies an appropriate flow rate to the particle counter while allowing the excess gas to be safely vented.

The monitoring process should ensure that the compressed gas flow to the particle counter does not fall below the point where outside air is introduced into the sample process. Sample points may supply process gas at flow or pressure below this critical threshold and a different technique should be used.

For such low flow/pressure sample points, the gas may be passed through a tube that would allow the pressure to diffuse while allowing the particle counter to sample the airflow without the gas stream being forced into the sensor. A qualification process should ensure that the particle counter is representatively counting particles in the gas stream.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Laser Particle Counter (LPC): The mainstream technology for process gas particulate monitoring. The principle is that gas flows through a laser beam, and the light scattered by particles is detected and counted/sized (usually ≥0.5 µm and ≥5.0 µm). It is commonly used in pharmaceutical plants and complies with the ISO 8573-4 and ISO 14644 standards.

The challenge of high-pressure sampling: Process gas is usually delivered at 6-8 bar or above, but the LPC sensor is designed to operate near atmospheric pressure, and high-pressure gas entering directly will damage the instrument. A flow restrictor or a diluter must be used so that the excess gas is safely vented.

Key Notes - Moisture Interference with Counts

Liquid moisture droplets are counted as "particles" in an LPC, causing falsely elevated counts. This is a source of systematic error, and it must be addressed by:

  • Ensuring the gas dewpoint is well below the sampling environment temperature (to avoid condensation)
  • Insulating or heating the sampling line
  • Using sampling tubing made of hydrophobic material

The converse problem on the low-pressure side: if the gas flow is insufficient, ambient air may enter through the sampling port (Venturi effect), so that ambient particles contaminate the sampling reading, which is the phenomenon of "negative pressure drawing in outside air".

Analogy

Using an LPC to monitor high-pressure process gas is like using an ordinary blood pressure monitor to measure the pressure of a hydraulic press: you first need a pressure-reducing valve to bring the pressure down to a range the instrument can accept and let the excess pressure vent safely before the instrument can measure correctly. The sampling line is like the cuff tube of a blood pressure monitor and must be made of non-shedding material, otherwise the particles from the line itself will contaminate the reading.

  2. What are the requirements for the material of the sampling line? Why should it be "as short as possible"?
6.3.2.4 Moisture Monitoring

Original Text ISPE

Moisture in process gases is usually measured as dewpoint or pressure dewpoint. Methods usually include condensation devices, e.g.:

  • cooled mirror dewpoint hygrometers
  • electrolytic hygrometers
  • impedance hygrometers
  • polymer film relative humidity sensors

Quartz crystal microbalance and Surface Acoustic Wave (SAW) devices are usually too complex for pharmaceutical process gas applications.

When using a hygrometer, the temperature and line pressure should be accounted for during dewpoint measurements. The instruments may be used in line for continuous monitoring or may be used to periodically monitor critical POUs.

The type used should be determined by the degree of accuracy required for downstream processes, maintenance and calibration schedules, costs, and equipment reliability.

Chilled Mirror Hygrometer

The chilled mirror is a widely used measurement device with an accurate field measured dewpoint over a wide range. A surface mirror or polished surface in contact with the gas stream is cooled until condensation forms. The temperature at which condensation is formed is the dewpoint. Chilled mirror hygrometers are negatively affected by particulates in the air stream and by hygroscopic inorganic salts and are considered an expensive instrument routinely used for process gases.

Electrolytic Hygrometer

This method is described in the PhEur and USP as a method for determining water in gases. The method is based on absorption of water by diphosphorus pentoxide to produce phosphoric acid as an electrical conductor. A voltage is applied to the water to produce electrolysis. The resulting current is proportional to the water content and based on Faraday's Law. The technique can provide stable and repeatable data but requires more attention to proper use to provide accurate results than other methods.

Impedance Hygrometers

These are typically ceramic, aluminum oxide, and silicon oxide based sensors. These sensors rely on a change in electrical property such as capacitance, impedance, or resistance of a porous layer in response to moisture in the gas stream. When properly calibrated and maintained, these instruments can provide good quality results. Calibration is required on a regular basis.

Polymer Film Relative Humidity (RH) Sensors

These sensors are similar to impedance hygrometers. These are constructed of a polymer with a hygroscopic dielectric material. As the water content changes, the dielectric properties change and the capacitance of the sensor changes to provide an electrical response. Hygrometers based on this principle offer relatively low cost and good quality results. These types of sensors are less susceptible to particulate contamination.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Dewpoint vs Pressure Dewpoint:

  • Dewpoint: The temperature at which gas condenses when cooled at atmospheric pressure, for example -40°C dp
  • Pressure dewpoint: The dewpoint of the gas at the actual line pressure (for example 7 bar). For the same gas, the pressure dewpoint in a high-pressure line is about 20-25°C higher than the atmospheric dewpoint (because compression increases the relative concentration of moisture).

When comparing against specifications, note that suppliers usually state the atmospheric dewpoint, but your equipment may need the gas as used at atmospheric pressure (after pressure reduction), so convert correctly.

Key Notes

The dual significance of dewpoint monitoring:

  • Product protection: Many drug products (powders, lyophilized products) are moisture-sensitive, and moisture carried in by the gas affects product stability and CQAs.
  • System protection: High moisture causes pipe corrosion, filter blockage, and microbial proliferation. Dryer failure → rising dewpoint → a chain of downstream problems.

The electrolytic method (P₂O₅ method) is listed as an official method by PhEur and USP, which means it carries the strongest credibility in regulatory documents, but it demands high operating skill and site personnel need to be trained.

Analogy

A chilled mirror dewpoint meter is like the gold-standard precision balance: accurate but expensive and delicate. A polymer film sensor is like an electronic scale: cheap, durable, and accurate enough for everyday online monitoring. Choosing between them is like choosing a measuring tool: use the balance for critical applications and the electronic scale for routine monitoring.

CDMO Application

In a CDMO lyophilization process, the N₂ dewpoint must be confirmed to meet specification (for example ≤-40°C dp) before the chamber is backfilled with N₂. Recommended strategy: install an online impedance or polymer film sensor at the POU where the N₂ enters the chamber for continuous monitoring, and periodically check it against a portable chilled mirror dewpoint meter (calibration check). If the online sensor drifts out of range, trigger an OOS investigation immediately and assess whether recent batches are affected.

Practice Questions

  1. Your N₂ dryer has a pressure dewpoint specification of -20°C pp dp at 6 bar. After reducing the pressure to atmospheric, what is the approximate theoretical atmospheric dewpoint? (Hint: the compression ratio is about 7, so a conversion is needed.)
  2. Why does the electrolytic dewpoint meter have "higher operating requirements than other methods"? What do you think needs special attention in the calibration program?
6.3.2.5 Hydrocarbons Monitoring

Original Text ISPE

Compressed air and other process gas systems intended for pharmaceutical applications should be designed and constructed with oil free compressors and hygienic piping. The installation of systems should include the concept of "build clean" where the equipment and piping are supplied in a clean condition and installed in a manner to prevent contamination.

The monitoring of hydrocarbons in compressed gas systems contributes to the qualification phase to ensure the piping is free of oils prior to use. When compressed air systems are appropriately designed and installed, the most significant risk to the system is the introduction of diesel (auto) fumes at the air intake for the compressor. Air intake for compressors should be considered in the design stage and care should be taken to avoid introduction of fumes into the system.

Depending on the air quality in the vicinity of a facility, compressed air quality may need to be monitored on an ongoing basis for high risk contaminants.

Two distinct methods may be identified based on the type of hydrocarbon component targeted:

  1. Total Hydrocarbon (THC)
  2. Total Volatile Hydrocarbons (TVHCs)

Total Hydrocarbon (THC) / Total Oil Mist: These may be detected and quantified using indicator tubes. The tubes contain chemicals that react with an introduced substance and change color. Oil indicator tubes are designed to monitor for specific oils and mists derived from a variety of mineral and synthetic oils. The detection limits are dependent on the volume of gas exposed to the tube medium and the specific type of oil. These tubes are not designed to detect hydrocarbon fumes.

Total Volatile Hydrocarbons (TVHCs): Volatile hydrocarbons are compounds that are either gases or liquids that can evaporate and act as a gas. The method does not detect oil mists. TVHCs may comprise many different types of volatile hydrocarbons; therefore, are measured and expressed as parts per million calculated as methane.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

THC vs TVHC: two different kinds of hydrocarbon contamination:

  • THC (Total Hydrocarbons / Oil Mist): Liquid oil mist, coming from compressor lubricating oil, shaft seal leakage, and so on. Even with an oil-free compressor, oil mist contamination on the intake side may remain. It is detected in real time with indicator tubes.
  • TVHC (Total Volatile Hydrocarbons): Volatile gaseous hydrocarbons such as gasoline vapor and solvent vapor, coming from the ambient air around the compressor intake (parking lots, boiler rooms, laboratory exhaust). They are detected with ppb/ppm instruments and expressed as methane equivalent.

Indicator tubes: Single-use colorimetric tubes, low cost and giving a quick on-site measurement, but with limited accuracy, suitable for qualitative/semi-quantitative use.

Key Notes - Intake Design

The location of the compressor intake is one of the most commonly overlooked safety details in system design. The correct siting principles are:

  • Keep away from parking lots, loading areas, and generator exhaust pipes
  • Place it above ground level (to reduce intake of ground dust and exhaust fumes)
  • Install an air filter (consider activated carbon pretreatment in areas with poor air quality)
  • Sample periodically to confirm the intake air quality

If a new parking lot or construction site opens near the plant, reassess whether the routine TVHC monitoring frequency needs to be increased.

Analogy

Oil mist (THC) is like kitchen oil fumes that are almost invisible to the eye and come directly from the equipment itself; volatile hydrocarbons (TVHC) are like the smoke from a neighbor's barbecue, coming from the external environment and drifting in through the window (the intake). The former is prevented by choosing an oil-free compressor, and the latter by choosing the right intake location and continuous environmental monitoring.

CDMO Application

In a CDMO qualification process, hydrocarbon testing of compressed air is usually performed at the IQ/OQ stage, to confirm that no oil contamination was introduced during installation. But ISPE points out that if the ambient air quality near the plant is poor (for example in an industrial area or next to a parking lot), it needs to be included in a routine monitoring program rather than a one-time qualification only. This is a risk that a CDMO must evaluate in advance when selecting a site or renovating a facility.

  1. Your site has just completed installing a compressed air system, and acceptance testing shows THC (oil mist) readings above specification. What are the possible root causes? How would you rule them out one by one?
  2. TVHC monitoring results are expressed as "ppm calculated as methane". What does this way of expression imply? When the target is a mixed organic solvent vapor, what are the limitations of this method?

Chapter Summary

  • Three types of control system: Compressed air (site-owned, dewpoint-controlled switching), cylinders (automatic changeover + BMS integration), and on-site generation (supplier-controlled, with the site duplicating instruments and managing calibration). Integration must be planned at the design stage.
  • ISPE's position that pressure is not a CPP: Header pressure is an engineering parameter; the real critical monitoring points are at each POU inlet, and the pressure switch built into the equipment is the key instrument.
  • Quality equivalence principle: When process gas enters the cleanroom, its particulate and microbial quality must be ≥ the at-rest classification of that room (Annex 1 / 21 CFR Part 210 requirement).
  • A low dewpoint does not mean sterile: Drying can inhibit microbial growth, but endospores can still survive for a long time in an extremely dry environment. The only reliable barrier is a 0.2 µm sterilizing-grade POU filter.
  • Sampling location strategy: Sampling should be done upstream of the final filter, and the end of the longest pipe run is the most representative risk point; sampling downstream of the filter is high-risk, since every sampling may contaminate the sterilized flow path.
  • Choosing a moisture monitoring technology: Chilled mirror (most accurate but costly), electrolytic (accepted by PhEur/USP), impedance (stable online), polymer film (low cost, particle tolerant). Choose according to the required accuracy and budget; dewpoint vs pressure dewpoint must be converted correctly.
  • The dual hydrocarbon risk: THC (oil mist, from the equipment) is controlled by oil-free compressors and indicator tubes; TVHC (volatile hydrocarbons, from the external environment) is controlled by intake siting and continuous environmental monitoring.
  • The CDMO OOS link: Any process gas quality OOS event must trigger a deviation investigation, assess the batch impact, and decide on release, quarantine, or rejection. Gas quality data are part of the batch record.

7.1 Introduction

Original Text

A risk assessment process is normally part of the Quality Risk Management (QRM) effort. The Quality Risk Management process, as applied to a process gas system, should ensure the reliable delivery of gas meeting the specifications to POUs. The delivery system should not present a high risk of affecting defined quality attributes of the process gas supplied; therefore assuring suitability for the application for which the process gas is used.

This chapter follows the science- and risk-based principles described in ICH Q9, ASTM E2500-07, and the ISPE Guide on the Science and Risk-Based Approach for the Delivery of Facilities, Systems, and Equipment.

Risk management should reduce risks to an acceptable level. Complete elimination of risk is neither practical nor necessary. In a process gas system, sources of risks to product quality and patient safety include:

  • distribution system design
  • operation of system safety features
  • method of generation or source of a gas

In order to apply a science- and risk-based approach, a thorough understanding of the product/process requirements, gas generation/source attributes, and the storage and distribution system are required. The utilization of process knowledge and understanding, which considers both Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs) and Critical Process Parameters (CPPs), will help to optimize the design of a new gas system, or revisions to existing systems.

Relevant CQAs and CPPs should be defined. They will typically be determined by the application for which a process gas is used.

Table 7.1 — Typical Quality Attributes for a Process Gas

The primary drivers of a process gas system's design are: (1) capacity, (2) usage, (3) output quality. A risk assessment should identify risk resulting from specific and critical aspects of a proposed design. Identified critical aspects should be the focus of the subsequent verification effort.

A risk assessment may be simplified for existing systems (i.e., legacy systems), where system functionality and capacity have been demonstrated during the period of use, and the system operating range, performance capabilities, etc., are known.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — QRM and Process Gases

Quality Risk Management (QRM): Derived from ICH Q9, QRM is a systematic methodology for identifying, assessing and controlling quality risks. When applied to gas systems, the core objective is to ensure that every point of use (POU) consistently receives gas that meets specification.

Three sources of risk: design flaws, failure of safety systems, and problems with the gas source. Together these cover the whole life cycle of a gas system.

CQAs vs CPPs in gas systems: CQAs include dew point, oil content and microbial counts; CPPs include pressure, flow and temperature. The essence of QRM is to protect these critical attributes from risk.

Analogy

Think of gas system risk assessment as a hospital infection control program (CCS): you cannot eliminate every bacterium, but you can identify high-risk areas (operating room vs corridor), set appropriate controls, and keep monitoring. Process gas QRM works the same way: the aim is not zero risk, but reducing risk to an acceptable level.

For example, gas used in a Grade A area must meet the Grade A particle specification. This is a frequent focus of CDMO audits.

7.2 Quality Risk Management for Process Gas Systems

Original Text

The Quality Risk Management approach should be applied throughout the life cycle of a process gas system and is described within this Guide to support:

  • development of the User Requirements
  • development and review of the process gas system design
  • determination of the scope of verification testing activities (commissioning, installation, functional, and performance testing)
  • determination of the optimum scope and scale of a calibration program
  • determination of appropriate preventive maintenance programs
  • determination of the scope and frequency of routine monitoring programs
  • to ensure assumptions made during the risk assessment were appropriate during system operation

Risk management provides an opportunity to generate a rationale for scaling activities appropriately. Results may be used to justify reduction, increase, or the omission or inclusion of an activity.

The risk-based approach activities should be appropriately resourced and focused. Effort and time devoted to a risk management process should be commensurate with the potential impact on the supported business process.

The principles used for QRM may be used to review other (risk) areas such as:

  • Environment, Health, and Safety (EHS)
  • identification of revalidation scope and frequency for existing systems
  • assessment of the potential impact of proposed changes

Though, there is usually a desire to keep quality related aspects separate to facilitate future audit.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — Applying QRM Across the Life Cycle

QRM is not a one-time document but an ongoing activity across the entire life cycle of the gas system: from writing the URS, design review, deciding the scope of commissioning, setting calibration frequencies and the preventive maintenance program, to adjusting routine monitoring frequency. Every step is driven by the results of the risk assessment.

Key Note — Effort Proportional to Risk

"Effort should be proportional to the business impact" is especially important for a CDMO. A Grade A gas system in direct contact with product deserves more QRM resources than plant utility compressed air. Do not over-validate low-risk systems, and do not skip critical steps on high-risk systems.

Analogy

QRM is like hospital triage: the emergency room allocates medical resources according to the severity of the condition, rather than giving every patient the highest level of treatment. Gas system QRM works the same way: give high-risk areas sufficient verification resources and reduce unnecessary qualification activity in low-risk areas.

CDMO Application

When a CDMO takes on a new client, it often needs to assess whether the existing gas systems are suitable for the client's product. QRM results directly affect whether additional qualification testing is needed, which POUs need POU filters added, and whether the preventive maintenance program needs adjusting. Decisions supported by a risk assessment are more convincing in client audits.

7.3 Risk Assessment Process

Original Text

7.3.1 Initiating a Risk Assessment

Determining the risks associated with a process gas system requires an understanding of factors such as:

  • site/organization standards and specifications
  • regulatory requirements
  • project approach (contracts, methods, timelines)
  • process/product requirements — used to determine required specification and potential risks
  • anticipated system design specifications: areas served, layout of distribution, environmental conditions at POU
  • system boundaries (what falls within scope)

For existing process gas distribution systems (legacy systems), factors include:

  • whether a single gas distribution system is shared between GMP and non-GMP applications
  • physical boundaries in place (non-return valves, filters) segregating critical and non-critical sections
  • available historical data (deviations, monitoring trends, performed changes)
  • design specifications: initial cleaning and commissioning specifications

The Risk Assessment process involves:

  1. Risk Identification / Hazard Severity
  2. Risk Analysis
  3. Risk Evaluation

7.3.2 Risk Identification / Hazard Severity

Risk identification involves the systematic use of data to identify hazards that could affect CPPs, CQAs, or critical aspects of a process gas system. Potential risks to typical product CQAs from a compressed air system include:

  • hydrocarbon (oil vapor and particulate) content
  • increased moisture levels
  • viable particulates
  • non-viable particulates (other than hydrocarbon)
  • gaseous impurities
  • process gas mix-up (e.g., connection of oxygen container to nitrogen distribution system)
  • reverse flow

Risk Controls may include: (1) system design features, (2) procedural controls.

7.3.3 Risk Analysis

Risk analysis is the estimation of the risk associated with identified hazards. It is the qualitative or quantitative process of linking the severity of a hazard, likelihood of occurrence, and the ability to detect harm.

A phased approach may be employed: initially using a qualitative assessment to differentiate low risks from medium to high risks, followed by quantitative scoring of medium-to-high risks.

7.3.4 Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation compares the identified and analyzed risk against given risk criteria. Risk evaluations consider the strength of evidence for the three fundamental questions:

  1. What might go wrong?
  2. What is the likelihood (probability) it will go wrong?
  3. What are the consequences (severity)?

Outcomes may be expressed qualitatively ("high," "medium," "low") or quantitatively (numeric ranking). Ranking systems should be pre-defined.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Three Elements of FMEA

Gas system risk assessments usually use an FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) framework with three scoring dimensions:

  • Severity (S): the impact of the failure consequence on product quality and patient safety
  • Occurrence (O): the probability that the risk event occurs under the existing controls
  • Detection (D): how easily the hazard is detected once it has occurred

Risk Priority Number (RPN) = S × O × D, used to rank the failure modes that need priority control.

Typical FMEA Example for a Compressed Air System

Failure mode          S  O  D  RPN  Control measure
Compressor failure    4  2  2   16  Standby unit + pressure alarm
Filter blockage       3  3  2   18  Differential pressure monitoring + scheduled replacement
Moisture breakthrough 4  2  1    8  Continuous dew point monitoring + high alarm
O₂ ingress into N₂    5  1  1    5  Check valve + gas analyzer
Oil vapor contamination 4 2  2   16  Oil-free compressor + activated carbon filtration
Microbial contamination 4 1  3   12  POU filter + microbial monitoring

S/O/D scored 1-5 (5 = most severe / most frequent / hardest to detect)

Key Note — Gas Mix-up Risk

Process gas mix-up is among the highest-severity risks: if an oxygen container is wrongly connected to a nitrogen distribution system, it can cause an explosion or product oxidation. Controls include different connector specifications, clear color coding, an ID test procedure before connection, and final confirmation by GC analysis. In a CDMO environment this is a control item that must be written into an SOP.

Analogy

The three-step risk assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation) is like a hospital diagnostic procedure: first list all possible symptoms (identification), assess the severity and likelihood of each (analysis), and finally decide whether it reaches the threshold for treatment (evaluation). Not every risk needs immediate action, but every high-RPN risk must have a corresponding control measure.

  2. Under what circumstances is "reverse flow" most likely to occur? What design controls should be applied?
7.4-7.6 Risk Control, Communication & Review

Original Text

7.4 Risk Control

Risk control includes decision making either to reduce risks or accept them, or both. The purpose of risk control is to reduce the risk to an acceptable level. The amount of effort applied to risk control should be proportional to the significance of the risk.

Risk control addresses the following questions:

  • Is the risk above an acceptable level?
  • What can be done to reduce or eliminate the risk?
  • What is the appropriate balance of risk likelihood/controls?
  • Are new risks introduced as a result of the controls being applied?

Risk reduction follows a hierarchy:

  1. reduction through design changes
  2. reduction through procedural change

Potential for New or Increased Risk: The implementation of risk reduction measures can introduce new risks into a system or increase the significance of existing risks. After implementation, the results of a risk assessment should be reassessed.

Risk acceptance is a decision to accept risk. Risk acceptance can be formal (documented acceptance of residual risk) or passive (residual risks not specified).

7.5 Risk Assessment Communication

Risk communication should ensure that the results of a risk assessment are shared with relevant team members, the system owner, and senior management. Communication can occur at any stage of the risk management process.

The output and result of the Quality Risk Management process should be appropriately documented and communicated. The risk assessment report may be used to assist in the definition of the scope of the required system verification.

The scores given in the risk assessment are normally based on the assumption that the controls defined are in place and operational; to accept the result of the risk assessment, the operation of the design controls should be verified and the procedural controls defined should be confirmed as being in place, with the relevant staff trained on the procedures.

7.6 Risk Review

The risk assessment should be periodically reviewed to ensure that the scoring used reflects the operational experience with the system. The period between these re-evaluations should be defined and based on potential risk and the novelty of the system.

A periodic review may also be invoked by events such as changes in the system use or system modifications.

Data gathered during a risk assessment may be used to identify opportunities to decrease GMP risks. The results may be useful for product review, inspections, audits, or root cause failure investigations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — Hierarchy of Risk Controls

ISPE explicitly specifies the order of priority for risk controls:

  1. Design change (highest priority): eliminate the source of risk at its root, for example switching to an oil-free compressor to eliminate the risk of oil contamination
  2. Procedural control (second): reduce risk through "soft" controls such as SOPs, personnel training and alarm settings

This order of priority is fully consistent with the design principles of the contamination control strategy (CCS) in EU GMP Annex 1.

Key Note — Prerequisite for the Risk Assessment

Key insight: "The risk assessment scores assume that the controls are in place and operating." This means that approving the risk assessment in the acceptance report is equivalent to confirming that the controls are actually running. A CDMO must be able to demonstrate this in a facility audit: the lower your RPN scores, the more robust the evidence of execution your controls require.

Analogy — Risk Acceptance vs Risk Reduction

Risk acceptance is like an insurance decision: you do not buy insurance for every possible accident, but for high-value loss risks (such as a batch scrapped because of gas contamination) you must have clear safeguards. "Passive acceptance" (never defining the residual risk) is not acceptable, like driving without any insurance.

CDMO Application — Triggers for Periodic Risk Review

In CDMO practice, the following events should trigger a review of the risk assessment:

  • System modification or piping change (change control initiated)
  • Introduction of a new product that changes the intended use of a POU
  • Deviation events or repeated OOS results
  • The periodic (for example annual) review cycle is due
  • Improvement requirements following a regulatory inspection

Original Text

8.1 Introduction

This section describes the installation, functional, and performance testing processes for process gas systems. These processes should demonstrate that a process gas system is "suitable for its intended purpose."

For process gas systems supplying product or product primary containers, performance testing should demonstrate that the supplied process gas quality meets the gas specifications required at the POUs.

Risk assessments may be used to develop an initial testing and ongoing performance monitoring strategy. Utilization of the results of a risk assessment can assist in optimizing system design, and define critical aspects of the system performance that require verification.

8.2 Engineering Turnover Package (ETOP)

The Engineering Turnover Package (ETOP) is considered one of the main construction deliverables. The ETOP is composed of three sections:

1. Construction Manager's Section — should contain:

  • a copy of the basis of design (User Requirements)
  • a set of issue for construction drawings and specifications
  • a copy of the submittals
  • a copy of any relevant permits
  • a change record history
  • a Request for Information (RFI) history
  • the construction quality check records, including third party inspections where appropriate
  • a copy of the final (closed out) punch list
  • the formal contractors certificate of completion

2. Installation Contractor's Section — should contain:

  • "red line" or "as built" record drawings and specifications
  • equipment list and instrument list
  • copies of calibration certificates or certificates of conformity
  • copies of warranties
  • welders certification or equivalent
  • weld procedures or equivalent
  • calibration certificates for construction equipment used
  • system and equipment operating and maintenance manuals

3. Commissioning Section — should contain:

  • walk down "as built" record drawings
  • verified equipment list and instrument list
  • commissioning report for the equipment/system/controls
  • initial system test records

Monitoring the construction and completion of a project using the ETOP throughout the project to ensure timely and competent completion can save both time and money at the end of a project.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Role of the ETOP

The Engineering Turnover Package (ETOP) is the bridge document between "construction complete" and "start of qualification". Its three components correspond to three different responsible parties:

  • Construction manager: ensures the design intent is correctly implemented
  • Installation contractor: provides complete documentation of the equipment installation
  • Commissioning team: records the initial performance baseline of the system

The ETOP is the main source of data for IQ: the validation engineer obtains drawings, equipment lists and calibration records from it and compares them with the actual site conditions.

Key Note — Common Audit Findings

The most common ETOP problems found in CDMO audits:

  • Missing or inconsistent P&ID versions: the "as-built" drawing does not match the actual piping, especially when later system modifications did not update the P&ID
  • Missing calibration records: instrument installation acceptance certificates or factory calibration certificates were not filed in the ETOP
  • Incomplete welding records: welder qualification certificates for high-purity gas piping are missing
  • Punch list not fully closed: remaining items for improvement were not recorded at handover

Analogy

The ETOP is like the inspection report when a new house is handed over: the builder must provide complete construction drawings, material specifications, plumbing and electrical diagrams, and all test certificates. Without complete handover documents, even if the house is built, you do not know what material the pipes buried inside are made of. The ETOP for a gas system follows exactly the same logic: it is your legal evidence that the system has a clean provenance.

Key Note — Differences Between Gas Systems and Water Systems

ISPE explicitly states that qualification of gas systems does not need to test drainage slope, dead legs, surface roughness of materials or other routine items of water systems. This is because gas does not collect water, and microorganisms cannot multiply in dry gas. A CDMO's qualification protocol should not blindly apply a water system IQ checklist to a gas system.

Original Text

The scope and extent of the commissioning activities for a process gas system depends on the system specifications and design.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Boundary Between Commissioning and Qualification

This is a key conceptual boundary in pharmaceutical engineering:

  • Commissioning: led by the engineering department and performed according to GEP, it demonstrates that the system is installed and operates as designed. QA pre-approval is not required.
  • Qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ): led by QA/validation and performed according to GMP requirements, it requires QA pre-approval of the protocol.

ISPE's "Science and Risk-Based Approach" (ASTM E2500) framework allows commissioning data to be fully leveraged to support qualification, avoiding duplicate testing and saving cost and time.

Analogy

Commissioning is like the quality control test before a new car leaves the factory: the factory tests the brakes, engine and electrical system to ensure everything works to specification. Qualification (in a GMP environment) is like the clinical validation of a medical device before launch: documented verification against predefined specifications in the actual use environment, with final approval and release by the quality unit.

Key Note — Pressure Testing and Purging Records

Pipe pressure testing and purging records are core commissioning items. For high-purity gas systems:

  • Pressure testing is usually performed at 1.5 times the maximum working pressure to confirm piping integrity
  • The system must be thoroughly purged before performance testing to remove construction residues (metal chips, welding residue)
  • Passivation records apply to stainless steel high-purity gas piping

CDMO Application — Project Management Perspective

When a CDMO undertakes a new plant build or capacity expansion project, it is advisable to specify the ETOP delivery requirements and timing clearly in the contract, and to designate which commissioning data will be referenced in the qualification protocols. 8.4 Installation and Operational Tests (IQ/OQ)

Original Text

Installation and operational tests are usually conducted in accordance with the design specifications and contracts, with the results documented in the commissioning document. The organization's requirements for engineering documentation should be specified, so that quality departments are able to use the engineering document to provide the evidence of installation and operation to meet the design specifications.

A typical P&ID walk down procedure:

  • Obtain the copy of the latest revision of the P&ID with all changes incorporated, usually as construction redline drawings.
  • Perform a walk down of the P&ID, red-lining it to reflect the as-built condition. Use an indelible ink pen of the following colors:
    • Red: indicate additions or corrections
    • Green: indicate deletions
    • Black: enter comments or notes
    • Yellow highlighter: show what sections have been verified
  • Highlight all equipment, valves, instruments, lines, etc., to indicate installation per latest P&ID.
  • Highlight tag numbers on the P&ID to confirm correct tagging.
  • Upon completion, the person performing the walk down signs and dates "Verified by" on the P&ID.
  • The system owner reviews the red-lined P&ID and signs "Reviewed by".
  • Place items such as missing tags, incomplete insulation, etc., on the Punch list.
  • Review the changes and errors and decide if the installation is acceptable or if errors need correction (punch list items).

Note that for compressed air systems, where the compressor/dryer capacities are critical, an independent certificate (e.g., a CAGI certificate) may be required as part of the specification.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Significance of the P&ID Walk Down

The P&ID walk down is one of the core activities of IQ. Its purpose is to confirm that the actual field installation fully matches the design drawings, record all differences on a "redline drawing", and finally decide whether these differences are acceptable or need to be added to the punch list for the contractor to correct.

For a CDMO, the IQ protocol usually references the P&ID walk down records completed during commissioning, without repeating the same steps. This is exactly the benefit of science and risk-based qualification.

Key Note — Managing the Punch List

The punch list is a "list of issues to be resolved". As a prerequisite for qualification, all punch list items that affect system function must be closed out. Open items that may remain are usually limited to cosmetic or administrative issues with no effect on system performance, and these must be supported by a written risk assessment. A common audit finding is that important punch list items are still open when the qualification report is approved.

8.5-8.6 Performance Testing & Acceptance and Release

Original Text

8.5 Performance Testing

Initial system performance testing and ongoing monitoring may be performed by an organization's quality unit. Performance testing should demonstrate correct system functionality under procedural control. This usually takes place after the commissioning tests have demonstrated that the system is capable of performing to meet the design specifications.

A test plan for verifying the system performance with a supporting rationale should be included in the performance testing document. Performance testing is normally performed in two phases:

  1. an initial set of tests to confirm installed system performance and release the system for use
  2. an ongoing test plan to provide assurance that the critical performance parameters are being maintained (typically part of the routine monitoring program)

Performance testing usually consists of sampling for the quality attributes at each POU under normal operating conditions. Each POU should be sampled at least three consecutive times over a defined period and the data obtained should be evaluated for compliance with predefined acceptance criteria.

Initially monitoring of the POUs may be performed at more frequent intervals to further demonstrate adequate system performance over time. Once sufficient confidence of correct system performance has been acquired, based upon a statistically representative data set, the monitoring frequency may be decreased appropriately.

8.6 Acceptance and Release

Completion of the verification phase should include a verification (qualification) summary report which states whether the system is fit for its intended purpose and can be released for use. This report should confirm that:

  • A system has been installed according to the design specification.
  • Instrumentation is calibrated in accordance with the design specifications.
  • The system operates in accordance with the design specifications.
  • Risk controls identified during any risk assessments are in place and operating as specified.
  • The system performance testing results complied with the acceptance criteria.
  • Operating and maintenance procedures are established.

Approval of the verification (qualification) summary report by the system owner, engineering department, and validation/quality representative is normally considered confirmation that the system is suitable for intended use and is released for operational use.

Ownership should be transferred from the project team to the operations/engineering departments. Once the report is approved, formal Quality Assurance (QA) change and deviation management should be applied to the system.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Link Between OQ and PQ

"Performance Testing" of a gas system is equivalent to a combination of OQ (operational qualification) and PQ (performance qualification) in the traditional qualification framework:

  • OQ-like: confirms the system operates within specification, such as pressure control, flow and alarm functions
  • PQ-like: samples at each POU to confirm gas quality meets specification (dew point, particles, microorganisms, oil content)

The requirement of "at least three consecutive samples" is the minimum standard for statistical confidence; for new systems or high-risk applications, consider increasing the number of samples.

Key Note — Managing OQ Failure Scenarios

In CDMO practice, if performance testing produces an OOS result (for example the O₂ analyzer reading exceeds the limit after an N₂ purge), the standard investigation process is:

  1. Confirm that the instrument calibration status is normal (to rule out measurement error)
  2. Extend the purge time and resample
  3. Check the sealing integrity of fittings and valves (to rule out ingress of outside air)
  4. Confirm that the system purging records are adequate
  5. Assess whether the OQ acceptance criteria need adjusting (this requires a scientific basis)
  6. All investigation steps must be documented in writing

Key Note — QA Control After Handover

After the qualification report is approved, the system is formally handed over to operations. All subsequent system modifications, changes to instrument calibration windows, changes to filter specifications and adjustments of operating parameters must be handled through formal Change Control. This is a frequent audit focus area, especially the question of whether "the system was modified after qualification without notifying QA".

CDMO Application — Approval Structure of the Qualification Report

A standard qualification summary report is approved by three parties: the System Owner, an engineering representative, and a QA/validation representative. The three signatures amount to a formal declaration that the system is fit for GMP use. A CDMO should ensure the approval level is consistent with client expectations, especially when an outsourcing client provides the qualification strategy.

9.1 Maintenance

Original Text

9.1.1.1 Risk Assessment Results
The results of a risk assessment should be reviewed to determine their effect on the maintenance strategy. Where maintenance is identified as a control, the scope and frequency of maintenance activities should be commensurate with the risk.

9.1.1.2 Changing of Filters

Key Concepts — The Regulatory Basis for Preventive Maintenance (PM)

EU GMP (EudraLex Vol. 4, Annex 1) and FDA 21 CFR 211.68 both require an equipment log to be kept for the maintenance of pharmaceutical manufacturing equipment. A gas system is a "utility that directly affects the process", so its preventive maintenance program must:

  • Be executed on schedule with execution records
  • Have a scientific/risk-based rationale for setting PM items

Key Note — Change Control for Filter Replacement

POU filters are the critical barrier between GMP and non-GMP gas systems. Any change to filter specifications (pore size, material, supplier) must go through formal Change Control, including:

  • Impact assessment (does it affect CQAs?)
  • Repeating the filter integrity test where necessary
  • Updating the PM procedure and equipment list
  • Notifying downstream user departments

"Simply replacing a consumable with the same specification" usually does not require full change control, but a clear Approved Supplier List is needed to define what "the same specification" means.

Key Note — Differential Pressure Is Not the Only Indicator for Filter Change

ISPE explicitly states that differential pressure should not be used alone as the filter change indicator unless it is measured under known flow conditions. The reason: if flow decreases (for example because actual consumption falls), differential pressure also falls, which may give a false impression that the filter is in good condition even though it has reached the end of its life. It is recommended to combine time-based and differential pressure criteria in the filter change program.

9.2 Calibration

Original Text

A calibration strategy should be defined during the system design. This should consider the features of the specific design, the reliability, function, and likely failure modes of sensors. Equipment suppliers usually can provide advice on calibration methods and frequencies.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — Risk-Based Calibration Strategy

ISPE emphasizes that the calibration strategy must be driven by the results of the risk assessment. In practice, gas system instruments are tiered by criticality:

  • Product Critical: POU dew point sensors, O₂ analyzers, microbial sampling equipment → strict calibration frequency (for example every 6 months) + investigation initiated on any calibration failure
  • Process Critical: system pressure sensors, flow meters → standard calibration interval (for example annually)
  • Non-Critical: engineering pressure gauges, instruments in non-GMP areas → extended calibration interval or replacement by visual inspection

Analogy

A calibration strategy is like the frequency of routine health checkups: every 3 months for patients at high risk of heart disease, once a year for the average person, perhaps once every 2-3 years for young, healthy people. You do not give everyone the same frequency of checkups; you decide based on "how serious are the consequences if this indicator is wrong?" The logic of gas system instrument calibration is exactly the same.

Key Note — Impact Assessment of Calibration Failure

When a critical instrument fails calibration (the reading exceeds the permitted tolerance), the CDMO must:

  1. Immediately isolate the instrument to avoid continuing to make decisions based on incorrect readings
  2. Assess all release decisions made using that instrument's readings since the last passed calibration date
  3. If batches were released during this period, initiate a retrospective deviation investigation
  4. Determine whether the cause is an equipment problem or a procedural problem, and take CAPA

The rigor of this process directly reflects the GMP requirements for control of measurement systems.

10.3 Moisture Content

The moisture contained in outside air is compressed with the air in the compressor. As the air is compressed the temperature will rise, increasing the moisture carrying content despite the smaller volume.

As the air cools after compression, moisture will separate (condense) from the air as it goes through the compressor after-cooler, where it drains (usually from a skid mounted post compressor filter – shown as a pre-filter/cyclonic separator in the schematic). The air will then go through the intermediate filters (filter A and B) into the dryer.

The dryer will reduce the air moisture content of the air until it reaches a condition of at least -40°F (-40°C) dewpoint. This is monitored by a pressure dewpoint instrument in the compressed air dryer discharge by a PDP instrument.

Note that the dewpoint is measured with the air at atmospheric pressure – a small amount of air is taken from the outlet, and passed over the measuring sensor. A decrease in the PDP, e.g., from -41°F to -38°F (-41°C to -39°C) (i.e., increase in the moisture content) will give an alarm that is given visually and audibly locally, and repeated to the building management system.

The system pipework is all metallic, thus providing a very robust moisture barrier, separating the compressed air from the atmosphere. There is, therefore, very little opportunity for moisture to be taken up by the air during distribution.

The system integrity can be confirmed initially by checking the moisture content in the air after the dryer, then at the outlet furthest from the dryer (i.e., the one with the longest length of pipework); therefore, testing the air that has passed though the longest pipe run with greatest surface area, providing the maximum opportunity for moisture ingress.

"As this aspect of the system performance will not change through the life of the pipe system, routine monitoring is not considered to be required. The PDP monitor, however, will be considered a critical instrument used to confirm continuously the condition of the compressed air supplied to the system."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Index Run (longest pipe run): The longest piping path from the compressor to the end outlet. The logic for sampling at this point is the "worst-case principle": if the gas quality on the longest path is acceptable, all shorter paths must also be acceptable. This is an efficient, scientific sampling strategy that avoids blindly sampling at every outlet.

Continuous monitoring vs. periodic sampling: A PDP monitor is continuous, whereas manual sampling is periodic. For critical parameters (such as dewpoint), continuous monitoring should be given priority and connected to BMS alarms; this is more reliable than sending someone to sample periodically.

Analogy

Imagine you manage the hot water supply system of a building. As long as the inlet temperature (water heater outlet) is acceptable and the water at the farthest bathroom (longest pipe run) is also acceptable, you can infer that the hot water quality throughout the building is on target; you do not need a thermometer in every bathroom. This is the essence of the "Index Run" strategy: using the worst-case point to represent the whole system.

Key Notes

Metal piping = a natural moisture barrier: Copper/stainless steel piping is itself an excellent moisture barrier and hardly lets external humidity permeate. Therefore, once the initial validation confirms pipe integrity, routine moisture monitoring can rely on instruments rather than manual sampling.

System modifications require re-validation: "Modification of the system would require appropriate testing." After piping is modified or extended, the Index Run must be re-validated. This is an important trigger point for change control, and in a CDMO environment it is especially easy for engineers to overlook.

10.4 Hydrocarbons/Oil

Hydrocarbon particulate and vapor contained in the outside air may be slightly reduced by the compressor air inlet filter – a coarse filter designed primarily to protect the equipment from damage. The remaining particles/vapor will then be compressed with the air. Depending on the type of hydrocarbon, it will remain as a vapor or form particulate; typically, the particulate size range is in the range 0.2 to 0.3 µm. The intermediate filters (Filters A and B) will remove particulate down to 0.01 µm; therefore, removing any hydrocarbon particulate, down to 0.01 mg/m³.

Another potential source of hydrocarbon contamination is the pipework, with the associated jointing process used. The engineering specifications call for degreased material, with any threaded joints in the system made without a hydrocarbon based lubricant.

Note that the final filters (Filters C and D) are not rated for oil vapor removal.

Initial testing will be pre- and post- the intermediate and from the point of use on the index run (longest pipe run) of each floor of the building served by the system.

The initial test will determine:

  • The challenge to Filters A and B (if the local environment is reasonable, the air delivered from the compressor may meet the specification with no further treatment)
  • The output air quality from the system intermediate filters (Filters A and B)
  • Any measurable contamination from the compressed air distribution pipework

10.5 Viable and Non-Viable Particulate

Coarse particulate in the outside air will be filtered out through the compressor air inlet filter. Particulate will be removed by the coalescing intermediate filters (Filters A and B) and by the commercial grade 1 µm final system filters (Filters C and D). Product protection (in this example, the product is an oral solid dose) would be provided by local 0.45 µm filters installed at each outlet where air will contact product, product contact surfaces, or product primary containers.

The initial test strategy is proposed to demonstrate that the system filtration is adequate to provide air meeting the specifications at the generation system, and that the distribution system is clean, i.e., free of particulate.

The ongoing monitoring proposed is intended to demonstrate that the system can reliably provide air meeting the specifications from the generation system.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Coalescing Filter: A special filter designed to capture oil aerosol. Oil mist droplets coalesce on the filter media into larger droplets, which then drain away by gravity. This type of filter removes both particulates and liquid oil contamination.

The dual source of hydrocarbons: This section reveals that hydrocarbon contamination has two sources: (1) hydrocarbons in the external ambient air, and (2) lubricants or solvents left over from pipe installation. The initial sampling must design test points for both sources.

Key Notes

Final filters cannot remove oil vapor: Filters C & D are particulate filters, not oil vapor filters. Oil vapor (vapor phase) can only be effectively removed by the intermediate filters A&B. If A&B fail, oil vapor may break through to the POU; this is a key scenario that design verification and ongoing monitoring need to cover.

0.45 µm POU filter: This is a typical specification for oral solid dosage (OSD) forms and can retain most microorganisms. For sterile products, the POU may need a higher specification (0.2 µm sterilizing grade).

Analogy

Imagine the ventilation system of a food processing plant: the coarse screen at the inlet (compressor intake filter) blocks leaves and large dust particles, but bacteria and cooking fumes (analogous to oil mist) need a finer HEPA filter (analogous to Filters A&B). The final filter at the air conditioning outlet (analogous to Filters C&D) can filter particulates again but cannot deal with oil that has already vaporized, so the cleanliness of the piping at the start (degreased installation) is critical.

Dewpoint as a microbial control mechanism: This is the most important insight in this section. The contents of this file may include, as applicable:

  • A system as built record drawing (P&ID showing test points)
  • Critical filter test data
  • Particle monitoring results
  • Hydrocarbon test results
  • Microbial test results
  • Gas identity
  • Moisture content
"The 'package' of data produced should be reviewed to ensure that the initial performance 'baseline,' established during the performance verification is maintained and performance deterioration is absent."

There are a number of approaches to the system risk assessment. One approach is:

Step 1 – Define Quality Attributes / CPPs

The quality attributes/CPPs for the gas required at the POU are defined and documented together with the supporting rationale, e.g.:

  • The compressed air is supplied to an ISPE Grade 8 area. The specified particle count and microbial limits applied to the free air delivered are the same as the environment being supplied.
  • Moisture content is considered a critical factor as: (a) it may influence the microbial performance of the gas distribution system; (b) the product is moisture sensitive.
  • The hydrocarbon limits are defined as 0.5 mg/m³ in the organization quality standards for this application, due to risk of taste/color influence on the product coating.
  • Note that pressure is not considered a critical parameter as far as the system is concerned; if it is critical for the correct function of the system supplied by the gas, then that system will incorporate a pressure monitoring device.

Step 2 – Define How CQAs/CPPs are Monitored

  • The particle count is routinely taken at the supply point to the distribution system – reference SP1, and at the point of use with the furthest run of distribution pipe work – reference POU28.
  • The microbial levels are routinely taken at SP1 and at POU28.
  • The system PDP is monitored by a calibrated instrument on the dryer discharge, with a hard wired connection to the dryer discharge valve operated from the PDP instrument alarm.

Step 3 – Define How CQAs/CPPs Are Achieved & Equipment Risks

  • The system particle count is controlled by the system filters – standard commercial units within the distribution system's boundary, changed following a PM procedure on a time base.
    • The system is performance verified by testing at SP1 and at POU28 – i.e., with the maximum pipe work length.
    • These two points will be routinely monitored to confirm satisfactory system performance.
  • The system microbial levels are controlled by the system filters and also influenced by the Pressure Dewpoint (moisture content) of the air.
    • Performance verified by testing at SP1 and at POU28.
    • These two points will be routinely monitored.
  • The system hydrocarbon content is controlled by the system filters. The compressor is an oil free unit, so the challenge is due to atmospheric pollution and any potential contamination due to materials or construction techniques.
    • Initial installation performance verified by testing at compressor discharge, SP1, and POU28.
    • Challenge routinely checked by monitoring the hydrocarbon content at the discharge from the compressor.
  • The system Pressure Dewpoint (PDP) is controlled by the dryer. The dewpoint is continuously monitored by a calibrated instrument on the dryer discharge, with a hard wired connection to the dryer discharge valve operated from the PDP instrument alarm.

The equipment to be verified and maintained under quality change control is therefore the PDP monitor on the dryer, the distribution system including the final filters and pipe work up to the points of use, i.e., the components in the system that the risk assessment has identified as risk controls, and detection mechanisms.

Other aspects of the system are controlled through good engineering practices.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

System Monitoring File: This is the core document package during an audit. The P&ID (piping and instrumentation diagram) marking the test points, filter test data and the historical monitoring data of all CQAs should be consolidated into one traceable "system health record". This is not only a quality requirement but also an engineering management best practice.

Three-step risk assessment method: (1) define CQAs/CPPs → (2) define how they are monitored → (3) define how they are achieved and identify equipment risks. This is a structured, end-to-end approach from requirements to design to monitoring, consistent with the spirit of FMEA in ICH Q9.

Analogy

This three-step risk assessment is like a doctor diagnosing a patient: Step 1 is confirming the patient's vital signs (blood pressure, temperature, blood oxygen); Step 2 is deciding which instruments to use for continuous monitoring (ECG, pulse oximeter, scheduled temperature checks); Step 3 is confirming how to keep these indicators normal (medication, surgery, infusion) and identifying equipment that could fail (batteries, calibration problems). Only equipment placed under change control is a "critical piece of equipment" of the system; the rest can be managed by good engineering practice.

Key Notes

Pressure is not a critical parameter, but this judgment needs a rationale: The conclusion that "pressure is non-critical" is not arbitrary; it is based on the engineering logic that "each point of use has its own pressure monitoring equipment, and the gas system itself cannot precisely control the pressure at every POU". Both the judgment and the rationale must be documented.

The boundary of Quality Change Control: Only equipment identified by the risk assessment as a "risk control" or "detection mechanism" (PDP monitor, system filters, piping) needs to be brought under quality change control; other equipment (such as general mechanical parts of the compressor) can be classified as engineering-managed. This distinction has real practical significance in a resource-limited CDMO environment.

CDMO Application

One of the questions a CDMO is most often asked during a customer audit is: "How do you know this gas system is under control?" A correct answer requires showing: (1) a P&ID marking the sampling points; (2) completed PQ test data; (3) an SOP and trend analysis for ongoing monitoring; (4) a change control procedure covering modifications to critical equipment. This system monitoring file is the best tool for responding.

It is considered good practice to review the instrumentation on a process gas system to determine the relative importance of the instruments, considering the system requirements.

Doing this helps to enable users to categorize the instruments into a hierarchy:

  • Product critical
  • Process or system critical
  • Safety or environmental critical
  • Non-critical or for information only

For further information refer to the ISPE GAMP Good Practice Guide: A Risk-Based Approach to Calibration Management (Reference 21, Appendix 8).

Once the instruments are categorized so that their function is clearly defined, an appropriate calibration strategy can be developed and applied.

System Description (Calibration Example)

The site compressed air is fed through a breathing air purifier then distributed to the use points through a medical grade copper pipe work. Each user point is fitted with a 0.3 micron filter, pressure regulator, and gauge connected to a female quick release coupling.

The users are all breathing air hoods – each hood is fitted with a male quick release coupling, and a variable area flow meter.

The breathing air unit is fitted with a number of alarms, including low inlet pressure, or high inlet pressure. These will be regularly checked as part of the equipment planned maintenance program.

However, the air purifier periodic maintenance will include a visual inspection for operation and damage; the unit will be replaced if it is found to be damaged or non-operational.

2. Purifier Outlet Pressure Gauge — Purchased as a commercial un-calibrated device; this is considered non-critical to be used for engineering information purposes only – flow should be checked at the outlets periodically. Routine calibration is not required. The air purifier planned maintenance will include a visual inspection for operation and damage; the unit will be replaced if it is found to be damaged or non-operational.

3. Low Pressure Switch on Air Purifier Discharge — This is considered safety critical and will be calibrated annually, with the associated alarm functionally checked.

4. Outlet Pressure Gauges — Purchased as commercial un-calibrated devices considered non-critical to be used for engineering information purposes only – flow will be checked at the outlets each time the breathing air hood is used and each time the hood is set up for use per the operating procedure.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Instrument Hierarchy:

  • Product Critical: Directly affects product quality attributes, such as the PDP monitor (affects the moisture CQA). Requires the strictest calibration program.
  • Process/System Critical: Affects normal operation of the system but does not directly affect product CQAs, such as the sensor of an automatic drain valve.
  • Safety/Environmental Critical: Does not affect product quality but affects personnel safety or environmental compliance, such as a low pressure alarm switch.
  • Non-Critical/Informational: For engineering reference only and does not affect any critical decision. Most local display gauges.

Analogy

This calibration hierarchy is like the importance grading of a car dashboard: the speedometer (critical, affects safety) needs regular verification; the engine coolant temperature gauge (system critical, affects vehicle life) needs regular checking; the cabin temperature display (non-critical, comfort information) can simply be replaced when it breaks and does not need calibration. In a gas system, the PDP monitor is the "speedometer" and a non-critical flow meter is the "cabin temperature display"; each has a different maintenance strategy.

Key Notes

"Non-critical" does not mean unmanaged: Non-critical instruments do not need periodic calibration, but they still need: (1) installation verification (confirming correct installation); (2) periodic visual inspection (for damage); (3) replacement when damaged. These activities should be included in the preventive maintenance (PM) program rather than left entirely unattended.

Safety critical = annual calibration + functional test: The low pressure switch is a safety device protecting users (personnel wearing breathing masks), and its failure could lead to oxygen deficiency. Therefore, even though it does not affect product CQAs, it must be calibrated annually with an added functional test (alarm test). This is a dual requirement of regulation and plant safety management.

"Single-point verification" vs. "full calibration": A non-critical outlet pressure gauge receives only a "mid-point single-point check" each year (the middle of 40-50 psi), rather than a full multi-point calibration curve. This is a pragmatic allocation of resources: use the minimum effort to confirm that the instrument is not obviously damaged, rather than pursuing full accuracy.

CDMO Application

The most common mistake when a CDMO builds a calibration plan for a gas system is a "one size fits all" approach: applying the same frequency and method to every instrument. The ISPE tiered strategy lets you: concentrate limited calibration resources on product critical and safety critical instruments; reduce the calibration frequency of non-critical instruments (without eliminating PM visual checks); and explain your decisions during an inspection with clear classification logic. This is more convincing than "we calibrate all instruments every 6 months" because it demonstrates risk-based thinking.

  • Sampling strategy derives from risk assessment: Not every outlet needs to be sampled. The "Index Run" (longest pipe run) strategy uses the worst-case point to represent the whole system and is a scientific and efficient design method.
  • Dewpoint control is the foundation of microbial control: A -40°C PDP drives system RH to about 5% (aw = 0.05), far below the growth lower limit of any microorganism (bacteria, mold), so microbial monitoring can follow the same logic as non-viable particulates.
  • Initial testing establishes the baseline; ongoing monitoring confirms it is maintained: PQ testing covers all CQAs (moisture, hydrocarbons, total particulates, viable particulates); ongoing monitoring frequency is adjusted dynamically based on the filter change cycle rather than a fixed schedule.

  • Instrument calibration has four tiers: product critical > process critical > safety critical > non-critical. The tiered strategy concentrates limited resources on the truly critical instruments, and non-critical instruments are managed by PM visual checks and replacement rather than blindly applying full calibration.
  • CDMO application: A gas system SOP must meet both quality (CQA monitoring) and engineering (PM, change control) needs; the system monitoring file (P&ID + all test data) is the core document package for inspections.

14.2 Pressure/Flow Controllers

System controllers, such as pressure/flow controllers, can improve the performance of systems. Their primary function is to stabilize system pressure separate from and more precisely than compressor controls. A pressure/flow controller does not directly control a compressor and is generally not included as part of a compressor package. A pressure/flow controller is a device that serves to separate the supply side of a compressor system from the demand side.

The type of control system specified for a given system is largely determined by the type of compressor being used and the facility's process demand profile. If a system has a single compressor with a very steady demand, a simple control system may be appropriate. A complex system with multiple compressors and widely varying demand will require a more sophisticated control strategy. This is the situation when the generation of compressed air emanates from the same source for both process and instrument air applications in a pharmaceutical manufacturing environment.

The pressure/flow controller can also be used as a back pressure flow device capable of maintaining pressure on critical applications during temporary interruptions in supply.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Why Leakage Is a GMP Issue and Not Just a Cost Issue

In general industry, gas leakage is mainly understood as a financial loss. In a pharmaceutical environment, however, leakage has more serious GMP implications:

  • Nitrogen blanketing systems: if a nitrogen line has a tiny leak point, ambient air (containing O₂, moisture and microorganisms) can back-diffuse into the system during pressure fluctuations, destroying the inert protection
  • Compressed air systems: a leak point can become an entry point for external contaminants, especially when the system is shut down (insufficient positive pressure)
  • Gases used in aseptic filling: any ingress of particles or microorganisms can result in batch rejection

  • Bubble test: suitable for detecting localized leaks at joints and valves
  • Helium tracer: highly sensitive leak detection, suitable for ultra-high-purity gas systems

The IQ protocol should clearly state the acceptable leak rate specification (for example, <0.1% per hour).

Think of the compressor as a city water tower and the equipment in each plant area as the water users. A pressure/flow controller works like a constant-pressure valve in each building: it ensures that pressure fluctuations on the supply side (the water tower) are not passed on to the demand side (the individual points of use). In pharmaceutical aseptic filling, pressure stability directly affects filling accuracy, so this isolation function is essential.

15. Appendix 6 – Nitrogen Gas Generation Systems

Nitrogen generation facilities operate based on one of three operating principles:

  1. membrane
  2. adsorption
  3. cryogenic

Membrane Process

The membrane process generates nitrogen purities ranging from 95% up to 99.5%. A unit would consist of an air compressor, filters, pre-heater (if required) membrane cartridges, piping, valves, and the control system. The process can be described as:

  • Compressed dry air enters the cartridge on one end.
  • Oxygen, carbon dioxide, and residual humidity permeate faster than nitrogen through the membrane wall, thus enabling a side venting of those gases to the atmosphere.
  • On the other cartridge end, the nitrogen exits and, depending upon the application, leaves the unit direct to the user's process or to a suitably dimensioned buffer tank.

The output of a membrane cartridge is dependent upon three factors:

  1. the pressure of the feed air
  2. the temperature of the feed air
  3. the required purity level of the nitrogen

For example, if temperature increases, then selectivity decreases, but capacity increases. If pressure increases, selectivity will increase. Pressure and temperature are adapted to the product specification by designing the air compressor and the pre-heater. System capacities can be changed by varying the number of cartridges in the unit.

Adsorption Process (PSA)

The adsorption process generates nitrogen purities ranging from 98% up to 99.99% or even higher. Each unit contains two molecular-sieve adsorbers together with the necessary air compressor, piping, valves, and control system. For adsorptive extraction of nitrogen from air, Carbon Molecular Sieves (CMS) are used as the adsorption agents. Under pressurized conditions, these adsorb oxygen, water vapors, and carbon dioxide, letting the nitrogen pass through the orifices of the sieve. To regenerate the molecular sieve after it has become loaded with oxygen, it is necessary to blow it back down to atmospheric pressure and purge it.

Production and regeneration phases are performed alternately at regular intervals. While one adsorber is in the production phase, adsorbing oxygen into the sieve under pressure and allowing the nitrogen to pass through, the other is regenerated by evacuating the pressure to the atmosphere. The performance of a PSA unit depends on the ambient temperature. The required purity and product pressure have an influence on the air demand and, of course, on the energy consumption.

Cryogenic Process

The cryogenic process generates nitrogen purities from 1 to 100 ppm oxygen. The cryogenic process separates air by means of rectification, which utilizes the different evaporation temperatures of the components of the air. A cryogenic plant mainly consists of a warm end (with an air compressor and a molecular sieve station) and a cold part (coldbox) with the rectification column where the actual air separation process takes place.

Inlet filters remove dust and other physical impurities from the air before it enters the air compressor, where it is compressed to the required process pressure. It is then precooled in the after-cooler or in a refrigeration drier. After passing through a moisture separator, the air enters one of two molecular adsorbers, where impurities (such as water, carbon dioxide, most hydrocarbons etc.) are removed. The process air is cooled down to almost liquefaction temperature in the main heat exchanger in counter-flow with the cold separation products and then fed into the bottom of the rectification column. There, the feed air is rectified. The pure nitrogen fraction is withdrawn at the top of the column, heated up in counter-flow in the main heat exchanger, and then fed into the product line. Cooling is supplied by Liquid Nitrogen (LIN) from the back-up system or is generated using an expansion turbine.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - Comparing the Three Nitrogen Generation Technologies

The three technologies differ fundamentally in purity, cost and scale, and the selection decision has a major impact on the long-term operation of a pharmaceutical plant:

  • Membrane: the simplest; purity 95-99.5%; suitable for low-purity needs (inert protection, general blanketing)
  • PSA (Pressure Swing Adsorption): purity 98-99.99%; the mainstream choice for on-site generation at medium to high consumption
  • Cryogenic: the highest purity (1-100 ppm O₂); suitable for ultra-high-purity needs; very high capital cost

Key Point - The Critical Limitation of PSA Nitrogen (a Selection Decision Point)

This is the most important engineering decision point in this appendix:

Typical maximum PSA purity = 99.5% N₂ = minimum 0.5% O₂ = 5,000 ppm O₂

If the application requires O₂ < 10 ppm (for example, oxygen-free packaging of highly potent APIs, lithium battery manufacturing, or inert blanketing of certain biological products), a PSA system simply cannot meet the specification. Liquid nitrogen (LIN) supply or a cryogenic generation system must be chosen.

A wrong technology selection is the error most easily overlooked during URS review, yet it is only discovered during OQ testing, causing substantial rework costs.

Selection Comparison Matrix

Technology     Purity limit    O₂ residual    Suitable application
Membrane       99.5% N₂       0.5% O₂       General inert protection
PSA            99.99% N₂      100 ppm O₂    Medium-to-high-purity on-site nitrogen
Cryogenic      99.9999% N₂    1 ppm O₂      Ultra-high-purity needs

Economic break-even (PSA vs liquid N₂):
Consumption > ~2,000 m³/month → on-site PSA generation is usually more economical
Consumption < ~500 m³/month  → purchased liquid N₂ plus a storage tank is usually more economical

Analogy - The Twin-Tower Alternation Principle of PSA

The two adsorption towers of a PSA unit are like two relay runners: one is running (producing nitrogen and adsorbing oxygen) while the other catches their breath (depressurizing and regenerating, purging the adsorbed oxygen). This alternation lets the system deliver nitrogen continuously, unlike a single-tower system that must stop for regeneration. The molecular sieve is the runner's stamina: it tires (becomes saturated with O₂) but recovers periodically.

In Practice - Nitrogen Selection for a CDMO Aseptic Filling Line

In an aseptic filling CDMO, nitrogen uses generally fall into two categories:

  • Product-contact: headspace nitrogen flushing of vials and solution blanketing → usually requires pharmacopoeial-grade purity, with O₂ <10-100 ppm → liquid N₂ or a cryogenic system must be used
  • Non-product-contact: equipment blanketing and safety inerting → a PSA system is sufficient

Many CDMOs therefore install both a PSA system (for non-contact use) and a liquid N₂ storage tank (for contact use) to balance cost and purity requirements.

After a hot shower, the bathroom mirror fogs up (condenses) because the moisture in the air exceeds the saturation level corresponding to the mirror's temperature. The same happens in a compressed air line: if any section of the line is colder than the dew point of the gas, moisture condenses on the pipe wall and forms standing water. This is both a source of pipe corrosion and an "oasis" for microorganisms. The dryer's job is to keep the dew point well below the lowest temperature the piping may reach.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Hierarchy of Standards for Gas System Quality

The quality specifications of pharmaceutical gas systems are jointly defined by a multi-tier system of standards, and it is essential to understand the order of precedence of each tier:

  • Tier 1: Regulatory requirements - 21 CFR 210/211 (US), EU Annex 1 (Europe), ICH Q7/Q9 - must not be undercut
  • Tier 2: Pharmacopoeial specifications - USP, PhEur, JP gas monographs - cited directly in CoAs and batch records
  • Tier 3: Industry standards - ISO 8573-1 (compressed air purity classes), ANSI/ISA (instrument air) - the design basis
  • Tier 4: Guidance documents - ISPE, PDA, CGA - best practice, not legally binding

Key Point - ISO 8573-1:2010 Is the Key Design Standard

ISO 8573-1:2010 (compressed air purity classes) is the core reference standard for setting the quality specification of a compressed air system, and classifies air by three parameters:

  • Particle class: Class 1 = most stringent (≥0.1 µm <20,000/m³)
  • Humidity/water class: defined by pressure dew point (PDP); Class 1 = PDP ≤ -70°C
  • Oil class: Class 1 = oil content ≤ 0.01 mg/m³

Pharmacopoeial requirements for medical air generally correspond to ISO 8573-1 Class 1-2. Citing ISO 8573-1 classes in the URS is the standard way to define the purity specification.

Analogy - The Role of Pharmacopoeial Gas Monographs

Pharmacopoeial gas monographs (such as USP <Medical Air> and PhEur <Nitrogen>) are like the "national standard ingredient list" of food standards: they define the minimum requirements for each gas to qualify as pharmaceutical grade. Pharmaceutical companies cite these monographs in their CoAs, and regulators use them to judge whether a gas qualifies for use in manufacturing medicinal products. An internal specification that goes beyond the pharmacopoeial specification may be tighter, but never looser.

18.2 Definitions

Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API)
Any substance or mixture of substances intended to be used in the manufacture of a drug (medicinal) product and that, when used in the production of a drug, becomes an active ingredient of the drug product. Such substances are intended to furnish pharmacological activity or other direct effect in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or to affect the structure and function of the body. (ICH Q7)

Blanketing
Blanketing is the term used to describe putting a layer of gas, typically nitrogen into the space in a vessel above the product generally with the intention of preventing oxygen in the air contacting and degrading the product, though it may also be used to maintain a low oxygen atmosphere to reduce risks of explosion.

Critical Aspects
Are typically functions, features, abilities, and performance characteristics necessary for the manufacturing process and systems to ensure consistent product quality and patient safety. They should be identified and documented based on scientific product and process understanding. (ASTM E2500-07)

Critical Process Parameter (CPP)
A process parameter whose variability has an impact on the critical quality attribute and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces the desired quality. (ICH Q8(R2) II 4.0)

Critical Quality Attribute (CQA)
A physical, chemical, biological, or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality. CQAs are generally associated with the drug substance, excipients, intermediates (in process materials) and drug product. (ICH Q8(R2) II 2.2)

Dewpoint
The dewpoint is the temperature to which a given parcel of humid air must be cooled, at constant barometric pressure, for water vapor to condense into water. The condensed water is called dew. The dewpoint is a saturation temperature.

GxP Compliance
Meeting all applicable pharmaceutical and associated life-science regulatory requirements.

Subject Matter Expert (SME)
Those individuals with specific expertise in a particular area or field. SME responsibilities include planning and defining verification strategies, defining acceptance criteria, selection of appropriate test methods, execution of verification tests, and reviewing results. (ASTM E2500-07)

Supply Air
Conditioned air supplied mechanically to the space (room), measured here in cubic feet per minute (CFM). (Divide by 0.6 to get cubic meters per hour CMH.)

System Owner
The person ultimately responsible for the system performance. This person may be the head of the functional unit or department using that system, or maintaining the system although the role should be based on specific knowledge of the process rather than position in the organization. The system owner is responsible for ensuring that the system and its operation is in compliance and fit for intended use with applicable Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) throughout its useful life.

Process Gases
Process gases are defined as gases which can affect product quality.

Turnover Package (TOP)
A collection of pertinent design, construction, vendor, and operational documentation. This collection of documentation is used for the qualification and process validation activity, as well as reference and single source information for the life of any particular system, process, or piece of equipment. Note: This term is often used interchangeably with Engineering Turnover Package (ETOP).

Non-Liquefied Gases
Non-liquefied gases are elements, or combination of elements, that have boiling points lower than -130°F (-90°C). Examples include: nitrogen, oxygen, argon. When specific elements become liquefied at extremely low temperatures they are referred to as "cryogenic liquids."

Liquefied Gases
Liquefied gases are those elements or compounds that become liquid in their containers at ambient temperatures and at specified pressures from 25 psig (172 kPa) to 1500 psig (10,340 kPa). These gases have boiling points in the range of -130°F (-90°C) to 68°F (20°C).

Supercritical Fluid Extraction (SFE)
Supercritical Fluid Extraction (SFE) refers to a process in which a Supercritical Fluid (SF) is used as a solvent. At critical temperatures and pressures, SFs possess the properties of both the liquid and gas. The most widely used SF gas is carbon dioxide (CO₂).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept - The Practical Use of the Glossary

This glossary is more than a dictionary. In a gas system qualification program it serves three practical functions:

  • Consistency assurance: ensures that the whole document set (URS, DS, IQ, OQ, PQ) uses the same term definitions, avoiding challenges during inspections
  • Cross-functional communication: engineering, QA and production staff share a common understanding of CPP, CQA and PDP
  • Regulatory alignment: all definitions come from official documents such as ICH Q8/Q9 and ASTM E2500, with the source cited directly

Key Point - 10 Key Terms Essential for Gas System Qualification

  • PDP (Pressure Dewpoint): the main measurement of compressed air dryness
  • POU (Point of Use): sampling and acceptance specifications should be verified at the POU, not at the supply end
  • Blanketing: the technical term for nitrogen protection, distinct from "purging"
  • TVHC (Total Volatile Hydrocarbon): the measurement for oil contamination in compressed air
  • ETOP/TOP (Engineering Turnover Package): the full document set handed over to QA after system completion
  • FAT (Factory Acceptance Test): pre-installation qualification testing completed at the supplier's site
  • LEL (Lower Explosive Limit): a key parameter in the safety design for flammable gases (O₂, H₂)
  • PRV (Pressure Regulating Valve): a pressure control element in the system whose set point must be confirmed in IQ
  • CQA/CPP: the basis for classifying which gas parameters require PQ verification
  • GEP (Good Engineering Practice): the design basis for low-risk facilities/equipment that do not require full qualification

Analogy - Division of Roles Between System Owner and Process Owner

The System Owner is like the building's facilities manager, responsible for ensuring that the elevators, air conditioning and fire protection systems operate properly and comply with regulations. The Process Owner is like a tenant of the building, responsible for the production activities carried out within that space. When a problem occurs in the gas system, the System Owner handles infrastructure compliance, while the Process Owner handles the batch impact assessment; the division of CAPA responsibilities between the two should be clearly defined in an SOP.

Core Concept - Blanketing vs. Purging vs. Pressurizing

These three nitrogen usage modes are easily confused, but their purposes and operating parameters differ:

  • Blanketing: maintaining an inert gas layer above a liquid surface or product to prevent contact with O₂/moisture. Usually a static or low-flow continuous make-up mode
  • Purging: actively displacing the air in a vessel or line with a nitrogen flow, performed before use or after cleaning. Requires a higher flow rate
  • Pressurizing: using nitrogen to establish positive pressure to prevent ingress of external gas (for example, a sealed reactor) or to drive liquid transfer

The URS must clearly distinguish between application modes, because they correspond to different purity specifications and flow calculations.
