1.1 Background and Purpose

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p7

Transfer of manufacturing processes and analytical procedures between facilities or laboratories is a necessary part of pharmaceutical development and commercialization. Technology transfers may utilize the outputs of process and method development activities and/or documentation of established processes and methods. Knowledge of the product and the manufacturing process is the basis for transfer to a different location (which could be a different site, country, or facility on the same site) where a process or analytical procedure will be operated.

This Guide focuses on how technology transfer can be achieved.

Technology transfer is defined in ICH Q10 paragraph 3.1.2 as follows:

"The goal of technology transfer activities is to transfer product and process knowledge between development and manufacturing, and within or between manufacturing sites to achieve product realisation. This knowledge forms the basis for the manufacturing process, control strategy, process validation approach and ongoing continual improvement."

Technology transfer projects may take place at various points during the product lifecycle. Successful transfers depend on robust project management processes combined with appropriate product and process understanding. They require partnership, cooperation, and coordination between the sending and receiving units to ensure successful and efficient completion, such that the receiving unit can manufacture, test, and release a safe, efficacious, and quality product comparable to that of the sending unit.

Technology transfer projects are dependent on the development and characterization of robust processes that allow consistent and predictable operation of these processes. Similarly, development of robust analytical methods enables timely transfer of methods. These are particularly important aspects; if processes are not well developed and sources of variation are not well known, e.g., due to incomplete or inadequate process/product knowledge, then technology transfer will not be robust.

This Guide has been designed to present industry good practices for successful and efficient execution of technology transfer projects and to achieve a balance between risk management and cost effectiveness while aligning with applicable regulatory expectations, as described in ICH Q7, Q8(R2), Q9, Q10, and Q11 and other regulatory documents.

The impetus for technology transfer varies and may be based on several factors:

  • Progression in a product development lifecycle, from development through scale-up to commercialization
  • The need for additional manufacturing capacity driven by increased demand or risk mitigation
  • The need to streamline capacity when volumes fall, e.g., at patent expiry, when products change commercial status from novel to generic, etc.
  • Strategic requirements to relocate business units, e.g., regional economic advantages, regional requirements for local manufacture, global expansion in different regions of the world, etc.
  • Identification and qualification of local analytical facilities for testing of intermediates and finished products to meet regional regulatory requirements
  • Dual sourcing strategies to mitigate demand variability or to reduce sourcing risks from manufacturing sites
  • Ensuring adequate turnaround times for analytical laboratories
  • Utilization of virtual organizational structures and increasing use of third parties

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Essence of Technology Transfer

Technology Transfer: It is not "copying a process" but the systematic transfer of product and process knowledge, so that the receiving party can independently manufacture a product of the same quality as the sending party.

The ICH Q10 definition points to a key idea: the goal of TT is product realisation. The end result is a patient receiving a safe and effective medicine, not just the completion of a document handover.

Three core elements:

  • Knowledge: Includes process parameters, analytical methods, CQAs, CPPs and tacit operating knowledge
  • Partnership: The sending and receiving parties must genuinely collaborate, not merely hand over in one direction
  • Process Robustness: A process that is not robust cannot be transferred successfully

Analogy: Moving House vs. Replicating a Restaurant

Most people think of technology transfer as "moving house": pack up the equipment and documents and reopen somewhere else.

In reality, it is closer to having a head chef replicate a Michelin-starred restaurant in a different city: the recipe (SOP) matters, but the chef's feel, the quality of the ingredient suppliers, the subtle differences in kitchen equipment and the habits of the local staff - this "tacit knowledge" is what decides success or failure.

The root cause of a failed technology transfer is often not an equipment mismatch, but that this "hard-to-articulate knowledge" was never fully passed on.

Key Point: How Risk Differs Across the Three Strategic Drivers

The motivations for TT listed in this guide fall into three categories, each with a very different risk profile:

  • Lifecycle progression (Dev→Commercial): Product knowledge is still evolving; the biggest risk is transferring in haste before the process is adequately characterized
  • Capacity expansion/risk diversification: The process is already validated; the risks are equipment differences between sites and scale-up
  • Strategic business relocation: Political, organizational and cultural factors dominate; technology is often not the biggest obstacle

As a receiving CDMO, you must determine before contract signing which type of transfer this is. Each type calls for completely different protective mechanisms.

1.2 Rationale for this Third Edition

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p8–p9

The Guide has been updated to ensure alignment with relevant guidance publications and terminology used by industry and regulators. Practical case study examples have been added to this third edition; see Chapter 9 (Appendix 2) and Chapter 10 (Appendix 3).

This third edition highlights the following:

  • Alignment with science and risk-based (Quality by Design (QbD)) principles described in ICH Q8(R2), ICH Q9 Quality Risk Management, ICH Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System, and ICH Q11, including reference to Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP), Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs), Critical Process Parameters (CPPs), material attributes, design space (where used) and, in particular, control strategy
  • Alignment with the process validation concepts and lifecycle approach as described in:
    • ICH Q10 and Q11
    • FDA Guidance for Industry – Process Validation: General Principles and Practices (January 2011)
    • EMA Guideline on process validation for finished products (November 2016)
    • EU GMP Annex 15 Qualification & Validation and similar PIC/S guidances
    • EMA Guideline on process validation for biotechnology-derived active substances (April 2016)
  • Recognition that knowledge management is a critical component of effective technology transfer. Knowledge includes:
    • Scientific and operational information
    • Product quality attributes (e.g., CQAs and material attributes)
    • Overview of process, including unit operations (CPPs, material attributes, etc.)
    • Control strategy
    • Continuous/continual improvement ideas and plans
    • Tacit knowledge (i.e., knowledge from experience rather than explicit knowledge) and means to communicate this knowledge
    • Health, Safety, and Environment (HSE) requirements
    • Learning points from previous collaborative activities
    • Implications for use of specialist tools and techniques such as Process Analytical Technology (PAT), multivariate data analysis, calibration of models, advanced data analytics, etc.
    • Current understanding of potential risks to product quality
  • Industry developments and potential regulatory impacts:
    • Technology transfers driven by acquisitions
    • Global expansion to developing countries
    • Facility consolidation within a company
    • Clinical to commercial transition within the same or different facilities
    • Supply chain driven events (e.g., economic drivers)
    • Utilization of Contract Manufacturing Organizations (CMOs) and contract development organizations, including virtual organizations
    • Increased utilization of platform and single-use systems
    • Introduction of generics and biosimilars
    • Cost efficiencies, either capital or revenue driven
    • Reduced cycle times
    • Local regulatory requirements
  • Recognition that having a robust quality culture, for both sending and receiving units, is important for ensuring successful technology transfer. This is particularly important where cultures are very different between sending and receiving units, which may be due to internal organizational structures, management styles, country specifics, or just individual approaches; these differences should be recognized early and steps taken to ensure appropriate understanding and alignment during the technology transfer.

Helpful guidance is also given in WHO Technical Report No. 961, Annex 7 and the Japan National Institute of Health Sciences Guideline for Technology Transfer (Draft). The FDA Guidance for Industry – Contract Manufacturing Arrangements for Drugs: Quality Agreement, November 2016 also mentions transfers.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Major Additions in the Third Edition

Compared with the second edition, the three most important developments in the third edition are:

  • QbD integration: The logic chain QTPP → CQAs → CPPs is now built explicitly into the TT framework. TT is no longer about "packing up documents and moving them over" but about transferring the entire body of scientific understanding
  • Elevated status of knowledge management: "Tacit knowledge" is explicitly listed for the first time as a type of knowledge that must be transferred - the hardest part, and the most important
  • Case studies (Appendices 2 & 3): Real-life examples for biologics and small molecules have been added, which is the most practical new content in this edition

Analogy: The Challenge of Tacit Knowledge

Tacit knowledge is like a master craftsman judging the state of a fermenter by sight: he knows "something just feels off" but cannot put a specific number on it.

In TT, this kind of knowledge includes: "This step has to be added more slowly; the SOP doesn't say so, but going faster causes problems," or "When this supplier's raw material changes lot, we always have to run a qualification."

Converting tacit knowledge into explicit documentation (such as process observation videos and operator interview records) is the most time-consuming investment in TT, but also the most worthwhile.

Key Point: Differences in Quality Culture Are the Most Underestimated Risk

The guide places particular emphasis on differences in "quality culture", which is especially critical in cross-border CDMO transfers.

Common scenario: A European or US originator is used to risk-based deviation management, while the Asian receiving site tends toward zero tolerance for deviations. Result: the receiving site stops the line at every minor deviation, and the transfer timeline stretches to three times as long.

Solution: Define the quality culture differences between the two parties explicitly at the Charter stage, and establish an agreed deviation handling protocol.

Practical Application: What the Third Edition Means for CDMOs

The "CMO utilization" newly added in the third edition is explicitly listed as one of the drivers of technology transfer, which signals that ISPE formally recognizes the central role of CDMOs in the global pharmaceutical supply chain.

For CDMOs this means:

  • The rapid spread of single-use systems requires CDMOs to have both SUS and SS (stainless steel) capabilities
  • The growth of biosimilars means many TT opportunities for established branded products (where the sending party holds mature knowledge)
  • The trend toward virtual organizations: the sponsor has no plant of its own, so the CDMO is both the receiving party and the actual custodian of the knowledge

1.2.1 ICH Q10 Pharmaceutical Quality System and Technology Transfer

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p10–p11

ICH Q10 illustrates the Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS), and states:

"The PQS covers the entire lifecycle of a product including pharmaceutical development, technology transfer, commercial manufacturing, and product discontinuation as illustrated by the upper portion of the diagram."

ICH Q10 covers the following topics in relation to technology transfer:

  • Product Lifecycle: The product lifecycle, in relation to technology transfers, includes "New product transfers during Development through Manufacturing" and "Transfers within or between manufacturing and testing sites for marketed products."
  • Knowledge Management: "Product and process knowledge should be managed from development through the commercial life of the product up to and including product discontinuation... Knowledge management is a systematic approach to acquiring, analysing, storing and disseminating information related to products, manufacturing processes and components. Sources of knowledge include prior knowledge; pharmaceutical development studies; technology transfer activities; process validation studies; manufacturing experience; innovation; continual improvement; and change management activities."
  • Management of Change in Product Ownership: "When product ownership changes, (e.g., through acquisitions) management should consider the complexity of this and ensure: (a) The ongoing responsibilities are defined for each company involved; (b) The necessary information is transferred."
  • Application of Process Performance and Product Quality Monitoring System throughout the Product Lifecycle: "Monitoring during scale-up activities can provide a preliminary indication of process performance and the successful integration into manufacturing. Knowledge obtained during transfer and scale up activities can be useful in further developing the control strategy."
  • Application of CAPA System throughout the Product Lifecycle: "CAPA can be used as an effective system for feedback, feedforward and continual improvement."
  • Application of Change Management System throughout the Product Lifecycle: "The change management system should provide management and documentation of adjustments made to the process during technology transfer activities."
  • Application of Management Review throughout the Product Lifecycle: "Aspects of management review should be performed to ensure the developed product and process can be manufactured at commercial scale."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The ICH Q10 PQS Lifecycle Framework

ICH Q10 explicitly lists "technology transfer" as a formal part of the product lifecycle, alongside "development, commercial manufacturing and product discontinuation." This means TT is not a one-off event but part of the ongoing operation of the PQS.

Knowledge Management as defined in ICH Q10 comprises four actions:
Acquiring → Analysing → Storing → Disseminating
Technology transfer is the extension of this knowledge management cycle along the dimension of "location."

Analogy: Technology Transfer Is Your Company's "Knowledge DNA Replication"

Imagine a company's process knowledge as the DNA in a cell. Technology transfer is cell division: the goal is for the daughter cell (the receiving party) to obtain a complete and functional copy of the DNA.

If the original DNA is defective (the process is not fully characterized), the daughter cell will carry the same defect. This is why the guide repeatedly stresses that process robustness must be assured before transfer.

Key Point: The Special Role of Change Management in TT

ICH Q10 explicitly requires that all process adjustments be recorded during TT. This has important implications for a CDMO:

  • Every process modification during TT must be covered by formal change control documentation
  • The fact that "we are still in the transfer phase" does not exempt anyone from change control requirements
  • These records will be reviewed in future regulatory submissions and in PPQ

In practice, the change management log from the TT period often becomes one of the most important appendices to the validation report.

  2. CAPA in TT: if a PPQ batch shows a deviation, should the CAPA be led by the sending or the receiving party? What is the rationale?
1.2.2 Product Lifecycle

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p11–p12

Technology transfer expectations are different during the various phases of the product lifecycle. This Guide addresses the transfer of technology from a sending unit to a receiving unit, which may occur at any time during the lifecycle. This Guide assumes all work performed prior to initiating a transfer phase is adequate for enabling transfer of drug substance and/or drug product processes and analytical methods during the product lifecycle.

Reference to the three stages of the process validation lifecycle described in FDA Guidance for Industry – Process Validation: General Principles and Practices is helpful, particularly Stage 1 (Process Design) which relates to product and process understanding.

The adequacy of product and process understanding is fundamental to successful technology transfer. It starts in design and development and captures knowledge in a development report (e.g., includes initial document review, summaries of experiments, QTPP, CQAs, material attributes, identification of sources of variation, and potential risks to product quality). For generics, historical data on product and process understanding should be documented.

Typically, limited development occurs during the technology transfer phase. Exceptions during transfers can be handled by using risk-based approaches when, for example:

  • There are incomplete characterization or process development
  • Scale-up is needed
  • It is decided to start transfer very early (e.g., due to differing production and development site locations) and to make feasibility batches and carry out development work

Utilization of stage gates is an effective approach to initiating and managing a transfer when development and/or characterization activities are occurring in parallel to the transfer.

Investment in experience in the sending unit with the elements of the process or method being transferred can be used to accelerate transfers. Examples include:

  • Solvent runs: Running the process with process solvents only. Useful to complete definition and test the effectiveness of site specific requirements in procedures, automation, and sampling strategies.
  • Live cell runs (in biological processes): Can elucidate site specific sensitivities to process equipment and utilities by executing cell culture stages and monitoring performance.
  • Simulation activities: Can be used to complete the transfer package and test effectiveness without requiring either plant or laboratory time. Often termed table tops, make-a-batch, simulation runs, virtual batch, or test-a-batch.
  • Engineering runs: Executed prior to the initiation of process validation and typically without full GMP controls. Valuable to obtain experience with processes which have significant differences from the sending unit knowledge base, e.g., changes in scale and equipment.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Stage Gate Mechanism

Stage Gates are a project management tool: a "must-meet condition" is set at each TT milestone, and if it is not met, the project cannot proceed to the next stage.

Typical TT Stage Gates:

  • Gate 1: Technology Package completeness confirmed → only then can site preparation at the RU begin
  • Gate 2: Equipment/facility qualification complete → only then can engineering batches be run
  • Gate 3: Engineering batch results meet acceptance criteria → only then can PPQ be initiated
  • Gate 4: PPQ successful → only then can formal commercial release proceed

Stage gates turn "parallel development and transfer" into a controlled risk rather than uncontrolled chaos.

Formulas and Calculations: Comparison of Four Techniques for Accelerating Transfer

Technique       GMP req.    Cost    Acceleration    Suitable scenario
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Solvent runs    Low         Low     Medium          Defining chemical synthesis processes
Live cell runs  Medium      Medium  High            Equipment sensitivity in bioprocesses
Simulation      None        Low     Medium          Verifying document completeness
Engineering     Low         High    Highest         Large scale-up or equipment differences

CDMO recommendation: use simulation first (lowest cost), then decide whether engineering runs are needed based on the degree of difference.

Key Point: The Golden Rule of "Limited Development" During Transfer

"Typically, limited development occurs during the technology transfer phase" - this is the principle most easily violated in TT.

Common failure scenario: after the transfer starts, the sending party says "let's optimize the formulation while we're at it," with the result that the receiving party has no validated baseline to compare against, and the subsequent comparability study cannot demonstrate equivalence.

Rule: the process must be frozen during transfer. Any optimization must go through formal change control, and the transfer plan must be re-evaluated.

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p12–p13

1.2.3 Intellectual Property and Confidentiality Agreements

Sending and receiving units would normally agree at an early stage what intellectual property can be shared between them or with third parties. From a commercial viewpoint, this is an important fundamental agreement that needs to be implemented early on before a transfer can be organized or its scope quantified. Intellectual property agreements and related confidentiality agreements are typically company and product specific.

1.2.4 Quality Agreements

A quality agreement is required when using a contract partner. It should be a separate document and includes the following aspects:

  • Data integrity
  • Data management and responsibility
  • Information technology requirements
  • Continued Process Verification and its reporting
  • Any continual improvement initiatives
FDA Guidance defines a quality agreement as:

"A quality agreement is a comprehensive written agreement between parties involved in the contract manufacturing of drugs that defines and establishes each party's manufacturing activities in terms of how each will comply with CGMP."

The FDA guidance states that quality agreements should cover manufacturing activities and change control associated with these activities. Manufacturing activities include quality unit activities, facilities and equipment, materials management, product specific considerations, laboratory controls, and documentation.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: IP Agreement vs. Quality Agreement

The two agreements serve different functions, but both must be in place before TT begins:

IP / NDA (confidentiality agreement): Determines "which knowledge can be shared and to what extent," which directly affects the completeness of the Technology Package. Until the IP agreement is signed, the sending party will not disclose core formulation or process secrets.

Quality Agreement: Defines "who is responsible for quality," in particular the allocation of CGMP compliance obligations. Under the FDA's 2016 guidance, a quality agreement is a legal prerequisite for CDMO collaboration.

Key Point: Five Contract Pitfalls for the Receiving CDMO

Before signing the TT agreement, the receiving party must confirm:

  1. IP ownership: Who owns the IP in process improvements made by the CDMO during transfer?
  2. Data integrity responsibility: Who is responsible for ensuring the integrity of electronic data?
  3. Change control authority: If the sending party wants to change a specification, does the CDMO have a right of veto?
  4. CPV reporting responsibility: Who performs Continued Process Verification after commercial manufacturing begins?
  5. Deviation handling: What are the notification timelines and who leads the investigation when the CDMO finds a process problem?

Analogy: A Quality Agreement Is a "Prenuptial Agreement"

A quality agreement is like a prenuptial agreement in a business partnership: it writes down every possible future dispute when the relationship is at its best and everyone is most willing to cooperate.

Many TT failures are not caused by technical problems but by the two sides wrangling over "who is responsible" once a problem occurs, wasting precious time that should go to correction. The purpose of the quality agreement is to make "who does what and who is responsible for what" clear and undisputed from the start.

1.2.5 Science and Risk-Based Approaches and Control Strategy

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p13–p15

Technology transfer can be considered to have these main components:

  • Business strategy
  • Regulatory strategy
  • Understanding and agreement of the knowledge to be transferred
  • Understanding of the control strategy to be transferred
  • Robust project management (including accounting for differences in culture and styles of working)

Using science and risk-based (or QbD) approaches enable understanding about a product and its process and the link to patient requirements, and hence enable effective technology transfer. For legacy products, historical information should be captured and documented, including agreed acceptance criteria and ranges within which product quality has been achieved.

Technology transfer should ensure knowledge of product, process, and operational aspects are transferred effectively between sending and receiving units and that risks and mitigation plans during each of the phases are understood. QTPP, CQAs, CPPs, material attributes, and control strategy, as described in ICH Q8(R2), are elements to product and process understanding and should be available during technology transfer.

An effective control strategy includes:

  • Understanding the relationship between CQAs, CPPs, material attributes, and in-process controls
  • Controls on material attributes (including raw materials, starting materials, intermediates, reagents, primary packaging materials for the drug substance, etc.)
  • Controls implicit in the design of the manufacturing process (e.g., sequence of purification steps or order of addition of reagents)
  • In-process controls (including in-process tests and control of process parameters)
  • Controls on drug substance
  • Understanding of analytical methods
  • Having appropriate data
  • Alarm strategies
  • Completed and qualified engineering modifications for equipment or utilities
  • Where applicable, PAT systems, closed loop controls, and validated PAT methods and models

Undertaking small scale batch observation runs can offer benefits (particularly when a CMO is being used):

  • Helps to get support and agreement from production teams
  • Improve knowledge of the process, reduce errors, and build bill of materials lists
  • Allows for the receiving unit team to view these at the sending unit's site and, for example, to make video recordings as appropriate

Ensuring operators understand what has to be implemented is important; some companies include a qualification and training program as part of operator training. Technical groups should explain to operators why the CPPs and controls have specific ranges and, in particular, those which will have a negative impact if out of range.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Full Definition of Control Strategy

ICH Q10 definition: A control strategy is "a planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality."

The seven layers of a Control Strategy (from source to end):

  1. Control of raw material/starting material attributes
  2. Controls implicit in the process design (such as order of addition)
  3. In-process controls
  4. Intermediate specifications
  5. Finished product specifications
  6. Alarm strategies
  7. PAT and closed-loop control (advanced)

Transferring the Control Strategy means that all seven layers above must be re-established and validated at the receiving site.

Analogy: A Control Strategy Is the Aircraft's Autopilot System

The process is like an aircraft and the Control Strategy is the autopilot: when a parameter drifts, the system knows how to correct it, and knows which boundary, once crossed, must trigger an alarm and human intervention.

Technology transfer moves more than the aircraft hardware (equipment); it moves the entire autopilot logic (why this parameter is set in this range, and what happens if it goes outside). Without transferring this logic, nobody at the new base would know how to fly the aircraft.

Key Point: Batch Observation Runs in CDMO Practice

The guide specifically mentions having receiving-unit personnel observe production at the sending site, including video recording. This is the gold-standard practice at the start of a CDMO engagement:

  • Send at least 2 receiving-site engineers to the sending site to observe 2-3 batches of production from start to finish
  • Key things to observe: operators' informal habits, special equipment handling practices, and the judgment basis for critical steps
  • Video recordings can capture the "hands-on details" that text cannot describe
  • Prepare an "observation report" immediately after the observation, confirmed by the sending party, to become part of the Technology Package

The Five-Pillar Framework of TT

Business Strategy     ← Why transfer? What are the business objectives?
       ↓
Regulatory Strategy   ← How to maintain registration status? Which supplements are needed?
       ↓
Knowledge Transfer    ← QTPP / CQA / CPP / Material Attr.
       ↓
Control Strategy      ← How to ensure the receiving site's controls are equivalent to the sending site's?
       ↓
Project Management    ← Timeline, resources, management of cultural differences

None of these five pillars can be omitted. The one most often neglected is the "Regulatory Strategy": many TTs succeed technically but are delayed to market because the supplemental filings were not planned in advance.

1.2.6 Quality Risk Management

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p15

Quality Risk Management (QRM), as described in ICH Q9, is an extremely important part of a technology transfer project. Successful technology transfer should ensure all high risk areas have been considered, appropriate mitigation has been put in place, and that any residual risks are assessed and agreed as acceptable between sites. The QRM process should be documented.

The sending and receiving units should follow QRM principles. SOPs related to QRM should be in place and followed in all aspects related to technology transfer.

The terminology, tools, and techniques from ICH Q9 should be used appropriately. Refer to Section 3.3 for additional information. Refer to Chapter 12 (Appendix 5) and Chapter 13 (Appendix 6) for examples of Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) and fishbone diagrams.

Risk assessments should ideally be carried out jointly by the sending and receiving units. There should be clear understanding of the purpose of each risk assessment. Any mitigations should be built into control strategies or other procedures at the receiving unit.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three Core Questions of QRM in TT

Applying the ICH Q9 QRM framework to TT requires answering three questions:

  • What can go wrong? Use FMEA or a fishbone diagram to identify risks systematically
  • What is the likelihood and severity? Quantify with a risk matrix and focus on high-risk items
  • What can be done to reduce the risk? Design mitigation measures and build them into the control strategy

Key requirement: the risk assessment must be performed jointly by both parties, not as a unilateral judgment by the sending party, because the receiving party understands the specific constraints of its own facility.

Key Point: Managing Residual Risk

Not every risk can be eliminated completely. The guide requires that residual risk be explicitly "assessed and agreed as acceptable" by both parties.

In practice, this means a formal Risk Acceptance document, signed by authorized personnel from both sides, becomes part of the TT documentation package. In future audits this document is an important record protecting both parties.

1.2.7 Communication

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed. | p15

The success of technology transfer is critically dependent on good communication and relationships between personnel in technology transfer teams. At the start of a technology transfer, roles and responsibilities should be defined for transfer team members (from both sending and receiving units) including who are responsible and accountable for the components of the transfer.

Sending and receiving units should have the same level of understanding for all aspects of the transfer.

In addition, communication channels and methods (e.g., reports) that may affect the transfer of information should be defined. It is essential that good verbal and written communication channels are put in place, particularly when personnel are often not in the same room and therefore are unable to have face-to-face discussions, e.g., use of teleconferencing and video conferencing. The need to build good relationships and trust between the sending and receiving units is of great importance; capturing and documenting all appropriate knowledge is crucial so that it can be easily communicated between the sending and receiving units.

For transfers between companies, communication should be formalized by leveraging documents such as quality and supply agreements. A similar formality in principle should be put in place for intra-company transfers.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point: Communication Is the Hidden Variable in TT Success

The guide uses the phrase "critically dependent" to describe the importance of communication - one of the strongest wordings in the entire chapter.

Analysis of failed TTs shows that over 60% of problems trace back to poor communication rather than technical defects. Common communication failure patterns:

  • The sending party believes "it is explained in the documents," while the receiving party finds "the documents too vague"
  • Problems are resolved verbally in informal settings but never documented
  • Information decay caused by different time zones and languages
  • A cultural barrier of "not daring to ask" - the receiving party is embarrassed to admit it did not understand

Analogy: TT Communication Is Like a Surgical Handover

Hospital operating rooms have strict "handover" protocols: when the lead surgeon hands over to the next, every key item must be confirmed one by one against a structured checklist, not glossed over verbally.

Knowledge handover in TT should be held to the same rigor: a written record after every communication, document tracking for every technical decision, and mutual signed confirmation every time "we both understand."

Practical Application: Designing the CDMO Communication Structure

Communication mechanisms recommended to be defined explicitly in the Charter:

  • Weekly technical meeting: Fixed time, both PMs plus technical leads, with minutes
  • Biweekly progress report: Standard template covering milestones, open issues and decision log
  • Escalation path: A 24-hour notification mechanism for critical deviations, with a single designated point of contact
  • Site visit plan: Reciprocal site visits scheduled in advance, rather than relying on "we'll discuss it if needed"

This Guide is intended to be used as a generic guide to technology transfer and compiles information under three main topics:

This section introduces key terms as they are used in the context of this Guide. Refer to Chapter 15 for an expanded listing of definitions.

Control Strategy

As defined in ICH Q10:
"A planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control."

Receiving Unit

An organization, which includes core and auxiliary functions, where a designated product, process, or method is expected to be transferred to.

Core functions normally include quality, manufacturing, and process engineering. Auxiliary functions may include regulatory, supply chain, warehousing, and HSE. The actual balance of functions would be project specific.

Process Validation

As defined in FDA Guidance for Industry – Process Validation: General Principles and Practices:

"Collection and evaluation of data, from the process design stage through commercial production, which establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product."

Note: This is a lifecycle definition covering the three stages as described by FDA.

As defined in EMA Guideline on process validation for finished products:

"Documented evidence that the process, operated within established parameters, can perform effectively and reproducibly to produce a medicinal product meeting its predetermined specifications and quality attributes."

Note: This definition refers to a single stage – equivalent to Stage 2.2 of FDA Guidance.

Quality Target Product Profile (QTPP)

As defined in ICH Q8(R2):
"A prospective summary of the quality characteristics of a drug product that ideally will be achieved to ensure the desired quality, taking into account safety and efficacy of the drug product."

Sending Unit

An organization, which includes core and auxiliary functions, where a designated product, process, or method is expected to be transferred from.

Core functions normally include quality, manufacturing, and process engineering. Auxiliary functions may include regulatory, supply chain, warehousing, and HSE.

Stakeholder

Any individual, group, or organization that can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a transfer. Decision makers might also be stakeholders.

Technology Transfer Charter

The technology transfer charter captures the strategic intent and defines the overall scope of the technology transfer project. It should document the team members and their roles and responsibilities, explain the effort and time required for the technology transfer project, and identify significant assumptions and risks. The charter helps to ensure that management and team members from both sending and receiving units understand the project and agree upon deliverables.

Note: The technology transfer charter and proposal may be the same document, depending on the complexity of the technology transfer project. If the charter elements change, the project needs to be reevaluated, no matter what stage it is at in the technology transfer.

Technology Transfer Proposal

The technology transfer proposal defines the overall scope of the project, documents the team members and their roles and responsibilities, estimates the effort and time required for the project, establishes high level success criteria, and identifies significant assumptions and risks. This document is intended to ensure that stakeholders and team members from both sending and receiving units understand the project and agree upon the deliverables. The technology transfer proposal also identifies information needed for the technology transfer package. The technology transfer proposal should be reviewed and approved by the appropriate stakeholders from both sending units and receiving units. The proposal may be managed using formal change control procedures.

Note: The technology transfer charter and proposal may be the same document, depending on the complexity of the technology transfer project.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy: Charter vs. Proposal as Building Permit vs. Construction Drawings

Charter is like the project's "zoning permit": the government (management) authorizes you to build this type of building on this plot (the strategic scope and objectives of the TT).

Proposal is like the "construction drawings": the architects (technical team) design, within the permitted scope, exactly where every wall goes and how every pipe runs.

If the permit is wrong from the start (the Charter defines the wrong scope), even the most beautiful construction drawings are wasted work - which is why the guide stresses that once the Charter changes, the entire project must be re-evaluated.

The Receiving CDMO's Five Must-Ask Checklist

Before signing the TT agreement, the receiving party must confirm with the sending party:

1. Completeness of process characterization
   → Is there a complete QTPP/CQA/CPP matrix?
   → Which CPP ranges are still being defined?

2. Completeness of the Technology Package
   → Has the document list been finalized? How will tacit knowledge be transferred?
   → Have the analytical methods been validated and are they transferable?

3. Regulatory status
   → What is the status of the existing registration dossier? Which supplements will be needed once the CDMO takes over?
   → Are there any unresolved regulatory commitments?

4. Equipment and facility differences
   → What are the key differences between the receiving site's equipment and the sending site's?
   → Are there any known scale effects or equipment sensitivities?

5. Timeline and resources
   → How much on-site support time can the sending party provide?
   → Is the target date for the key milestone (first PPQ batch) realistic?

Key Point: Knowledge Asymmetry Between the Sending Unit and the Receiving Unit

The guide's definitions show that the sending unit "possesses the process knowledge, history, and operational experience," while the receiving unit "needs to acquire that knowledge."

This asymmetry is the deepest source of tension in TT:

  • The sending party may not know what it knows (tacit knowledge) and so cannot proactively identify what needs to be transferred
  • The receiving party does not know what it does not know and so cannot ask the right questions
  • Both parties may overestimate the completeness of the knowledge transfer ("we feel we explained it clearly")

Solution: use a structured Technology Package Checklist (see Appendix 1) that forces both parties to confirm item by item, rather than relying on subjective feeling.

Key Point: Differences Between the FDA and EMA Definitions of Process Validation

The guide lists two definitions side by side, and the difference has practical significance in TT:

  • FDA: A lifecycle definition (covering Stages 1-3) - meaning PPQ is only the beginning of validation and subsequent CPV is equally important
  • EMA: A single-stage definition (equivalent to Stage 2.2) - emphasizing "documented evidence," with PPQ batch data at the core

For global products that must satisfy both FDA and EMA requirements (common at CDMOs), the TT Process Validation Strategy must be designed with both logics and explained explicitly in the Regulatory Strategy.

  1. If the Charter's scope needs to change partway through TT (for example, adding a dosage form), what is the correct procedure? Who decides whether the entire project needs to be re-evaluated?
  2. The QTPP definition says "prospective summary" - does this mean the QTPP must exist before the transfer begins, or can it be established during the transfer? What does this imply for legacy products?
  3. Is your CDMO a "Stakeholder" in a complex TT? What legal implications does the definition of Stakeholder have for the CDMO's participation rights?

Core Concept: Charter vs. Proposal: The Essential Difference Between the Two Documents

The guide's Note says "the two may be the same document" - but in a complex CDMO transfer they are usually separate documents with distinctly different functions:

Golden rule: the Charter establishes "whether to do it and what to do"; the Proposal establishes "how to do it." The Charter must be signed first for the Proposal to have legitimacy.

Chapter Summary

  • The essence of TT is knowledge management: The ICH Q10 definition is clear - the goal of technology transfer is "product realisation," and the core is moving product and process knowledge from development to manufacturing, not merely copying equipment or SOPs.
  • Knowledge asymmetry between sending and receiving parties is the root of TT failure: tacit knowledge and quality culture differences are often harder to overcome than technical gaps and must be addressed from the Charter stage.
  • The three TT drivers (lifecycle progression, capacity expansion, strategic relocation) each carry a different risk profile, and the receiving party must first determine which type applies when evaluating a TT opportunity.
  • The Charter comes before the Proposal: The Charter is the strategic authorization document (management consensus); the Proposal is the detailed technical plan (a guide for the execution team) - both are indispensable and the order cannot be reversed.
  • Analytical method transfer is a prerequisite for DS/DP transfer: Without a transferred analytical method, process equivalence cannot be verified - the three types of TT must be planned in coordination and cannot proceed independently.
  • The boundary between TT and Process Validation: TT is complete after close-out; PPQ is the ultimate verification marker of TT success, and CPV falls outside the scope of TT - but all three must be designed together from the early planning stage.

Chapter 2 — Technology Transfer: Planning Considerations and Success Criteria
2.1 Introduction

Original Text

This chapter provides guidance on what to consider for a transfer, including establishing success criteria, prior to the formal formation of the full technology transfer team. Guidance provided is applicable to drug substance, drug product, and analytical procedure technology transfers.

A technology transfer typically starts with an agreement (referred to as a charter in Chapter 3) normally formalized or on a contractual basis between a sending unit and a receiving unit for the transfer of, e.g., a developed process or a commercial process or an analytical method.

The success of a technology transfer project will be largely dependent upon the skill and performance of individuals assigned to the project from both the sending unit and the receiving unit. It is, therefore, critical that a clear objective for any technology transfer project be developed. It is also critical that a project team comprised of individuals from both the sending unit and the receiving unit is established and that there is a precise understanding of each team member's role and responsibility prior to initiation of the technology transfer project.

"Skill alone will not ensure a successful technology transfer project. Once a project has been identified and a team chosen, a clear and realistic project implementation plan is required to guide the project, manage expectations, and handle the inevitable deviations and changes that may present themselves during implementation."

Along with that plan comes the need to consider the relationship between the various tasks associated with a successful technology transfer. It should be clearly understood that each technology transfer assignment is unique; therefore, it is impossible to provide a generic technology transfer plan.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Sending Unit: The party that holds the process or analytical method, usually an R&D unit, the originator site, or another manufacturing site.

Receiving Unit: The party that takes over and performs the process. In a CDMO setting, this is usually us.

Success Criteria: A predefined statement of the conditions under which the technology transfer counts as successful. The central argument of this chapter is that these criteria must be defined before the transfer begins, not added afterwards.

Analogy

Technology transfer is like a chef handing a recipe to another restaurant. The recipe alone is not enough: the receiving chef needs to know the "standard" for the dish. What taste counts as success for the customer? What if the kitchen equipment is different? If the standard is argued about only after the dish is served, both sides will suffer.

Key Notes

The guide states clearly that every technology transfer is unique and there is no universal plan template. This is particularly important for a CDMO: you cannot apply one SOP to every client's transfer. Each project needs a tailored plan.

2.2 Technology Transfer Planning Considerations

Original Text

In the early planning phase, a charter may not be fully developed yet. In this case, the team should document, at a minimum, the major deliverables and assumptions not expected to change. Examples include:

  • High level program deliverables such as delivery of material to clinical trials or early planning (e.g., for analytical methods)
  • Agency submission strategies
  • Site licensure milestones
  • Budget
  • Mechanism for handling changes, e.g., in scope or budget

As stated earlier, technology transfer can be considered a transfer of knowledge using well founded project management principles.

2.2.1 Developing an Early Agreement Between the Sending and Receiving Units

An agreement (referred to as a charter in Chapter 3) is typically high level and developed with input from sending and receiving unit Subject Matter Experts (SMEs); input is rarely from the full technology transfer team. Securing an early agreement, sometimes referred to as site selection, may be necessary to release the full resources required for detailed planning and execution.

It is increasingly common that early stages with long lead times are initiated prior to formation of the entire team. It is also often necessary to make an early assessment of HSE aspects; an example is a facility that is not capable of handling particular compounds from an HSE standpoint.

By its nature, a technology transfer requires effective management of incomplete information; consideration of information and SME availability is required to determine appropriate planning timelines.

2.2.2 Sending Unit and Receiving Unit Working Closely

Technology transfer planning should be initiated as early as possible. Roles and responsibilities for sending and receiving units should be defined early and updated frequently in the early planning stages. Effective planning and diligent tracking of potential issues can help to expedite technology transfer projects and prevent delays.

A plan created by collaboration between sending and receiving units can help to unify the technology transfer team and to ensure that there is agreement on deliverables, success criteria, and ownership of the transfer at different stages. Project management tools such as the RACI matrix can be useful to formalize roles.

RACI: R = Responsible (the person who actually does the work), A = Accountable (the person with ultimate responsibility), C = Consulted (needs to be consulted), I = Informed (needs to be told the outcome)

The relationship within the technology transfer team should be actively maintained. Partnership between sending/receiving project managers and accountable functional area leads is key to actively managing the flow of known and emerging process, method, and site capability knowledge.

It is important to understand the critical path timetable and ensure that the technology transfer planning and execution timelines are consistent with the critical path timing. Communicating an unrealistic timeline to management could result in the team's acceptance of undesirable risks in order to meet their commitment.

People to people interaction should be encouraged, especially between counterparts in the sending unit and receiving unit. This is especially important where different cultures exist between sending and receiving units.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

RACI Matrix: A project management tool used to clarify, for each task, who is responsible for execution (R), who has final accountability (A), who must be consulted (C) and who must be informed (I). In a technology transfer, the RACI of the sending unit and of the receiving unit must be documented, to avoid grey areas where each side "assumed the other would do it".

Critical Path: The longest chain of tasks in a project that determines the final completion date. If any task on the critical path is delayed, the whole transfer is delayed.

Analogy

A RACI matrix is like a restaurant's HACCP plan: every control point must specify who measures, who records, who approves and who is notified. A technology transfer without a RACI is like a kitchen where everyone assumes someone else is measuring the oil temperature, and in the end nobody does.

Key Notes

Assess HSE early: The assessment of Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) must be completed early in planning, because it may require facility modifications or permit applications with a long lead time. For a CDMO taking on a project involving toxic compounds or special substances, this assessment often decides whether the project can be accepted at all.

Realistic timelines: The guide specifically warns that reporting unrealistic timelines to management forces the team to accept risks that should not be accepted. This matters especially under the commercial pressures of a CDMO.

2.2.3-2.2.4 Roles, Responsibilities & Effective Knowledge Transfer

Original Text

2.2.3 Clear Understanding of Roles and Responsibilities

A clear understanding of roles and responsibilities for team members, from both the sending and receiving units, serves to minimize redundancies and oversights. Knowing what is expected by other team members can increase accountability and help to minimize confusion and conflicts.

Additional recommendations include:

  • Performing an assessment of the team capabilities relative to the skills required to complete tasks for which team members are accountable
  • Defining decision rights (i.e., who will make the decision regarding particular aspects or differences of view)
  • Defining clear communication routes both within the team and external to the team using, e.g., meetings, shared network or other project sites, meeting minutes
  • Documenting roles and responsibilities with appropriate project management tools such as a RACI matrix

2.2.4 Effective Knowledge Transfer

It is very important that both sending and receiving units discuss and agree the knowledge to be transferred — scientific, regulatory, operational (including tacit knowledge), business, and cultural.

Scientific aspects include:

  • Process description, rationale, history, flow diagrams, unit operations, scale, and material transfers
  • Understanding what is important to control for both drug substance and drug product manufacture (see ICH Q8(R2)) including impact on packaging
  • Ensuring CQAs are understood by both sending and receiving units
  • Analytical technology required to measure process performance (HPLC equipment, columns, etc.)
  • Transferring the control strategies and operationalizing it — note: even for legacy products, regulators are increasingly expecting a QTPP, CQAs and control strategies to be in place
  • Being clear on CPPs and material attributes for each unit operation; design space if used
  • Multivariate approaches (design space, PAT tools, real time release testing, continuous manufacturing)
  • Potential sources of variation

Regulatory aspects include:

  • Compliance with regulatory requirements in both the operating country and the supply country (individual countries may have their own variation guidelines)
  • Some countries require additional bioequivalence studies
  • Validation carried out appropriately including lifecycle approach
  • Adequacy of the QMS/Pharmaceutical Quality System (ICH Q10)
  • Checking no regulatory changes in progress that could impact implementation

Operational aspects (including tacit knowledge) include:

  • Facility and utility requirements; facility fit
  • Operator training and/or qualification requirements
  • Equipment principles that may differ (mixers, dryers using different engineering principles)
  • Developing new manufacturing instructions and SOPs
  • HSE requirements (containment, operator protection)
  • Differences in shifts, automation, PAT tools, scale, batch sizes, process flows, sampling techniques
  • Understanding tacit knowledge — knowledge not explicitly documented but shows actual practices (e.g., scalability, vessel charging, analyst technique)
  • Long term planning for raw materials (import license restrictions)
  • Operators from sending unit working at receiving site (knowledge transfer, visa requirements, QA, HSE concerns)

Business aspects include:

  • Importance of the product being transferred (profitability may differ between units)
  • Determining who pays for what (new equipment, training)
  • Capital responsibility — who invests, who owns the asset, depreciation

Cultural aspects: Gaining understanding of a company or country's culture can significantly contribute to success. Some cultures may be procedural, hierarchical, unstructured, or relationship dependent. Language differences in SOPs and operator communication are also a factor. (Refer to ISPE Cultural Excellence Report [13].)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Tacit Knowledge: Implicit operating knowledge that people can do but cannot easily explain. Examples are how material is fed to a mixing impeller, or how an analyst actually performs sample preparation. This information is often missing from SOPs. The guide explicitly requires this kind of knowledge to be documented, which is the hardest but most important step in a technology transfer.

QTPP / CQA / CPP:
QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile): the quality standard the final product should meet
CQA (Critical Quality Attribute): a quality characteristic that directly affects safety and efficacy
CPP (Critical Process Parameter): a process parameter with a significant impact on a CQA

Analogy

Tacit knowledge is like the "feel" of a top chef: the book says "stir to the right consistency", but nobody tells you what that consistency feels like. If a technology transfer passes on only the SOP and not this feel, the first batches at the receiving kitchen (the CDMO) often fail. The best solution is to have the sending unit's chefs come to the kitchen and demonstrate in person a few times.

Key Notes

Five types of knowledge: The guide divides the knowledge to be transferred into five types: scientific, regulatory, operational (including tacit knowledge), business and cultural. When receiving a new project, a CDMO tends to focus on the scientific and operational aspects and easily overlooks differences in regulatory compliance (filing strategies in different countries) and cultural differences (clients from different management cultures).

Hidden regulatory landmines: The guide specifically reminds us that clients with legacy products are also required by regulators to provide QTPP and CQA documentation. When a CDMO takes on a legacy product transfer and the client cannot provide these documents, the CDMO may need to help rebuild them, and this must be built into the quotation and timeline.

Practical Application

When evaluating a new transfer, a CDMO receiving unit should establish a standard knowledge checklist covering the five categories above. In particular, confirm: (1) whether the sending unit can provide design space documentation; (2) whether the analytical methods use the same equipment platform (HPLC brand, column model); (3) in which markets the product is sold and whether the regulatory requirements of each market are consistent. Raising these questions at the commercial negotiation stage avoids huge remediation costs later.

2.2.5 Information Package

A sufficiently complete technology transfer package of information should be developed to meet the requirements of the technology transfer. Refer to Chapter 3, Chapter 8, and Chapter 13 for additional information.

2.2.6 Quality Risk Management

QRM is integral to technology transfer and should follow ICH Q9 principles (see Figure 2.1🔍) or similar. It is important that teams are rigorous in their application of Q9 and spend sufficient time during the initiation phase to ensure full understanding by both sending and receiving units of the purpose of the QRM exercise.

Once the initiation phase is completed, a detailed risk assessment should be carried out (e.g., using tools such as cause and effect matrices, Ishikawa (fishbone) diagrams, gap analysis, and FMEA). After agreement on acceptable and unacceptable risks, then the unacceptable risks should be mitigated to an acceptable level and then controlled through a defined risk mitigation plan.

QRM is an iterative activity. All technology transfer projects require risk assessment. Existing risk assessments may need to be adapted to acknowledge changes in risk during the technology transfer. It is helpful to have a QRM plan if there will be multiple risk assessments. Note that there can be several types of risk assessment — for the project, the process, the product, the equipment, etc. Normally, the project manager would own the project risk assessments and the technical team would own the technical risk assessments.

2.2.7 Phases and Stage Gate Approach

The technology transfer project may be approached in phases (stages). Documentation of the project should include the important activities for each phase, e.g., reviews, deliverables, and projected timelines. Refer to Chapter 3 for the six phases of technology transfer.

Throughout the project, stage gate reviews should be used to ensure that all critical items from the current project phase have been completed satisfactorily before moving on to subsequent phases. This approach helps to ensure critical project items are completed on time and that later phases of technology transfer are not put at risk. Stage gate reviews should be attended by stakeholders and the technology transfer team.

Stage Gate: A formal review point at the end of each phase, where progress to the next phase is released only after all key deliverables are complete. This prevents the common situation where problems pile up and only explode late in the project.

2.3 Technology Transfer Success Criteria

Original Text

Technology transfer is considered successful if the receiving unit can consistently produce material through the qualified process and demonstrate that quality attributes have been maintained through process monitoring and analysis of the material produced.

"Simply stated, technology transfer can be considered successful if the receiving unit can routinely reproduce the transferred product, process, or method against a predefined set of specifications as agreed upon with the sending unit and/or development unit."

A comparability exercise may be completed as an assessment of sending versus receiving process/method performance. Interim success criteria are developed to track progress toward these goals. Success is ultimately demonstrated by regulatory agency licensure in the desired region(s). In addition, business performance parameters are established to balance risk versus cost, including resources.

Evaluation of the success of a technology transfer should be based on a pre-established set of criteria, which are evaluated as the project progresses. These criteria may be used to track progress of the transfer into subsequent phases and hence manage risk.

Success criteria usually take into account the business situation and product requirements, for example:

  • Stage within the product lifecycle
  • Availability of product and process knowledge
  • Experience of the sending units and receiving units
  • Number of sites involved
  • Whether it is a transfer between internal sites or to an external site

From a business perspective, the success of a technology transfer is based on efficiently meeting the requirements of product quality, patient safety, and efficacy, and business requirements. Business requirements may include:

  • Cost, including capital cost, expense and Cost of Goods Manufactured (COGM) targets
  • Capacity/volume
  • Equipment and facility capabilities
  • Time frames
  • Risk management
  • Regulatory requirements or strategy, including meeting existing commitments
  • Supply chain strategy

Success criteria can take several forms and may be dependent on the technology being transferred and the phase of development. The criteria should be measurable and achievable. Success criteria may include:

  • Evaluation of the technology performance
  • Adherence to the project timeline
  • Adherence to the budget

Specific success criteria should be documented in the technology transfer plan. Main aspects may include:

  • Process performance — comparing in-process data (either traditional or PAT generated) with historical data
  • Product quality that meets pre-established criteria (data sets for comparability analyses)
  • Confirmation that the control strategy is executable
  • Confirmation that the product control strategy from the sending unit has been successfully delivered by the receiving unit, including meeting requirements of product CQAs and specifications
  • Process performance as expected based on historical data:
    • Large molecule examples: cell growth rates, titers, step yields, chromatogram profiles, impurity clearance
    • Small molecule examples: impurity levels (solvents, elemental impurities), quality attributes (dissolution), material attributes (particle size distribution), process parameters (compression force), process yield, and throughput
  • Analytical method transfer and equivalency
  • Successful routine operation over an agreed number of batches
  • Successful routine operation of facility and equipment (operability, labor, OEE)
  • Training of staff
  • HSE objectives achieved
  • Documentation fully and satisfactorily completed
  • Regulatory inspection outcomes and regulatory submission on time
  • Improvement of process robustness and efficiency (exact 1:1 transfers are rare)

Consideration should also be made to establish longer term criteria (e.g., during process validation Stage 3) following closure of the technology transfer report. Technology transfer studies may fail to predict variance inherent in longer term evaluations. Success criteria could include permitting natural material attribute variance or intentional material attribute variance.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — Three-Dimensional Success Criteria

ISPE divides technology transfer success criteria into three dimensions:

  • Technical: Does process performance meet specification? Are the analytical methods equivalent?
  • Regulatory: Has regulatory approval been obtained in the target markets? Were regulatory submissions made on time?
  • Business: Is cost (COGM) on target? Is the timeline met? Has equipment utilization (OEE) improved?

None of the three dimensions can be missing. Technical success with a commercial loss, or technical success with a regulatory rejection, does not count as real success.

Analogy

Success criteria are like the scoring rules of an Olympic event: they must be announced "before" the competition starts, otherwise neither the judges nor the athletes know which direction to work toward. If a technology transfer waits until the end to define "what counts as success", it is the same as announcing the scoring criteria after the competition is over, which inevitably leads to disputes.

Key Notes

Measurable and achievable: Success criteria must be measurable and achievable. "Stable process" is not a success criterion; "API recovery ≥ 95% with RSD < 2% over three consecutive batches" is.

"Perfect 1:1 replication" is a myth: The guide states clearly that exact 1:1 transfers are rare. The receiving unit will almost certainly need to adjust some parameters to fit its own equipment. This means success criteria should allow reasonable room for process optimization, rather than demand zero deviation.

Do not neglect long-term criteria: After the short-term TT report is closed, batch-to-batch variation during Stage 3 process validation may be larger than during the TT phase. It is advisable to plan long-term monitoring indicators in the TT plan in advance.

Technical success criteria
├─ Process performance vs. historical data
│   ├─ Large molecules: cell growth rates, titers, yields
│   └─ Small molecules: impurities, dissolution, particle size
├─ Analytical method equivalency
└─ Executability of the control strategy

Regulatory success criteria
├─ Regulatory submissions on time
├─ Sufficient comparability data
└─ Regulatory authority review passed

Business success criteria
├─ COGM target achieved
├─ Timeline achievement rate
├─ OEE improvement
└─ Staff training completed

Practical Application

Before signing a technology transfer contract with a client, a CDMO should require the client to provide (or jointly define) documented success criteria, and clearly distinguish "contractual success criteria" (which trigger payment milestones) from "technical monitoring criteria" (for continuous tracking of process status). The former are usually looser (for example, completing 3 validation batches), while the latter should be stricter (batch-to-batch CPP consistency). If the client insists on not documenting success criteria, this is an important red flag.

Original Text

This chapter provides a description for each of the six phases of a technology transfer. It is intended to be generic and describes general practices that apply to all transfers regardless of the technology being transferred. To note, at the time of publication, it is increasingly common for phases to overlap and/or be executed in parallel. The same principles used to manage and control a traditionally phased technology transfer can be leveraged for transfer with overlapping phases. Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Six-phase framework: ISPE divides technology transfer into six ordered but potentially overlapping phases. This section covers the first three, planning phases (Phases 1-3). An important point: the guide acknowledges that in reality the phases may overlap (parallel execution), reflecting industry pressure to shorten time to market. The key principle is to identify the critical dependencies rather than insist on a linear sequence.

Key Notes

For a CDMO, Phases 1-3 determine whether the whole project gets "a good start". If these three planning phases are executed carelessly, Phase 4 (execution) will inevitably be full of problems, typically showing up as batch failures, an explosion of deviation handling, client complaints and cost overruns. The time invested in planning is always cheaper than the time spent fixing mistakes.

Phase 1 (3.1) — Form Transfer Team and Develop Charter

Original Text

The initiation phase of technology transfer should establish the team, strategy, and goals of a project. Sending and receiving units should establish an integrated team of the required SMEs from the functional areas involved in the transfer. This team should develop a technology transfer charter to capture the goals, milestones, and roles and responsibilities for the project.

3.1.1 Establishing a Team

A formal technology transfer team should be established. The team structure, roles, and responsibilities should be identified. Team structure should include leadership from both sending units and receiving units, along with SMEs from the relevant functional areas.

At the start of the technology transfer program, key stakeholders, technical leads, and project managers should be identified. An overall project manager is usually responsible for delivery of the technology transfer and should coordinate activities such as finance, quality/regulatory, and materials management.

Technical leads are usually responsible for ensuring that project managers have the technical information to enable delivery of the technology transfer. Depending upon the complexity of the project, a technical lead and project manager may be the same person. If the lead serves this dual role, it is important to ensure that project management responsibilities cannot be neglected when competing priorities arise.

The core team should include representation from both the sending and receiving, as appropriate. Examples of functions represented in the core team include:

  • Analytical sciences / Analytical/Quality Control (QC)
  • Engineering
  • HSE and sustainability
  • Late stage development
  • Manufacturing operations / Manufacturing technical
  • Material management / Planning
  • Process development
  • Project management
  • QA
  • R&D
  • Regulatory affairs
  • Supply chain

Project stakeholders (e.g., senior management, budget managers) provide oversight and guidance. Examples of stakeholder functions: Finance, Manufacturing, Process development, Program management (commercial), Quality, R&D, Regulatory affairs, Technical services.

Technology transfer team leads and project stakeholders should define the appropriate governance and controls, generally covering:

  • Scope of the project
  • Timelines and key milestones
  • Resources and budget
  • Success criteria
  • Reporting and escalation structure
  • Key decision gates/approval process
  • Change and risk management processes
  • Quality management

Communication between the members of the team can be more effective when team members:

  • From both units have a similar level of knowledge and experience of the techniques involved
  • Have good team working skills
  • Have the time to ensure that actions from risk/gap assessment are progressed
  • Define mutually accepted means of communication and collaboration (face-to-face kickoffs + regular teleconferences)
  • Acknowledge and manage cultural differences

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Project Manager vs. Technical Lead: These two roles have different responsibilities in a technology transfer. The PM manages delivery, timeline and resource coordination; the Technical Lead manages technical correctness. The guide cautions that if one person holds both roles, the PM duties are easily pushed aside when a technical issue becomes urgent, which is a very common failure mode.

Core Team vs. Stakeholders: The Core Team carries out the day-to-day work; Stakeholders are the senior people who oversee and provide resources. Both must be identified in Phase 1 to ensure resources and decision-making support.

Analogy

A technology transfer team is like a surgical operation: the lead surgeon (Technical Lead) focuses on technical execution; the anesthesiologist, nurses and coordinators each have their own role; and the head of the operating theatre (Project Manager) makes sure the whole process runs smoothly. If the lead surgeon also has to act as coordinator, the quality of the surgery will inevitably drop.

Key Notes

The role of QA: Note that QA is listed in the core team, not merely as a stakeholder. This means QA should be deeply involved from Phase 1, rather than passively reviewing documents during Phase 4 execution. Early QA involvement can prevent a large number of compliance problems later.

Regulatory Affairs is also in the core team: The regulatory strategy of the technology transfer (which changes require a filing and which can be covered in the annual report) must be set from Phase 1, rather than asking the RA team only when validation is almost finished.

Practical Application

A CDMO should establish an internal "technology transfer RACI template" that clearly states the Responsible / Accountable roles of QA, RA, engineering, analytical and other departments in each phase. It is recommended to formally confirm this RACI at the kick-off meeting of each new project and record it in the meeting minutes. This document can later serve as the basis for resolving disputes.

Original Text

The technology transfer team may develop a technology transfer charter. The charter can include:

  • Assumptions and constraints
  • Sites impacted (sending and receiving) — scope including overview of the project and business drivers; items out of scope are equally important to document
  • Objectives and technical success criteria (see Chapter 2) — e.g., number of batches required, anticipated yield, successful registration and launch
  • Roles and responsibilities — clear understanding of expectations for each leadership role, deliverables for functional areas (documentation, testing, disposition, stability)

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Nature of the Charter

Charter: The formal agreement document that launches a technology transfer. It is usually a high-level "collaboration framework" rather than a detailed execution plan. The Charter establishes the "Why (why transfer), Who (who is responsible for what), What (scope of transfer) and When (key milestones)", not the "How" of technical details.

Charter vs. TT Proposal vs. TT Plan:
Charter = high-level framework agreement (1-5 pages, for signature by senior management)
TT Proposal = detailed technical requirements document (covers Phase 2)
TT Plan = item-by-item execution plan (covers Phase 3)

Analogy

The Charter is like the "contract" of a construction project, the TT Proposal is the "design drawings", and the TT Plan is the "construction log". The three have different functions and cannot substitute for one another. A mistake many CDMOs make is to cram everything into a single "TT Protocol", which ends up neither high-level enough nor detailed enough: the contract is unclear and the technical content is not explained.

Key Notes

"Out of Scope" is equally important: The guide emphasizes that items outside the scope of the transfer also need to be documented in the Charter. This prevents the client (sending unit) from later asking "shouldn't this also be included in the transfer?" A clear out-of-scope statement is an important tool for a CDMO to guard against scope creep.

Two-way deviation reporting: Note that the guide requires deviations to be reported not only from the receiving unit to the sending unit; if the sending unit also uses the same method or process, reporting in the reverse direction is equally important. A CDMO should specify this two-way communication mechanism clearly in the Quality Agreement and the Charter.

Original Text

3.2.1 Technology Transfer Proposal

A technology transfer proposal may be useful to define, facilitate review, and garner agreement of incomplete elements of the charter. In addition, prior to developing a detailed execution plan, a proposal can be used to develop detailed requirements and acceptance criteria for either key, or yet undefined, aspects of the plan.

The items listed below are recommended for inclusion in the technology transfer proposal:

  • HSE required assessments and known impacts
  • Overview of project and business drivers
  • List of team members and management/budget approvers (stakeholders)
  • Broad objectives and deliverables — projected clinical manufacture (successful number of runs), agreed process upgrades, successful registration
  • High level timeline and resources needed — divided into phases with "approval to proceed" at each phase end; realistic timeline (not assuming 100% success); significant capital requirements and estimated FTE; responsibility for funding

3.2.2 Technology Transfer Package

The technology transfer package should be organized efficiently to provide easy access to necessary information. It should also be comprehensive, so that a receiving unit can become self-sufficient in running a process or analytical procedure.

Basic requirements for the technology transfer package include:

  • HSE related requirements — prioritize HSE impact assessments early (long lead activities such as facility modification, regional licenses, training/monitoring programs)
  • Business requirements
  • Product specific requirements, particularly CQAs and control strategy and how these will be delivered at the receiving unit
"Note: It is not the role of technology transfer to identify quality attributes and process parameters and their relationships; this is the basis of the control strategy which is typically defined during process development. The control strategy is required to be available to the receiving unit to provide the scientific rationale which supports the manufacturing process and supporting analytical methods."
  • Process flow diagrams, unit operations, parameters and material attributes, design space (where used), process trends, detailed historical data (including sequence and justification of any process changes), risk assessments, identified CPPs and CQAs
  • Facilities capabilities assessment relative to the ability to implement and consistently execute requirements of the control strategy

The technology transfer team should review the completed technology transfer package and request SME input, as appropriate, to ensure that all relevant knowledge is captured. The team should also summarize the available knowledge and conduct a high level technical risk assessment.

The technology transfer team should ensure that the receiving unit understands the information available and can obtain clarifications. The knowledge transfer should take into account the level of expertise at both units.

For transfers to an external partner or CMO, the transferring organization should determine what kinds of intellectual property it would consider to be confidential and determine how to redact documents appropriately.

The technology transfer package information will be used by team members to work on a detailed process description and list of requirements for the receiving unit to execute the process. The team should then work through this list as part of a gap analysis, to identify, assign scores (if needed), and propose mitigation activities for gaps. The information gathered from the process description and gap analysis should be incorporated into a detailed technology transfer plan.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The "Recipe" Nature of the TT Package

Technology Transfer Package: The complete body of knowledge that enables the receiving unit to run the process independently. The essential core content includes:

  • Process description and history (including the reasons for all process changes)
  • CPP set points and acceptable ranges
  • CQA specifications and control strategy
  • Analytical methods and equipment specifications
  • Known failure modes
  • Design space (if any)
  • Facility capability assessment

If any one item is missing, the receiving unit is like someone handed a recipe with pages torn out: it may be possible to make the dish, but hidden hazards will inevitably remain.

Analogy

The TT Package is like the "complete operations manual" a franchisor gives a franchisee. The manual must be detailed enough that a new franchisee, even without ever being trained in person, can produce a product of the same standard as headquarters. If the manual lacks the recipe for a secret sauce, that franchise outlet's product will inevitably fall short.

Key Notes

TT is not for "defining" CQAs: The guide clearly reminds us that the purpose of technology transfer is to "transfer" the control strategy, not to "establish" it. The relationship between CQAs and process parameters should already have been established during process development. If a CDMO takes on a project where the sending unit cannot even state its CQAs clearly, this is a serious red flag, indicating that the sending unit's own process understanding is insufficient.

IP confidentiality management: For transfers to an external CMO (i.e., a CDMO), the sending unit needs to decide which information is confidential IP and redact the documents appropriately. The CDMO should explicitly require in the contract that it receives "complete information sufficient to run the process", while respecting the client's IP protection needs. Where necessary, technical consulting services can replace document delivery.

Phase 3 (3.3) — Identify Risks, Conduct Risk Assessments & Develop TT Plan

Original Text

Following approval of a proposal for the technology transfer and exchange of the technology transfer package, the transfer team should perform risk assessments on identified gaps between the process or procedure requirements and the receiving unit capabilities and practices.

The technology transfer package can be used by both units to initiate risk assessments and begin developing the technology transfer plan for process or method implementation, along with the information, data, or work that will be required to ensure the technology can be successfully executed at the receiving unit. These assessments should compare the process or procedure history with the receiving unit capabilities and operations to identify gaps or misalignments that will require modifications.

In addition, the receiving unit should perform assessments of the following:

  • Process/method performance and validation packages
  • Raw materials/reagents for supply risk
  • Impact of incoming technology
  • Impact of changes needed as part of the implementation

This planning phase concludes once the sending and receiving units have completed the required assessments and have initiated plans to either remediate identified risks or receive risk acceptance from an appropriate governance.

Planning and execution of a technology transfer should include a comprehensive risk assessment process (i.e., risk identification, risk analysis, and risk evaluation as per ICH Q9), followed by risk control (i.e., risk reduction and risk acceptance) and development of a detailed action plan to mitigate risks.

"As part of a technology transfer, QRM is the documented procedure by which differences between sending unit and receiving unit can be identified and managed. Risk assessment is the process by which the criticalities of these differences are qualitatively or quantitatively estimated and prioritized. Once key gaps that require mitigation of risk are determined, a specific action plan for each gap should be defined and performed as part of the technology transfer execution."

The aim of QRM is to lower risks to acceptable levels. Where this is not possible or readily achievable, the risks are presented to the stakeholders for their approval to proceed "at risk" to the process qualification stage. Any differences between the sending unit and the receiving unit have the potential to increase risk to the receiving unit's ability to demonstrate product realization, cause delays, increase technology transfer costs, and have negative business impacts.

Sufficient time should be provided before technology transfer execution to identify and address differences. The sending unit and the receiving unit should perform these activities to help to improve knowledge transfer. These activities can promote teambuilding and improve future communication during later stages of a technology transfer.

It is usually preferable to address issues prior to the process qualification exercise, as opposed to during. For instance, if a process change is required due to conditions/constraints at the receiving unit, risk could be better defined and potentially mitigated by conducting studies in the small scale process model to examine the effects of the change.

It is advised that risk identification, risk analysis, and mitigation be conducted as early as possible since some risk mitigation activities may have long lead times and thus could significantly delay technology transfer. Although site selection is not discussed in this Guide, facility fit and site expertise are important factors to consider as they can have significant impact on the timeline and costs.

This risk assessment should be used to define the overall transfer approach.

3.3.1 Identifying Risks

As noted in Section 1.2, effective knowledge management (including tacit knowledge) is critical for effective risk management. Teams are advised to critically review information and knowledge utilized in risk identification, e.g., gaps in development reports.

Identification of risks is typically performed by appropriate SMEs and ad hoc members from the sending unit and the receiving unit (i.e., process/technical experts, manufacturing, quality, facility and process engineers, automation, materials/procurement, HSE, analytical, Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls (CMC) regulatory, etc.).

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts — The Logic of Gap Analysis

Gap Analysis: A systematic comparison between the sending unit's process requirements (what is needed) and the receiving unit's existing capabilities (what is available). Gaps may arise from:

  • Equipment gaps: The receiving unit uses equipment of a different make/model, whose operating principle may differ
  • Raw material gaps: The same raw material may come from a different supplier, with slightly different specifications
  • Analytical platform gaps: Different HPLC systems, column models and software versions
  • Regulatory history gaps: The receiving unit may not have the sending unit's history of regulatory approval
  • Personnel capability gaps: How familiar the receiving unit's staff are with a particular technology

Analogy

The Phase 3 gap analysis is like taking inventory of furniture before moving house: what do you have in the new home? What do you need to bring from the old one? What needs to be bought new? Which old items will not fit in the new home? This inventory must be done before the move (Phase 3), rather than discovering after moving in that the bed cannot get through the door (batch failure in Phase 4).

Key Notes

The golden rule of "study at small scale first": If the receiving unit needs process changes because of equipment or condition limitations, the guide recommends studying the impact of the changes in a small-scale model first, rather than experimenting directly in GMP batches. This is especially important in the transfer of a CDMO's aseptic filling: adjustments to filling parameters should first be verified in an engineering run.

The "At Risk" decision: If some risks cannot be fully mitigated before execution, the appropriate level of governance must formally approve "proceeding at risk". This decision must be documented in writing and cannot be a verbal agreement. When a CDMO accepts such an "at risk" decision, it should make sure the client (senior management of the sending unit) also signs to confirm, stating clearly who bears the risk.

Identify long lead time activities early: Some risk mitigation actions themselves take a long time (such as equipment procurement, regulatory filings, switching raw material suppliers). If these needs are discovered only in Phase 3, it may no longer be possible to complete them within the planned timeline. These long lead items should be preliminarily identified by the end of Phase 2.

Practical Application — CDMO Receiving Unit Perspective

When executing Phase 3, a CDMO is advised to assess risks systematically along four dimensions:

  • Technical dimension: Compare equipment specification sheets and confirm that the process parameter ranges are within the operating capability of the receiving equipment
  • Analytical dimension: Confirm that the equipment model, reagents and columns required for each analytical method are the same as at the receiving unit
  • Regulatory dimension: Confirm whether this equipment/site change triggers a variation filing in the target markets, and estimate the approval time
  • Personnel dimension: Assess the operating team's familiarity with this technology and develop a training plan

Each identified gap should be recorded in the Risk Register: gap description, severity score (S), probability of occurrence (O), detectability (D), the calculated RPN, and the proposed mitigation action with owner and due date.

  • QA and RA deeply involved from Phase 1: Rather than reviewing only after execution, this is the key practice for shortening the overall TT cycle and avoiding late remediation.

3.4 Operational Readiness

Original Text ISPE TT 3rd Ed.

3.4 Operational Readiness

The goal of the operational readiness phase is to achieve a state of readiness required to move into the qualification phase, as described in Section 3.5. Both the process and methods are required to be in a state of control prior to entering qualification phase. A critical review of the operational readiness should be performed and formally documented.

Analytical methods are required to be qualified prior to full scale process qualification in order to be able to measure process performance. In some cases, method qualification may be concurrent with process qualification; in this case, the risk to the overall qualification risk must be documented and accepted by management.

Closure of actions defined by gap analysis and corresponding risk assessments drive the requirements to complete this phase.

Operational readiness activities required may include:

  • Additional process or method development
  • Equipment installation, modification, or re-validation
  • General commissioning, qualification and validations as needed (refer to ISPE Baseline® Guide: Commissioning and Qualification)
  • Engineering runs
  • Procurement
  • Training runs or training of staff

Having identified gaps and risks, then subsequently prioritizing activities, technology transfer operational readiness may include the following:

  • Facility modifications identified in risk identification/gap analysis, including appropriate equipment validations
  • Testing of full scale manufacturing equipment to ensure proper function, e.g., mixing studies, buffer or water runs, cleaning studies, etc.

In the case of cleaning, it is recommended that a comprehensive review of the requirements (e.g., soil types, required cleanability limits, and methods of detection) are compiled into a planning document. It is highly likely that changes in equipment type, scale-up, and process optimization implemented during the transfer will require new cleaning and monitoring methods, sample types, and corresponding analytical method qualifications.

As noted in Sections 2.2 and 3.3.1, effective knowledge management is crucial throughout all the previous phases. Operation readiness requires generation of documentation at the receiving unit required to implement and control changes necessary for implementation of the transferred process and/or methods. Diligence in document review and reconciliation in this phase is of paramount importance prior to proceeding to the qualification phase.

Examples of required documentation are as follows:

  • Flow chart of process steps
    • Quantities of all materials and stoichiometry of the starting materials, intermediates, and reagents
    • Specific processing conditions (e.g., times, temperatures, and pressures)
    • Sequence of activities (e.g., order of addition of reagents)
    • Expected theoretical yield and output weight/volume of product
    • CPPs, i.e., factors impacting product quality
    • Permitted tolerance ranges for key parameters (e.g., yield impacting parameters)
    • Detail of other materials and by-products generated and their fate
    • Mass balance information
  • Development and approval of receiving unit manufacturing instruction documents (batch records, SOPs, automation recipes, etc.)
    • A clearly defined structure for review and approval of the manufacturing documents should be agreed upon by the sending unit and receiving unit, including expectations for duration and number of reviews. A well-organized system for tracking and managing edits throughout the process should be used by the teams.
  • Implementation of small scale laboratory models at receiving unit
    • As part of the transfer, small scale models of some or all the unit operations may be transferred to the receiving unit. These can be used for process training before large scale runs begin, for additional process development work identified in the risk identification/gap analysis, and can also be used for efficient troubleshooting during the initial large scale runs.
    • A suggested practice is to use the small scale model in a "satellite" mode alongside the initial large scale runs with the same buffers or media. This can help to troubleshoot problems that may be observed at scale by providing a control for some of the parameters.
    • An overall assessment comparing the small scale runs to the production runs should be documented or referenced in a campaign summary report.
  • Process training
    • Training should be conducted by SMEs prior to the start of the GMP campaign. It may be helpful to have some elements of this training led by personnel from the sending unit where they may have more extensive process experience. It also may be useful to have personnel from the sending unit as support staff during operation of first full scale batch.
  • Models
    • Various models can be used by companies to develop pharmaceutical products. The rigor and documentation associated with the technical transfer of such models should be commensurate with the complexity and risk associated with their implementation and intended use.
    • Where models are established only for the purpose of understanding a process and its unit operations, it is usually sufficient to transfer the model as part of the knowledge base for the product, as these models are not part of ongoing control decisions during production. Where models are established as elements of a control strategy for ongoing production, consideration should be given to scale, sampling, and instrument dependencies as applicable.
    • In addition, procedures to calibrate, verify, maintain, if applicable validate, and update models should be established, as appropriate to the nature of the model being used.
  • Creation of real time processing models and data trending template
  • Identification of in-process controls for monitoring
  • Cleaning requirements per process step, including required analysis and forward processing criteria
  • Generation of sample plan

In a GMP environment, some of these activities may require additional risk assessments and change controls as a part of the activity.

Additionally, the operational readiness phase of the technology transfer project will include the generation of the documentation and/or electronic systems that will be used to execute the technology at the receiving unit. This activity should be supported through the information and deliverables that were included as a part of the technology transfer package generated in the pre-transfer planning phase.

The operational readiness phase of the technology transfer is complete when the process and analytical procedures are qualified. The sending and receiving units should be confident in the receiving unit's ability to successfully execute analytical procedures or manufacturing processes.

Following successful completion of the technology transfer operational readiness phase, the project will move into the process/procedure qualification phase of the project.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Five Pillars of Operational Readiness

ISPE defines Operational Readiness as "the state of readiness that must be achieved before entering the qualification phase". In practice, it breaks down into five pillars, none of which can be missing:

  • Equipment: installation and qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) fully complete, with a plan in place for cleaning validation
  • Personnel: process SMEs trained, and GMP documentation competence assessed
  • Materials: raw materials, consumables and reference standards obtained and qualified, supply chain ready
  • Procedures: batch records, SOPs and automation recipes all issued and reviewed by both parties
  • Regulatory: required filing changes submitted and the regulatory timeline confirmed

All five pillars must be green before the first batch can start: this is where most delays occur.

Analogy

Operational readiness is like the checklist routine in a hospital operating room: before surgery, you must confirm that the anesthesia machine (equipment) works, the surgical team (personnel) is in place, the blood bags (materials) are ready, the surgical procedure (SOP) is approved, and the pre-operative consent form (regulatory documents) is signed. If any one is missing, the operation cannot begin, no matter how experienced the surgeon is. The first GMP batch of a technology transfer is that "operation".

Key Point: Why Do Most TT Delays Occur in Phase 4?

Engineering Runs expose the real gaps, and the following issues are often underestimated during risk identification in Phase 3:

  • The cleaning validation method fails with the geometry of the new equipment, requiring the analytical method to be redeveloped
  • Raw material qualification (vendor qualification) is delayed by long supplier audit cycles
  • Batch production record (BPR) review rounds between the two parties run 2-3 rounds longer than expected
  • Automation recipes show parameter drift after being ported to the new platform, requiring additional qualification

Warning for CDMOs: contract timelines usually underestimate the Phase 4 buffer, which is the most common source of commercial disputes.

Practical Application: The Strategic Value of a Small-Scale Satellite Model for a CDMO

ISPE recommends running a small-scale laboratory model in "satellite mode" in parallel with large-scale manufacturing. For a CDMO, this strategy brings three competitive advantages:

  • Fast troubleshooting: when a large-scale batch shows an anomaly, the small-scale model can be used immediately to reproduce it and isolate variables, without waiting for the next batch
  • Knowledge retention: even if the client withdraws, the CDMO keeps a working small-scale model, which is a technical asset for any future re-transfer
  • Regulatory support: satellite laboratory data can serve as qualification evidence for the scale-down model, supporting FDA/EMA submissions

Key Point: Timing Risk in Analytical Method Qualification

ISPE states clearly that Method Qualification must be completed before Process Qualification. If concurrent execution is allowed, management must formally accept and document the risk.

Why is this a risk? If process batches start before the method is qualified and the method then fails, the batch results cannot be interpreted. The GMP batches are wasted, costing both time and money. This is exactly the trap that makes transfer of cleaning validation analytical methods so often underestimated.

Practice Questions

  1. If a CDMO finds in Phase 4 that the equipment cleaning method is not suitable for the new equipment, what is the best risk-handling path? Should it start a small-scale cleaning effectiveness study, or ask the client directly to provide an alternative method?
  2. How much data from the "satellite mode small-scale model" is enough to support scale-down model qualification? What does ICH Q5E require for comparability studies of biologics?
  3. How are the review rounds of batch records between the two parties usually defined in the contract? When the rounds are exceeded, how should cost and time responsibility be allocated?
3.5 Process (Procedure) Qualification

Original Text ISPE TT 3rd Ed.

3.5 Process (Procedure) Qualification

As noted in Section 3.3, a documented review of all readiness criteria and transfer requirements in the plan should be completed in a gated review with management oversight prior to proceeding to the process (procedure) qualification phase.

This phase may be executed through a demonstration of the technology at the receiving unit and a comparison of the process and product and data generated to ensure it aligns with expectations. This activity may be performed as a part of a formal qualification or validation exercise, in which case the data generated may be useful for supporting the regulatory approval processes.

The risk assessment from the technology transfer planning phases should be signed off and documented in accordance with the QMSs for both the sending and receiving units prior to proceeding to process (procedure) qualification. For further information, see ICH Q9 and the ISPE Guide Series: Product Quality Lifecycle Implementation (PQLI®). As previously noted in Section 3.3, risk assessments are most effective when utilized in phase gate reviews.

This phase should involve completion of the assessment of the data and product produced as a part of the transfer execution and identification of required mitigation plans to remediate any issues observed during qualification. The sending unit and receiving unit should define a plan for routine support of the technology at the receiving unit.

Execution of a qualification protocol should not begin until the protocol has been reviewed and approved by all appropriate departments, including the appropriate quality units. Any departures from the protocol should be made according to established quality procedures.

A report documenting and assessing adherence to the written qualification protocol should be prepared in a timely manner after the completion of the protocol. This report should:

  • Discuss and cross-reference all aspects of the protocol.
  • Summarize data collected and analyze the data, as specified by the protocol.
  • Evaluate any unexpected observations and additional data not specified in the protocol.
  • Summarize and discuss all non-conformances, such as deviations, aberrant test results, or other information that has bearing on the validity of the analytical procedure/process.
  • Describe in sufficient detail any corrective actions or changes that should be made to existing procedures and controls.
  • State a clear conclusion as to whether the data indicates the analytical procedure met the conditions established in the protocol and whether the analytical procedure is considered to be in a state of control. If not, the report should state what should be accomplished before such a conclusion can be reached.
  • Include all appropriate department and quality units review and approvals.
  • Provide the basis for continued process monitoring and control strategies.

It is recommended that a statistician or person with adequate training in statistical techniques help develop the data collection plan and any statistical methods and procedures used in measuring and evaluating the process or analytical method. If the process or analytical method qualification does not meet the acceptance criteria, the cause of the failure should be investigated and addressed before repeating the exercise.

Tutorial Commentary

Analogy

Engineering Runs are like the chef's "tasting day" before the grand opening: you can try, fail and adjust the recipe, and you do not charge customers. PPQ batches are like the restaurant's "opening day": every dish must be perfect, the head chef (QA) must sign, failure records stay in the health inspection file, and the food (product) is to be sold to patients. The cost of failure is entirely different in the two cases.

Statistical Framework for Analytical Method Comparability

Analytical Comparability: core question:
Can the receiving unit's analytical method produce results
"statistically equivalent" to those of the sending unit?

Verification levels (following ICH Q2(R1) logic):
1. Precision Comparison
   RSD(receiving) vs RSD(sending) - F-test or equivalence test

2. Accuracy/Bias Comparison
   Mean bias = |μ receiving - μ sending| / μ sending × 100%
   Typical acceptance criteria: bias < 2% (small molecule)
                                bias < 5% (bioassay)

3. Platform Comparison
   n samples analyzed simultaneously in both laboratories → paired t-test or
   Bland-Altman plot - visualizes systematic bias

4. Measurement Uncertainty
   EU Annex 15 / USP <1033> require uncertainty to be quantified

Statistical support is indispensable: ISPE explicitly recommends that people with a statistical background design the data collection plan.

Key Point: Regulatory Attitude Toward Concurrent Validation

In certain situations (such as rare diseases or urgent need), FDA allows "Concurrent Validation", meaning batches are released before all analysis of the qualification batches is complete. This requires:

  • The rationale is clearly stated in the application dossier (BLA/NDA/ANDA)
  • A robust control strategy with real-time release testing (RTRT)
  • Subsequent qualification results must be back-filled and reported

ISPE stresses here that whichever strategy is adopted, the risk assessment must be formally accepted in writing by management. If a CDMO accepts this requirement, it must explicitly reserve the right to recall batches in the contract.

Practical Application: The "Six Essentials" of a PPQ Report

ISPE lists six elements that a PPQ report must cover. For a CDMO, this is also the core document framework delivered to the client:

  • Full cross-reference to the protocol (cross-reference all aspects of protocol)
  • Data summary and statistical analysis (summarize and analyze data)
  • Evaluation of unexpected observations (unexpected observations)
  • Complete list of non-conformances (non-conformances, deviations)
  • Description of corrective actions (corrective actions)
  • Clear conclusion statement (clear conclusion)

None of these six can be omitted; among FDA 483 observations, "unclear conclusion in the PPQ report" is a common finding.

  2. In a biologics technology transfer, how many PPQ batches are usually enough? What is the criterion for judging "enough"?
  3. If the receiving unit's analytical data show a systematic bias that is still within specification, does this count as a successful transfer? How would you convince the regulator?

3.6 Finalize Transfer and Perform Review

Original Text ISPE TT 3rd Ed.

3.6 Finalize Transfer and Perform Review

The final actions of the project should be focused on performing a review (sometimes called an after action review) to collect specific knowledge from the technology transfer. This should consider how each stage in the transfer went, how effective the communication and team work were, and whether any issues experienced during the qualification exercises were related to the effectiveness of the knowledge transfer.

The review may identify the need for a remediation plan and any ongoing support required from the sending unit. Where appropriate, key knowledge from the review should be shared with the teams involved in the technology transfer.

The complete evaluation of the success of the technology transfer through analysis of data or product (e.g., unexpected variability) generated as a part of the transfer execution will involve the sending and receiving units evaluating the performance of the technology, as well as the laboratory or manufacturing facility, and determining if any corrective action is required prior to declaring the transfer successful.

3.6.1 Planning for Transition of Support and Disengagement of Sending Unit

This can potentially include accountabilities and responsibilities for process or method improvements identified as a part of the transfer or through lifecycle management. There should be a clear transfer of responsibility to the receiving unit.

Agreement should be defined regarding the intended level of continuing support and involvement from the sending unit members of the technology transfer team.

Examples of aspects that should be considered for ongoing operations include:

  • The receiving unit will operate the specified process.
  • A change control procedure should be in place at the receiving unit.
  • Process deviations should be noted and evaluated for communication with the sending unit.
  • Process changes (continuous process improvement/lifecycle management) may be needed and initiated by either the sending or receiving unit. The initiated change must have prior agreement and be performed through a change control procedure. In some cases, process changes may require re-validation of the process. Global change control (e.g., when multiple sites are making the same product) is outside the scope of this document.
  • Process monitoring should be performed by the receiving unit. Any anomalous process trends should be evaluated for communication with the sending unit. If the product is manufactured at multiple sites, regular manufacturing review meetings should be conducted to troubleshoot processing problems between sites.

3.6.2 Support of Regulatory Activities

Post-transfer activities can include support of regulatory inspections or questions as a result of submissions associated with the transfer. Members from either the sending or receiving unit may be needed to provide assistance with regulatory activities by authoring documentation for filings or participating in site inspections.

3.6.3 Lessons Learned

An ongoing review during the transfer and/or a review at the conclusion of transfer phases that captures best practice and shared lessons learned should be performed. This should be documented and accessible to other teams from a continuous improvement perspective.

Where lessons learned are not captured during the execution of the transfer, a lessons learned meeting should be held as soon as possible after the completion of the process qualification. The meeting should provide an opportunity to review the technology transfer project and overall GMP performance. At a minimum, the meeting should involve members of the technology transfer team and focus on the successes and challenges faced throughout the project. It may be valuable to break out into specific areas that need to be addressed, for example:

  • Process
  • Documents
  • Analytics
  • Quality
  • Project management

The output from the meeting should be documented and shared with the technology transfer team.

The sending and receiving units should formally document lessons learned and determine future actions that would improve the success and efficiency of transfer projects or product commercialization.

Teams should strive to distinguish knowledge that is known (e.g., CQAs, CPPs, formal reports, Annual Product Review summaries, SOPs, etc.) from that which can be described as tacit (i.e., the knowledge that may be crucial for a successful transfer but is not actually written down.) Such tacit knowledge might include:

  • Historical knowledge
  • Informal knowledge
  • The way an operator or a laboratory analyst performs certain steps
  • Details of the order of operations, e.g. powder addition rates in the preparation of intermediates
  • The unusual operation of items of equipment
  • The actual way an operation is carried out (rather than just what the SOP says)
  • Ranges within which operations proven to be effective
  • Interventions
  • Other (non-critical) quality attributes or process parameters that might have an impact on, for example, ease of operation rather than directly impact product quality
  • Converting data into knowledge (e.g., data points can be plotted on a control chart and the knowledge is gained by assessing trends, limits, robustness, etc.)
  • Use of work arounds (which, in practice, should be built into formal procedures)
  • Taking learning from new events that arise (e.g., ensuring CAPAs do deliver the prevention as well as the correction)

It should be noted that ICH Q10 recommends knowledge to be managed over the whole product lifecycle.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Legal and Operational Definition of "Transfer Complete"

Phase 6 must answer one core question: who has the authority to declare the transfer successful, and on what criteria?

ISPE recommends that the transfer completion criteria include:

  • Technical: all PPQ batches meet the predefined acceptance criteria, with no open Critical OOS
  • Documentation: the Technology Transfer Package (TTP) is fully handed over and knowledge transfer is documented
  • Organizational: the receiving unit formally takes over responsibility, and change control has been handed over
  • Regulatory: the relevant filings (NDA/BLA Supplement or Site Change) have been submitted or approved

Key lesson: if the Charter does not clearly define "who" declares completion, commercial disputes are unavoidable when the two parties disagree.

Analogy

Phase 6 is like the handover of a house: it is not finished just because construction is done. You need a full "inspection report" (After Action Review), a clear "handover list" (defect list and assignment of responsibilities), and the "title transfer registration" (transfer of legal responsibility). Without any one of these, the house may be built, but legally it has not yet been "handed over to you". The same is true of technology transfer: passing PPQ does not equal legal "transfer complete".

Key Point: Three Conditions for the Sending Unit to Be Released from Its Obligations

ISPE 3.6.1 stresses that disengagement of the Sending Unit must be supported by a clear contract and must meet three prerequisites:

  • Technical responsibility transferred: the receiving unit has independent capability for process improvement and deviation handling
  • Change control mechanism in place: the receiving unit has established a Change Control procedure, and both parties have a written agreement on their obligation to communicate future process changes
  • Monitoring responsibility handed over: the obligation to report anomalies in Process Monitoring data is clearly defined

CDMO warning: "the client comes back with questions after the technology transfer is complete" is a common dispute. The contract must specify the rates and the upper limit of obligations for technical support after Phase 6.

Core Concept: Explicit Knowledge vs. Tacit Knowledge

ISPE raises the most important knowledge management challenge in 3.6.3: the transfer of Tacit Knowledge.

Explicit Knowledge can be written in documents: CQA lists, CPP ranges, SOPs, validation reports.

Tacit Knowledge cannot be found in SOPs but is critical to success, for example:

  • "The agitator shaft on this equipment vibrates slightly, so the speed must be set at 85% rather than 100%"
  • "The powder addition rate must be reduced in the last 5 minutes, otherwise dissolution is inconsistent"
  • "Analysts habitually wait 30 seconds for stabilization when sampling, but the SOP only says to sample immediately"

If this knowledge is not transferred, PPQ may pass, but the yield of subsequent production batches will be systematically lower than at the sending unit.

A Structured Framework for Lessons Learned Meetings

Five-dimension Lessons Learned analysis framework:

Dimension     | Key question
--------------|----------------------------------
Process       | Which process steps were hardest to transfer? Why?
Docs          | Which documents were of insufficient quality? How efficient was the review?
Analytics     | Was method transfer on time? Which metrics drifted?
Quality       | What were the root causes of OOS / Deviations?
Project Mgmt  | In which Phase did schedule deviations occur? Why?

Distinguishing Observation from Lesson Learned:
- Observation = problem found but no action → it will recur next time
- Lesson Learned = problem found + prioritization + action plan + tracking and monitoring

Practical Application: CDMO Contract Strategy for Phase 6

After a technology transfer is complete, a CDMO typically faces two major "post-transfer obligations" requested by clients:

  • Regulatory inspection support (3.6.2): responses to FDA/EMA before the client's application is approved. The CDMO must define the scope of support in the contract (response within how many hours? who writes the response documents? how are costs calculated?)
  • Formulation/process updates: if the originator updates the process after the transfer, is the CDMO obliged to requalify? Who bears the cost?

It is recommended to add the following explicitly to the TT Charter: "After Phase 6 is complete, technical support beyond the agreed scope will be charged separately according to the [rate schedule]. The client is obliged to provide the latest process update information, and a client that delays doing so may not claim breach by the CDMO."

Key Point: ICH Q10 Knowledge Management Requirements

ISPE cites an important statement in ICH Q10 Section 1.6.1: knowledge management must cover the whole lifecycle, from development through commercial life to product discontinuation. The technology transfer activity itself is part of knowledge management. This means:

  • Data and knowledge generated during the transfer are product knowledge and must be retained systematically
  • ICH Q10 compliance requires the CDMO to establish a knowledge management system (not just a document management system)
  • The Annual Product Review (APR) must be traceable to the baseline data established during the TT

Practice Questions

  1. Suppose a Lessons Learned meeting finds that a process risk was already identified in Phase 3 but no action was taken because of low priority, and it finally caused a deviation in PPQ. Is this a "lesson learned" or an "observation"? What should the next action be?
  2. Three months after Phase 6 completes, a sending unit SME leaves and takes a large amount of tacit knowledge with them. How should the receiving unit respond? Whose responsibility is this? How should the contract prevent it?
  3. After the technology transfer is complete, the client asks the CDMO to revalidate because the originator updated the specification of a critical raw material. Is this inside or outside the scope of the TT contract? What is your argument?
3.6.3.1 Transfer of Ownership and Closure

Original Text ISPE TT 3rd Ed.

3.6.3.1 Transfer of Ownership and Closure

Upon completion of technology transfer, a final assessment of the remaining risks should be conducted and documented as part of technology transfer close-out. A review should be conducted by the technical transfer team in order to document and agree that all critical issues have been resolved, knowledge transfer is documented, and any outstanding items have been assigned for owners to resolve.

Successful technology transfer should ensure that all high risks have been addressed and justification has been documented as to why some residual risks are acceptable and agreed by the receiving unit. At the time, any additional support that is required should be agreed to by the receiving unit.

An important step to improve future technology transfers is for the receiving unit to provide feedback to the sending unit. Depending on the cycle times of technology transfer, production schedules, and regulatory agency filings, this feedback may occur during the transfer, throughout PPQ and during regulatory agency driven events such as filings and inspections.

ICH Q10, Section 1.6.1: "Product and process knowledge should be managed from development through the commercial life of the product up to and including product discontinuation. For example, development activities using scientific approaches provide knowledge for product and process understanding. Knowledge management is a systematic approach to acquiring, analysing, storing and disseminating information related to products, manufacturing processes and components. Sources of knowledge include, but are not limited to prior knowledge (public domain or internally documented); pharmaceutical development studies; technology transfer activities; process validation studies over the product lifecycle; manufacturing experience; innovation; continual improvement; and change management activities."

The following ISPE concept papers provide useful supporting information and refer to technology transfer and knowledge management:

  • ISPE Concept Paper: Implementing Knowledge Management in Bioprocess
  • ISPE Concept Paper: The Role of Process Capability in Monitoring Product Quality

The ISPE Cultural Excellence Report also has some indicators regarding the importance of knowledge.

It is important for the team to distinguish between observations and true lessons learned. The key distinction is the effectiveness and sustainability of the actions taken related to the observation. The following points are offered as a tool for teams to distinguish lessons learned from observations:

  • Lessons learned could include observation, prioritization, and an action and monitoring plan.
  • Observations without actions can result in reoccurrence of the respective event. Depending on the impact of reoccurrence, CAPAs may be developed and tracked in a company's QMS.
  • Collect "work arounds" can be short term solutions but are often an unsustainable means to maintain performance.

Communication of the observations to the appropriate stakeholders, particularly to the receiving unit, should be confirmed prior to conclusion of the technology transfer project. In addition, it is recommended that the receiving unit manage a decision process for which observations warrant action plans and tracking.

Lessons learned documentation may be in addition to corrective actions captured and tracked through a QMS.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three Gates to Closure

ISPE 3.6.3.1 defines three necessary conditions for formal close-out of a technology transfer:

  1. All Critical Issues resolved: the transfer team confirms in writing that no high-risk items remain open
  2. Residual risks have documented justification: not "there is no risk", but "the residual risks have been knowingly accepted by the receiving unit"
  3. Outstanding items have an owner: outstanding items cannot be ownerless; an owner and a completion deadline must be assigned

If any one is missing, the technology transfer is not complete in either the legal or the GMP sense.

Analogy

Closure is like the "completion acceptance" of a construction project: it does not mean every problem has been solved before acceptance, but all major defects must be corrected, and minor defects may be remedied within an agreed period, with the contractor listing them in writing and stating responsibilities. Without a formal acceptance procedure, any later dispute between the owner and the contractor can only rely on memory and verbal accounts, which is completely unacceptable in the GMP world.

Key Point: The "Informed Acceptance" Principle for Residual Risk

ISPE's wording is precise: "justification has been documented as to why some residual risks are acceptable and agreed by the receiving unit". This has two implications:

  • Acceptability is justified (Justified): it is not enough to write "low risk"; you must explain why it is low and what data support it
  • Receiving unit agrees knowingly (Agreed): the receiving unit's QA and management must sign in writing to confirm acceptance, otherwise responsibility is unclear if problems occur in future

This is the core requirement of "Risk Communication" in ICH Q9 risk management.

Document checklist required for formal Phase 6 close-out:

[ ] PPQ report - approved, clear conclusion
[ ] Method comparability report - completed
[ ] Residual risk assessment - signed by the receiving unit
[ ] Technology Transfer Package (TTP) - fully handed over

Key Point: The Danger of Work-Arounds

ISPE specifically warns about "work arounds": informal ways of operating that "everyone knows but are not written in the SOP". These are usually:

  • Solutions that work in the short term but are not sustainable in the long term
  • "Unwritten rules" that new employees do not know and veterans take for granted
  • If they are not incorporated into formal procedures, a knowledge gap forms when key personnel leave

For a CDMO: after the Lessons Learned meeting, all known work-arounds must be systematically converted into formal SOP revisions. This is the most direct action to prevent the next batch from failing.

Practical Application: How Receiving Unit Feedback Improves Future TTs

ISPE 3.6.3.1 stresses that the receiving unit must provide feedback to the sending unit, and not only at close-out but throughout the whole TT cycle (including during PPQ and during regulatory inspections). For a CDMO, building a systematic feedback mechanism brings two commercial benefits:

  • Internal learning: Lessons Learned from each TT accumulate into the CDMO's institutional knowledge, shortening the time of the next similar transfer
  • Client relationship: proactively sharing Lessons Learned reports (after appropriate redaction) demonstrates transparency and builds long-term trust

Mature European CDMOs such as White Raven can shorten TT cycles partly because they hold a systematic Lessons Learned database that avoids repeating mistakes.

Practice Questions

  1. If, after Phase 6 close-out, the receiving unit finds that a Work-Around was not incorporated into the SOP but has already been used in the PPQ batches, how should the CDMO handle it? Is a deviation investigation required?
  2. ICH Q10 Section 1.6.1 mentions the "six sources" of knowledge management. List these six sources and explain which step technology transfer is.
  3. If a CDMO identifies in Lessons Learned that the poor quality of the sending unit's TTP documents was the main cause of this TT's delay, how can this observation be converted into a "lesson learned"? What should the specific action plan include?

Chapter Summary

  • Phase 4: Operational Readiness is where technology transfer delays are most frequent: the five pillars of equipment, personnel, materials, procedures and regulatory must all be ready before the first GMP batch can start; analytical method qualification should precede process qualification.
  • Engineering Runs vs. PPQ: engineering runs are informal learning, while PPQ batches are formal GMP regulatory evidence. The two differ sharply in deviation handling, batch release and regulatory implications; none of the six essential elements of a PPQ report can be omitted.
  • Analytical method comparability is not just "within specification" but statistically demonstrating that the receiving unit's method is equivalent to the sending unit's (three-dimensional verification of precision, accuracy and systematic bias, designed by people with a statistical background).
  • The definition of transfer completion in Phase 6 must be set out in advance in the Charter, including who declares completion and on which technical, documentation and regulatory criteria; residual risks must be knowingly accepted in writing by the receiving unit.
  • Tacit Knowledge is the hardest risk to transfer and the most often underestimated: operators' habitual practices, unconventional equipment behavior, and effective operating ranges. These cannot be found in SOPs but are critical to production success.
  • CDMO contract strategy: continuing technical support obligations after Phase 6, rates for regulatory inspection assistance, and responsibility for process updates must be clearly defined in the contract to avoid commercial disputes.

ISPE TT Ch.4

This chapter highlights those aspects of a technology transfer process specific to analytical methods.

The goal of an analytical method technology transfer is to ensure that knowledge from the sending unit is translated into an effective analytical method control strategy (that supports the overall product control strategy) in the receiving unit. When a receiving unit is required to perform an analytical method transfer, the company should execute an SOP to ensure:

  • Consistent practices are followed
  • Exchange of knowledge between the sending unit and the receiving unit is clear and robust
  • Data produced by the receiving unit is fit for purpose, equivalent to data from the sending unit, and defensible from both quality and regulatory standpoints

Method transfer may be required for several reasons, including:

  • Initial introduction of an analytical method into a unit that has not previously executed an equivalent method
  • Introduction into a manufacturing facility by a development group
  • Transfer from a manufacturing facility or development facility to another
  • Transfer into a third party testing unit

The level of effort, formality and documentation of the method transfer, and any testing (performed to demonstrate the success of the transfer) should be commensurate with the criticality of the respective analytical data. For example, companies may not want to spend the same amount of time on transfer specific activities for products in the early versus late stage of its lifecycle. Late stage method transfers should be rigorously performed in order to ensure success of the method transfer, in support of product release and/or inclusion of the data into regulatory registration filings.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Analytical Method Technology Transfer: This is not just "handing the SOP to the other side"; it is making sure the receiving unit can generate data that is equivalent to the sending unit's and defensible.

Three key elements:
1. Consistency: following the same SOPs and conventions
2. Equivalence: receiving unit data = sending unit data (statistically demonstrable)
3. Defensibility: holds up from both a quality and a regulatory standpoint

Analogy

Transferring an analytical method is like a chef moving to a new restaurant: the recipe (SOP) can travel, but the same dish made in a new kitchen with different stoves and different water may taste different. Proving it is "just as good" is the standard for a successful transfer.

A late-stage transfer is like the signature dish of a Michelin-starred restaurant: the diners (regulators) already have expectations, and any deviation needs a full explanation.

Key Notes

Commensurate with criticality: The level of rigor invested differs sharply between an early clinical method and a pre-launch commercial method. For the latter, regulators expect a complete transfer protocol, statistical acceptance criteria and a formal report.

Transfer of analytical methods to a third party (such as a CRO or contract laboratory) also falls within the scope of this chapter; CDMOs in particular need to watch for this scenario.

4.2.1 Form an Analytical Method Transfer Team

Key Concepts

Analytical Method Transfer Team: A joint working group spanning both parties. It is not a one-way "sending unit instructs receiving unit" arrangement but a two-way exchange of knowledge.

4.2.2 Consolidate Knowledge for the Analytical Method Transfer

Original Text

For analytical methods, key information that needs to be collated to ensure effective knowledge transfer usually includes:

  • Stage of the product lifecycle that the product is in
  • Documented history of experience with the analytical method(s) including any development and validation reports
  • Summary reports of any issues identified from data trends, analytical method deviations, and out of specification (OOS) results associated with the methods being transferred

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Analytical Target Profile (ATP): Similar to the QTPP in product development, the ATP defines the expected performance requirements of the analytical method, including the maximum allowable measurement uncertainty. It is an important output document of QbD method development.

OOS / OOT history records: This is the requirement a CDMO most easily overlooks. The sending unit may hold past OOS investigation records or deviation reports; these documents reveal the conditions under which the method tends to fail and serve as an important risk map for the receiving unit.

Key Notes - Quick Reference: The Five Transfer Strategies

From low risk to high complexity:

  • Waiver: The receiving unit is already familiar with the same technique → no testing required, but a documented scientific justification is needed
  • Method Verification: Compendial methods (pH, osmolality, etc.) → a single acceptance test is sufficient
  • Qualitative Transfer: Appearance, NIR identification, etc. → training-based, no comparative data needed
  • Quantitative Transfer Study: Assay, impurities, etc. → both parties compare data, with statistical acceptance criteria
  • Co-validation: The method is not yet fully validated → both parties perform validation concurrently, with shared responsibility

Analogy

The five transfer strategies are like different degrees of "handing over a recipe" between restaurants:
• Waiver = the chef already knows the dish and simply cooks it from the SOP
• Verification = a standard recipe (compendium); cook it once to confirm the taste meets the standard
• Qualitative = visually confirm color and appearance; training is enough
• Quantitative = each restaurant cooks a batch and a third party runs a blind taste comparison
• Co-validation = two chefs develop the dish together and earn the "star certification" at the same time

Practical Application - Knowledge Requirements Checklist for the CDMO Receiving Unit

As a CDMO, the package is only complete once you have requested the following documents from the client (sending unit):

  • Method Development Report - shows why the method was designed the way it was
  • Method Validation Report - shows the performance boundaries of the method
  • OOS/OOT history records - show under which conditions the method tends to fail
  • Method revision history (change control records) - shows how the method evolved
  • Typical chromatograms/spectra - serve as the baseline comparison reference for the receiving unit
  • Reference standard information - supplier, grade, stability and current inventory status

Contract clause recommendation: Include in the technology transfer agreement an obligation to deliver a "complete method history document package", with a delivery deadline.

4.2.3 Identify Risks by Conducting Risk Assessments

Original Text

For analytical method transfers, risk assessments may be divided into two types:

  • Technical risk assessments: Assessment of all high level technical risks associated with introducing the analytical method into the receiving unit. Technical risks include aspects such as analytical issues with the method, frequent failures to meet system suitability and assay acceptance, difficulties in training staff, etc.
  • Operational risk assessments: Detailed mapping exercise aimed at understanding all potential sources of variability in the operation of the analytical method and developing appropriate controls.

It may be possible to abridge risk assessments if methods, procedures, equipment, and/or equipment parameters are identical between the sending and receiving units.

4.2.3.1 Conduct a Technical Risk Assessment

The technical risk assessment should identify risks associated with utilization of the method in the new environment of the receiving unit. Table 4.2 lists questions to facilitate the risk assessment and provides suggested remediation opportunities.

The analytical method transfer may be considered low risk when:

  • For a new product with a composition that is comparable to that of an existing product and/or the concentration of active ingredient is similar to that of an existing product and is analyzed by analytical methods with which the receiving unit already has experience
  • The same as or very similar to an analytical method already in use
  • Personnel who performed the method development, method validation, or routine method analysis of a product at the sending unit are moved to the receiving unit

If the risk is considered low, a technology transfer using comparative test data may not be required.

4.2.3.2 Conduct an Operational Risk Assessment

Where the possibility exists that a receiving unit generates data that is not "right the first time" or is of a more complex nature than the receiving unit has experience, an operational risk assessment can be performed. This risk assessment is used to identify, in detail, the differences between how the analytical method is operated at the sending unit versus how it will be operated in the receiving unit.

It is good practice to use tools, such as process mapping and Ishikawa (fishbone) diagrams, to systematically identify different variables that may exist in the sending and receiving units.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Technical Risk Assessment: High level; a systematic question list on whether "the method itself" can work in the new environment. It answers "Will there be problems transferring this method?"

Operational Risk Assessment: Detailed level; maps all sources of variation in "how the method is executed". It answers "For the same method, how does practice differ between the two sites?"

Analogy

A technical risk assessment is like "surveying the house before moving": Does the new home have enough power outlets? Is there a gas line? These structural questions must be confirmed before the move.

An operational risk assessment is like "trial living after moving in": discovering that the refrigerator does not fit in the kitchen, the hallway lighting is too dim, the curtains do not block the sun - these are execution-level differences in detail.

Key Notes - System Suitability: The Hidden Trap of Analytical Method Transfer

Real scenario: The receiving unit (CDMO) used a different brand of HPLC (e.g. Waters vs. Agilent). Although the method conditions were the same, differences in injector dwell volume, gradient delay time and column temperature control accuracy meant that system suitability (SS) could not be met.

How to handle it:

  • Identify instrument differences early (item 5 of the technical risk assessment question list)
  • Predefine an "instrument equivalence" verification step in the protocol
  • If necessary, adjust the SS criteria (the client must be notified and the scientific justification documented)
  • In extreme cases: advise the client in advance that a method revision is needed before the transfer

Ishikawa Fishbone Application - Classifying Sources of Operational Risk

Sources of operational variation in analytical methods (6M framework):

Man (personnel): operator training level, consistency across shifts
Machine (instrument): HPLC model, integration software version, calibration status
Material: reagent lots, water quality for mobile phase preparation, reference standard expiry
Method: SOP version, calculation formulas, SS acceptance criteria
Measurement: injection volume, wavelength calibration, temperature settings
Environment: laboratory temperature and humidity, vibration sources, lighting

4.2.4 Readiness to Ensure Receiving Unit "Right the First Time" Execution

Original Text

The analytical method transfer team needs to determine the number of assays to be performed and sample types to test during training and/or readiness. Prior to performing any live transfer activities, it is recommended to perform one or more assays at the receiving unit. To evaluate unit readiness and to enable the design of readiness training and experiments, a test-a-batch exercise may be performed. This exercise is used to obtain alignment around the following requirements for the receiving unit:

  • Access to the current method
  • Access to and performed review of any supporting documentation (e.g., method validation report, method development report, control charts, etc.)
  • Appropriate equipment that are qualified as applicable
  • Appropriate software, templates, scripts, etc. that are verified and qualified
  • Appropriate materials, standards, and samples at the unit ready for use
  • Qualified staff
  • Appropriate quality systems required to support analysis in place
  • Access to any additional sample handling training for testing
  • Proficiency in similar analytical methods
  • Performed assessment of the complexity of the analytical method to be transferred
  • Performed assessment of the lifecycle state of the method (validated versus non-validated)
  • Understanding the testing that the method is for (e.g., testing for stability indicating methods depend on the degradation pathway)
  • Predetermination of the number of readiness assays that may be completed at the receiving unit if training occurs at the sending unit

If readiness results are determined to be unacceptable, a plan should be developed to assess the root cause including, but not limited to, training, method performance, equipment, materials/samples, and calculations.

Depending on the complexity of each analytical method, it may be useful for representatives from the receiving unit to directly observe the operation of analytical methods at the sending unit in order to ensure effective transfer of all tactical knowledge. Additionally, it may be useful for the sending unit team to observe the receiving unit in order to build a good understanding of the equipment and establish capability at a practical level.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Test-a-Batch Exercise: Before the formal transfer protocol is executed, the receiving unit runs one or more "warm-up runs" with real or simulated samples. The purpose is to confirm readiness, not to generate data to be used for acceptance.

The "Right the First Time" principle: The data from the formal transfer protocol should be right the first time, not the result of repeated trial and error. This requires thorough preparation up front rather than rushing straight into protocol execution.

Analogy

A readiness assessment is like a dress rehearsal: before the formal performance (execution of the transfer protocol), run a full rehearsal. Problems exposed in the rehearsal can be corrected without affecting the formal records.

The receiving unit's visit to the sending unit is like actors observing the original production crew to learn "tacit performance techniques"; the sending unit's visit to the receiving unit is like the director inspecting the new venue to confirm the stage equipment.

Key Notes

Practical points of the readiness checklist (CDMO perspective):

  • Equipment Qualification: Instruments must have valid calibration and IQ/OQ/PQ records before being used for GMP testing
  • Software version: The chromatography data system (CDS) version must be compatible with the method SOP; integration algorithms in different versions may cause systematic differences
  • Stability Indicating Method: Samples containing the relevant degradation products must be prepared to verify the ability to separate degradants; fresh samples alone are not sufficient

4.2.5-4.2.6 Analytical Method Transfer Plan and Protocol

Original Text

4.2.5 Analytical Method Transfer Plan

An analytical method transfer plan, with a project plan to evaluate timing and resources, is recommended for the transfer of two or more methods. The implementation strategy should account for the following factors:

  • Experience and knowledge of the receiving unit
  • Degree of familiarity of the receiving unit with the methodology or technology used
  • Specification of the product
  • Complexity of the analytical method

The method transfer plan should include details of how many repetitions will be performed and how the performance of the analytical method will be assessed. The number of repetitions should be adequate to provide sufficient statistical confidence.

At this stage, it may be useful to define the maximum acceptable uncertainty in the data (as per the ATP, if available). This can be established by setting equivalency criteria using a specific statistical test for demonstrating comparability between units. It requires that the two-sided 90% confidence interval (or two, one-sided 95% confidence bounds) for the difference in units fits entirely inside pre-established bounds.

4.2.6 Analytical Method Transfer Protocol

An analytical method transfer protocol should be generated to address each method being transferred. The cumulative knowledge available should be used to establish a transfer protocol that ensures appropriate experiments are performed to demonstrate that the analytical method produces data in the receiving unit which are fit for purpose.

The criteria should include a description of any statistical methods to be used in analyzing collected data and any provision for addressing deviations from expected conditions and handling of nonconforming data. Data should not be excluded from further consideration without a documented, science-based justification.

For each test involved in the transfer, the type of transfer should be clearly articulated. Transfer types may comprise:

  • Paper exercise/scientific justification, i.e. waiver
  • Method verification
  • Local validation of particular aspects of the method
  • Full side by side comparison of data generated on the appropriate sample by each of the sending and receiving units

The transfer protocol needs to contain predetermined success criteria and be approved prior to execution.

Further recommendations for the analytical method transfer protocol include:

  • Engaging support from the method developer and statisticians to set the study design and success criteria
  • Using historical data to fulfill the success criteria if justified in the protocol versus performing comparative testing
  • Selecting sample types that are representative of the materials being tested at the receiving unit:
    • Consider lots of the same formulation, different formulations/strengths, and different sample types
    • Use the reference standard or assay control as one of the samples for bias assessment
    • For stability indicating methods, include a stability sample, preferably one with a relevant level of degradation
    • For products with multiple strengths, bracketing may be appropriate
    • For qualitative assays, include a negative control sample where possible
    • Set aside ample retain samples in all instances
    • Consider the impact of any shipments of samples between units
  • Justifying the use of any alternate equipment or reagents at the receiving unit
  • If a method revision is required prior to the approval of the protocol, providing a draft of the method along with the protocol for approval
  • Including both the receiving and sending units in protocol review and/or approval
"The samples selected for confirmation or comparability testing should suitably challenge the analytical method. For example, it is recommended to utilize samples with impurity levels close to acceptance and/or threshold criteria in order to evaluate the receiving unit's ability to reach the same quality decisions as the sending unit."

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Difference between the Transfer Plan and the Transfer Protocol:

• Plan: The overall blueprint, covering all methods, strategy selection, resources and timeline; it can span multiple methods

• Protocol: The detailed experimental design for an individual method, including specific acceptance criteria, statistical methods and the sample list; it must be approved in advance

Analogy: the plan is the "project master plan" and the protocol is the "construction drawings for each building".

Statistical Equivalence Criteria

Equivalence testing (Two One-Sided Tests, TOST):

Criterion:
The two-sided 90% confidence interval of the difference
between the receiving unit mean and the sending unit mean
must fall entirely within the predefined
equivalence bounds

Example (HPLC assay):
Equivalence bounds set at: ± 2.0% (absolute)
Observed 90% CI: [-0.8%, +1.2%]
→ Entirely within the bounds → equivalence demonstrated

If the CI extends beyond the bounds (e.g. [-0.8%, +2.5%])
→ Equivalence not demonstrated → investigation required

Common metrics:
• Mean difference
• 90% CI for difference
• SD / %CV (intermediate precision)
• %RSD (repeatability)

Key Notes - The Importance of Predefined Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria must be defined "beforehand" and never adjusted "afterward": This is the data integrity issue regulators care about most. Changing criteria after seeing the data, even with a reasonable justification, can raise doubts about data reliability.

Data exclusion rules: Excluding an invalid assay requires criteria predefined in the protocol, plus a documented scientific justification afterward; both are mandatory.

Challenging sample selection principle: Select samples containing impurities near the specification limit (stressed samples) rather than the "cleanest" samples. Only then can the receiving unit's resolving ability be genuinely assessed.

Analogy

Predefined acceptance criteria are like exam grading standards: they must be announced before the exam, and you cannot decide whether the pass mark is 60 or 70 after seeing the students' answers. Otherwise it is "adjusting the criteria after seeing the data", no different from academic misconduct.

4.2.7-4.2.8 Execute and Report the Analytical Method Transfer

Original Text

4.2.7 Execute the Analytical Method Transfer

The receiving unit staff performs the transfer or qualification experiments as outlined in the analytical method transfer protocol. Data review and assessment of the results should be performed with the sending unit staff, preferably as the method transfer is being executed to prevent any delays in understanding issues with the data being generated throughout protocol execution. The sending unit should be notified of any invalid assays and unexpected results.

Care should be taken to ensure that the integrity of the samples is unaffected by the shipping process between units.

The transfer protocol should define which reference standards are to be used in any testing and from where they are to be obtained.

The defined local operating instructions should be followed during execution of the transfer protocol. The transfer exercise should be performed under normal conditions by the personnel routinely expected to perform the analytical method. Any testing performed during the execution of the transfer should be performed by operators that have been fully trained in all relevant aspects of the required techniques.

A review of the transfer should be performed by the sending unit and the receiving unit. Questions to consider during the review include:

  • Is the data being produced in the receiving unit considered fit for purpose?
  • Is the analytical method operating reliably in the receiving unit?
  • Are there any improvements to the analytical method to consider?

4.2.8 Analytical Method Transfer Report

The receiving unit should generate a report outlining the pre-approved protocol success criteria and an assessment against the criteria for each of the analytical methods involved in the transfer.

If the success criteria are not met, it is important to follow the receiving unit's quality system to investigate. Consideration should be given to such aspects as training, equipment, reagents, environmental conditions, success criteria (i.e., not set correctly), etc. to determine the root cause. All exceptional conditions outside of the pre-approved protocol should be documented and justified, including all invalid assays.

If an analytical method transfer is unsuccessful, an investigation should be performed per the appropriate QMS and next steps should be determined.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Small-Molecule vs. Large-Molecule DS

Small-molecule API: A chemically synthesized, well-defined compound with a low molecular weight and a structure that can be fully characterized. Comparability is easier to establish, mainly through impurity control per ICH Q3A and specification confirmation per ICH Q6A.

Large-molecule biologic: Includes recombinant proteins and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). These molecules are large and structurally complex, and their biological activity is deeply affected by process conditions (cell line, upstream/downstream processing). Comparability requires extensive analytical and functional characterization, per ICH Q5E.

Core difference: "Chemical equivalence" of a small molecule is relatively easy to determine; "biological equivalence" of a large molecule can only be confirmed through multi-level testing.

Analogy

Transferring a small-molecule API is like moving a dish with a fixed recipe to a new kitchen: you change the stove and the wok, but the ingredients stay the same, so as long as heat and proportions are right, the result is nearly identical. Transferring a biologic is more like relocating a living winery: copying the equipment is not enough, because the vines (cell line), the soil (culture medium) and the climate (environmental conditions) all shape the final flavor of the wine (protein folding and glycosylation).

Key Point

The chapter states clearly that not all elements apply to every transfer. Early clinical and laboratory-scale transfers have lower requirements than commercial-scale transfers. The team must decide which steps are necessary based on the product lifecycle stage, avoiding over-engineering that delays the schedule.

5.2 Technology Transfer Process

Original Text

The focus of this chapter is on the product and process specific requirements of the technology transfer (from the initial high level technology transfer proposal to operational readiness and process/procedure qualification) as analytical method requirements are covered in Chapter 4. Where relevant, aspects that are specific to only large molecule or small molecule drug substance will be indicated.

Information on forming a technology transfer team and developing a charter is presented within Chapters 2 and 3.

5.2.1 Initial Risk/Feasibility Assessment

An initial risk assessment will be required to determine whether the technology transfer of the process and product to the receiving unit is feasible (see also Section 2.2). Prior to obtaining a detailed technology transfer package from the sending unit, sufficient information to perform an initial, high level assessment of the process at the receiving unit should be obtained. At this stage, especially if the process is being transferred to an external company, it may not be possible to share very much information due to confidentiality restrictions and documents may be redacted.

During this feasibility assessment, a skeleton cross-functional team at the potential receiving unit (representing process, analytical, operations, HSE, materials management, regulatory, and quality) should perform an initial risk assessment based on the targeted markets and desired volumes against the product specification, mass balance, raw materials, and the block/process flow diagram.

For example, potential safety hazards, toxicity of the materials, corrosive nature, unique analytical requirements, raw material supply, and plant capacity are factors that should be assessed as part of this early stage risk assessment.

The rationale for evaluating product specification, mass balance, raw materials, and block/process flow diagram in the initial risk assessment are as follows:

  • Depending on the stage in the product lifecycle, the regulatory strategy required to meet the business strategy should be part of the initial focus of the technology transfer. If a product specification has been filed at the sending unit, this will form the basis of the technology transfer to ensure the product at the receiving unit meets the targeted market requirements, with justification of differences from existing pharmacopoeia requirements. The CQAs in the product specification are not the only items needing to be successfully produced as other quality attributes may be difficult to achieve in a new manufacturing facility.
  • A mass balance defines the plant capacity highlighting where equipment changes may be required. For large molecules, equipment changes are likely to be required if the product titer is low at harvest and the drug substance final product concentration is high. For small molecule API manufacture, the mass balance serves to provide process stream composition based on both analytical data and computational analysis. Balanced chemical equations identifying all side- and by-products of the process chemistry need to be part of the initial risk assessment.
  • Assessment of the raw materials establishes critical supply chain, analytical complexity, and any safety constraints.
  • The block/process flow diagram provides an overview of the process defined at the sending unit to gain better understanding of the plant requirements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Four Dimensions of the Initial Risk/Feasibility Assessment

  • Product Specification: The filed specification is the baseline for the transfer. The receiving unit must confirm it can achieve the same or equivalent CQA levels, and any differences need a scientific justification.
  • Mass Balance: For large molecules it drives equipment scale (for example, a low-titer harvest needs a larger purification column); for small molecules it reveals by-products and waste streams.
  • Raw Materials: Supplier qualification, analytical complexity and safety restrictions (such as genotoxic impurities under ICH M7).
  • Process Flow Diagrams (BFD/PFD): These let the receiving unit understand the overall process before it receives the full data.

Analogy: Due Diligence Before Assessment

A preliminary feasibility assessment is like a home inspection before buying a house: you do not need to open every wall, but you must confirm that the structure, electrical capacity and foundation can carry your needs. A CDMO intake assessment works the same way: before signing a contract, confirm that the facility, equipment and analytical capability can "carry" this process.

Key Point: Practical Limits of Confidentiality Agreements (NDA/CDA)

In the initial assessment stage, the sending unit can often provide only limited information because of confidentiality restrictions (key parts may even be redacted). This is exactly why the BFD and the mass balance matter so much: they are the minimum information set that still allows an assessment without disclosing full process details. The CDMO business development team should build a standardized "intake feasibility questionnaire" that lists the minimum required information.

Practical Application: CDMO Receiving Readiness Checklist (Initial Assessment)

  • Does the facility have a bioreactor or reactor that matches the process scale?
  • Does it have the qualifications to handle the relevant API (such as highly potent compounds or biological products)?
  • Does the analytical platform support the required tests (HPLC, bioactivity assays, cell-based potency assays)?
  • Does the existing raw material supplier list cover the key starting materials?
  • Does the regulatory history (inspection records) meet the requirements of the target market?

5.2.2.1 Process Information

  • Block Flow Diagrams (BFDs) are a Lean tool used to improve processes. For technology transfers, they ensure that the process steps are understood, in the correct order, and identify all process streams including waste and by-products. They can also serve the same function as the Process Flow Diagram and since they are typically easier to develop, can be used to quickly compare processing options. An example BFD is shown in Figure 5.1.
  • Process Flow Diagrams (PFDs) aid visualization of key data, e.g., processing times, in-process testing, etc. PFDs from all previous installations of the process (different sites or scales) can be helpful, especially for supporting updates to regulatory filings.
  • Process overview descriptions written in a site and scale-independent way so that the particulars of the existing implementation(s) are not confused with the generic requirements or validated ranges. Examples include: tabular data for process setpoints, acceptable ranges for unit operations as well as media and buffer preparations, descriptions of operating procedures and strategies, critical and other important process parameters, CQAs, and KPIs.
  • Manufacturing batch records (blank and/or examples of executed documents where relevant) can be the starting point for the receiving unit to generate their internal batch records.
  • SOPs from the sending unit provide information about detailed operation procedures (e.g., particle sizing for small molecules), although these may be related to site specific equipment and procedures.
  • Drug substance/process control strategy and the link between drug product CQAs, CPPs, potential CPPs, and drug substance manufacturing requirements.
  • CPV data generated to support process parameter trending and any statistical process control analysis.
  • Process observation for transfer of the often tacit process knowledge that arises from hands-on operating experience — e.g., addition of component #4 always causes a color change, pictures of typical cell morphology.
  • PAT (Process Analytical Technology), as relevant.
  • Storage and shipping conditions for final drug substance, including container type, packaging requirements, and any specific transportation requirements or validation.
  • Process intermediate and final drug substance stability information to identify stability constraints that affect hold times between steps or timing of product release for shipping.
  • Process specific deviations, investigations, and CAPAs from GMP batches.
  • Extractables/leachables/elemental impurities knowledge and to clarify which disposable equipment is suitable for use in the process.
  • Spreadsheet-based process models to facilitate process fit at the receiving unit — e.g., predicting column sizes depending on a range of harvest titers.
  • Product and reagent safety documentation detailing any potency concerns and cleanability issues.

Historical data is valuable for product comparability and includes: historical process manufacturing reports, historical process and analytical data (SPC trend charts, tabulated means/ranges), process development reports, virus clearance data (for mammalian-origin molecules), small scale model qualification, process characterization reports (DoE studies, design space, CPPs, Proven Acceptable Ranges), process validation reports, reports from prior transfers, annual product reviews, regulatory license documents, and change control summaries.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Four Categories of Data in a Technology Transfer Package

A complete technology transfer package has four levels, from coarse to fine:

  • Process Information: BFD, PFD, batch records, SOPs, control strategy, CPV data
  • Materials Information: Raw material specifications, cell banks (large molecules), supplier list
  • Analytical Method Information: Compendial methods, in-house methods, validation reports
  • Facility/Equipment Information: P&IDs, URS/FRS, automation control requirements, cleaning validation

Analogy: Transferring Tacit Knowledge

A written SOP conveys "explicit knowledge", like a musical score; what brings the music to life is the performer's "tacit knowledge": the force of the wrist, the rhythm of the breath. Process observation and SME on-site support let the receiving unit's operators see for themselves experience that cannot be written into an SOP, such as "the liquid changes color when the fourth raw material is added". This is why learning on site is more valuable than reading documents.

Key Point: Functional Difference Between BFD and PFD

BFD (Block Flow Diagram): Quick and high-level, suited to early assessment and cross-site comparison. The focus is the sequence of process steps, material flow and waste streams.

PFD (Process Flow Diagram): A detailed engineering drawing that includes processing times, in-process testing points, equipment volumes and other data. Suited to later engineering evaluation and regulatory filing updates.

Both are needed; neither can replace the other.

Core Concepts: Cell Bank Transfer, a Challenge Unique to Large Molecules

5.2.3 Technology Transfer Operational Readiness

Original Text

Gap Analysis / Risk Mitigation

Once the technology transfer package has been obtained, the receiving unit can then initiate a process gap analysis in order to compare how the process was operated at the sending unit to how the process will be operated at the receiving unit. This will identify:

  • Where there are gaps in process knowledge
  • Where process changes may be required to fit the new facility
  • Whether any engineering modifications are required for the new facility

Section 3.3.2 describes approaches to risk analysis and evaluation and the tools that can be used. By considering the severity (impact), occurrence (probability) and detection, a risk priority number (RPN) can be derived. This can be used as a basis for determining the level of risk and whether mitigations are required. Examples of process gap analyses are provided in Section 5.4. The gap analysis is often the first opportunity for the transfer team to intensively review the process together, and thus can serve as a good learning exercise for the receiving unit.

Transfer and Implementation of Laboratory Scale Process

If possible, the laboratory scale version of the process should be transferred prior to (or concurrently with) the full scale process transfer. This provides the receiving technical unit the opportunity to work with the process hands-on and catch any details that may have been overlooked in the technology transfer package. Prior to implementation of an engineering batch at the receiving unit, a full laboratory scale run of the process is advisable as soon as possible to help in scaling up the process, to minimize risks and establish acceptance criteria.

The knowledge gained from this exercise can be used for drafting preliminary process descriptions and as a basis for scale-up. Technical SMEs from the sending and receiving units can align on process details. The laboratory scale process may also be used to qualify raw material suppliers or to evaluate revised buffer formulations. For small molecule APIs, laboratory scale/pilot runs can be conducted outside the GMP facility for evaluation/consistency in order to demonstrate reproducibility under GMP conditions.

Low Risk Transfers

If there is sufficient knowledge of the operating procedure of the receiving unit, based on previous transfers, a transfer straight into the manufacturing facility is possible. However, it remains advisable to implement a laboratory scale process that can be operated in satellite mode (using the manufacturing process intermediates, materials, and solutions at each step) during initial full scale operations.

"In a large molecule purification process, buffer pH and conductivity are important measurements for ensuring the required product quality and impurity clearance on chromatography steps. However, the use of different meters between the sending and receiving units may result in small differences in measurements. One way to mitigate this is to send buffer samples between sites and compare measurements."

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Three Outputs of Gap Analysis

  • Knowledge Gaps: Which process parameters lack sufficient scientific justification? Which tacit knowledge has not yet been documented?
  • Process Changes: Operating conditions that must be adjusted to fit the new site's equipment (such as flow rates and temperature setpoints)
  • Engineering Modifications: Facility hardware items that require investment to modify (such as steam pressure upgrades and dust explosion protection design)

The output of the gap analysis directly determines the investment scale and timeline of the transfer plan.

Analogy: Laboratory-Scale Transfer Is a "Rehearsal"

Before the actual performance, a rehearsal exposes the movements the actors are not yet familiar with. A laboratory-scale transfer is that rehearsal opportunity: at small scale, operators can run the process by hand and find details the documents do not make clear (for example, filtration that is too slow, or a step where mixing needs a particular technique). Rehearsing before the engineering run is much cheaper than troubleshooting after an engineering run fails.

Key Point: Systematic Bias in pH/Conductivity Meters

The example in the quoted text illustrates a detail that is often overlooked: pH meters and conductivity meters from different vendors may have a systematic bias. For a chromatography process, a buffer pH deviation of 0.1 can affect purification performance and impurity clearance.

Mitigation: Cross-check measurements of the same buffer batch between the two sites, and if there is a bias, establish the basis for correcting the offset in the receiving unit's documents. This must be fully documented, so that it does not later look like an unreported specification change.

Practical Application: The CDMO Value of Satellite Mode

"Satellite Mode" is a risk mitigation strategy: while a full-scale GMP batch is running, small-scale parallel operations are carried out in the laboratory using full-scale intermediates. If the full-scale batch shows an abnormality, rapid root cause analysis can be done immediately with the satellite-mode intermediates, without waiting for the next GMP batch. For a CDMO, this is an important tool for keeping the timeline on track early in a transfer.

5.2.4 At Scale Process Evaluation and Qualification

Original Text

This is the principle of demonstrating the process at full scale at the receiving unit. Proceeding to full scale runs may require approval via the stage gate reviews to demonstrate that the required preparation has been completed. When these runs are intended to be fully GMP and released for use (clinical or commercial), it is good practice to document the decision making leading to the conclusion of readiness.

The initial run(s) at full scale may be designated as engineering runs or commissioning runs. These may be fully GMP runs intended for release, depending on the perceived risk and mitigation strategies. Engineering runs are defined here as trial full scale runs performed in the same equipment to be used for GMP manufacturing using the intended procedures and without the intent to be released for use.

  • Engineering runs/testing:
    • If an engineering run is processed, the data should be reviewed prior to the first GMP batch to identify any potential issues. An engineering run provides opportunity to ensure that the data generated would meet acceptance criteria (if performing process validation) or is comparable to previous campaign data.
    • Developed manufacturing instructions should be used during the engineering run and updated with necessary changes if applicable.
    • In-process sampling can be used for testing, including evaluating the status of assay procedures to be used. Samples may also be used for any further studies identified which may be run concurrent with the first GMP batch (e.g., resin reuse).
  • Qualification/validation:
    • The main objectives of validation include ensuring that all facilities, processes, systems, procedures, and equipment that may affect the quality of the product operate reliably and reproducibly and that the process is run under a state of control.
    • A validation master plan should be generated by the receiving unit prior to validation activities.
    • Equipment qualification should be completed in advance of the process validation. Typically, cleaning validation may be performed concurrently with process validation.
    • Process validation protocols should be approved prior to initiation of validation runs, summarizing specific parameters and tests to be monitored and compared to acceptance criteria.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Roles of Engineering Runs vs. PPQ Batches

Engineering Run: Full scale but not intended for GMP release. Its purpose is to uncover problems, verify that the process description can actually be executed, and build a first draft of the batch record. The data can be used to evaluate whether acceptance criteria are met, but the batch itself is not used for release.

PPQ batch (Process Performance Qualification): A formal GMP validation batch that must be executed under a pre-approved protocol; its data directly support commercial release and regulatory filings.

Key principle: The engineering run is the "trial run" and PPQ is the "real match". The engineering run data must be reviewed before the first GMP batch.

Key Point: Validation Master Plan

The VMP is the "map" of validation activities. It should be completed before validation and cover:

  • Equipment qualification plan (IQ/OQ/PQ)
  • Scope of process validation and number of batches
  • Cleaning validation strategy (usually synchronized with process validation)
  • Analytical method validation timeline
  • Definition of success criteria

A missing or incomplete VMP is a common FDA/EMA inspection observation and does serious damage to a CDMO's credibility when taking on contracts.

Analogy: The Engineering Run Is a "Dress Rehearsal"

An engineering run is like the final dress rehearsal before the performance: the full set, the full costumes and the full sequence are all used, but no audience has bought tickets. This gives the director (the process owner) a chance to find problems with lighting cues or awkward blocking and make final corrections before the real performance (PPQ).

Practical Application: CDMO PPQ Batch Timeline Planning

A typical commercial-scale DS transfer timeline (using a large molecule as an example):

  • Receipt of technology transfer package → gap analysis: 4-8 weeks
  • Equipment procurement/modification: 3-12 months (depending on the gaps)
  • Equipment qualification (IQ/OQ): 2-4 months
  • Laboratory-scale transfer: 2-3 months
  • Engineering runs (1-2 batches): 2-4 months
  • PPQ batches (usually 3 batches): 3-6 months
  • Total: 12-24 months (depending on complexity)

5.2.5 Finalize Transfer and Perform Review

Original Text

5.2.5.1 Evaluate Transfer Performance Against Goals

In order for the technology transfer to be considered successful, the project manager needs to establish clear boundaries and goals for the outcome of the project. Typically, a commercial-commercial technology transfer is considered to be complete upon successful validation at the commercial scale of the receiving unit with the required regulatory filing. For clinical products, the technology transfer may be considered complete after the manufacture of the clinical batch supply and filing of the investigational new drug.

The goals and boundaries should be defined and agreed upon with management using a formal process such as a charter (see Section 3.1.2). Upon completion of the planned full scale runs, the data needs to be carefully reviewed against the performance goals. Key elements may include:

  • Process data which demonstrates successful execution of the control strategy
  • Satisfactory in-process and drug substance product quality
  • Successful operation of the specified process over an agreed number of batches
  • Material usage within the predicted ranges
  • Successful operation of the plant and equipment with regard to operability, labor, and plant occupancy times
  • Successful manufacture of drug product from the drug substance manufactured at the receiving unit
  • A remediation plan, if necessary, should be agreed to prior to wrapping up the technology transfer activities

5.2.5.2 Project Close-Out

There are likely to be unique goals for each technology transfer. However, a process and product comparability report should be written to demonstrate that the transfer was successful, with justification of any differences which may have occurred due to use of different equipment, different analytical methods etc. Any major changes or deviations should be documented and justified. This report may form the basis of the next stage in the product lifecycle (e.g., from clinical to commercial batch) or may be required for a filing update.

A lessons learned activity between the sending and receiving units, preferably via face-to-face meetings, is useful to identify what went well and what could be improved (covering e.g., program management, technical aspects, analytical/QC, GMP documentation, QA, raw materials). The output should be documented as a summary report to provide a basis for subsequent technology transfer between units and to identify future improvements.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Six Dimensions of Transfer Success Criteria

A technology transfer is not successful just because a batch was produced. Success criteria include:

  • Technical: The control strategy is executed successfully and process data fall within target ranges
  • Quality: In-process and drug substance quality attributes meet specifications
  • Process consistency: All of the specified number of batches succeed
  • Material usage: Actual consumption is within the predicted range (affects cost)
  • Operational efficiency: Labor and equipment occupancy time meet expectations
  • Downstream manufacturing: Drug product batches are successfully completed using drug substance from the new site

Key Point: The Regulatory Function of the Comparability Report

The comparability report is not just a "close-out document"; it may be used directly as:

  • Clinical → commercial scale transition: Supports the process comparability section of a BLA/NDA filing
  • CMC documentation update: If the transfer involves a process change within the scope of the regulatory filing, this report is a filing attachment
  • Starting point for later transfers: Serves as the historical data baseline for future transfers

The scientific justification for any "differences" in the report is therefore critical and must not be glossed over.

Analogy: The Review Meeting Is a "Post-Game Analysis"

Top sports teams review video and debrief tactics after every game. The Lessons Learned activity in a technology transfer is that post-game analysis: a face-to-face, cross-organization review of what went well and what needs improvement. 5.3 Points to Consider

Original Text

For large molecules, development of a platform process (for use with multiple products where possible) facilitates process transfer, whether between internal sites or to a CMO. After transfer of the first platform process, the receiving unit will then have some knowledge of process operating conditions. In addition, as a process transfers into a CMO from a process development sending unit, the sending unit will gain more knowledge of the CMO capabilities, which facilitates future process development.

A technology transfer policy and SOP are recommended at the sending unit and receiving unit to define the framework of the transfer, to include, e.g., where decision points should be made and which functional groups should be involved at each stage of the transfer.

During process development for large or small molecules at a sending unit, with the intent of transferring to a CMO selected for partnering, it is recommended to work with the CMO during process development to ensure the process will fit the manufacturing facility in order to reduce risks. The CMO will have standard operating conditions for equipment (such as flow rates on chromatography steps) or there may be equipment constraints in volume handling and there may be a defined timeframe for the manufacturing slot.

To ensure more rapid large molecule process transfers, the use of animal component free, chemically defined media is preferred. The use of media such as hydrolysates or that contain animal components can introduce more variability into a process, which can affect process comparability between sites. If animal components are present, these must be of suitable quality for use in a GMP facility, with good traceability of supply.

Any raw materials or consumables new to the receiving unit may cause delays due to establishment of the supply chain (e.g., new supplier agreements may be required) and availability of appropriate paperwork (demonstrating appropriate quality).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Strategic Value of a Platform Process

A platform process for large-molecule biologics is a standardized framework of upstream (cell culture) and downstream (purification) processes that can be applied to multiple similar molecules (such as mAbs of the same class).

Significance for a CDMO: Once a platform process is established, the time to transfer subsequent similar molecules can be greatly shortened (because the facility, equipment and analytical platform are already in place), which is an important source of CDMO competitive differentiation.

Typical examples: Lonza's Ibex platform and Samsung Biologics' mAb manufacturing platform: both build capability through the first customer and then serve later customers at lower cost.

Key Point: Involve the CMO Early in Process Development

This is one of the most important practical recommendations in the chapter: if you plan to transfer the process to a CMO, involve the CMO during process development rather than "throwing" the process over after development is complete.

Reason: The CMO's equipment has fixed standard operating conditions (such as chromatography flow rates) and volume limits. If these limits are not considered during development, the process must be modified after transfer and studies repeated, wasting time and money.

This is also why forward-looking CDMOs such as White Raven build an "early partnership" model: they come in during the client's process development phase to reduce later transfer risk.

Analogy: Chemically Defined Media vs. Hydrolysates

Chemically Defined Media (CDM) are like standardized instant coffee packets: the composition is almost identical every time. Hydrolysate media (containing animal-derived components) are like grinding natural coffee beans: the flavor varies from batch to batch with the origin and harvest season of the beans. For cross-site comparison, batch-to-batch variability of the medium is the hardest "source of noise" to control, so CDM greatly reduces the complexity of cross-site comparability.

Practical Application: A CDMO Framework for Building a Technology Transfer SOP

A complete CDMO technology transfer SOP should define:

  • Decision points (stage gates) for each phase and the approval matrix
  • Division of responsibilities among functional groups (process, QA, QC, regulatory, materials management)
  • The receiving checklist and confirmation procedure for the technology transfer package
  • Templates for gap analysis and the RPN scoring matrix
  • Record requirements for knowledge transfer activities

RPN Scoring Methodology

RPN = Severity (S) × Occurrence (O) × Detectability (D)
S: 1–10 (10 = most severe: patient harm/batch rejection)
O: 1–10 (10 = highest: almost certain to occur)
D: 1–10 (10 = hardest to detect)

Example: pH meter difference risk:
S = 8 (affects a critical process parameter, may cause batch failure)
O = 6 (cross-site instrument differences are common)
D = 4 (can be found through method comparison)
RPN = 8 × 6 × 4 = 192 → high priority for mitigation

RPN > 100: High priority, must be mitigated before proceeding
RPN 50-100: Medium priority, requires planned management
RPN < 50: Low priority, monitoring is sufficient

  • Raw material suppliers: Have the suppliers of key raw materials already been approved by the receiving site, or is there a plan for rapid approval?
  • Utilities: Do the capacity and pressure of high-pressure steam, cooling water, nitrogen and so on meet process needs?
  • Documentation system: Does the batch record system support electronic transfer, or is screenshot/paper conversion needed?
  • Regulatory history: Does the receiving site's GMP compliance record satisfy the regulatory requirements of the target market?

Chapter 6 - Technology Transfer of Drug Product
6.1 Introduction

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer 3rd Ed.

As a drug product transitions through each stage of the clinical program and a higher degree of confidence in the clinical profile of the product materializes, the strategy should shift to focus upon commercialization of the dosage form. This should include defining process scale-up and transfer plans to the intended commercial manufacturing site.

This activity traditionally occurs between the phase 2 and phase 3 clinical stages, in order to ensure phase 3 clinical and pivotal stability supplies are produced within a commercial facility. This also can be advantageous in supporting lifecycle validation activities for the product and helping to build manufacturing experience in a commercial facility.

In addition, there may be a need to transfer a drug product to a new commercial manufacturing site post-registration and launch. Although more information may be available while transferring a commercial product (filed release specifications, CPPs, etc.), the general knowledge needed and steps to successfully transfer a product remains the same.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Two Scenarios for DP Technology Transfer

Scenario 1: Transfer during development (Phase 2 → Phase 3)
Once efficacy is confirmed in Phase 2 clinical trials, the company needs to transfer the drug product process to a commercial site so that Phase 3 clinical supplies and key stability batches are manufactured in the commercial facility. This is an important regulatory step, because Phase 3 batches must be representative of future commercial production.

Scenario 2: Post-commercialization transfer (post-registration)
After launch, production is moved to a new site for reasons of capacity, cost or strategy. Although the full CPPs, specifications and other data already exist, the rigor of the transfer process must not be reduced, because the regulatory authority will also require a filing.

Analogy

A development-stage transfer is like a test-kitchen chef (the R&D kitchen) handing a recipe to the central kitchen of a restaurant chain (the commercial site). It is not enough to hand over the recipe: the chef must be on site to demonstrate it and to verify that the taste stays consistent at mass-production scale. A post-commercialization transfer is like a chain moving the entire meal service of one outlet to another location, where every step has to be reconfirmed.

Key Point - The CDMO Perspective

For a sterile CDMO, the most common situations are accepting a Phase 2→3 transfer or taking on a post-commercialization transfer. The regulatory risks differ: if Phase 3 batches fail, the clinical timeline is affected; if a post-commercialization transfer is filed under the wrong category, it may trigger a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) instead of a CBE-30, directly affecting the time to market.

6.2 Technology Transfer Process

Original Text

This chapter highlights those aspects of a technology transfer process specific to drug product. Analytical requirements are covered in Chapter 4. Note that many of the principles of drug substance transfer, covered in Chapter 5, are applicable to drug products.

In general, a technology transfer can be described by answering the following questions:

  • What is the current process/product?
  • What is the desired final product/process, based on any constraints/criteria related to the new site?
  • What needs to be done to prove that the final product is correct and the process is robust?

The technology transfer is defined by how these questions are answered.

6.2.1 Identify Risks, Conduct Risk Assessments, and Develop Technology Transfer Plan

An initial risk assessment should be conducted to determine whether the technology transfer of the process and product to the receiving unit is feasible. This high level risk assessment may be challenging to complete if the product is being transferred to or from an external company and information is difficult to share due to confidentiality agreements.

A cross-functional team consisting of, at minimum, process, analytical, supply chain, operations, HSE, and quality should evaluate the data available to determine feasibility. Items for consideration include:

  • Projected volume and plant capacity
  • Target markets
  • Safety hazards
  • Special equipment, PAT, and analytical needs

For aseptic processes, additional consideration should be given to facility capabilities to maintain an aseptic area. The assumption is that the receiving facility will have the appropriate controls in place to produce aseptic products (e.g., HEPA filtration, air classification, gowning procedures, etc.).

Based on a positive outcome from this analysis, conducting an in depth risk assessment with technical experts from both the sending and receiving units is highly recommended. It is highly advisable, if possible, to conduct this assessment in a face-to-face environment, to better facilitate the transfer of knowledge. The risk assessment should also capture gaps in product knowledge/process understanding, or highlight the additional information needed from the external site to guide the technology transfer plan.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - The Three Basic Questions of TT

The essence of technology transfer can be framed as three questions:

  • What is the current state? (a full understanding of the sending unit's process)
  • What is the target? (the final shape of the process within the constraints of the receiving facility)
  • How will success be demonstrated? (comparability, validation, PPQ)

The answers to these three questions define the scope and workload of the entire transfer program.

Key Point - Feasibility Assessment for Sterile Processes

ISPE specifically notes that the feasibility assessment for a sterile process cannot rely on an equipment list alone. It must confirm that the receiving facility has:

  • HEPA filtration systems and air classification (ISO 5/7/8)
  • Gowning procedures and a personnel training system
  • Environmental monitoring (EM) baseline data
  • Sterilization validation capability (steam sterilization, dry heat sterilization, filter sterilization)

These are not "nice-to-haves"; they are the minimum threshold for sterile manufacturing. If a CDMO has not yet established its EM baseline, this work must be completed before the technology transfer.

Analogy - The Confidentiality Dilemma in Inter-site Transfer

Confidentiality agreements (NDA/CDA) make early feasibility assessment feel like "guessing an object blindfolded": the sending unit cannot fully disclose the formulation, and the receiving unit cannot adequately assess equipment fit. The practical approach is to sign a CDA first and then disclose in layers: first share the dosage form type (sterile liquid), then the ranges of critical parameters, and reveal the full process only after the project is confirmed.

6.2.2 Consolidate Knowledge for Technology Transfer Package

Original Text

Block Flow Diagrams (BFDs) are a Lean tool used to improve processes. For technology transfers, they ensure that the process steps are understood, in the correct order, and identify all process streams including waste and by-products. They can also serve the same function as the PFDs and since they are typically easier to develop, can be used to quickly compare processing options.

Process Flow Diagrams (PFDs) aid visualization of key data, e.g., processing times, in-process testing, etc. PFDs from all previous installations of the process (different sites or scales) can be helpful, especially for supporting updates to regulatory filings.

The following list indicates useful information about the current process being transferred:

  • Manufacturing batch records (blank and/or examples of executed documents) can be the starting point for the receiving unit to generate their internal batch records.
  • Drug product/process control strategy and the link between drug product CQAs, CPPs, potential CPPs, critical aspects, and drug product manufacturing requirements.
  • Risk Assessments that may have been conducted, including any excipient analysis work or packaging analysis.
  • Process observation for transfer of the often tacit process knowledge. It can be a worthwhile exercise to observe the process operations with the existing manufacturing instruction documents and look for any additional elements that may not be well documented.
  • Storage and shipping conditions for drug product to include container type, packaging requirements, freeze/thaw cycle data, and any specific transportation requirements or validation.
  • Process intermediate stability information to identify stability constraints that affect hold times between steps (e.g., blend hold time prior to tableting, suspension hold time prior to filling).
  • Extractables/leachables/elemental impurities/materials compatibility knowledge (risk assessments or testing data relevant to the product/process).
  • Spreadsheet-based process models, if available, to facilitate process fit at the receiving unit.
  • Product safety documentation detailing any hazard concerns and cleanability issues.
  • Equipment cleaning procedures or cleaning validation information.
  • Historical process and analytical data including statistical process control trend charts, tabulated means and ranges of KPIs, as well as in-process trends for continuous data.
  • QA documentation — deviation reports, laboratory investigations, and incident reports.
  • Process development reports — especially for transfers from process development to manufacturing.
  • Process characterization reports — results from Design of Experiment studies or other studies evaluating the design space around process parameters and resulting CQAs. This informs the process control strategy, guides the establishment of Normal Operating Ranges for the process.
  • Product characterization reports — results evaluating the product characteristics and how they are impacted by processing parameters.
  • Process validation reports — demonstrating the acceptable ranges and robustness of the process at the sending unit.
  • Regulatory license documents (relevant sections) and existing commitments for the process.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Key Categories of the DP Technology Package

The ISPE technology package list can be grouped into four categories, which help the receiving unit organize the required documents systematically:

  • Process understanding: BFD, PFD, process characterization reports, development reports
  • Control strategy: batch records, CQA/CPP linkage, in-process control specifications
  • Material compatibility: E&L, elemental impurities, container compatibility
  • Historical data: SPC trend charts, deviation reports, validation reports

Key Point - Tacit Knowledge Is the Hardest to Transfer

ISPE particularly stresses the value of process observation. Many critical operating details do not appear in the batch record, for example: "When excipient #3 is added, the temperature drops sharply and stirring must start immediately." This kind of tacit knowledge can only be transferred effectively by observing the process in person, or by having the sending unit's SMEs coach the team on site at the receiving unit.

Tacit knowledge that is especially important for a sterile CDMO includes: when to fine-tune the filling speed, the hand feel of crimping torque, and the reference sample library for visual inspection.

Key Point - Special Technology Package Items for Sterile DP

For sterile products, the technology package must also include:

  • Specifications of the container-closure system and the CCI test methods
  • Filter validation data (compatibility, integrity, efficiency)
  • Lyophilization cycle (for lyophilized products): equipment at different facilities may differ
  • Historical aseptic process simulation (APS) data
  • Sterilization validation reports (including the bioburden baseline)

Practical Application

As the TT lead at a CDMO, once you receive the sending unit's technology package, it is advisable to build a "Gap Register" and check item by item against the ISPE list whether each document has been received. Request any missing items immediately and assess whether the data need to be generated in-house during an engineering run. Do not wait until just before PPQ to discover that the process characterization report is missing.

6.2.3 Technology Transfer Execution / Pilot Scale Batches

Original Text

Scale-up or pilot batches are often used to gather initial information using the new equipment train while minimizing use of raw materials and API. It is important to design these batches with the final scale/equipment in mind.

For example, for a direct compression tablet process, it would be advisable to collect stratified tablet core samples across the run to determine if there is a tendency toward segregation or a lack of uniformity. For a liquid product, it may be desirable to collect multiple samples during API dissolution to gain an understanding of the dissolution profile.

Pilot batches are also useful in addressing gaps that were identified in the transfer risk assessments. For example, process understanding Design of Experiments that may not be practical on a large scale may yield product understanding across a range of conditions.

For aseptic processes, consider using these batches to aid in filter validation, container/closure integrity testing, and developing protocols for inspection processes.

Depending on the phase of the product being transferred, it may be worthwhile to conduct another risk assessment or gap evaluation once the pilot lots are complete, in preparation for moving to the full scale work.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - The Layers of Purpose of Pilot Batches

A pilot batch serves three layers of purpose:

  • Learning: understanding process behavior on the new equipment during training
  • Verifying assumptions: confirming the high-risk items identified in the risk assessment
  • Establishing a baseline: providing the receiving unit's process data for the PPQ comparison

A pilot batch should not be treated as mere "familiarization with operations"; it needs a clear sampling plan and acceptance criteria.

Key Point - Special Uses of Pilot Batches for Sterile Processes

ISPE states clearly that pilot batches for sterile processes should also be used for:

  • Filter validation: confirming that the filter is compatible with the formulation and does not adsorb the API
  • Establishing the CCI test method: confirming that the seal integrity test parameters are suitable for the receiving unit's equipment
  • Developing the visual inspection procedure: establishing the receiving unit's inspection standards and sample library

If these are not done at the pilot batch stage and problems are discovered only at PPQ, the cost rises substantially.

Analogy

A pilot batch is like a chef's first practice run in a new kitchen: the aim is not to serve guests but to test the workflow, learn the characteristics of the equipment and find hidden problems (for example, uneven heating in the new oven). Finding problems during the rehearsal is far cheaper than making mistakes on opening day.

  1. In a pilot batch of a sterile product, why can filter validation not wait until just before PPQ?
  2. If the pilot batch results show a high CV for fill weight, how should you decide whether to proceed to an engineering run?
6.2.4 Engineering Runs / Pre-PPQ Runs

Original Text

Where an engineering run is processed, the data should be reviewed prior to proceeding to batches to be released to identify any potential issues. An engineering run provides opportunity to ensure the data generated will meet acceptance criteria if performing process validation (or is comparable to previous campaign data.)

The initial run(s) at full scale may be designated as engineering runs or may be full GMP runs intended for release, depending on the perceived risk and mitigation strategies. Engineering runs are defined here as full scale trial runs performed in the same equipment to be used for GMP manufacturing using the intended procedures and without the intent to be released for use.

Developed manufacturing instructions should be used during the engineering run and updated with necessary changes if applicable.

In-process sampling can be used for testing, including evaluating the status of assay procedures to be used. Samples may also be used for any further studies identified, i.e., prototype stability studies.

A development report should be compiled to document the process, parameters, findings from development work (edge of failure, sensitive processes), control strategy, etc. prior to moving into qualification runs.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - The Regulatory Status of an Engineering Run

An engineering run is defined very precisely: full-scale production on GMP equipment using formal procedures, but not intended for release. This means:

  • It is not a GMP batch, but it must use GMP equipment and documentation
  • The data can be used for process understanding but cannot be counted directly as PPQ batches
  • If the engineering run performs well, it can serve as a reference for setting PPQ acceptance criteria

Key Point - The Development Report That Must Be Completed Before PPQ

ISPE requires a Development Report to be completed before entering PPQ, covering:

  • Process parameters and set points
  • Results of edge-of-failure studies
  • Identification of sensitive process steps
  • Scientific rationale for the control strategy

This report is background documentation when the FDA and EMA review the PPQ report; if it is missing, a request for additional information will follow.

Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, resource allocation for engineering runs is a common point of conflict: the client wants to save cost by skipping the engineering run and going straight to PPQ. The CDMO QA's responsibility is to explain clearly that if PPQ is performed without an engineering run and PPQ then fails, the cost of repeating batches is far higher than the cost of an engineering run. For sterile products in particular, the direct cost of one failed PPQ batch (materials, labor, facility time) can exceed USD 1 million.

6.2.5 Process Qualification (PPQ)

Original Text

Pre-requisites for qualification activities in terms of facility, equipment, skill capability, and documentation should be established. The timing of qualification activities may be dependent on the completion of these pre-requisites.

Qualification of Technology Transfer / Process Qualification Runs

Prior to validation activities, a plan should be generated by the receiving unit to determine the validation strategy. Process validation protocols should be approved prior to initiation of validation/qualification runs.   • Stability protocol

  • Storage and packaging conditions

Key Concepts - The Regulatory Framework for PPQ

Process Performance Qualification (PPQ) is the final verification activity of a technology transfer. Its regulatory basis includes:

  • FDA 2011 Process Validation Guidance: a three-stage validation framework (design → PPQ → continued process verification); sterile products generally require at least 3 PPQ batches with enhanced sampling
  • EU GMP Annex 15: the EU requirements for PPQ, which must be completed before formal commercial production
  • EMA Guideline on Process Validation: places particular emphasis on comparability data requirements for biological products

  [x] HVAC system qualification completed

Key Point - Special Requirements for Sterile PPQ

According to the FDA 2011 guidance, PPQ for sterile products requires the following in addition to the general requirements:

  • Enhanced sampling: statistically meaningful within-batch sampling; it is not sufficient to take only the minimum number of samples stated in the specification
  • APS (aseptic process simulation): media fills must be completed before PPQ, and must be performed separately for each filling line
  • Concurrent validation: in some cases the FDA allows concurrent validation, but the justification must be explained to and accepted by the regulatory authority in advance

7.3 Quality by Design and Control Strategy

Original Text

QbD principles should be considered when the control strategy is developed; these include QTPP, CQAs, CPPs and design space (as applicable). For more information on these principles, refer to the following ICH quality guidelines:

ICH Q8(R2): "demonstration of greater understanding of pharmaceutical and manufacturing sciences can create a basis for flexible regulatory approaches"
ICH Q10: "The goal of technology transfer activities is to transfer product and process knowledge… This knowledge forms the basis for the manufacturing process, control strategy, process validation approach and ongoing continual improvement."

CQAs are the basis of the control strategy and should be available to the receiving unit to provide the scientific rationale for manufacturing process controls and supporting analytical methods. In combination with product quality risk assessments, CQAs ensure adequate controls are in place to produce consistent product quality.

To increase the likelihood of successful technology transfer, changes should be minimized as follows:

  • Materials (including raw materials, starting materials, intermediates, reagents, primary packaging materials, resins, disposables, etc.) should be well understood and, ideally, as similar to the materials used at the sending unit as possible.
  • Differences in the design of the manufacturing process should be limited to differences in scale to minimize any potential product quality impact.
  • Any changes to in-process controls (including in-process tests and process parameters) and release testing should be limited to enable comparison of data to the sending unit.
  • Analytical methods should be consistent between sites. If analytical methods need to change, then ensure equivalency studies are performed so results can be compared.
  • Consider the various levels of controls within the manufacturing process, specifications, in-process controls, batch records, and automation controls. Any differences need to be clearly documented and justified.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Challenges in Transferring QbD Documentation

QTPP (Quality Target Product Profile): defines the quality characteristics the final product should have. It is usually transferred together with the product, and the receiving unit must confirm that its equipment can produce a product that meets the QTPP.

The design space problem: the design space was established on the sending unit's equipment. Because the receiving unit's equipment is different, the same parameter ranges do not necessarily apply. ISPE's position is that the receiving unit needs to demonstrate that its equipment can operate within the same design space, rather than simply inheriting it.

Transfer of CQAs: this is the relatively straightforward part. CQAs are based on product science and do not depend on particular equipment, so they can be transferred in full.

Key Point - The Principle of Minimizing Change

ISPE clearly recommends that during technology transfer, the fewer the changes, the higher the success rate. This has a direct commercial implication for a CDMO:

  • Do not modify the process on your own initiative to suit the receiving unit's equipment habits
  • A change of raw material supplier requires a compatibility assessment and cannot simply be substituted
  • If an analytical method must change, an equivalency study is mandatory, not optional

Analogy - Design Space Transfer

A design space is like a menu standard of "saltiness range 6-8". That standard was tested on the stove of a specific kitchen. In a new kitchen, even with the same ingredients, you need to retest whether the new stove at the same settings can still produce a dish rated 6-8 in saltiness. Applying the old standard directly without verification is a major quality risk.

7.4 Quality Risk Management

Original Text

As described in Chapter 1, QRM per ICH Q9 is an extremely important part of a technology transfer project. Successful technology transfer should ensure all high risk areas have been considered, appropriate mitigation has been put in place, and that any residual risks are assessed and agreed as acceptable between sites. The QRM process should be documented.

  • A detailed risk assessment (e.g., using tools such as cause effect matrices, Ishikawa (fishbone) diagrams, FMEA, gap analysis, etc.) should be put in place so that risks are reduced to an acceptable level and controlled through a defined risk mitigation plan.
  • Most technology transfer projects require various risk assessments. Existing risk assessments may need to be adapted to acknowledge changes in risk during the technology transfer.
  • Introduction of new technology to replace obsolete systems or equipment is a common challenge during technology transfers. A risk assessment evaluating new risks caused by new technologies is recommended.
  • Transfers frequently need to be made into existing equipment and facilities. The fit-for-use assessment when the receiving unit equipment is not identical to the sending unit is required to ensure all significant risks are mitigated.
  • Segregation risk assessments are needed to confirm that proper segregation measures have been incorporated into the facility design or via procedural controls. In the case of a multi-product facility, a cross-contamination risk assessment is recommended.
  • Contamination risk assessment (in alignment with EU GMP Annex 1) should be conducted.
  • Consider use of a consolidated risk register to track mitigation of each high risk item.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Choosing QRM Tools in TT

ISPE lists several QRM tools, each suited to different situations:

  • Fishbone: suited to identifying the root causes of CQA deviations; useful for problem identification early in the transfer
  • FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis): suited to analyzing process steps one by one; provides a quantitative risk score based on severity, occurrence and detectability
  • Cause-Effect Matrix: links process parameters to CQAs; identifies which parameters have the greatest impact on which quality attributes
  • Gap Analysis: compares the differences between the sending and receiving units; suited to the tabular format of Section 5

Key Point - The Risk Register

ISPE recommends using a single Risk Register to track the mitigation status of all high-risk items. It is one of the documents most often requested for review during GMP inspections. The risk register should include:

  • Risk description and category (technical / regulatory / commercial)
  • Initial risk score (severity × occurrence × detectability)
  • Mitigation actions and owners
  • Residual risk score after mitigation
  • Closure status and date

Key Point - Special Risk Items for a Sterile CDMO

For a sterile multi-product facility, the following risks require separate assessment documents:

  • Cross-contamination risk: when several products are made in the same facility, confirm that equipment cleaning validation covers the new product
  • Segregation risk: whether the personnel and material flow design of the aseptic area supports the production flow of the new product
  • Alignment with EU GMP Annex 1: the contamination control strategy (CCS) must address the specific risks of the new product

7.5 Analytical Comparability / Similarity and Stability Strategy

Original Text

Analytical comparability is a consideration at any point after the first toxicology lot, when there is a change in the manufacturing process which may potentially impact product quality. During technology transfer, it is advantageous to minimize process changes as much as possible to ensure analytical comparability is maintained.

Analytical comparability strategies are tailored to each product and each change. The comparability strategy incorporates the existing knowledge of the product and process as well as the risks associated with the change itself. In general, products in the early lifecycle stage have limited non-clinical and clinical data associated with the pre-change product and the safety or efficacy profile of the product have not yet been defined. Therefore, the expectations and requirements for comparability increase as the product matures throughout the lifecycle stages.

The analytical comparability strategy depends on the significance of the process changes and their potential to impact product quality, safety, and efficacy. Considerations for potential impact at the following manufacturing stages include:

  • Cell culture changes – consider attributes that may be influenced by cell bank, cell growth, media composition, etc. (e.g., primary sequence, glycans, size, and charge heterogeneity)
  • Purification changes – consider attributes that may be influenced by the performance of the unit operation and processing conditions (e.g., impurity clearance, shear forces, light exposure, temperature changes)
  • Drug product changes – consider attributes that may be influenced by process conditions and product handling, including primary and secondary containers, shear forces, light exposure, or temperature changes (e.g., aggregates, oxidation, deamidation, leachables, extractables)
  • Combination products (devices) – consider changes that may impact device functionality and the interface with the drug product
  • Synthetics – consider process changes that may impact lot release testing

Stability studies at the recommended storage condition confirm the integrity of the product through expiry. Studies at accelerated stability conditions and/or stress stability conditions are often conducted to satisfy regional filing requirements as well as to support product impact outside of the recommended storage condition.

The comparability assessment criteria should be established prospectively. The product quality risk assessment and the QTPP should be utilized to provide additional information for establishing a comparability strategy based on the product CQAs.

In the event a comparability test result yields an abnormal result, teams will need to determine if there is an impact of the aberrant product quality attribute on patient safety and/or product efficacy. A lot selected for comparability may be a lot rejected for clinical or human use.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - The Regulatory Definition of Comparability

Analytical comparability means demonstrating, by analytical methods, that product quality attributes remain similar or equivalent before and after a process change, so as to ensure that patient safety and efficacy are not affected.

Regulatory basis: ICH Q5E (for biological products) is the clearest guideline, and it requires comparison of:

  • Physicochemical properties (molecular weight, isoelectric point, glycoform analysis)
  • Biological activity (potency, receptor affinity)
  • Immunochemical properties (antigenicity)
  • Impurity profile (process-related impurities and product-related impurities)

For small-molecule products the comparability requirements are relatively simple, but clear acceptance criteria are still needed.

Comparability Strategy Design Principles

Lifecycle stage vs. strength of comparability requirements:

Phase 1-2 TT:
  → Limited comparability data
  → Focus: confirm physicochemical equivalence with a small number of batches
  → Clinical bridging not required

Phase 3 TT (Pre-pivotal):
  → Full comparability data set
  → Release testing + characterization testing required
  → Supported by accelerated stability studies

Commercial TT (Post-approval):
  → Most stringent requirements
  → May trigger a regulatory filing (CBE-30 / PAS)
  → Multiple batches to confirm consistency
  → Stability bridging study

Key Point - Acceptance Criteria Must Be Set Prospectively

ISPE stresses that comparability acceptance criteria must be set before the experiment begins (prospectively) and cannot be decided after seeing the data. This is one of the most common GMP violations: adjusting acceptance criteria after seeing poor data is unacceptable and appears repeatedly in FDA 483 observations and EMA deficiencies.

Key Point - Handling Abnormal Comparability Results

If comparability data show an abnormal result, the investigation logic should be:

  1. Confirm whether it is an analytical error (retest, method verification)
  2. If a true difference is confirmed, assess the impact on patient safety and efficacy
  3. Determine whether additional nonclinical or clinical studies are needed
  4. Decide whether the batch can still be used for comparability purposes (even if it cannot be released)

A failed comparability batch is not automatically discarded: its analytical data may still have scientific value and must be judged case by case.

  1. Why must comparability acceptance criteria be set prospectively? What are the regulatory consequences if they are set after seeing the data?
  2. Can the analytical data from a comparability batch that was rejected for release still be used in the comparability assessment? How would you decide?
7.6 Process Validation / Process Performance Qualification Strategy

Original Text

Process validation for technology transfer should align with the process validation concepts and lifecycle approach as described in, e.g., ICH Q7, FDA Guidance on process validation, EU GMP Annex 15, EMA Guideline on process validation for finished products, and EMA Guideline on process validation for biologics.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Alignment Across Multiple Regulatory Frameworks

ISPE requires the PPQ strategy for a technology transfer to align with multiple regulatory guidelines, because the same CDMO may produce products for both the US (FDA) and European (EMA) markets:

  • FDA 2011 Process Validation Guidance: a three-stage framework that emphasizes continued process verification (CPV); enhanced sampling is expected for sterile products
  • EU GMP Annex 15 (2015): explicitly requires a PPQ report and specifies the conditions for concurrent validation
  • EMA guideline on validation for biological products: places stricter demands on comparability data, particularly glycoform analysis
  • ICH Q7: validation requirements for API manufacturers, applicable to the transfer of active substances

Summary of PPQ Strategy for Sterile Products

Standard PPQ requirements (FDA + EU aligned):
  Number of batches: at least 3 (FDA recommendation, not mandatory)
  Batch size: commercial scale (must not be reduced)
  Sampling: statistically meaningful enhanced sampling

Additional requirements for sterile products:
  APS (media fill): completed before PPQ
  CCI confirmation: performed for every PPQ batch
  Sterility test: per Ph. Eur. 2.6.1 / USP <71>

Conditions for concurrent validation:
  → The process has sufficient historical data
  → The justification has been explained to and accepted by the regulatory authority
  → An enhanced real-time monitoring plan is in place
  → Full PPQ must be confirmed retrospectively once the number
    of commercial batches reaches the defined value

Key Point - CDMO Commercial Pressure vs. Regulatory Requirements

The most common conflict in technology transfer is a client asking for "commercial release from the first batch" when the regulations require the full PPQ process. The CDMO QA's responsibility is to agree on the number of PPQ batches and the strategy explicitly in the project contract, to avoid later disputes over the process validation strategy. If the client insists on concurrent validation, it must be supported by a regulatory basis and declared in advance in the NDA/MAA.

7.7 Change Management

Original Text

Technology transfer inherently involves changes to the manufacturing process. These changes need to be managed through the change management system at both the sending and receiving sites. The classification of the technology transfer as a regulatory change (e.g., CBE-30, Prior Approval Supplement (PAS), or equivalent in EU) must be determined before execution, not after. QA must classify the TT change, assess the regulatory filing impact, and ensure appropriate notifications are made to health authorities.

The change management process for technology transfer should ensure:

  • All changes are documented and justified with scientific rationale
  • Impact on the approved regulatory dossier is assessed before implementation
  • Risk-based approach determines the level of change control documentation required
  • The transition point from technology transfer to routine commercial manufacturing is clearly defined, with quality responsibility formally transferred

Tutorial Commentary

Key Point - Classification Must Be Completed Before Execution

ISPE's position is clear: regulatory classification must be completed before the technology transfer is executed, not afterward. If it is discovered after PPQ is completed that a PAS (requiring FDA approval before manufacture) is needed, and the production plan has already been scheduled, the commercial loss will be significant.

QA's list of responsibilities:

  • Start the regulatory classification assessment at project intake
  • Confirm whether prior approval is needed, working together with the Regulatory Affairs (RA) department
  • State the filing timeline and constraints to the client clearly in the contract

Key Point - A Clear Definition of the TT Completion Point

When is a technology transfer "complete"? The CDMO and the sending unit must agree this explicitly in the contract:

  • After approval of the PPQ report?
  • After release of the first commercial batch?
  • After the first successful regulatory inspection?

The completion point defines the moment technical responsibility is handed over: before it, the sending unit's SMEs remain obliged to provide technical support; after it, the CDMO bears full responsibility for manufacturing.

Practical Application - CDMO Risk Management on Project Intake

When CDMO Business Development (BD) is quoting for a technology transfer, QA and RA must be asked to assess in parallel:

  1. Which category of regulatory change does this TT fall under?
  2. How long is the filing timeline? What is the impact on the commercialization timeline?
  3. What CDMO-side data are needed to support the client's filing documents?

Answering these questions before signing the contract costs far less than discovering problems after signing.

7.8 Execution - Quality Oversight During TT

Original Text

Quality oversight during technology transfer execution is essential. The quality unit should ensure that:

  • All deviations during the transfer are documented, investigated, and resolved in a timely manner
  • Critical decisions during execution are made with quality representation
  • The transition from technology transfer activities to routine commercial manufacturing is formally completed with documented quality sign-off
  • The accumulated knowledge from the technology transfer is captured and available to support the commercial manufacturing process

Good documentation during technology transfer execution builds the foundation for the commercial quality system at the receiving site. Every finding, decision, and deviation captured during the transfer contributes to the process knowledge base that will support ongoing manufacture and continuous improvement.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts - Four Aspects of Quality Oversight During Execution

Quality oversight during TT execution is not just "reviewing documents"; it actively ensures:

  • Real-time deviation management: if deviations during TT are not recorded promptly, the sequence of events may not be reconstructable later, affecting the integrity of PPQ data
  • Decision quality: key decisions such as "whether to proceed to the next batch" need QA participation and sign-off
  • Knowledge capture: the process knowledge generated by TT (including "what does not work") must be documented and become the knowledge base for routine manufacturing
  • Formal handover: the handover from TT to commercial manufacturing must have formal quality sign-off documentation; it cannot be just "PPQ passed, so carry on"

Analogy - The Importance of Knowledge Capture

A technology transfer is like constructing a building. If the foundation problems, construction method adjustments and material substitutions encountered along the way are all recorded in the site log, there is a complete basis for future repair or renovation. If only "building completed" is recorded, problems later cannot be traced. The TT documentation is the site log of this building.

Key Point - The Moment CDMO Technical Responsibility Is Handed Over

ISPE stresses that the formal documentation of "TT completion" is one of the most important milestones between the CDMO and the client. After the completion point:

  • The CDMO bears full GMP compliance responsibility
  • The sending unit's technical support obligation becomes advisory in nature
  • The CDMO's QA becomes the primary quality authority

It is recommended that the TT contract clearly define the "Handover Criteria" and require the QA heads of both parties to sign a handover confirmation.

9.1   Summary

This chapter presents case studies that illustrate key technology transfer concepts presented in this Guide, with an emphasis on utilizing CMOs.

The key components of any technology transfer are the technical challenges and effective program management. With any technology transfer program, technical challenges can arise that could subsequently impact the planned timelines, process performance, and product quality. These challenges should be addressed in a collaborative way between the sending and receiving units to minimize delays in the program and frustrations arising between sites.

In this chapter, five case studies are presented, the first two from a client perspective and the subsequent three from a CMO perspective. These examples demonstrate a range of challenges which occurred during technology transfer programs and how they were subsequently resolved.

The molecules in these case studies are primarily monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) but the lessons learned are generally applicable across all molecular classes. Manufacturing processes for mAbs have become relatively well-defined and are often based on platform technologies that can be effectively leveraged; there is a generally broad knowledge base. In addition, the advent of single-use technologies is facilitating transfers by minimizing changes in equipment and operating parameters.

The amount of drug substance manufacturing experience that smaller companies typically have is modest, often at different scales and with limited development history and process knowledge. Transfers represent the opportunity to right-size a process and implement process improvements with an eye to minimizing changes that might impact product comparability. Technology transfers also potentially offer an opportunity to assess new technologies, such as continuous manufacturing and PAT, if the CMO has these capabilities.

9.2   Main Challenges

One of the main challenges for successful technology transfer utilizing a contract manufacturer is selecting the right CMO and developing a good partnership. From an operational perspective, this involves ensuring capacity (i.e., having manufacturing slots available within an acceptable time frame) and ensuring appropriate process and analytical capabilities necessary for project success. Demand for manufacturing slots will vary over time, but demand for capacity continues to grow and is a potential constraint that is a priority planning consideration. Recent experience with these case studies has resulted in successful transfers (i.e., completion of first GMP batch in approximately 12 months).

Another main challenge for a small to mid-size company executing technology transfer is how to deal with a lack of development history or process knowledge, either due to limited manufacturing experience or insufficient process development. Performing a gap assessment prior to initiating the transfer and then sharing this assessment with the CMO at initiation (to gain alignment and ensure transparency) can be critical for success. Early involvement of manufacturing teams during technology transfer and development phases is also critical for successful implementation of the process at scale.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Why Is the mAb the Lead Example?

This appendix chooses the monoclonal antibody (mAb) as its main case, for three reasons:

  • Mainstream in the market: mAbs account for more than half of the global biologics market value, and are the product type CDMOs encounter most often
  • Mature platform process: the CHO cell culture → Protein A affinity chromatography → viral inactivation → polishing chromatography platform path is well established, so a CDMO can quickly assess the feasibility of acceptance
  • Widespread single-use technology: single-use bioreactors, filtration and similar technologies greatly reduce differences in equipment configuration, lowering equipment compatibility risk in TT

Although mAb experience can be extrapolated to other biologics, molecules such as fusion proteins, ADCs (antibody-drug conjugates) and cell and gene therapies (CGT) each have their own unique challenges and cannot be handled with the same approach entirely.

Key Point: What "First GMP Batch Within 12 Months" Means

The text gives the benchmark for a successful TT as "completing the first GMP batch within about 12 months". This figure runs from CMO selection to completion of GMP manufacture. For CDMO business development, this is one of the questions clients ask most often.

Key factors affecting these 12 months:

  • Document completeness (missing documents are the largest source of delay)
  • Procurement time for long lead time materials (such as single-use bag sets, chromatography media and cell culture media)
  • Whether the CDMO's process engineers are familiar with the client's platform process
  • Regulatory strategy (whether the engineering run requires an ICH Q5E comparability study)

Analogy: The Small Company's TT Dilemma

A situation small to mid-sized biotech companies often face: the preclinical/Phase I process was run in an academic lab or a small CRO, at small batch sizes (1-10 L) with incomplete records, and now it must be transferred to 2,000 L commercial scale.

It is like a cook who developed a dish at home in a small pot and now has to reproduce it for mass production in a factory. Not only must the recipe be complete, but heat transfer, mixing efficiency, ingredient batch variability and more must be considered. Conditions that held naturally in the small pot may fail completely in the big one.

This is why "gap assessment up front" matters so much: finding gaps early lets the contract define clearly who will fill them and how the cost will be shared.

9.3   Overview of Technology Transfer Phases

As described in Chapter 3, there are six phases of technology transfer:

  1. Project Initiation: Form technology transfer team and develop charter
  2. Knowledge Transfer: Consolidate knowledge for transfer and agree on high level technology transfer proposal

  5. Project Implementation: Process (procedure) qualification

  1. CMO Selection
  2. Project Initiation
  3. Project Execution

In relation to the six phases presented in Chapter 3: CMO selection precedes establishment of a technology transfer team but is a necessary precursor for outsourcing.

  • Project initiation incorporates aspects of phases 1 to 3.

The CMO selection process is initiated by developing a list of candidates based on industry knowledge and experience that meet baseline criteria, including scale, capability, and compliance history.   • 1 Introduction — Background, Objectives, Response to RFP
  • 2 Scope of Services Requested — Technology Transfer, Analytical Services, Engineering and Clinical Batches, PPQ Batches, Process Validation, Stability, Additional Services
  • 3 Supporting Information — development reports, batch records (if available), PFDs, specifications, test methods, bill of materials, etc.

For the first case study, this approach started with engagement of six candidate CMOs. The proposals received were reviewed and a head to head analysis was performed on work scope, cost, and timing. From this assessment, two lead candidates were identified and due diligence audits were performed, resulting in a lead and a backup CMO.

9.3.2   Project Initiation

Once the CMO is selected, contract and quality agreement negotiations are immediately initiated, utilizing CMO templates for efficiency. A project kickoff meeting should be scheduled as soon as possible at the CMO and is critical for establishing the personal connections and lines of communication.

The composition of the client project team may vary, but will typically include a project leader, project manager, process and analytical SMEs, a CMC regulatory representative, and a quality representative. The CMO project team will be comparable and complementary.

Establishment of personal connections between these team members and their counterparts at the CMO are invaluable in anticipation that there will be challenges during the technology transfer and manufacturing that will require good working relationships to ensure appropriate and timely resolution. Face-to-face meetings are critical for establishing relationships and lines of communication.

9.3.3   Project Execution

The third aspect of project initiation is document transfer and sharing of the QTPP, CQAs, and CPPs that define the control strategy. These documents are important because they:

  • Define the intended use and product quality profile
  • Ensure that technology transfer process development/optimization begins with the end in mind and that client and CMO goals and expectations are aligned
  • Define those product characteristics or CQAs that must be monitored and controlled during technology transfer

Project execution follows a standard course of events. The following is an overview of activities undertaken in both case studies:

  • Process assessment and identification of the need for process development/optimization for facility fit
  • Identification and immediate ordering of long lead time materials/consumables
  • Small scale/development studies, as appropriate
  • Method transfer and qualification, as appropriate
  • Development of GMP documentation, including batch records, test methods, specification, etc.
  • Pilot scale run, if needed
  • Engineering run, if needed
  • GMP run(s)
  • Analytical comparability
  • Regulatory submission

Key Point: The Role of Stage Gates

A Stage Gate is a mechanism by which the CDMO protects itself and the client: progression to the next stage is allowed only once the previous milestone is confirmed complete. For example:

  • Document transfer gate: documents are fully received and reviewed and approved → only then begin writing batch records
  • Method transfer gate: analytical method qualification confirmed → only then begin the engineering batch
  • Engineering batch gate: process data review passed → only then proceed to GMP batches

Stage gates catch problems early, avoiding the discovery at an expensive GMP batch that an analytical method simply cannot be used on the receiving site's equipment.

Analogy: Why Face-to-Face Meetings Cannot Be Skipped

The text specifically stresses that "Face-to-face meetings are critical". Even in the age of video conferencing, this advice still holds, because problems (deviations, batch failures, customer complaints) inevitably arise during technology transfer.

It is like an emergency room team: day to day you can communicate by phone, but in an emergency the rapport, trust and speed of real-time decisions that come from being face to face cannot be replaced by a phone call. The TT kick-off meeting builds that foundation of trust before the real crisis arrives.

CDMO Practice: Managing Long Lead Time Materials

"Identification and immediate ordering of long lead time materials/consumables" is the second action item in the execution stage, and it comes earlier in the timeline than most people expect.

Long lead time materials commonly seen in biologics TT:

  • Protein A chromatography media (MabSelect Resin): lead time 6-12 weeks
  • Single-use bioreactor bag sets (SU Bioreactor Bag): 8-16 weeks
  • Cell culture media (Basal + Feed Media): up to 12 weeks if a custom formulation
  • Virus filters: 6-10 weeks

If sourcing and purchasing start only after the kick-off meeting, the arrival of these materials is likely to become the bottleneck for the engineering batch. Best practice is to ask the client for the BOM at the RFP stage, so the CDMO can assess immediately which items are long lead time materials.

Key Point: "Quantitative Risk Assessment Has Limited Utility for Small Companies"

The text says frankly: "a quantitative risk assessment for a small company may have limited utility". This statement deserves reflection: with the traditional ICH Q9 RPN scoring (severity × occurrence × detectability), when data are insufficient the scores themselves may lack credibility.

In such cases, the judgment of experienced senior staff is often more valuable than a fully completed RPN table. Regulators share this view: for new molecules and new processes, qualitative risk assessment combined with expert judgment is an accepted approach.

The key is that, whichever method is used, it must be documented. Even a note such as "Based on SME judgment, the risk of this item is acceptable, for the following reasons" is better than no record.

Analogy: The Critical Path = the Key Route up a Mountain

"Critical path activities" are the chain of activities in the whole TT project where a delay in any one means the entire project cannot finish on time.

It is like a climbing team that must reach the summit before sunset: it must identify which checkpoints along the route have to be passed by what time. If the first checkpoint is delayed, no amount of speed later can make up for it.

In biologics TT, a typical critical path includes: cell line characterization confirmed → long lead time materials on site → method transfer completed → engineering batch executed → data review → GMP batch. If any step is blocked, the date of the first GMP batch moves back directly.

CDMO Practice: Best Practice for the Weekly Teleconference

The text mentions "weekly teleconferences" as a mechanism for continuous risk management. An effective weekly meeting is structured as follows:

  • Action Item Review (5 minutes): confirm the completion status of last week's action items
  • Critical Path Status (10 minutes): are critical path activities progressing on schedule
  • Issues & Risks (10 minutes): newly arising issues, the responsible person and the expected resolution date
  • Next Week Plan (5 minutes): preview of important milestones for the coming week

Key principle: the PM must be present (to ensure follow-up), discussions between technical SMEs should not bypass the PM to make decisions directly, and all decisions must be recorded in meeting minutes and filed to support future audits and investigations.

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer | p104–106

The first two case studies are interrelated in that they involve two products, one CMO company, and three CMO sites. The first case study describes a product in early phase 2 development that held significant clinical potential to move to a pivotal study. With the advent of breakthrough therapy designation and accelerated product development, the timelines for process optimization and the timeframe for gaining manufacturing experience to achieve a robust and reproducible manufacturing process is significantly reduced. Therefore, the process was transferred from the client's internal early phase manufacturing site to a US-based commercial CMO (site A) fairly early in clinical development.

This CMO was selected for the following reasons:

  • Commercial experience
  • Appropriate scale – 2000 L single-use bioreactors (SUB)
  • Available capacity – manufacturing slots available with ~12 months lead time

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Breakthrough Therapy Designation: An FDA expedited pathway for drugs of major clinical significance. The consequence is a compressed development timeline, so technology transfer must start before the process is fully confirmed. For a CMO this is a routine challenge.

Research Cell Bank (RCB) vs. Master Cell Bank (MCB): An RCB is a research cell bank that does not yet fully meet GMP requirements; an MCB is the formal GMP cell bank. In principle TT should start from the MCB, but when the MCB is not ready the RCB has to be used first, with additional testing to confirm equivalence.

Facility Fit: Adapting the sending unit's process to the receiving site's equipment configuration, including equipment size, operating sequence, and the types of consumables already in use. It is not "copying" a process but "transplanting" it.

Analogy

It is like a head chef (the client) who is still developing the menu (process development not finished) but sends the recipe to a new restaurant (the CMO) to practice. The new cooks must not only learn the recipe but also refine it at the same time so that it suits the stove size of the new kitchen (facility fit). This "changing the engine in flight" model is very high risk and requires strong technical capability and communication mechanisms on both sides.

Key Notes

Issue 1: Transfer before process development is complete. This is the most frequent starting point of biologics TT failures. The solution in this case was to run process optimization at the CMO in parallel, on the condition that the CMO has sufficient process development capability. When selecting a CMO, "process development capability" is not a bonus; it is a requirement.

Issue 2: EU viral clearance compliance gap. The CMO held FDA certification but lacked EU QP certification, which directly affected the marketing application in Europe. For multi-market filings, compliance eligibility must be confirmed at the CMO selection stage; fixing it afterwards is extremely costly.

Issue 3: Unfamiliarity with the batch release process. The client did not understand the CMO's batch review mechanism, which caused delays. The fix relied on the CMO's flexibility rather than process design. This is a "people-dependent" solution and carries long-term risk.

Practical Application

If your CDMO receives a "breakthrough therapy" mAb project whose process development is not yet complete:

  • Confirm immediately: What is the expected MCB completion date, and if it is delayed, what supplemental tests are required for the RCB?
  • Set a "process lock" milestone: after which point will change requests from the sending unit no longer be accepted?
  • Confirm whether the CMO has an Ambr® or similar high-throughput screening system able to carry out the optimization work.
  • Confirm EU QP compliance status. If the client plans to enter the European market, this must be confirmed before contract signing.

9.5.3 Biologics Case Study #3 — Buffer Formulation Discrepancy in Protein A Purification

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer | p109–110

An mAb purification process was developed at a small biotechnology company and transferred to a CMO for evaluation at laboratory scale, prior to scale-up to a non-GMP pilot and first GMP clinical batch. The process consisted of a Protein A chromatography step, low pH virus inactivation, and pH adjustment followed by cation exchange and anion exchange chromatography steps.

At the sending unit, the product was eluted from Protein A with acetate buffer pH 3.2 and the product eluted from the column within the required range for the viral inactivation step. The eluate was pH adjusted to pH 5.0 for the cation exchange step using 1 part product to 4 parts titrant. Prior to manufacture of the first GMP batch, a laboratory scale and pilot scale evaluation was performed at the receiving unit. During the laboratory scale evaluation at the receiving unit, two issues arose with the Protein A step.

A root cause analysis (Ishikawa diagram) was performed on the Protein A step. A number of items were highlighted for further investigation between the two units, relating to elution buffer and titrant preparation. During the transfer, only the names of the buffers were shared and not the preparation details. Two key differences were identified:

  • At the sending unit, the elution buffer and the titrant were prepared using the trihydrate salt, compared to use of the anhydrous salt at the receiving unit.
  • At the sending unit, the elution buffer was titrated with hydrochloric acid, compared to titration with acetic acid at the receiving unit, which resulted in much higher buffering capacity.

Key Concepts

Trihydrate vs. Anhydrous Salt: Two forms of the same chemical. The trihydrate contains water of crystallization, so the same "gram" amount of trihydrate contains less actual active component. If the formulation is specified in "mg/mL", using the wrong form changes the buffering capacity and therefore the pH behavior.

Low pH Viral Inactivation: An important viral clearance step for biologics. The Protein A eluate typically has a pH of 3-4 and must be held for a defined time to ensure the virus is inactivated. If the pH is too low, the risk of protein denaturation may increase; if too high, viral inactivation may be insufficient. Precise pH control is the core of this step.

Ishikawa Diagram (fishbone diagram): A structured root cause analysis tool that systematically lists the categories of possible causes of a problem (people, machine, material, method, environment). It is one of the standard tools for GMP deviation investigations.

Analogy

This case is like two cooks who are both told to "add one teaspoon of salt", but one uses coarse salt (trihydrate: large grains, so a teaspoon weighs less) and the other uses fine salt (anhydrous: dense grains, so a teaspoon weighs more). The recipe only says "one teaspoon of salt" without specifying which kind, and the taste turns out completely different. This is the danger of "passing on only the buffer name and not the preparation details".

Key Notes

This is one of the most common and most easily preventable TT failure modes: buffer preparation details not included in the transfer documents. Proper TT documents should include the chemical form of the salt (trihydrate / anhydrous), the type of titrant acid, the order of pH adjustment, and water quality requirements (WFI vs. purified water). Passing on only the "buffer name" is an incomplete technology transfer.

The pH range for low pH inactivation must have a safety margin: In this case the pH was lower than expected; it was still within the viral inactivation range but close to a region that could damage the product. For a situation that is "only just within range", validation must confirm that the operating space (design space) is sufficiently wide.

Complete Documentation Checklist for Buffer Transfer

Minimum requirements (must be transferred):
  1. Buffer name and target pH / concentration
  2. Chemical form of the salts (trihydrate vs. anhydrous)
  3. Type and concentration of the acid/base used for titration
  4. Preparation sequence (which component is added first)
  5. Water quality specification (water for injection WFI / purified water)
  6. Filtration specification (0.2 μm, pre-use / post-use)
  7. Storage temperature and shelf life

Recommended additions (to reduce the risk of misunderstanding):
  - Worked preparation example (calculated for a specific batch size)
  - Measured pH range from batches already prepared
  - Representative buffer curve (titration curve)

Key Concepts

IgM (immunoglobulin M): Its molecular weight is about 5-10 times that of IgG (mAb), and it cannot be purified by standard Protein A affinity chromatography (because Protein A mainly binds the IgG Fc region). Process development is far harder than for a typical mAb, and every step is full of unknowns.

Cumulative Recovery: The product of the recovery of multiple purification steps. At 70% per step, after three steps: 70% × 70% × 70% = 34%. The <10% in this case means some steps had extremely low recovery, so the cost-effectiveness case could not be made.

UV Treatment for Virus Reduction: An alternative viral clearance approach when the molecule is too large to pass through a 20 nm nanofiltration membrane. However, UV may promote aggregate formation in large protein molecules, so dose and operating conditions must be precisely controlled.

Analogy

The pastries that come out are "edible" (quality acceptable), but the loss is so great (recovery <10%) that each pastry costs 10 times the normal amount. The boss (the client) finally decided to switch dishes (to another pipeline molecule). This is a case that failed commercially no matter how hard the technical effort.

Key Notes

Starting the transfer without batch records: This was the biggest root problem in this case. The sending unit had no batch records or development reports, so the receiving site had to develop its own transfer document framework and ask the sending unit to fill in key product knowledge (pH stability, aggregation tendency, solubility, etc.). This made up for part of the gap, but the process transferred was essentially a process "still to be completed".

The UV viral clearance step was not validated before transfer: The sending unit merely "suggested" using UV, with no experimental data. This meant the receiving site had to develop a brand-new viral clearance step from scratch at the CMO, and evaluate it at non-GMP scale (because large amounts of material are required). This is a typical "incomplete process transfer".

The commercial meaning of <10% recovery: At commercial mAb scale, if normal process recovery is 60%, the room for improvement is more than 50-fold. Low recovery directly drives up Cost of Goods (COGS) and makes it almost impossible to compete in a competitively priced product environment. Technical feasibility ≠ commercial feasibility.

Practical Application

When you receive an inquiry for a non-standard molecule with "no batch records" (such as IgM or a bispecific antibody):

  • Immediately ask the sending unit to complete a "molecule characteristics questionnaire": pH / concentration stability, aggregation tendency, special solubility requirements, known process issues
  • Assess whether you have the corresponding equipment (special resins, UV equipment, UF/DF capability)
  • Clearly distinguish in the contract between "technology transfer" and "process development contract work"; the latter carries higher risk and should cost more
  • Set "go/no-go" milestones triggered by indicators such as recovery and impurity removal, to avoid endless investment of resources

Key Concepts

Starting work under a Letter of Intent: Beginning experimental work on the basis of a letter of intent before the formal contract is signed. "Schedule pressure" is the factor most likely to trigger skipped steps: In this case experiments began 1 month after receiving the technology transfer documents, and the pilot batch was run 10 weeks later. At this speed, material verification and procedure review are easily compressed. With ample time, the "wrong resin" issue would very likely have been caught in the SOP review before the experiment.

The three-layer verification of TT documents, "transfer → translation → execution": The problem in this case occurred at the "execution" layer: the correct specification was in the documents, but at the time of operation nobody verified that the dispensed material matched the specification. A complete TT system needs to ensure an independent check at every layer, including: (1) whether the documents were transferred completely; (2) whether the receiving site correctly understood them and translated them into local SOPs; (3) whether execution followed the SOP and verified the materials.

Practical Application

For execution-layer errors such as "use of the wrong resin", the CDMO should establish:

  • Two-person material verification: Chromatography resins, culture media, and buffer components must be checked by a second person against the SOP for lot number, specification, and appearance before use.
  • Resin log: Record each resin lot's use history, number of cleaning cycles, and performance test results to ensure correct traceability.
  • "Ultra-tight timeline" red flag mechanism: When the TT schedule is extremely compressed, QA should activate an enhanced oversight mode, adding pre-experiment review meetings to ensure that speed does not sacrifice procedural integrity.

9.6 Conclusions: Lessons Learned from Biologics Technology Transfer

Original Text

ISPE Tech Transfer | Appendix 2 Synthesis

The five biologics case studies presented in this appendix — spanning Phase 2 mAb transfers, CMO-to-CMO late-stage transfers, purification process evaluations, and complex molecule development — collectively illustrate the unique complexity of biologics technology transfer compared to small molecule manufacturing.

Across all five case studies, several universal themes emerge:

Theme 1: Cell line and cell bank availability is a recurrent critical path item. In Case Studies #1 and #2, the unavailability of the Master Cell Bank (MCB) at the start of transfer required use of a Research Cell Bank (RCB), adding risk and requiring enhanced testing. Cell line readiness — including third-party cell bank testing timelines — must be factored into every biologic TT project plan as a potential critical path constraint.

Theme 2: Process development completion is prerequisite to successful transfer. Multiple case studies describe transfers initiated before process development was complete. While sometimes unavoidable under breakthrough therapy or accelerated development timelines, this approach requires a CMO partner with strong in-house development capability and a clear, contractually defined boundary between "technology transfer" and "process development."

Theme 3: Documentation detail — not just document titles — must be transferred. Case Study #3 demonstrates that transferring buffer names without preparation details led to a reproducible but unexpected pH result. Complete transfer packages must include chemical forms of salts, titration reagent identities, preparation sequences, water quality specifications, and working concentration ranges.

Theme 4: Facility fit and equipment equivalence require proactive small-scale modeling. All case studies involve some degree of facility fit work — changes in scale, equipment type, or process sequence. The successful transfers used small-scale modeling at a development site (site C in Case Study #2) to confirm process feasibility before committing to full-scale GMP manufacturing.

Theme 5: Communication infrastructure is as important as technical capability. Across every case study, the "What Worked" section highlights dedicated project management, regular cross-functional meetings (core team + SME sub-teams), documented action items, and — particularly for international transfers — person-in-place representation. These are not "soft" factors; they are structural enablers of transfer success.

Theme 6: Commercial feasibility must be assessed alongside technical feasibility. Case Study #4 demonstrates that a technically successful process development effort (achieving acceptable product quality) can still result in program termination if cumulative recovery is insufficient to support commercial cost-of-goods. Go/no-go criteria based on yield and COGS should be defined at project initiation.

Theme 7: Regulatory compliance scope must be confirmed before CMO selection. Case Study #1 reveals a gap in EU viral clearance certification that was not identified until late in the transfer. For products targeting multiple markets, regulatory compliance coverage (FDA, EMA, PMDA, etc.) must be a CMO selection criterion, not an afterthought.

10.2 Main Challenges

Original Text

Supply chains for the manufacture of APIs are constantly requiring technology transfers to be performed in the pursuit of lower cost options, process improvements, market requirements, and continuity of supply. In recent years, many of the API technology transfers have been from domestic to global manufacturing sites, often at an external company.

Development of API processes has also moved to centralized, global development centers that do not have large scale manufacturing capabilities; this requires a technology transfer to another site for commercial production.

The movement to global supply chains for API manufacture requires the technology transfer to understand the differences between the sending and receiving units, specifically:

  • Differences in their Quality Management Systems (QMSs)
  • Risk tolerance levels
  • Operating/compliance standards

These differences need to be identified early in the technology transfer process so they can be addressed and factored into the overall transfer plan.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Four business drivers of technology transfer:

  • Lower Cost: Finding a site with lower production costs, commonly moving from Western countries to India, China, and similar locations
  • Process Improvement: Upgrading the process at the same time as the transfer
  • Market Requirements: Local regulations, import restrictions, or market access requirements that call for local manufacture
  • Continuity of Supply: Reducing the supply risk of a single site

Risk Identification Framework

Three prerequisites for a successful transfer:
1. Identify QMS differences (regulatory interpretation, documentation requirements)
   → This must be done in the "planning phase" of the transfer, not discovered during execution
2. Align risk tolerance
   → Are the standards for "acceptable deviations" the same at the sending and receiving sites?
3. Compare operating/compliance standards
   → Which SOPs need to be re-established at the receiving site?

Key Notes

The trend toward globalized supply chains has made cross-border technology transfer the norm, but it brings a consequence: API development centers often lack commercial-scale manufacturing capability (they are purely R&D centers). This means that "development-to-commercial" knowledge transfer almost inevitably involves cross-organizational and cross-cultural gaps. For a CDMO, this is exactly where the opportunity lies: clients need a partner who can receive early development knowledge and scale it up to commercial scale.

  • The US-based development team was already working on a next generation process and analytical methods were already being used

At the kickoff meeting, the full cross-functional team was provided the technology transfer charter with the goals and boundaries of the transfer. Due to the condensed timeframe, a full risk assessment could not be performed until after the knowledge transfer had begun. The risk assessment identified key challenges in the initial assumptions as summarized in Table 10.2.

Challenge: CPP Knowledge Transfer

Although the technology transfer was between two internal company sites, access to operations information and data was limited. Review of historical manufacturing batch records, validation reports, and CPPs identified significant gaps:

  • Accurate translation of batch records to English impacted ability to understand local operational practices
  • Philosophy in the identification and control of CPPs
  • Process data availability
"pH was to be controlled tightly in a specified range as measured using an in-process control sample. However, information such as the location of the sample, how the measurement was obtained and validated, and accuracy of the data was not well understood based on the technology transfer information provided."

Another example was interpretation of the allowed flowrate of a reactant into the reactor. The documented criticality justification stated that the flowrate was a critical parameter to be maintained between 0.5–1.0 liters/min. However, the batch records appeared to measure only the total time rather than a rate across the entire reactant charge — a common practice at the sending unit which differed slightly from the criticality justification.

Learning

Face-to-face meetings with the sending unit cross-functional team allowed the receiving unit to obtain the required process information. A crucial element was the involvement of the technicians with expertise performing the actual batch operations. These interactions developed a stronger relationship between sites, facilitating remote cooperation throughout the transfer. Technicians/operators and engineers from the sending unit participated at the receiving unit during the manufacture of process validation batches.

Outcome

Successful process validation was performed at the receiving unit three months after the start of the technology transfer, allowing the receiving unit to be filed as a manufacturer with no gap in API supply for the US market.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Commercial-to-Commercial Transfer: Both the sending and receiving sites are commercial GMP manufacturing sites, and the API is already approved. Such transfers are theoretically lower risk, but this case shows that a regulatory change (import restrictions) can suddenly create a compressed timeline of 3-6 months, forcing the team to compromise between the completeness of knowledge transfer and speed.

Technology Transfer Charter: A formal document defining the objectives, scope, boundaries, and responsible persons of the transfer. It is the "contract" of the technology transfer, and all cross-functional members must understand and commit to it at the kickoff meeting.

Key Note: The Tacit Knowledge Gap

The core lesson of this case is the gap between the documented CPP and actual operating practice:

  • The batch record states "flow rate 0.5-1.0 L/min", but in practice the operators measured the "total addition time"
  • The sampling location and measurement method for pH control were not clearly described in the documents

This kind of gap is even harder to detect when compounded by a language barrier. Conclusion: the batch record alone is not sufficient to transfer process knowledge, and the hands-on involvement of operators is irreplaceable.

Key Note: The Milling Equivalence Trap

"Same equipment = same process" is one of the most dangerous assumptions in technology transfer. In this case, the receiving site installed milling equipment of the exact same model as the sending site, yet could not meet the particle size distribution specification, and additional plant-scale milling trials were ultimately required to find the correct process parameters. Possible causes include differences in ambient humidity, feeding method, and degree of mill wear.

Analogy

CPP knowledge transfer is like asking a chef to hand a recipe to a new chef. The recipe says "stir-fry over medium heat for 3 minutes", but the original chef knows this refers to a specific stove model, and the new chef's flame may be twice as strong, so 3 minutes would burn the dish. The batch record is the recipe, but the real knowledge is in the chef's head. Having the original chef teach on site (technician on-site) is the solution.

Actual completion: from transfer start to completion of process validation = 3 months
Outcome: no supply interruption, and the new manufacturing site was filed with the FDA on time

Key success factors:
+ Early identification of method validation gaps → co-validation started immediately
+ Long Lead Items procured immediately after Kickoff
+ Technical staff on site at the receiving site for the process validation batches

CDMO Application

As the receiving CDMO, when a client asks for a compressed timeline of "3-6 months to complete the transfer", this case provides an important operating framework:

  • Act immediately: Method gap assessment + identification of long lead equipment (after Day 1 Kickoff)
  • Arrange technical staff exchange visits: Have the client's operators come to the CDMO site to take part in the first batch or the validation batches
  • Language management: If the client's documents are not in English, require an official English translation, and have technical staff from both sides confirm the operating practice
  • Equipment equivalence assessment: Even when the equipment model is the same, perform confirmation trials of the process parameters

10.4.2 Small Molecule Case Study #2: Alignment of Quality Management Requirements for Method Transfer

Original Text

This case study is for a development-commercial transfer of a set of related APIs being developed in Europe for commercial manufacture in the US. While these APIs were new to the company's portfolio, they were already commercially available in the US using a different process route. Each API already had CQAs developed and filed with the drug product manufacturers.

The development and technology transfer teams were internal to a large, global pharmaceutical company, following the same global QMS guidelines. The sending unit (development team) was responsible for designing all aspects of the process including analytical method development. Program management of the technology transfer was performed at the receiving unit with cross-functional members having oversight in the ongoing development activities.

The technology transfer of each API was to be performed sequentially under tight timelines to achieve contract deadlines with the drug product manufacturer. However, development of each API process was being performed in parallel with analytical methods that significantly overlapped the APIs.

Challenge

While both development and manufacturing were at internal locations, interpretation of the global QMS for analytical method transfer, qualification, and validation requirements differed locally between Europe and the US. This was going to cause excessive redundant analysis and documentation activities.

For compendial methods, the global policy required the method to be confirmed by running on a reference sample. The sending unit used material generated in development laboratory batches. The receiving unit required a method qualification involving testing using a qualified reference standard. In summary, global policy required validation of non-compendial methods but local differences were found in:

  • Testing requirements for the sending and receiving laboratories performing the validation
  • Documentation requirements

Learning

The project team worked with the drug product manufacturer, internal business team, and quality/regulatory to establish boundaries for the method development work:

  • CQAs and limits already filed by the drug product manufacturer were to align where possible
  • Drug product manufacturer analytical methods would be used where applicable and if not proprietary
  • New impurities formed from the new process would be identified if greater than 0.10%

The development and technology transfer project managers facilitated negotiations over a six-month period between the local quality groups. A blend of approaches (shown in Tables 10.3 and 10.4) was implemented to satisfy the key desired QMS elements yet reduce the required amount of sample materials, testing time, and number of resources.

"The receiving laboratory acted as the second laboratory verification required for completion of the method validation with both laboratories using the same sample; this approach reduced the time for method validation in half."

Prior to any analytical work, an agreement was signed between the sending and receiving units establishing criteria for all analytical work associated with these technology transfers — boundaries establishing the workflow for all analysts developing and transferring the methods.

Outcome

The negotiated approach was formally documented. Alignment of the QMS significantly reduced costs and allowed proper definition of required resources. The blended approach allowed 21 methods to become verifications/qualifications rather than validations, resulting in a time savings of over a year for one analyst. In total:

  • 80 method verifications/qualifications
  • 8 full method validations

Additionally, a system was agreed upon allowing multiple verifications and products to be combined into fewer documents. This system was adopted internally for future technology transfers.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

Development-to-Commercial Transfer: The sender is the R&D department and the receiver is a commercial manufacturing site. The core challenge of this type of transfer is that analytical methods at the development stage are often still under development and cannot be handed over in their final state.

Three levels of method transfer:

  • Validation: Full demonstration of method capability (precision, accuracy, linearity, specificity, etc.); it takes the longest
  • Qualification: Confirmation that the method runs properly on the receiving site's instruments; the requirements are lower than for validation
  • Verification: The minimum confirmation for compendial methods (USP/EP, etc.); it only needs to show that the laboratory can perform the method

Key Note: The Cost of QMS Interpretation Gaps

The European and US sites of the same multinational company interpreted the same global QMS policy differently. The root cause was that each region had made different commitments to its regulatory authority (responses given during audits created the divergent interpretations).

This case quantified the benefit of alignment:

  • 21 methods were reduced from "full validation" to "qualification/verification"
  • A single analyst saved more than one year of work time
  • In total, 80 qualifications/verifications and 8 full validations were completed

This shows that QMS alignment is not only a compliance issue but also a major resource-saving strategy.

Analogy

The three levels of method validation are like a chef's exams. Validation is the "comprehensive exam", requiring a demonstration of all cooking skills (accuracy, consistency, handling of special situations). Qualification is the "practical test", where you only need to produce an acceptable dish in the exam kitchen. Verification is the "menu-reading check", where you only need to show that you can read the menu and follow it. The dispute between the European and US sites was whether every dish should require the comprehensive exam, or whether the exam level should be chosen according to the difficulty of the dish. The six months of negotiation were spent building a system of "exams graded by dish difficulty."

Method Difficulty Stratification

Difficulty level 1 (visual/simple, no special equipment):
  → Apply the lightest verification requirements
  → Analyze each compendial material once

Difficulty level 2 (dedicated equipment, simple software):
  → Intermediate requirements: add confirmation of intermediate precision
  → LOD/LOQ verification required

Difficulty level 3 (dedicated equipment, complex software, e.g. chiral HPLC):
  → Non-compendial methods: full validation at the sending site + partial qualification at the receiving site
  → Both sites use the same sample, with the receiving site acting as the second laboratory for validation
  → Saving: method validation time is halved

Key Note: The Effect of Import Restrictions on Method Transfer

This case contains a practical obstacle that is often overlooked: import restrictions on the API itself made it impossible to ship enough sample to the receiving site for method validation. This was the practical driver for having both sites share the same sample (with the receiving site acting as the second laboratory). For a CDMO, when planning a method transfer it is essential to assess in advance whether the client's API or intermediates are subject to import/export restrictions. This directly affects the sample availability plan for the method transfer.

CDMO Application

The most common problem when a CDMO receives a client's analytical methods is unclear definition of the method transfer level. It is recommended that the Technical Transfer Agreement clearly specify:

  • The classification of each analytical method (validation/qualification/verification) and the rationale for it
  • Whose SOPs are used as the validation standard
  • Who is responsible for supplying reference standards
  • The identification threshold for new impurities arising from the new process (0.10% in this case)

Completing these definitions before signing the agreement avoids resource disputes later and protects the CDMO's timeline commitments.

  2. How does having the receiving site act as the "second laboratory for validation" halve the method validation time? What are the potential risks of this approach?

11.1 Introduction — Engineering Knowledge in Technology Transfer

Original Text

ISPE TechTransfer 3rd Ed.

Transfer of engineering knowledge can be an important consideration for transfers, whether for single or multiple sites. Importantly, it should be an ongoing two-way exchange of information as the intricacies and experience gained may enhance process understanding and product quality.

In the context of modern biopharmaceutical manufacturing, engineering knowledge can cover a wide range of information, including activities and information related to facilities/equipment design and maintenance through to chemical and process engineering knowledge and decisions related to manufacturing activities.

This chapter focuses on those activities that do not fall within the scope of process development or product realization as such activities should be captured within the development activities and control strategy for the process.

Overall, it is the success of the transferred process and delivery of product quality (drug substance or drug product meeting the requisite CQAs) that determines whether a transfer is successful. Well developed products and processes will identify critical aspects of processes that support achievement of consistent and reliable manufacturing; where these are of significance, they will form part of the control strategy.

Engineering controls are critical to maintenance of CPPs and can be key to detection and/or management of potential process influence from material attributes. Additionally, engineering requirements and specifications are important risk mitigations in processes — for example, where materials compatibility and cleanability is required to be assured by control of materials of construction, welding, surface finishes etc; or where instrument suitability is managed by instrument selection and calibration.

It is important that technology transfer planning clearly differentiates the critical engineering aspects of processes and that these decisions are clear to engineering groups at the receiving unit as well as their suppliers. If engineering teams and suppliers are disconnected from this information, this situation can lead to lack of focus and inefficiency or, worse, may result in failure to achieve the required manufacturing outcomes or delays, which lead to potential shortages in critical products.

Engineering information may include:

  • Details and information on the design, fabrication, or procurement of process equipment or supporting utilities:
    • Equipment drawings and specifications, including materials of construction compatible with process solutions and geometry
    • Details of critical measuring instruments, including range/capabilities
    • Physical layout or arrangement and integration of equipment
  • Details on the types of equipment to be used for running or supporting a process
  • Arrangement for equipment transfer, such as equipment transfer plans and commissioning/qualification methodology
  • Control programming/logic and configuration information
  • Maintenance and setup instructions
  • Equipment service history
  • Cleaning process development information
  • Details of engineering studies that are of importance to determining process benchmarks for equipment or processes

Proper technical assessment needs to be carried out across GxP and GEP (Good Engineering Practice) systems to ensure that key knowledge in these areas is captured to avoid delay or complications with technology transfers.

"Engineering information may include tacit knowledge that exists within an organization in systems and locations outside of the mainstream production control systems — often assumed to exist within qualification and validation files; however, the outputs of the engineering knowledge are frequently the only portion that is visible, not necessarily the process to get to that point."

There are three possible broad scenarios in terms of knowledge transfer:

  • Scenario 1: Sending unit transfers the process and like-for-like equipment as part of the project. Knowledge around use and behavior of the equipment related to the product and process under transfer is the focus.
  • Scenario 2: Sending and receiving units use the same or similar equipment (either new or existing) but that exists separately on both sites. Experience of operation, recommended configuration, and details of setup are required.
  • Scenario 3: Sending and receiving units use similar but not identical equipment, where the receiving unit may need to procure or operate equipment to achieve the process. The product and process outcomes are important; the means of getting there operationally may differ.

The scope of a technology transfer often includes the use of plant automation and/or some level of enterprise level information technology (such as manufacturing execution systems). Specialist engineering resources should be identified for replication or implementation of suitable automation and/or network infrastructure; particular attention should be given to risks to data integrity and cybersecurity.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — The Two-Way Nature of Engineering Knowledge

Engineering knowledge in technology transfer is not a one-way flow of "the sending unit teaches, the receiving unit learns." This chapter stresses "continuous two-way exchange," because during execution the receiving site often finds problems the sending site has never encountered, and if these new findings are fed back, the whole organization benefits.

GEP (Good Engineering Practice) is the engineering-side counterpart to GxP. It covers best practices in equipment design, construction, and maintenance. It is not mandated by regulation, but it forms the foundation of GxP validation.

Tacit knowledge is the key warning of this chapter: much critical engineering experience lives in engineers' heads, informal records, or old versions of operating manuals, and cannot be read from validation reports. This is the kind of knowledge most easily lost during transfer.

Analogy — Moving House and Renovating

The engineering side of technology transfer is like moving a Michelin-starred restaurant to a new location:

  • The menu (process) can be taken along
  • The equipment (kitchen tools) can be relocated or bought anew
  • But the chef's experience that "this old oven needs to be set 10 degrees lower" cannot be written into a recipe. That is tacit engineering knowledge.

The three equipment scenarios are like: (1) moving the original oven over, (2) buying the same model of oven, (3) buying an oven of a different brand with similar functions. The further along this list you go, the more has to be worked out again from scratch.

Key Point — Linking Engineering Controls to CPPs

The document stresses that engineering controls are the key means of maintaining CPPs (critical process parameters). This means:

  • Agitation speed control of a mixing tank → maintains the blend uniformity CPP
  • Accuracy of the temperature control system → maintains the reaction temperature CPP
  • Material specifications (stainless steel grade, surface roughness) → maintain cleaning reliability

Engineering specifications are therefore not a "hardware purchasing" issue but a process control issue that directly affects product CQAs. The transfer plan must ensure the engineering team understands which CPPs are directly tied to which engineering settings.

Risk Scoring Framework for the Three Equipment Scenarios

Scenario 1: Relocating identical equipment
  Risk level: Low
  Focus: Operating behavior and product-contact surface history
  Transfer documents: Operating SOPs, maintenance records, service history

Scenario 2: Same model of equipment at each site
  Risk level: Medium
  Focus: Setting differences, calibration frequency, usage practices
  Transfer documents: Configuration list, standard calibration plan

Scenario 3: Different equipment (functionally equivalent)
  Risk level: High
  Focus: Process output equivalence, revalidation needs
  Transfer documents: Equivalence assessment report, additional PPQ batches

Practical Application — Initial Engineering Readiness Assessment for a Receiving CDMO

As the receiving CDMO, when the technology transfer program starts, the engineering team should ask the following questions:

  1. Which models of critical equipment does the sending unit use? Which scenario does our equipment fall under?
  2. Which process parameters (CPPs) are maintained directly by engineering controls? Does the control range of our equipment cover them?
  3. Does the sending unit have records of maintenance anomalies for this equipment? Have any deviations been caused by equipment?
  4. Are the recipe formats of the automation systems (SCADA/MES) compatible, or does reprogramming have to be done?

Practice Questions

  1. Why does ISPE say engineering knowledge should be exchanged in "both directions"? What new knowledge can the receiving unit contribute to the sending unit?
  2. What are the common forms of tacit engineering knowledge? How can it be captured effectively in a transfer plan?
  3. If your company is a CDMO receiving a new product, how would you decide whether it falls under Scenario 1, 2, or 3? How do the follow-up actions differ for each?

Analogy — Example 1: The "Island Effect" in Car Maintenance Knowledge

A technology transfer program must have a built-in "feedback loop" so that new findings at the receiving site automatically flow back into the organization's knowledge base.

Key Point — Example 6: The Most Dangerous Assumption

"The equipment already exists, so it does not need to be re-commissioned" is one of the most dangerous assumptions in technology transfer.

When existing equipment must be reconfigured to take on a new process, the automation recipe, control logic, and parameter settings all need to be revalidated. In Example 6, the receiving site assumed that "the equipment is unchanged, so we can just apply the recipe," and the very first production batch failed.

Transfer of process related information in the form of specifications, quality documents, qualification and validation documents, production data, work instructions, and the many written technical resources within the GxP frameworks of a biopharmaceutical organization is expected under a robust technology transfer plan. However, GxP systems may be insufficient for capturing/transfer of tacit engineering knowledge.

The examples in Section 11.2 highlight the types of issues that may be encountered during technology transfer projects. The lessons learned from these examples are summarized in Table 11.7.

11.3.1 Useful Techniques for Capturing Engineering Information

Engineering SMEs should plan for knowledge and information to be transferred and shared as part of a technology transfer project. The following is a list of useful tools.

1. Planning for Technology Transfer During Procurement

It is often known or expected that a process will be transferred. As part of the project planning process, it is recommended to have a structured program. Deliverables for the project charter should include a process to capture useful engineering information/decisions that would assist any future execution of a similar project.

2. Structured Reviews of Engineering Databases for Existing Processes

Engineering databases may include computerized maintenance management systems (CMMS), calibration management systems, configuration management systems, equipment history files or logs, setup instructions or check-sheets, training/familiarization materials.

Typical data to be considered may include:

  • Maintenance management data:
    • Maintenance and calibration procedures and schedules (current practice)
    • Routine setup procedures and operational checks
    • Maintenance and calibration history (or selected history)
    • Information from engineering files/manuals relating to knowledge gained post-qualification
    • Checklists or training resources associated with key engineering activities
    • Log book reviews
  • Configuration data:
    • Control/automation configurable settings lists
    • Copies or images of automation or configuration records
    • Program version number information and history of upgrades
    • Equipment/process setting checklists
    • Tools/aids or techniques developed to assist setup or checking configuration
  • Engineering data supporting qualification:
    • FMEAs, Hazard and Operability, and other risk assessments conducted for operation
    • FMEA analysis to anticipate the need of specific commissioning or wet testing during start-up
    • Commissioning reports or data
    • Details of engineering studies conducted
    • Changes/lessons learned from start-up or initial operation
  • Operational excellence data:
    • OEE information
    • Value stream mapping exercises, Lean/Six Sigma projects (DMAIC improvements)
    • Engineering KPIs
    • Known troublesome areas for operations arising out of breakdowns, ineffective maintenance, difficulty in setup
    • Reliability analysis, mean time between failures (MTBF), mean time to failure (MTTF), or similar data

3. Responsibilities (RACI) Matrix

SMEs from the engineering functions of sending and receiving units should meet, ideally face-to-face, to review the proposed transfer after receiving details of the project scope and proposed process. A result of this meeting should include a list of required deliverables and a RACI responsibilities matrix. Matters to consider include:

  • Role of the engineering SMEs in the sending and receiving units:
    • Involvement in planning of items/knowledge to be shared
    • Strength and weaknesses in the necessary technical areas at the receiving unit
    • Identification of skill matrix for required support for the project
    • Future site visits and training needs for both the sending and receiving units
    • Specific areas where collaboration may be required between SMEs from different backgrounds (engineering, scientists, operations specialists)
  • Role of engineering suppliers and vendors:
    • Areas where potential exists to leverage existing vendor/supplier networks from either site
    • Capability analysis of supplier/vendor networks at the receiving unit
    • The need for ongoing supplier/vendor engagement and support
    • The scope and responsibility for preparation of contracts and service agreements
    • The criticality of third party support packages and the potential need for guarantees and incentivization

4. Engineering Equivalence Assessments (Gap Analysis)

The level of existing capabilities for key processes or equipment may not be identical at the sending and receiving units. Even where processing and equipment are considered identical (same make, model of equipment, starting materials, specifications, etc.), these systems may be performing differently. Equivalence assessments involve identification and comparison of aspects including:

  • Design and specifications of equipment and utility services
  • Performance data under similar input and operating conditions
  • Engineering data

5. Ongoing Review and Benchmarking

Such projects also present a valuable benchmarking opportunity. The following activities are useful to undertake and may highlight opportunities to improve the process at both the sending and receiving units:

  • Review of the original information on one or more occasions during the receiving unit's implementation, to identify areas where information was unnecessary and where gaps exist
  • Planning for data sharing and monitoring of performance and engineering metrics, including:
    • Ongoing monitoring and reporting metrics, reporting strategies
    • One-time performance/comparability reporting, Pareto analysis
    • Targeted engineering reviews

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept — Five Techniques for Capturing Engineering Knowledge

ISPE proposes five tools, each with its own time of application:

  • Procurement planning: plan knowledge transfer as early as equipment procurement. This is the lowest-effort moment, because the engineers are still on site.
  • Engineering database review: systematically mine tacit knowledge from the CMMS, calibration records, and configuration management systems
  • RACI matrix: clearly assign roles: who is Responsible, who is Accountable, who is Consulted, and who is Informed
  • Engineering equivalence assessment: even when the model is the same, compare actual performance data; do not assume "same model = same performance"
  • Ongoing benchmarking: continued monitoring after transfer, so that both parties can learn from the comparison

Analogy — The RACI Matrix: The Engineering "Wedding Coordinator"

A large wedding needs a coordinator to make sure everyone does their part: the bride and groom (Accountable), the wedding planner (Responsible for logistics), the parents of both families (Consulted on preferences), and the guests (Informed of the schedule).

The engineering RACI matrix in a technology transfer plays the same role: it clearly defines "who leads the calibration program for this equipment? What must the sending unit be consulted on?" Otherwise, in a cross-border, cross-company transfer, everyone assumes someone else is handling it, and in the end nobody does.

Key Point — What Engineering Equivalence Assessment Really Means

"Engineering equivalence" has different meanings at the regulatory level and the technical level:

  • Technical equivalence: the equipment can perform the same function and achieve the same output within the critical parameter ranges, which can be demonstrated through engineering comparison data
  • Regulatory equivalence: if the equipment type differs (for example, changing from magnetic stirring to mechanical agitation), even if the function is equivalent, additional PPQ batch data, or even a change notification, may be required by regulation

When receiving a new product, the CDMO must complete the equivalence assessment first and then decide whether additional PPQ batches are needed. This decision must be made before PPQ execution, not discussed after problems are discovered during execution.

Practical Application — Agenda for the CDMO Engineering SME Face-to-Face Meeting (Recommended Practice)

Per ISPE recommendations, the first face-to-face meeting of the engineering SMEs should cover:

  1. Process overview: the sending unit presents the critical engineering control points in the process (30 minutes)
  2. Equipment comparison: compare critical equipment item by item and complete a preliminary equivalence scoring (60 minutes)
  3. Known issues inventory: the sending unit openly shares known equipment defects, wear patterns, and recurring deviations (30 minutes)
  4. Automation recipe review: map the critical recipe parameters to the receiving site's control system (45 minutes)
  5. Capability gap identification: the receiving site's engineers describe their own technical strengths and weaknesses (30 minutes)
  6. Draft RACI: jointly draft the responsibility matrix, clarifying delivery responsibility for each type of document (30 minutes)

This meeting should be completed within the first month after the technology transfer program formally starts.

Key Point — Confirm Utility Capacity Before PPQ

The document particularly stresses that the receiving site must confirm utility capacity before technology transfer execution (not during PPQ):

  • WFI: water consumption and flow demand during peak production periods
  • Clean steam: SIP requirements and consumption
  • Compressed air: quality (dew point) and flow
  • Chilled water system: process cooling demand

Shortfalls in these are often discovered only during PPQ execution, but by then it is too late: engineering modifications take weeks, and the window for the PPQ batches may already be taken. Confirming utility capacity is a mandatory item on the engineering readiness checklist.

Section Summary

  • Engineering knowledge is the invisible backbone of technology transfer: process documents can be copied, but equipment wear patterns, instrument drift behavior, and operators' setting techniques are the tacit knowledge that decides whether a transfer succeeds, and capturing it must be planned proactively.
  • Core warnings from the six examples: mechanical wear needs a knowledge feedback mechanism (#1); instrument selection needs procurement history (#2); maintenance priorities need to be compared in advance (#3); cleaning validation requires transferring engineering trial data, not just conclusions (#4); receiving-site capability needs contractual safeguards (#5); and "the equipment did not change" does not mean "the recipe needs no verification" (#6).
  • Automation recipe mapping is a high-risk blind spot: when transferring a process between different control system platforms, item-by-item mapping and verification of recipe parameters is a necessary step; one cannot assume that "the same parameter name means the same setting."
  • Timing of the five tools: procurement-stage planning (earliest involvement) → engineering database review (before transfer) → RACI matrix (immediately after program start) → equivalence assessment (completed before PPQ) → ongoing benchmarking (long term after transfer).
  • CDMO readiness check: utility capacity, automation recipes, equipment service history, cleaning validation engineering data, and maintenance priority comparison: these five items must be completed before PPQ execution; if late, they become batch risk.

Appendix 5 — Example of a Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA) for a Non-Sterile Drug Product

The following table illustrates an FMEA applied to the Tableting and IPC controls process step. Columns are: Item/Process Step | Potential Failure Mode | Potential Failure Effects | SEV (Severity) | Potential Causes | OCC (Occurrence) | Current Controls | DET (Detection) | RPN (Risk Priority Number) | Actions Recommended.

Note: IPC = In-Process Controls; SPC = Statistical Process Controls; RFT = Right First Time. Bullet (•) denotes RPN requiring action.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts — The Three FMEA Scoring Factors

RPN = SEV × OCC × DET (Risk Priority Number = Severity × Occurrence × Detectability)

  • SEV (Severity): The impact of the failure on the patient / product quality. 1 = negligible, 10 = life-threatening.
  • OCC (Occurrence): How often the cause of the failure actually occurs. 1 = very rare, 10 = almost certain to occur.
  • DET (Detectability): The ability of existing controls to "detect" the problem. Note: a lower DET score = easier to detect; a higher DET score = harder to detect.

In this table every DET = 8, indicating poor detectability: IPC is performed only every few hours, so problems cannot be intercepted in real time.

Analogy — Why DET = 8 Is a Warning Sign

Imagine driving on a highway where the speed camera takes a photo only once every 4 hours: almost every speeder gets away with it. An IPC disintegration check performed only every 4 hours is that kind of "barely threatening" detection. A DET score of 8/10 means that even when there is a problem, we are unlikely to catch it in time.

Key Point — RPN Thresholds and Triggering Action

The two RPN = 160 entries marked with "•" in the table require action:

  • Punch breakage, fragments not found (SEV=10): For a non-sterile tablet SEV=10, because tablets may contain metal foreign matter that directly endangers patient safety. Detection relies on a metal detector, but there is still a risk of escape.
  • Black spots on tablet surface (SEV=10): Under the strictest interpretation, a visual defect is rated 10 (it may indicate contamination).

Action is usually required at an RPN of about 100–125 or above. Each company may set its own threshold, but it must be stated explicitly in the FMEA SOP.

Extended Application — How FMEA Severity Differs in Sterile Processes

This example is a non-sterile drug product. Mapped onto a sterile process technology transfer, the SEV of the following failure modes becomes significantly higher:

  • Sterility breach → SEV = 10 (direct risk of infection to the patient)
  • Particulate contamination → SEV = 9–10 (can be fatal for intravenous products)
  • Container closure integrity (CCI) failure → SEV = 9 (sterility cannot be assured through to expiry)
  • Labeling error → SEV = 9 (medication error)

This is why almost all major failure modes in a sterile process FMEA carry SEV of 8–10 and RPNs routinely exceed the threshold, calling for tighter OCC control (lower occurrence) and stronger DET (for example, 100% in-line monitoring).

Formula and Calculation — Strategies to Reduce RPN

RPN = SEV × OCC × DET

Strategy 1: Reduce OCC (improve process stability)
  Example: strengthen the punch maintenance program → OCC: 2 → 1
  RPN: 160 → 80 ✓

Strategy 2: Reduce DET (improve detection capability)
  Example: from IPC every 4 hours → real-time 100% visual inspection
  DET: 8 → 3
  RPN: 160 → 60 ✓

Strategy 3: Do both
  OCC: 2→1, DET: 8→4 → RPN: 160 → 40 ✓✓

Note: SEV is hard to reduce (the impact of a failure is
determined by the nature of the product), so prioritize
reducing OCC and DET.

  1. Why is DET = 8 for every failure mode in this FMEA? If it were changed to 100% automated optical inspection (AOI) of every batch, what would DET drop to, and what would the effect on RPN be?
  2. In a sterile filling technology transfer, is a detection frequency such as "IPC every 4 hours" acceptable? Explain why sterile processes need higher-frequency or continuous monitoring.
  3. OCC = 1 means a very low occurrence, yet the RPN of that failure is still 40. At the TT receiving unit, how would you confirm that the OCC assessment is reasonable (that is, how would you verify that the sending unit's historical data apply to the receiving unit)?

Core Concepts — The Role of the Technology Transfer Report

The Technology Transfer Report is the final deliverable of the whole TT project. It does more than record "what was done": above all it establishes regulatory visibility, demonstrating to regulators that the technology transfer is complete and that the receiving unit's product is comparable to the sending unit's (Comparability).

This report is key evidence in CMC regulatory filings, particularly when:

  • Adding a manufacturing site (site transfer → DMF / MAA must be updated)
  • Scaling up (scale-up → batch-to-batch comparability data must be shown)
  • Transferring analytical methods (method transfer → method transfer validation results are needed)

Analogy — What Is a TT Report Like?

A technology transfer report is like a restaurant's "brand licensing document package". When McDonald's licenses its recipe to a new franchisee, it does not just hand over a recipe; it also supplies the ingredient list, batch sampling records, equipment specifications, cleaning validation and staff training records. The TT report serves to ensure that the receiving unit (the new franchisee) can make the "same" product to the same standard, with documents to prove it to an inspector.

Key Point — The Six Core Modules a Report Must Cover

Although Appendix 6 is presented as a checklist, a complete TT report should contain the following six core narrative modules:

  1. Executive Summary: TT scope, key conclusions, remaining open items
  2. Scope & Team: RACI, roles, milestones
  3. Development Info: formulation history, process understanding, risk assessment
  4. Production Info: batch records, equipment qualification, cleaning validation
  5. PPQ Outcomes: statistical comparison of the process performance qualification batches
  6. Approval Signatures: confirmation by both the sending unit QA and the receiving unit QA

Practical Application — CDMO Scenario: Who Writes the TT Report?

In a CDMO setting, the TT report is usually authored by the receiving unit's (the CDMO's) technical services department, but it needs the client (sending unit) to supply the material for the development information section. A common pain point is that the client declines to provide the full formulation development history on the grounds of "trade secrets", although this information is essential for the receiving unit to understand the critical points of the process.

Solution: agree in advance in the Quality Agreement which information must be provided, and reach consensus between both parties at the TT proposal stage.

The Role of the Ishikawa Diagram in TT

Appendix 6 mentions the Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram as a tool for process knowledge transfer. Its value is that it visually categorizes the factors affecting CQAs (such as dissolution and content uniformity) into materials, equipment, people, environment and methods, so that receiving unit engineers can quickly see "which factors are critical and which are secondary".

In a technology transfer, the sending unit should specifically flag the items "confirmed as significant factors in engineering studies" (shown in bold) so that the receiving unit does not repeat past mistakes.

Core Concepts — Regulatory Anchor Documents for Technology Transfer

Of the 44 references, the most important regulatory anchors fall into three tiers:

Tier 1 — The ICH core trio (must-read):

  • ICH Q10 (Pharmaceutical Quality System): defines TT as one of the core elements of the PQS and requires both the sending and receiving units to have a documented TT procedure.
  • ICH Q8(R2) (Pharmaceutical Development): the QbD framework, which defines QTPP, CQA and CPP and provides the scientific basis for packaging TT knowledge.

Tier 2 — Process validation:

  • FDA Process Validation Guidance (2011): the three-stage validation model (Process Design → PPQ → Continued Verification), which all products for the US market must follow.
  • EMA Process Validation Guideline (2016): the requirement for the EU market; the concepts are similar but the terminology differs slightly (for example, "Concurrent Validation" is accepted differently).

Tier 3 — Analytical method transfer:

  • USP <1224>: procedural guidance for analytical method transfer (comparative testing, covalidation, and waiver of transfer with direct revalidation).
  • ICH Q2(R1): the foundational standard for analytical method validation.

Key Point — ICH Q11 and TT of Drug Substances

ICH Q11 (Development and Manufacture of Drug Substances) is the key document when handling the technology transfer of an API (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient), particularly regarding the definition of starting materials and manufacturing steps for biologics. For a CDMO, when a client needs to transfer an API production line, ICH Q11 determines which information must be disclosed to regulators.

Key Point — ICH Q5E: Comparability of Biologics

ICH Q5E is the dedicated guideline for assessing the comparability of biotechnological/biological products after a process change. When a biologics CDMO must submit comparability data to FDA/EMA after a technology transfer, ICH Q5E defines the levels of analytical, functional and clinical data required. It is a key regulatory document for the TT of sterile injectable biologics.

Analogy — What Regulatory Anchors Do

Regulatory anchor documents are like case law in a courtroom. When an inspector questions your TT approach, you do not defend it by saying "I think this is reasonable"; you point to a specific ICH guideline: "According to ICH Q10 section X.X ... according to section Y of the FDA Process Validation Guidance ...". With these anchors, your TT decisions have regulatory traceability.

Practical Application — CDMO Regulatory Preparation

CDMO technical services teams are advised to build a "regulatory document matrix", with the TT activities (process transfer, method transfer, validation, stability) on the horizontal axis and the corresponding ICH/FDA/EMA reference documents on the vertical axis. Every TT report can then quickly cite the correct regulatory basis, and the team can demonstrate regulatory literacy when responding to FDA 483 observations.

15.2 Definitions

Control Strategy (ICH Q10 [1])
A planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, that assures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control.

Critical Process Parameter (CPP) (ICH Q8(R2) [3])
A process parameter whose variability has an impact on a critical quality attribute and therefore should be monitored or controlled to ensure the process produces desired quality.

Critical Quality Attribute (CQA) (ICH Q8(R2) [3])
A physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property or characteristic that should be within an appropriate limit, range, or distribution to ensure the desired product quality.

Functional Requirement Specification (FRS)
Specification document, which builds on the URS (User Requirement Specification) and provides a basic narrative on what functions the process and its control system are expected to perform.

Lifecycle (ICH Q8(R2) [3])
All phases in the life of a product from the initial development through marketing until the product's discontinuation.

Process Analytical Technology (PAT) (ICH Q8(R2) [3])
A system for designing, analyzing, and controlling manufacturing through timely measurements (i.e., during processing) of critical quality and performance attributes of raw and in-process materials and processes with the goal of ensuring final product quality.

Process Validation
(FDA definition) The collection and evaluation of data, from the process design stage through commercial production, which establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product.
(EMA definition) The documented evidence that the process, operated within established parameters, can perform effectively and reproducibly to produce a medicinal product meeting its predetermined specifications and quality attributes.

Any individual, group, or organization that can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a risk. Decision makers might also be stakeholders. For the purposes of ICH Q9, the primary stakeholders are the patient, healthcare professional, regulatory authority, and industry.

The technology transfer charter captures the strategic intent and defines the overall scope of the technology transfer project. It should document the team members and their roles and responsibilities, explain the effort and time required for the technology transfer project, and identify significant assumptions and risks. The charter helps to ensure that management and team members from both sending and receiving units understand the project and agree upon deliverables.

The technology transfer package is the collection of all (including local and process related) knowledge required to run the process and analyze the product.

The technology transfer plan is based on the technology transfer proposal. It provides a more detailed description of the elements of the project (both technical and project management) that are to be completed by the team to achieve the overall goals.

The technology transfer proposal defines the overall scope of the project, documents the team members and their roles and responsibilities, estimates the effort and time required for the project, establishes high level success criteria, and identifies significant assumptions and risks.

A description of the requirements of the facility in terms of product to be manufactured, required throughput and conditions in which the product should be made.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts — Comparison of the Most Confused Terms

The following five pairs of terms are the ones most often confused in technology transfer practice and must be clarified:

1. Technology Transfer Package vs. Technology Transfer Proposal

The "proposal" is a project scope document: written before the TT begins, it defines the objectives, team, timeline and success criteria. The "package" is a collection of technical knowledge: it contains all the knowledge needed to run the process and analyze the product, it is the substance of the TT, and it is continuously supplemented and refined throughout the TT.

2. Technology Transfer Plan vs. Technology Transfer Charter

The "charter" is a high-level strategic document that ensures the sending and receiving unit management share an understanding of the project scope and objectives. The "plan" is a more detailed execution-level document that lists the specific technical and management tasks.

3. QMS vs. PQS

A QMS (Quality Management System) is a general ISO concept applicable to any industry. A PQS (Pharmaceutical Quality System) is the pharma-specific version defined by ICH Q10, and in particular includes requirements for product lifecycle management and continual improvement.

Key Point — Differences Among the Three Definitions of "Process Validation"

Appendix 8 specifically lists the FDA, EMA and ICH Q8(R2) definitions of Process Validation, and the differences are instructive:

  • FDA (2011): emphasizes "data collection throughout the lifecycle from process design through commercial production". The focus is the systematic accumulation of scientific evidence.
  • EMA: emphasizes "documented evidence that the process, operated within established parameters, can perform effectively and reproducibly to produce a product meeting its specifications". It leans toward written proof of batch reproducibility.
  • ICH Q8(R2): the definition is the same as for QbD, "a systematic approach to development that begins with predefined objectives and emphasizes process understanding and control". This shows that under the QbD framework, validation is not a remedy applied after the fact but is built in from the start of development.

In the TT technical report, it is advisable to cite explicitly the definition that applies to the target market, to avoid misinterpretation by regulators.

Extended Analysis — The Reciprocal Obligations of the Sending Unit and Receiving Unit

The glossary deliberately defines the two units in "mirror-image" terms, both including "core functions and supporting functions". This wording signals a key principle: technology transfer is a two-way responsibility, not a one-way "handover".

Obligation of the sending unit: to provide a complete and accurate technology transfer package of technical knowledge (Technology Transfer Package).

Obligation of the receiving unit: to be capable of receiving, understanding and reproducing that knowledge, and to provide adequate infrastructure and qualified personnel.

In a CDMO contract, if a TT fails, responsibility often depends on which party failed to fulfill its corresponding obligations.

Analogy — What Is a Control Strategy?

A Control Strategy is like an aircraft's "safety system matrix". It is not just one gauge or one alarm; it is a complete control system running from the cockpit instrument panel, autopilot, pilot training and maintenance records to air traffic control communications. In a pharmaceutical process, the Control Strategy covers raw material specifications, equipment conditions, IPC frequency, finished product specifications and monitoring methods, and the system as a whole assures that every batch is safe and effective.

Key Point — The Order of Priority Among Stakeholders

The ICH Q9 definition of Stakeholder explicitly names four main stakeholders: patient > healthcare professional > regulator > industry. This ranking has deep regulatory significance: in any decision (including TT decisions), when the interests of these four parties conflict, the patient's interest must take priority.

In a technology transfer, "accepting slightly reduced efficiency to ensure product quality comparability" is always the right decision, because it is the patient who is ultimately affected.
