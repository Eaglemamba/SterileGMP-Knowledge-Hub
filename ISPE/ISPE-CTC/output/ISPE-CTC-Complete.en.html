Chapter 1 — Introduction

What is a CTC? Why does this guide exist?

Original Text (ISPE 2016)

A Controlled Temperature Chamber is defined as a system, unit, equipment, or room in which the environmental conditions (usually temperature) of a chamber are controlled/maintained/regulated to meet specific user requirements.

The ISPE Good Practice Guide: Controlled Temperature Chamber Mapping and Monitoring expands on the ISPE Concept Paper on Controlled Temperature Chamber Mapping to include topics such as:

  • Commissioning
  • Testing strategies
  • Acceptance criteria
  • Qualification approaches
  • System monitoring
  • Operational issues
  • Periodic review
"Types of Controlled Temperature Chambers considered by this guide range from purchased Commercial Off the Shelf items, such as freezers and incubators, walk-in cold rooms and walk-in freezers, to custom built units, such as warehouses."

This Guide provides industry good practice for the temperature mapping of controlled temperature chambers, development of test acceptance criteria and a risk based approach to practices for periodic review of system performance.

CDMO Commentary

Core definition: controlled temperature chamber (CTC)

Put simply, a CTC is "any space or piece of equipment in which you deliberately hold the temperature within a defined range". It can be a benchtop refrigerator or an entire cold-storage warehouse; if the temperature is controlled, it counts. Keywords: controlled / maintained / regulated.

In a CDMO environment the variety of CTCs is especially wide. You may be managing, all at once: raw material freezers (-20°C), finished product cold rooms (2–8°C), stability chambers (25°C/60%RH) and ambient warehouses (15–25°C). Every one of them is a CTC, and every one needs mapping.

Analogy: a CTC is a constant-temperature safe

Imagine putting expensive medicines into a safe. The safe must not only be locked; the temperature inside must also stay within the specified range at all times. "Temperature mapping" means placing a thermometer in every corner of the safe to confirm that no corner is quietly running too hot or too cold.

Key point: the status of this guide

The ISPE GPG is not a regulation; it is industry good practice. Regulators (EMA, FDA, WHO) do not require you to follow it, but when you tell an auditor "we follow the ISPE GPG", it is a strong defensible statement: it shows you are aligned with industry best practice.

1.1

1.1 Purpose and Objectives

Why was this guide developed?

Original Text (ISPE 2016)

With the increasing complexity of global distribution for medicines requiring controlled temperatures and the recently introduced regulations, the ISPE Good Practice Guide was developed by an industry team in order to provide guidance on good practices for:

  • Definition of requirements (producing a user requirements document)
  • Design
  • Purchasing
  • Commissioning (including temperature mapping)
  • Qualification
  • Maintenance of controlled temperature chambers, warehouses, and refrigerated storage areas

These systems are used to store raw material, work in progress, or finished product, and operate under current Good Manufacturing Practice.

The approach described is consistent with that described in the ISPE Good Practice Guide: Cold Chain Management.

CDMO Commentary

Lifecycle view: from URS to maintenance

This list is the complete lifecycle of a CTC. Note the order: define the requirements (URS) first, then design, then purchase; do not buy the equipment and then think about the specification. This matters especially in a CDMO, where many companies "buy the equipment first and write the documents afterwards", which makes qualification difficult.

CDMO application scenario

At a contract manufacturer you may be storing raw materials and finished products with different temperature conditions for more than five clients at the same time. Each lot has its own storage requirement (set by the client SOP or CoA), and your CTC must be able to demonstrate that it maintained the correct conditions throughout the storage period.

This is why temperature mapping records are among the documents most often requested in CDMO client audits.

Link to cold chain management

This guide is meant to be used together with the ISPE Cold Chain Management GPG. A CTC is a static node of the cold chain: the storage step for product inside warehouses and plants. Transport falls under a different guide, but the principles are the same.

1.2

1.2 Scope

What is IN and OUT of scope

Original Text (ISPE 2016)

This Guide provides guidance on Controlled Temperature Chambers operating under Current Good Manufacturing Practices (CGMPs). Types of Controlled Temperature Chambers considered by this guide include:

  • Purchased "commercial off the shelf" items (such as freezers, refrigerators, and incubators)
  • Warm rooms designed to maintain consistent temperatures
  • Ovens
  • Custom built units, such as controlled room temperature environments (e.g., warehouses, walk-in cold rooms and freezers)

This Guide does not include systems designed for heating/cooling of material such as blast freezers, although some of the concepts described may be useful when commissioning/qualifying these systems.

CDMO Commentary

Why the scope boundary matters

The guide clearly separates "CTCs used for storage" from "process equipment for heating/cooling". This boundary is practical:

  • In scope: refrigerators, freezers, stability chambers, cold rooms, ambient-temperature warehouses
  • Out of scope: blast freezers, sterilization ovens (used for processing)

Note, however, the wording "some concepts may be useful": you can apply the mapping principles to process equipment, but it is outside the main discussion of this guide.

The boundary between cGMP and GDP

This guide focuses on the cGMP environment (storage at the manufacturing site). GDP (Good Distribution Practice) governs transport and distribution once product leaves the plant. The two overlap, but a CDMO's first concern is GMP compliance on site.

Analogy: storage vs process

Keeping ingredients fresh in the refrigerator = a CTC (within the scope of this guide). Heating ingredients in a wok = process equipment (out of scope). This guide covers only the "refrigerator that keeps food fresh", not the "wok used for stir-frying".

1.3 Benefits of using this guide

Why follow this guide?

Original Text (ISPE 2016)

There is an increased regulatory interest in cold chain driven by the growing number of products requiring controlled temperature shipping and storage, the complexity of the distribution network for the products and governmental requirements for distribution of vaccines.

This is reflected in the recent revisions to the related regulatory guidance. Utilizing this Guide provides a number of benefits in an area where there is little authoritative guidance, including:

  • Provision of industry good practice for the temperature mapping of controlled temperature chambers, considering the impact of load, and the use of the data to determine the location and number of monitoring sensors
  • Development of test acceptance criteria
  • A risk based approach to practices for periodic review of system performance

CDMO Commentary

Regulatory trend: global pressure on the cold chain

Biologics (mAbs, vaccines, gene therapies) are a fast-growing share of the global pharmaceutical market, and almost all of them require tight temperature control. Regulators (EMA, WHO, TFDA) have correspondingly increased the intensity of their cold chain inspections.

For a CDMO this means: when your clients (big pharma) audit your facility, CTC mapping records are a must-check item.

Three concrete benefits

  • Benefit 1: A standardized mapping methodology, so you know how many sensors to use and where to place them
  • Benefit 2: A clear framework of acceptance criteria, so you know what "pass" means
  • Benefit 3: A risk-based periodic review frequency, so you do not have to repeat full mapping every year

1.4 Regulatory Guidance

Key regulations and standards governing CTCs

Original Text (ISPE 2016)

Regulatory guidance covering Controlled Temperature Chambers issued or updated at time of publication includes:

  1. HPRA: Guide to Good Distribution Practice of Medicinal Products for Human Use
  2. HPRA: Guide to Control and Monitoring of Storage and Transportation Temperature Conditions for Medicinal Products and Active Substances
  3. EMA Guidelines (7 March 2013) on Good Distribution Practice of Medicinal Products for Human Use
  4. PIC/S Guide PE 011-1 to Good Distribution Practice for Medicinal Products

Also see:

  1. European Commission Guidelines (5 November 2013) on Good Distribution Practice
  2. Health Canada GUI-0069: Guidelines for Temperature Control of Drug Products during Storage and Transportation
  3. WHO Technical Report Series No. 961, Annex 9: Model Guidance for the Storage and Transport of Time- and Temperature-Sensitive Pharmaceutical Products
  4. MCA Part II, Chapter 6: Control and Monitoring of Storage and Transit Temperatures

In the United States Pharmacopeia (USP):

  1. USP <659> Packaging and Storage Requirements

Regulatory analysis and CDMO application

Regulatory hierarchy

These regulations fall into three levels:

  • Mandatory regulations: EU GDP Guidelines (EMA/European Commission); compliance is required to sell product on the EU market
  • Quasi-mandatory guidance: WHO TRS 961, the regulatory basis for WHO member states; TFDA often uses it as a reference
  • Product specification standards: USP <659>, which defines the official temperature ranges for terms such as "refrigerator", "freezer" and "room temperature"

Key requirements of WHO TRS 961 Annex 9

WHO TRS 961 Annex 9 provides the most complete guidance on the storage and transport of time- and temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical products (TTSPPs), including:

  • Minimum requirements for temperature mapping (number of sensors, duration)
  • Procedures for handling temperature deviations
  • Application of risk assessment
  • Triggers for requalification

For a CDMO exporting to emerging markets (Africa, Southeast Asia), the WHO guidance is often the basis of the local NRA's inspections.

USP <659> temperature definitions

USP <659> is the legal basis for the "storage conditions" field on a client's CoA:

  • Freezer: -25°C to -10°C
  • Refrigerator: 2°C to 8°C
  • Cool: 8°C to 15°C
  • Controlled Room Temperature: 20°C to 25°C

When a client says "Store at 2–8°C", this is the USP standard, and your CTC must demonstrate that it can maintain this range.

1.5

1.5 Key Concepts / Key Terms

Commissioning, Qualification, and Rounding Rules

Original Text (ISPE 2016)

Commissioning

A well planned, documented, and managed engineering approach to the start-up and turnover of facilities, systems, and equipment to the End-User, that results in a safe and functional environment that meets established design requirements and stakeholder expectations.

Qualification

Activities undertaken to demonstrate that utilities and equipment are suitable for their intended use and perform properly. (FDA)

Rounding of Temperature Values

This Guide follows USP <32> Section 7.20 Rounding Rules:

"When rounding is required, consider only one digit in the decimal place to the right of the last place in the limit expression. If this digit is smaller than 5, it is eliminated and the preceding digit is unchanged. If this digit is equal to or greater than 5, it is eliminated and the preceding digit is increased by 1."

In-depth look at the terminology

Commissioning vs qualification: still confusing them?

These two terms are often used interchangeably on site, but the ISPE definitions are very clear:

  • Commissioning: engineering-oriented; ensures the equipment "works" and meets the design specification. The focus: Does it work as designed?
  • Qualification: regulation-oriented; demonstrates that the equipment is "fit for its intended use". The focus: Is it fit for GMP use?

Commissioning comes first and qualification second. Commissioning documentation can support qualification but cannot replace it.

Analogy: commissioning vs qualification

The factory test before a new car is delivered = commissioning (confirming that the engine runs and the brakes work).

The road test at driving school = qualification (demonstrating that you can drive safely under defined conditions).

You can pass the factory test but fail the road test (the equipment works but does not meet GMP requirements); you can also pass the road test but be unable to repair the car (GMP-compliant but poorly maintained day to day).

Practical meaning of the rounding rule

This seemingly minor rule matters a great deal in practice. For example:

  • Upper limit 8°C, measured 8.4°C → rounded = 8°C → Pass
  • Upper limit 8°C, measured 8.5°C → rounded = 9°C → Fail

When setting alarm limits and qualification acceptance criteria, this rule determines how you judge "borderline values"; you cannot round arbitrarily.

Chapter 2 — Related Standards

International standards applicable to controlled temperature chambers

Original Text (ISPE 2016) — Section 2 & 2.1

Regulatory guidance for Good Distribution Practice (GDP) is updated on a regular basis. There is normally a requirement to demonstrate that product is being stored in the appropriate conditions. This can be accomplished through the mapping that all storage locations used in the Controlled Temperature Chamber remain within the defined conditions.

2.1 Standards

There are no Standards written specifically for Controlled Temperature Chambers as used in the Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology industry. Available Standards are written for Environmental Test Chambers and Stability Chambers. Although the same principles may be applied, the details of how the systems are to be used in an operational environment should also be considered.

A list of applicable Standards known at time of publication:

  • JTM K 03 — Environmental Temperature and Humidity Rooms – Test and Indication Method for Performance
  • JTM K 05 — High Temperature Chambers – Test and Indication Method for Performance

France:

  • FD X15-140 — Measurement of Air Moisture – Climatic and Thermostatic Chambers – Characterization and Verification

  • DIN 12880 — Electrical Laboratory Devices – Heating Ovens and Incubators

Australia:

  • AS 2853 — Enclosures – Temperature Controlled Performance Testing and Grading

IEC:

  • IEC 60068 — Environmental Testing Series

Controlled Temperature Chambers used in the Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology industry are usually used for either long term storage (stability) or for short term storage Work in Progress (WIP)/distribution warehousing storage.

Note: China (PRC) and South Korea use Good Supply Practice (GSP) rather than GDP.

Analysis and application of the standards

Key insight: there is no standard written specifically for pharmaceutical CTCs

ISPE openly acknowledges an important fact here: no international standard has been written specifically for pharmaceutical CTCs. The existing standards were written for "environmental test chambers" or "stability chambers", and the pharmaceutical industry simply "borrows" their principles.

This is precisely why the ISPE GPG exists: it fills the gap between regulatory requirements and industrial standards.

Analogy: borrowing a standard is like wearing someone else's uniform

IEC 60068 was designed for electronics factories; a pharmaceutical plant "borrowing" it is like wearing a slightly ill-fitting uniform. The principles can be used, but details (such as sensor positions and typical load conditions) must be adapted to the actual use case in the pharmaceutical facility.

This is why the ISPE GPG stresses: "although the same principles may be applied, the details of how the systems are to be used in an operational environment should also be considered."

Two main usage modes

At the end of the chapter, ISPE points out two core usage scenarios for CTCs:

  • Long-term storage (stability): requires high stability and tight temperature and humidity control, with the most stringent mapping requirements (ICH Q1A conditions: 25°C/60%RH, 40°C/75%RH, etc.)
  • Short-term storage (WIP / warehousing): high throughput, frequent door openings and widely varying loads, so mapping must consider the worst case (worst-case loading)

Different usage modes call for different mapping strategies; this is the core theme of the following chapters.

GSP vs GDP: differences in the Asia-Pacific market

A footnote in the guide points out that China and Korea use GSP (Good Supply Practice) rather than GDP. For a Taiwanese CDMO exporting products to China or Korea, confirm whether the client has GSP-specific requirements. Taiwan's TFDA itself uses PIC/S as its benchmark, which is closely aligned with EU GDP.

Chapter 3 — User Requirements Specification

Write the URS before you buy — not after

The URS is the contract between what the business needs and what the equipment must deliver — write it before you buy, not after.

The URS is the contract between the business need and the delivered equipment: write it before purchase, not after the fact.

Original Text (ISPE 2016) — Chapter 3

A User Requirements Specification (URS) is a document that defines the user's functional requirements for equipment or systems. By defining specific requirements in the URS, and not how those requirements are met, the designer is given freedom to provide the optimum design solution to meet the requirements.

Aspects to be considered in relation to stored material include:

  • Unit capacity (i.e., quantity and nature of the material to be stored)
  • Whether the unit is providing cooling or heating (i.e., preconditioning) of the stored material, and if so:
    • What criteria have to be met, e.g., maximum load size, specific heat capacity supply, temperature, heating/cooling time?
    • How long and how frequently is the door to load/unload the unit open?
    • Which environmental conditions are critical, e.g., light/temperature/humidity?

Qualification is intended to demonstrate through testing (verification) that the requirements specified in the URS have been met. Confirmation that the user requirements have been met should be traceable in the qualification documentation.

Appendix 1 provides discussion of typical content and an example of a URS for a cold room.

"Note: information included in specifications or the purchase order general requirements (such as manuals, training, spare parts) are not included in the URS to avoid duplication; the URS should be a simple concise definition of the user requirements."

For a standard "catalogue" item, the URS may be developed in the form of the purchase order.

URS in depth | CDMO application

What is a URS? Why is it so important?

The URS is the starting document for the entire equipment lifecycle. It answers one core question: "What does this equipment need to do?"

Note the subtle but important ISPE wording: the URS defines what is needed, not how it is achieved. This gives the supplier design freedom while ensuring that the final delivery meets the user's real needs.

Analogy: a URS is like a brief to an architect

You commission an architect to build a house and say, "I need a house for 4 people, with two bathrooms, where the indoor temperature does not exceed 26°C in summer, with a budget of NT$5 million"; this is the spirit of a URS.

You do not say "use this brand of brick" or "put the air conditioner in this position"; that is the job of the Design Specification (DS) and the Installation Specification (IS).

URS → qualification traceability chain

ISPE stresses that qualification documents must traceably demonstrate that every URS requirement has been met. This means that every test item in your OQ/PQ protocols should have a corresponding URS number.

If your qualification report cannot show a corresponding test for a URS requirement, that is the source of an audit finding (observation).

Special handling for small equipment

For standard catalog items (such as a small laboratory refrigerator), ISPE allows the URS to take the form of a purchase order; that is, the technical specifications on the purchase order are the URS. For customized equipment such as walk-in freezers and large warehouses, a separate formal URS document is still required.

Chapter 4 — Types of Controlled Temperature Chamber

Overview: how CTC type drives qualification strategy

Original Text (ISPE 2016) — Chapter 4 Overview

The ISPE Good Practice Guide recognizes the following main types of Controlled Temperature Chambers used in pharmaceutical and biotechnology manufacturing and storage:

  • 4.1 Freezers — Ultra-low (-80°C), standard (-20°C), and other sub-zero temperature units. Used for long-term storage of biological materials, vaccines, and temperature-sensitive APIs.
  • 4.2 Refrigerators — Units maintaining 2–8°C. The most common CTC for finished pharmaceutical products, including biologics, vaccines, and drug substances.
  • 4.3 Cold Rooms (Walk-in) — Large-scale refrigerated spaces, typically 2–8°C, designed for bulk storage of materials with high throughput and frequent access.
  • 4.4 Stability Chambers — Chambers designed to maintain precise temperature and humidity conditions for ICH Q1A stability testing. Typical conditions: 25°C/60%RH (long-term), 40°C/75%RH (accelerated), 30°C/65%RH (intermediate).
  • Incubators / Incubator Shakers — Units for microbiological and cell culture applications, typically 30–37°C.
  • Controlled Temperature Rooms — Large spaces maintained at controlled room temperature (typically 15–25°C per USP) for ambient storage of products not requiring refrigeration.

The type of CTC determines the applicable testing strategy, the number and location of mapping sensors, the qualification approach, and the frequency of periodic re-qualification.

CDMO perspective: the strategic significance of chamber type

Why does chamber type determine the qualification strategy?

Different types of CTC differ fundamentally:

  • Volume: from a benchtop laboratory refrigerator to a warehouse of thousands of square meters, the sensor requirements are completely different
  • Usage pattern: a stability chamber is almost never opened; a walk-in cold room is entered dozens of times a day
  • Temperature range: the mapping challenges of a -80°C ultra-low freezer and a 25°C ambient warehouse are entirely different
  • Regulatory requirements: stability chambers must meet ICH Q1A; warehouses must meet GDP

A special CDMO scenario: the complexity of multi-client storage

At a contract manufacturer, a single cold storage area may hold at the same time:

  • Client A's drug substance (requires 2–8°C, held after incoming inspection)
  • Client B's intermediates (require 2–8°C, awaiting QC release)
  • Client C's finished products (require 2–8°C, awaiting shipment)

These three types of material may sit in the same cold room, but there must be clear physical or visual segregation. Mapping must demonstrate that the entire space, including each client's storage area, stays between 2 and 8°C.

CDMO incoming inspection staging area

A frequently overlooked CTC is the incoming inspection staging area. When raw materials that require refrigeration arrive at the site, they must be held in an area that meets the storage conditions while waiting for QC sampling and release.

This staging area is often a separate small refrigerator or a segregated section of a cold room. It is also a CTC and also needs mapping and qualification.

4.1

Freezers

-20°C to -196°C storage — design, defrost cycles, and GMP monitoring requirements

Original Text (ISPE 2016, pp.16–18)

4.1 Freezers

Freezers are used for short to long term preservation of pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical materials, including: Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs), process intermediates, stability samples, laboratory materials, and cell banks. Temperature uniformity is maintained within a qualified range to ensure product quality is preserved. Freezers with set points ranging from -20°C to -80°C (-4°F to -112°F) are frequently used.

Operation can be controlled via a temperature controller that maintains the locally configured temperature set point and also provides a local alarm. Manual defrost chambers are usually equipped with the general freezer refrigeration components of: compressor, receiver, condenser, evaporator, tubing, insulation, and structural support.

The refrigerant compressors are usually hermetically sealed for integrity. The evaporator is typically a tube and fin design. The chamber control monitors interior temperature using a permanently installed thermocouple or Resistance Temperature Detector (RTD) — referred to as the control probe. Typically, the control probe is calibrated at the factory based on the temperature at the geometric center of the chamber. The evaporator is usually located within the upper portion of the chamber interior and chamber temperature uniformity is obtained through natural air circulation.

For units with automatic defrost, the control actuates an automatic defrost cycle at a predetermined frequency (typically 6 to 24 hours) to minimize ice accumulation. The defrost cycle is terminated by: elapsed time, evaporator coil temperature set point, or a combination of time and temperature. The frequency of defrost is adjustable to allow for conditions of use (frequency of door openings, length of time for door openings, and exterior air humidity level).

For GMP applications these systems should be monitored by an independent system. It can be difficult to calibrate the system control sensor, and the use of an independent system allows routine calibration, and the flexibility to place the sensor in a location that measures the "worst case" temperature. This system is used as the quality "system of record" to provide the data used to demonstrate that materials are being stored at the specified conditions.

4.1.1 Conditioning / Blast Freezers

Facilities may process large quantities of bulk product that needs to be frozen from ambient conditions to a specified temperature, e.g., -70°C, prior to transfer into -70°C storage locations. Dedicated conditioning freezers are needed to perform this function, as storage freezers are not capable of handling the large volume of product and significant temperature disparity without potentially compromising the temperature of stored material. This type of equipment is outside the scope of this guide.

4.1.2 Liquid Nitrogen Freezers

Used for storage of material below -120°C (-184°F), e.g., cell banks. If materials are immersed into liquid nitrogen, they eventually assume the temperature of the liquid (-196°C). Materials stored in the vapor above the liquid become warmer as they are moved away from the liquid (up to -120°C). The temperature of the vapor can be changed by varying the amount of liquid nitrogen in the chamber.

Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

Analogy: Control Probe vs. Independent Monitoring System

The control probe built into the freezer by the manufacturer is like the oil temperature gauge on a car dashboard: it keeps the engine running, but it cannot be used for regulatory reporting. The independent monitoring system is your quality record system, comparable to a dashcam, providing continuous, auditable data.

ISPE explicitly requires that GMP applications have an independent monitoring system as the "system of record". The control probe only maintains the set point; the independent system is what is used for compliance reporting.

Multi-Client CDMO Scenario: Why Independent Freezer Monitoring Matters

In a multi-client CDMO environment, the same -80°C freezer may hold API samples from three different clients at the same time. The data from the independent monitoring system must be able to:

  • Trace every door-opening event by timestamp
  • Show whether temperature fluctuations during defrost cycles affected the stored material
  • Provide client-specific deviation records (reports must be separable by client)
  • Support batch release decisions with evidence of temperature compliance

The sensors of the independent system should be placed at the "worst-case" location (the corner most likely to exceed limits), not at the geometric center.

Impact of Defrost Cycles on GMP

During a defrost cycle (every 6-24 hours), the evaporator temperature rises briefly to melt accumulated frost. The monitoring system must:

  • Be able to distinguish a short temperature rise caused by defrost from a genuine equipment failure
  • Confirm that product temperature stays within the acceptable range during defrost
  • Record the actual effect of the defrost cycle on chamber temperature during qualification

During qualification, temperature mapping must be performed across a defrost cycle to record its effect.

4.2

Refrigerators

2–8°C storage — forced air vs. convection, wire shelving, auto-defrost

Original Text (ISPE 2016, p.17)

4.2 Refrigerators

Refrigerators are typically used to store material and vaccines within a qualified range of 2°C to 8°C (36°F to 46°F). The capability of the equipment to achieve temperature uniformity should be considered when assigning the chamber set point.

Operation is typically controlled via a temperature controller that is used to maintain the locally configured temperature set point, and may also provide a local alarm. Refrigerators may be equipped with fans which cool via forced air circulation and utilize a hermetically sealed compressor and refrigeration system. Alternatively, refrigerators may use a heat exchanger located in the storage space, relying on convection to maintain an even temperature.

Refrigerators should have wire shelving to improve air circulation.

Refrigerators will usually automatically defrost the evaporator to maintain system performance. Evaporator defrost occurs by re-directing the hot refrigerant gas into the evaporator coil for a short time to melt any ice/frost accumulation.

Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

Key Design Points for Refrigerators

Set point vs. qualified range: ISPE cautions that the choice of set point must take into account the equipment's ability to achieve temperature uniformity. If the corner temperature reaches 8.5°C with a 4°C set point, the qualified range may not cover the 2-8°C specification, and the set point needs to be adjusted.

Forced air vs. convection: Forced-air units with fans generally give better temperature uniformity, but cold air is lost faster when the door is opened. Convection units are gentler but more sensitive to how goods are loaded.

Shelving requirements: ISPE explicitly calls for wire (mesh) shelving rather than solid shelves, so that air can circulate between goods and local hot spots are avoided.

Analogy: Set Point ≠ Qualified Range

The set point is the instruction you give the refrigerator to "hold this temperature"; the qualified range is the temperature band that you prove, using mapping data, is actually achieved throughout the chamber. The gap between the two is the "buffer" you must leave when choosing the set point. If mapping shows a deviation of ±1.5°C, the set point should be 4-5°C, not right up against the 8°C upper limit.

Multi-Product CDMO Scenario: Zoning Within the Refrigerator

A common problem at multi-client CDMOs is that different clients' vaccines, APIs or intermediates share one refrigerator. Recommendations:

  • Assign shelf zones by client/product and label them for identification
  • During qualification, simulate the actual maximum load (worst-case loading)
  • Solid shelves go against ISPE recommendations and will be raised in audits
  • Before adding any new client's product, confirm that the placement will not block airflow

  2. A client asks for solid metal shelves to store their product (out of concern about particulate fallout). How would you respond, and how would you find a solution that meets both parties' needs?
  3. How should the mapping strategy differ between a forced-air refrigerator and a convection refrigerator?

Cold Rooms (Walk-In)

Walk-in cold rooms — PLC control, redundant condensing, multi-sensor configurations

Original Text (ISPE 2016, pp.17–18)

4.3 Cold Rooms

Cold rooms are typically used for storage of material within a qualified range of 2°C to 8°C (36°F to 46°F). Cold rooms should be equipped with a refrigeration compressor/condensing unit. The air-cooled condensing unit is usually located outside, e.g., on the roof of the building. Cold rooms may be provided with redundant condensing systems, each capable of handling the total system load.

Walk-in cold rooms may be prefabricated, metal-clad structures, furnished and installed as self-contained units. These may include all essential refrigeration equipment, controls, air circulation, and other equipment necessary to reach the specified environmental conditions.

Cold room control systems usually consist of a local Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) to monitor and control the temperature within the cold room. Within the main chamber, the temperature may be measured by temperature sensors placed in different locations. One sensor should be routed to the main controller for controlling interior temperature. The second sensor should be connected to a monitoring system for temperature trending and alarm; for larger units there may be multiple monitoring sensors.

Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

PLC Control and CSV Requirements

A cold room PLC is a computerized system and, in a GMP environment, may trigger computerized system validation (CSV) requirements under 21 CFR Part 11 or Annex 11. Key considerations:

  • PLC software version control must be brought under change control
  • Access rights management (who can change the set point)
  • Audit trail: record every setting change
  • Data integrity: can the data recorded by the PLC be tampered with?

If the PLC handles GMP-critical data, even a cold room requires IQ/OQ qualification and a CSV assessment.

GMP Significance of Redundant Condensing Units

Fitting a cold room with redundant condensing units (each able to carry the full load) is best practice for high-risk stored materials. The following must be tested during commissioning:

  • Time taken to switch from the primary unit to the standby unit
  • Range of chamber temperature fluctuation during the changeover
  • Stability of the standby unit during prolonged operation (not just a brief start-up test)

These data likewise need to be generated during qualification so that they form a formal quality record.

Discussion Questions

  1. What potential impact does a roof-mounted condensing unit have on cold room performance in hot summer conditions? How can the worst case be simulated during commissioning?
  2. If the cold room PLC software is upgraded, does the room need to be requalified? On what basis do you decide?
  3. How many monitoring sensors should a 200 m³ walk-in cold room have? What factors determine the number of sensors?

Stability Chambers & Incubators

ICH conditions 25/30/40°C — humidity, CO₂, multi-parameter control

Original Text (ISPE 2016, p.18)

4.4 Stability Chambers, Incubators / Incubator Shakers

Stability chambers are chambers that are designed to maintain a stable environment for incubating materials or holding samples. These may include cultures for microorganisms or cell culture and hybridization in production or Quality Control laboratories. The incubator may accommodate cell culture vessels from small tissue culture flasks to roller bottles and small spinner flasks.

A local controller should control system operation for process parameters such as temperature, humidity, and CO₂. Incubator shakers can also control shaker speed (Revolutions Per Minute (RPM) of the drive).

Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

Qualification Complexity of Multi-Parameter Control

Compared with freezers and refrigerators, stability chambers and incubators involve several controlled parameters:

  • Temperature: uniformity within ±2°C is typically required
  • Humidity: uniformity within ±5% RH is required (also a challenge for the equipment)
  • CO₂ concentration: specific to incubators; affects the pH of cell cultures
  • Shaking speed (RPM): an additional parameter of shaking incubators

Each parameter needs its own sensor, calibration records and qualification tests. The qualification protocols (IQ/OQ/PQ) are far more complex than for simple storage equipment.

Analogy: A Stability Chamber Is an "Artificial Climate Room"

A stability chamber is essentially a precision artificial climate room that must simulate the climate conditions of different regions of the world. A CDMO in Taiwan that wants to serve the global market usually needs a bank of stability chambers covering all ICH conditions. Once any condition drifts out of limits, the whole set of stability data may have to be regarded as invalid, with a major impact on the client's filing.

  3. Which ICH stability conditions are needed for new drug applications in the Taiwan market? How does this affect the purchase specification of stability chambers?

5.1

Scope of Commissioning

Standard catalogue systems vs. site-built systems — documentation expectations

Original Text (ISPE 2016, pp.19–20)

5.1 Scope of the Commissioning

The commissioning process should be a well-planned, documented, and managed engineering approach to the start-up and turnover of both the system and the equipment to the End User. Commissioning should result in a safe and functional environment that meets established design requirements and stakeholder expectations (see also the ISPE Baseline® Guide: Commissioning and Qualification). The complexity of commissioning can vary, depending on the type of system.

If a risk-based approach is being used, the commissioning documentation may be used to provide evidence of the installation and operation. The commissioning documentation needs to be of an acceptable standard. Typical expectations for the commissioning documentation are provided (see Section 6).

5.1.1 Standard "Catalogue" Systems

Standard "Catalogue" Systems are comprised of mechanical components assembled and/or produced by a manufacturer for general industry applications. There is a limited standard menu of options with respect to hardware and/or software. A manufacturer normally provides a standard operations manual that applies to the operation and maintenance of the system. Standard "Catalogue" Systems are usually set to work following the manufacturer's instructions. Examples include freestanding freezers and refrigerators.

Commissioning is usually simpler for standard catalogue equipment, as the control system is an integrated part of the standard catalogue system, with data on all the components contained within the supplier documentation. A typical commissioning approach could follow the steps listed below:

  • Recording of system data
  • Verification of correct installation (installation verification)
  • Confirmation of satisfactory operation / temperature mapping (operational verification)

Generally, Standard "Catalogue" Systems have been set up in a factory by the manufacturer; typically, the scope of work does not include mapping — this is usually added to the vendor's requirements.

5.1.2 Site (Custom) Built Systems

Site (Custom) Built Systems normally require additional verification to ensure that operation is as specified, usually with more adjustment (set up and tuning) of the refrigeration and associated control systems, in order to ensure that performance meets the user requirements and design specifications.

Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

Core Definition of Commissioning

Commissioning is an engineering verification activity carried out before, or in parallel with, formal installation qualification (IQ). Its purpose is to confirm that the equipment is safe, functions correctly and meets the design requirements, and to complete the handover to the end user.

ISPE stresses that commissioning is the "bridge" linking completed installation to the start of formal qualification. Without sound commissioning, the foundation of the qualification activities is not solid.

Analogy: Handover Inspection of a New Home

Commissioning is like the inspection before you take over a new home: the builder walks you through every room to confirm that the lights work, the taps run and the doors and windows close properly. It is not the government's building inspection (qualification), but it is the engineering prerequisite for handover. If the home fails the inspection, the builder cannot hand it over; if commissioning is not passed, GMP qualification cannot begin.

Commissioning Documentation under a Risk-Based Approach

ISPE allows commissioning documentation to be used on a risk basis to support qualification activities (the "leveraging" or "bridging" strategy). This means:

  • Vendor-supplied FAT data can serve as part of the evidence for IQ
  • Functional tests performed during commissioning can support the design of OQ
  • However, the documentation must meet GMP-acceptable standards (traceable, verifiable and compliant with data integrity requirements)
  • A formal document review and approval process is required; an engineering report alone is not enough

Commissioning Strategy

Commissioning Plan objectives, roles, and acceptance criteria definition

Original Text (ISPE 2016, pp.20–22)

5.2.1 Execution Process

The testing strategy and the acceptance criteria should be documented in a Commissioning Plan prior to test execution. The objective is to:

  • Show that the work will meet Owner and regulatory agency expectations
  • Provide clear and early definition of deliverables and handover requirements
  • Define roles and responsibilities
  • Define the Engineering Change Management System
  • Define the approach to be used for training of the maintenance department and End Users
  • Ensure consistency of approach and testing across the project
  • Ensure full transparency on progress metrics (test execution and productivity)
  • Scope the testing required
  • Provide review and approval of test method statements
  • Provide progress reporting
  • Provide reporting and handling of test discrepancies
  • Define the location and marking (identification) of mapping points
  • Define the acceptance criteria / expected results
  • Witnessing requirements and notification procedures
  • Handling/retention of raw data
  • Format and content of the commissioning report
  • Defined responsibilities for operating and maintaining a facility during commissioning
  • Identify who is responsible for maintaining the operating log from start-up to handover
  • Define how handover is to be managed

The Commissioning Plan should also define how safety is considered. When executing the plan, Commissioning and Qualification may be performed in two distinct phases, or the phases can be combined.

Note: acceptance criteria are "non-negotiable" test results; expected results may be acceptable across a broader range, or are results taken for reference purposes.

5.2.2 Roles and Coordination

Typical roles and responsibilities of parties involved include:

  • Commissioning Lead — responsible for coordinating and managing commissioning activities and allocating resources; writes and/or executes the activity; responsible for accuracy of content
  • System Owner — reviews content; by signing the Plan and Test Report, agrees to the content; has decision making authority and is accountable for compliance to the URS; during construction, determines acceptance of the "as built" system at mechanical completion
  • Commissioning Team Member — involved in execution, documentation, and expertise (site activities: verification, safety analysis, protocol execution, punch items, maintenance training; office activities: document preparation, red-line updating, archiving)
  • Engineering/Technical Services and Validation Group — on distribution list; may review as SMEs but do not take direct action on commissioning documents
Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

Core Function of the Commissioning Plan

The Commissioning Plan is the "constitution" of commissioning: it must be completed and approved before any testing is executed. Its existence ensures that:

  • All parties agree in advance on the test scope and acceptance criteria
  • No one can "backfill" criteria after testing (preventing data manipulation)
  • The handling of test discrepancies is defined in advance
  • The format for data retention and handover is clearly specified

Role Mapping in a CDMO

In a CDMO environment, the commissioning roles are usually filled by the following departments:

  • Commissioning Lead: head of engineering/facilities or the project manager
  • System Owner: representative of QA or production (accountable for regulatory compliance)
  • Commissioning Team Member: engineers, instrumentation and control technicians, vendor representatives
  • Validation Group: responsible for the qualification protocols; may review commissioning documents as a basis for IQ

The System Owner's role is particularly important: by signing the commissioning plan, he or she formally accepts accountability for compliance with the URS.

  2. What is the significance of the System Owner "accepting the as-built system" during commissioning for the subsequent IQ stage of qualification? Can it be omitted?
  3. If a test fails during commissioning and the technician simply corrects the problem and retests without updating the commissioning plan, what issues would this raise in an audit?

5.3

Commissioning at the Design Phase

Design Review, FMEA risk assessment, URS verification, utility interactions

Original Text (ISPE 2016, pp.22–23)

5.3.1 The Design Review

From the commissioning perspective, the Design Review is intended for the commissioning team to become familiar with the project and to verify that the design has adequate provision for the tests required, as defined in the equipment specification and URS.

Requirements in the URS should be clarified with attendees. Key design documents should then be reviewed. Issues relevant to continuous operation of the Chamber are critical aspects to consider and include:

  • Chamber loading scenarios
  • Loading and unloading of products
  • External/adjacent environmental conditions

Typical documents for verification include:

  • Layout and HVAC zoning layout
  • Room environmental parameter schedule
  • Piping and Instrumentation Diagram (P&ID)
  • Functional specifications

During the Design Review phase, documents relevant to the areas surrounding the chamber and to the utilities serving it (e.g., hot water, chilled/glycol water, electricity) should be included in the review in order to properly understand the interaction between the Controlled Temperature Chamber and the rest of the facility.

The Commissioning Team should define the list and sequence of at-site activities and verifications needed, and complete the Commissioning Plan. A tailored approach is usually required as the sequence and activities are dependent upon the specific site conditions, utilities available, operating conditions and type of Controlled Temperature Chamber.

In this phase, the Commissioning Team should be given opportunity to provide suggestions to the designer, in order to obtain a design that will facilitate Commissioning Tests and activities. Suggestions may include availability of access to regulation points, provision of testing points in an appropriate location, or access ports to the room for installation of cabling, if required during temperature mapping.

5.3.2 Design Risk Assessment

A risk assessment may be used to help critique and develop a robust design. A Failure Mode Effects Analysis (FMEA) risk assessment can be used to identify possible failures that could lead to the loss of the required performance, identified by review of design and vendor documentation.

Risks should be evaluated as to their probability and severity, in order to determine the impact to process variation and effect.

Unacceptable risks should be mitigated following the preferred hierarchy of:

  • Elimination by design
  • Elimination by automated control
  • Elimination by procedural controls

The risk management requirements identified should be designated as critical and include all components, functions, and features of the design that serve to control risks. These functions and features should be tested during commissioning.

Analysis and CDMO Application Scenarios

Role of the Commissioning Team in Design Review

The purpose of the commissioning team's participation in design review is not to approve the design, but to ensure the design is "testable". Common commissioning testability issues include:

  • No adequate conduit is provided for sensor wiring, so cables cannot be routed during mapping
  • The cold room walls have no pre-installed penetrations for cables, and ad hoc openings affect airtightness testing
  • The compressor unit has no pressure test connection points, which makes preventive maintenance difficult
  • The control valve has no bypass line, so maintenance requires a shutdown

Raising these issues at the design stage costs far less than making changes after construction.

Core Role of the P&ID in CTC Commissioning

The piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID) is one of the most important design documents for commissioning. It records:

  • The location and identification number of all piping, valves and sensors
  • The connection logic of the instrumentation and control system
  • The set values of pressure/temperature safety devices
  • The supply points of utilities (chilled water, electrical power, compressed air)

After commissioning is complete, the P&ID must be updated to a "red-line version" (as-built) that reflects all changes made during construction. This document ultimately becomes the foundation for facility lifecycle management.

5.4

Commissioning During Installation

Physical inspection, safety checks, setting to work, functional testing, red-line drawings, FAT/SAT

Original Text (ISPE 2016, pp.23–26)

5.4 Commissioning Activities during the Installation Phase

Commissioning activities may be divided into four broad categories:

  1. Inspection of the physical installation — field verification of installation drawings and documentation/test verification (e.g., pipework pressure and evacuation tests) to ensure compliance with design specifications
  2. Pre-operational safety checks
  3. Setting to Work — setting a static system into motion ("shake down") and regulation and adjustment
  4. Functional testing — regulation, adjustment, and functional testing may be iterative activities within fine tuning of the system

5.4.1 Inspection of the Physical Installation

The Commissioning Team scope should include confirmation that:

  • The equipment and materials are checked to ensure compliance with design data sheet and specifications
  • Installation has been completed in accordance with the design specifications and manufacturer instructions
  • As-built documentation is available and correct

Usually, 100% of the installation is verified (packaged units such as the compressor would be considered as a single system component).

It is usually a duty of the Commissioning Team to maintain a "red line" copy of the construction record documents. Comments and changes should be marked on the document following a defined procedure, to be turned over to the responsible party for updating the record documents to be provided to the System Owner.

Some of these activities may be performed at the vendor's factory and documented within specific Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) protocols/reports. Completion of this stage should verify that the installation meets specifications.

5.4.2 Pre-operational Safety Checks

This phase is intended to eliminate as many risks as possible in regard to the safety of personnel during commissioning and also during operation. The "Lock Out/Tag Out" procedure can determine that utilities (chilled water/gas/steam and electrical power) are provided to the system once the following are available and documented:

  • Conformity of electrical system and correct settings (e.g., overloads/fuse ratings)
  • Evidence of pressure test of utilities lines
  • Presence of pressure/temperature safety release devices (with right settings)

5.4.3 Setting to Work

This is the initial startup and tuning of the system. This may include:

  • Energizing the system
  • Filling with fluids
  • Check motor drives (mechanically, electrically) and direction of rotation checks
  • Internal layout of the Chamber verification and as-built dimension measurements
  • Chamber and doors tightness verification
  • Input/Output (I/O) check of the control system
  • Alarms check
  • Calibration of the instruments
  • Balancing and fine tuning

5.4.4 Functional Testing

Checks performed in previous phases should have been executed successfully as a prerequisite to functional tests. The system should be tested to confirm that the design performance is achieved within the acceptance ranges specified in all working modes.

Where feasible, the worst case conditions for the surrounding environment should be simulated during testing. Where the area being tested has duty/standby conditioning units, these should both be tested to ensure they will perform adequately. Studies of trends of the transition phase between the stop of the main system and the full operation of the standby system should be included in testing.

The operation and influence of the surrounding environment on defrosting systems should be verified and documented during the Commissioning and Qualification Phase.

5.4.5 Engineering Change Management

During Design — cumulative changes should not influence the ability of the design to meet user requirements. During Construction and Commissioning — changes proposed should be agreed with by the engineer of record. If a test fails and can be repeated after adjustments, it is acceptable to annotate the test sheet with the adjustments made and repeat the test; where changes are required, an engineering change control should be used to gain approval prior to execution and repeating the tests.

Once Qualification is complete, change should be managed through the site GMP change control system.

Red-Line Drawings Explained

Red-line drawings are a real-time documentary record of all design changes made during construction. The name comes from the traditional practice of marking changes on blueprints in red pen. Their importance lies in the following:

  • They record the "as-built" state: how the actual installation differs from the design drawings
  • Any change not recorded on the red-line drawings is a violation (unauthorized change) in an audit
  • Red-line drawings are maintained in real time by the commissioning team, not written up after the fact
  • Once complete, they must be formally converted into updated versions of the engineering drawings and cannot remain in "red-line" status indefinitely
"If it is not on the red-line drawings, it did not happen. If it happened but is not on the red-line drawings, it is an unauthorized change."

Outcomes

What temperature mapping is designed to confirm — Source pp. 28

6.1 Outcomes

Testing is intended to:

  1. Confirm that the space that will be used for storage remains within defined limits with respect to temperature uniformity and stability over time across usable space.
  2. Determine locations representing operating temperature extremes to establish the strategy for monitoring and alarming the space used for storage and/or processing.

The operational temperature limits should be defined, together with any allowable excursions (time periods, acceptable limits).

Why Two Outcomes, Not One?

Outcome 1 answers: "Does this chamber work uniformly?" Outcome 2 answers: "Where do I put my monitoring sensors permanently?" Both are required — you cannot claim compliance without addressing both.

Commentary

ISPE reduces the purpose of a temperature mapping study to two core questions:

Outcome 1: Confirmation of Temperature Uniformity

Confirm that the temperature throughout the entire usable storage space stays within the defined limits for the whole period of use. This is the most basic compliance threshold. If one corner is consistently 2°C high but the monitoring probe sits in the center, you get no warning of the deviation.

Outcome 2: Identify the Extreme Locations → Determine the Monitoring Points

This is the objective that is often overlooked but is more strategically significant. The final output of a mapping study is a scientific basis for selecting monitoring locations: the hottest point, the coldest point and the location where temperature is hardest to maintain are where the permanent monitoring probes should go.

Analogy: The Logic of Choosing a Weather Station Site

You would not put a weather station on the most comfortable flat ground; you would put it on the mountaintop or coast where extreme weather is most likely to occur. Temperature mapping follows exactly the same logic: find the extremes, and monitor the extremes.

Implications for a Multi-Client CDMO Environment

In a contract development and manufacturing organization (CDMO), the same cold store may hold products from several clients at once, each with a different temperature specification (for example 2–8°C vs. 15–25°C). Outcome 2 becomes even more important: if the monitoring locations are poorly chosen, the product of the client with the strictest specification cannot be protected.

Risk Assessment

Factors to evaluate before developing the test strategy — Source pp. 28–29

6.2 Risk Assessment

Prior to developing the test strategy, evaluate potential sources of excursions taking into account the following factors:

(Note: a company may use Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) modeling to evaluate impact of these factors but still needs some level of confirmation of the results through actual testing.)

  1. Purpose of the facility: storage, process cooling, transportation preconditioning
  2. Size: speed and magnitude of changes impacted by the footprint as well as the height, with attention to potential stratification with heights over 15 ft (457 cm)
  3. Layout/configuration: walls and obstructions, fixed racks, temporary racks, pallets on floors, duct supply and return locations
  4. Location of facility: geographic with respect to external climate exposures, physical location within temperature-controlled facility, windows, doors, traffic and access frequency
  5. Operating characteristics of the HVAC system: defrost cycles, redundant systems, number of units supporting storage space and how they interact with each other

CFD Modeling — Supplement, Not Substitute

CFD can help visualize airflow patterns and predict temperature gradients before physical testing. However, it cannot account for all real-world variables (door seals, product loading behavior, actual defrost timing). ISPE is explicit: actual testing is always required.

Commentary

ISPE requires that a risk assessment be completed before the testing strategy is designed. This is not a paperwork formality; it is the fundamental basis for deciding how many sensors you need and which conditions to test.

Practical Meaning of the Five Risk Factors

  • Purpose: The testing requirements for pure storage and for conditioning are quite different. Conditioning equipment must be shown to bring items to the target temperature within a specified time.
  • Size: 15 feet (457 cm) is the alert height for temperature stratification. In a warehouse taller than this, the top-to-bottom temperature difference can reach 3–5°C, so sensors must be placed at both upper and lower levels.
  • Layout: Fixed racking vs. movable pallets directly affects the airflow path. In theory, every change to the racking configuration should be assessed for whether a new mapping study is needed.
  • Location: A west-facing exterior wall can raise the indoor temperature by more than 1°C on a summer afternoon in Taiwan.
  • HVAC characteristics: The defrost cycle is the most common source of short-term temperature excursions, and the test design must state explicitly how it is handled.

Analogy: CFD Is Like a Weather Forecast

A weather forecast can tell you it may rain tomorrow, but it cannot replace measuring actual rainfall. CFD simulation can predict where hot spots may occur, but it cannot replace a real temperature mapping study, because the real world always has variables that a simulation cannot capture.

Risk-based Approach

6.2.1 Risk-based Approach

With the advent of ASTM-E2500 "Standard Guide for Specification, Design, and Verification of Pharmaceutical and Biopharmaceutical Manufacturing Systems and Equipment," an organization may use a risk-based approach, with the equipment installation and operation being verified. For further information, see:

  • The ISPE Good Practice Guide: Cold Chain Management
  • The ISPE Guide: Science and Risk-Based Approach for the Delivery of Facilities, Systems and Equipment

There should be a clear definition of the acceptance criteria; typically, this is provided in the URS or equivalent document.

Temperature mapping is used to confirm that the system is operating to meet specifications — i.e., the temperature range within the unit is acceptable.

It is standard practice in the pharmaceutical industry to provide monitoring of critical parameters using an independent system. Advantages include:

  • Calibration of the monitoring sensors can be carried out without impacting the system operation
  • The independent system becomes the quality "system of record"
  • The data can be used to provide continuous verification of acceptable system performance

Mapping results may be used to define the number and location of monitoring sensors for a warehouse, cold room, or chamber.

Commentary

ASTM E2500 and Risk-Based Validation

ASTM E2500 moves the traditional IQ/OQ/PQ validation framework toward a science-based approach of "specification, design, verification". The key shift: a fixed documentation format is no longer mandated; instead, every decision must be supported by a scientific rationale. The practical impact on temperature mapping is that your protocol must explain "why these locations were chosen", not merely "I chose these locations".

Three Advantages of an Independent Monitoring System

  • Independent calibration: Monitoring probes can be calibrated without disturbing normal operation of the system. (Consider: if the monitoring system and the control system shared the same probe, what would you do during calibration?)
  • System of record: The monitoring system's data are the official record and are not restricted by the data format of the equipment control system.
  • Continuous verification: Every monitored data point is a confirmation that "the system is still operating within specification", forming continuous evidence of compliance.

Design Points for an Independent Monitoring System in a CDMO Environment

In a multi-client CDMO facility, the independent monitoring system must also meet the following:

  • Data access control (each client can see only its own temperature records)
  • 21 CFR Part 11 electronic records compliance (audit trail, electronic signatures)
  • Alarm recipient assignment (who receives the alarm when a given warehouse has a deviation)
  • Management of multiple calibration cycles (different equipment may have different calibration frequency requirements)

6.3

When to Perform Temperature Mapping

Timing within commissioning and relationship to supporting systems — Source pp. 30

6.3 When to Perform Temperature Mapping

Temperature mapping should start as early as possible during commissioning. This allows:

  • Early detection and resolution of any performance issues
  • Identification of potential operational constraints to discuss with End Users, to determine if the constraints are acceptable or require resolution

Where there are systems that support operation of a system (e.g., the warehouse, HVAC system, or a condenser water cooling system), these systems should be:

  • Identified and commissioned or in a known (and monitored) state prior to temperature mapping
  • This helps ensure complete and representative testing, and facilitates investigation in the event of performance problems

Acceptable Exception — Chilled Water System

It can be acceptable to temperature map an area served by an HVAC system where a chilled water system has not been fully balanced, IF the chilled water system is known to provide an adequate supply of chilled water at a constant temperature. The BMS/BAS could provide supporting data that the chilled water supply temperature remains within operating limits and that the HVAC cooling control valve does not fully open (indicating adequate supply).

Commentary

Strategic Significance of "Start Early"

The earlier the temperature mapping study is done, the easier it is to correct problems at the design stage (low cost, small impact). If a persistent hot spot in one corner is found only after the facility is complete, large changes to the HVAC ductwork may be needed, and the engineering cost far exceeds the cost of an early test.

In a CDMO environment in Taiwan, summer heat and humidity are the harshest test conditions. If a new facility is completed and tested in winter, repeat (seasonal) testing in summer should be explicitly required.

Criteria for Judging "Prerequisite System Readiness"

The results of a mapping study represent only "the state of the system at the time of the test". If the chilled water system was in an unstable state during testing, the test data may not represent normal operating conditions. The exceptions ISPE allows are strict:

  • The chilled water must be known to provide a stable supply
  • There must be BMS data demonstrating that the chilled water temperature is within range
  • The control valve is not fully open (demonstrating that the chilled water supply is sufficient)

If these three conditions cannot be met at the same time, the mapping test data are not representative and the study must be repeated once the prerequisite systems are stable.

Analogy: The Importance of Seasonal Testing (Taiwan Perspective)

Summer in Taiwan (July–September) has average outdoor temperatures of 35°C+ and humidity above 80%. The compressor efficiency of refrigeration equipment drops in hot environments (because heat rejection conditions worsen). A cold room that passed testing in March may show temperature anomalies in August that never appeared before. This is why "summer worst-case" testing is necessary.

Commentary

Why "Quality Unit Approval" Is a Prerequisite

A temperature mapping study is a formal validation/qualification activity. Under GMP, every validation protocol must be approved by the quality unit (QA) before execution. The reasons:

  • To ensure the test design meets regulatory expectations
  • To ensure the acceptance criteria are defined in advance (not decided after the test whether it passes)
  • To ensure there is a clear deviation handling procedure if the test fails

If the test is executed first and approval is added afterwards, this is a Major Observation in an FDA inspection.

Practical Guidance on Measurement Uncertainty of Calibrated Equipment

ISPE specifically notes that the equipment used for temperature mapping must be calibrated. Having a calibration certificate is not enough; the key is to understand how the measurement uncertainty of the calibration affects the acceptance criteria:

  • If the acceptance criterion is 2–8°C (a range of 6°C)
  • If the sensor accuracy is ±0.5°C
  • Then the range that the sensor can reliably detect is 2.5–7.5°C
  • A cheap data logger with an accuracy of only ±2°C cannot reliably evaluate a 6°C range at all

Rule: Sensor accuracy ≤ 1/6 of the acceptance criterion range (industry convention).

Measurement Uncertainty Calculation Framework

Acceptance criterion range = upper limit - lower limit
            = 8°C - 2°C = 6°C

Required sensor accuracy (recommended):
  ≤ acceptance range / 6
  = 6°C / 6 = ±1.0°C (maximum allowed)

Stricter recommendation (ISPE example):
  ±0.5°C (gives an effective evaluation range of 3–7°C)

Cheap data logger (±2°C):
  -> Not suitable for this acceptance criterion
  -> If it is to be used, the acceptance criterion must be redefined
     or a scientific justification for an equipment waiver obtained

Factors Influencing the Mapping Strategy

Comprehensive factor list including load state, probe types, and sampling rate — Source pp. 30–32

6.4 Factors Influencing the Mapping Strategy

  • Purpose: storage, processing, transportation, conditioning
  • Type of facility: general warehouse, CRT warehouse, cold storage, freezer, refrigerator, incubator, stability chamber
  • Size of facility: span (air change rates and flow patterns), height (potential stratification)
  • Number of conditioning units controlling environment
  • Layout/configuration: open space versus walls, racks, obstructions; fixed vs. variable racks; duct supply/return positions; variable obstructions (moveable pallets, racks)
  • Location: geographic impact of external climate conditions; physical relationship within facility (conditioned space, windows, doors, exposed walls, high traffic areas, frequency and duration of access)
  • Contents of facility: motors, coils, lights, people; exothermic/endothermic reactive substances; hot material being cooled within facility
  • HVAC operating characteristics: defrost cycles, redundancies, interactions between evaporators, Fan Coil Units (FCUs), Direct Expansion (DX) coils; failure modes; hysteresis and time lags
  • Empty versus Full state:
    • Testing using a simulation of fully loaded conditions should be performed
    • Testing with a partial load can be considered unnecessary (system tested at operating extremes)
    • Note: typically, chambers are tested empty
  • Probe types: accuracy, reliability, ease of use and calibration — all impact measurement uncertainty
  • Buffered probes:
    • May be used for monitoring if performance relationship to stored product is known
    • Should NOT be used as controlling probes
    • Should NOT be used for mapping (when goal is to see actual room temperatures)
  • Sample rate: at least once every 5 minutes; may need to be more frequent if temperature spikes anticipated (defrost, door openings)
  • Operational requirements: how long doors open, how many people, traffic patterns, equipment

Commentary

Empty vs. Fully Loaded vs. Partially Loaded: Comparison of Test Scenarios

Limits on the Use of Buffered Probes

A buffered probe (usually a probe encased in glycerol or sand) is used to simulate the temperature the product experiences rather than the air temperature. ISPE clearly distinguishes three usage scenarios:

  • Acceptable (OK): As a permanent monitoring point, provided the relationship between the buffered probe reading and the product temperature has been confirmed
  • Not acceptable (No): As the equipment control probe (the buffering delay would cause poor control)
  • Not acceptable (No): As a mapping probe (because the goal is to measure the true air temperature, not the product temperature)

Logic for Selecting the Sampling Frequency

ISPE's minimum requirement is one reading every 5 minutes. For the following scenarios, a higher frequency is needed:

  • Defrost cycle: each defrost may raise the temperature by 3–10°C within 2–5 minutes, and a 5-minute interval may miss the peak
  • Frequent door opening (for example, logistics sorting areas): each door opening may cause a brief temperature excursion
  • Conditioning chambers: the heating and cooling curves of the items must be tracked

Recommendation: For 2–8°C refrigerators, use a sampling frequency of one reading every 1–2 minutes to ensure the full temperature profile of defrost events is captured.

6.4.1 Selection of Mapping Equipment

Selection of the equipment to be used for mapping should consider the required accuracy and repeatability related to the predefined acceptance criteria.

Example: A unit with acceptance criteria of 2°C to 8°C (USP standard storage conditions). If the mapping system has an accuracy of ±0.5°C, this provides results (allowing for rounding) to meet the acceptance criteria of 2°C to 8°C.

The user requirements may have a tighter design specification, e.g., expected results of 3–7°C.

Important: Some low-cost mapping dataloggers have accuracy specifications of ±2°C. Use of these devices would not be appropriate for this application unless the acceptance criteria or expected results were revised to allow for the potential error.

Temperature sensors with a rapid response time are typically used for temperature mapping, to provide accurate information on system performance.

Humidity mapping considerations:

  • If humidity has been defined as a critical quality attribute, humidity sensors should be included
  • If there are no significant sources of humidity in the warehouse, it may be assumed that absolute humidity will equalize — a small number of sensors may suffice (one at low level, one at high level for stratification)
  • If there are significant sources of moisture, a greater number of humidity sensors will be required — number and location developed using similar concepts as temperature mapping
  • Where RH is the criteria, levels should be checked based on temperature variations using measured RH at the local temperature
  • Duplex sensors (T+RH combined) can simplify data analysis

Mapping sensor locations should be documented to allow a test to be repeated if necessary. The proposed load test strategy should be reviewed and approved by the Quality Unit and the System Owner.

Commentary

Effect of Measurement Uncertainty on Acceptance Criteria (Examples)

Scenario 1: Standard refrigeration (2–8°C)
  Acceptance criterion range = 6°C
  Sensor ±0.5°C → suitable
  Sensor ±2.0°C → not suitable (±2°C exceeds 1/3 of the range)

Scenario 2: Room temperature storage (15–25°C)
  Acceptance criterion range = 10°C
  Sensor ±0.5°C → very suitable
  Sensor ±2.0°C → marginal (additional assessment needed)
  Sensor ±3.0°C → not suitable

Scenario 3: Ultra-low temperature (-80°C ± 10°C)
  Acceptance criterion range = 20°C
  Sensor ±1.0°C → suitable
  Sensor ±3.0°C → marginal (assessment needed)

Conclusion: criterion for selecting sensor accuracy
  Sensor accuracy ≤ acceptance criterion range × 1/4
  (conservative: × 1/6; very strict: × 1/10)

Humidity Management Considerations for Taiwan CDMO Facilities

Outdoor relative humidity in Taiwan in summer often exceeds 85%. Humidity mapping is especially important for the following spaces:

  • CRT warehouses (15–25°C): Without air conditioning in a Taiwan summer, relative humidity may exceed 60%, affecting the stability of hygroscopic drug products
  • Freezer/refrigerated warehouses: Frequent door opening lets in humid air, and long-term accumulation may cause frost build-up that affects HVAC performance
  • Stability chambers: Monitoring temperature and humidity together is a basic requirement (ICH Q1A)

Analogy: Fast-Response Probe vs. Slow Probe

If you were timing a run, would you use a stopwatch or a calendar? A fast-response probe is like a stopwatch: it captures brief temperature changes such as defrost events and door-opening impacts. A slow probe (or buffered probe) is like a calendar: it only tells you the general trend and cannot see the critical short-lived excursions.

7.0 When Is Qualification Required?

Systems that have the potential to impact product quality require Qualification. This includes systems used to store GMP raw materials, intermediates, and finished products.

Where individual gel packs are verified before use (confirming system performance/adequate pack conditioning time), the systems used for their conditioning and storage may be categorised as engineering systems (i.e., commissioned systems).

The decision to Commission only versus Commission and Qualify is a judgment based on a comprehensive understanding of the product, process, and the nature of the system by appropriately qualified personnel. Refer to the ISPE Baseline® Guide: Commissioning and Qualification.

The traditional approach uses separate IQ and OQ protocols to provide documented evidence that installation and operation meet the user requirements/system specifications after commissioning. IQ and OQ may be combined into a single document.

Typically, a system Performance Qualification (PQ) is not required; any operational testing needed may be addressed within OQ. Where product-specific or loading-specific evidence is necessary, PQ is applied.

Commentary: What Triggers Qualification

GMP System Classification Principle

ISPE divides temperature-controlled equipment into two categories:

  • Commissioned systems (engineering systems): Systems with only an indirect impact on product quality, for example a cold room used to condition gel packs. Commissioning is sufficient; IQ/OQ is not mandatory.
  • Qualified systems: Systems that directly store GMP raw materials, intermediates or finished products. IQ + OQ must be completed (PQ is decided on risk).

Analogy: Factory Acceptance vs Annual Service

IQ = accepting a new car at delivery (the hardware matches the order); OQ = the test drive (handling is within specification); PQ = a real load test (performance is met when fully loaded). Commissioning = the factory's internal quality control after assembly, which provides the foundation documents for IQ.

CDMO Site Tip

In a multi-product CDMO, the same 2–8°C refrigerator may hold both biologic drug substance and finished product. Such equipment must be qualified, and the PQ should cover representative load configurations so that a change of product does not alter the temperature distribution unnoticed.

7.1 Installation Qualification (IQ)

IQ should include:

  • A record of the major components — model numbers, serial numbers, supplier details
  • Confirmation that calibration has been conducted as required by the specifications
  • Confirmation that the specified utilities have been correctly connected — electrical supply, drainage, alarm cabling
  • Reference to the User Requirement Specification (URS) to confirm that the installed equipment matches the agreed specification
  • Listing of all critical instruments and their calibration due dates
  • As-built drawings or photographs showing equipment layout, probe locations, and utility connections

IQ may leverage commissioning documentation (FAT/SAT records, vendor certificates) provided the documents are referenced, controlled, and meet GMP traceability requirements.

Key Point

7.2 Operational Qualification (OQ)

OQ testing provides evidence that the system temperature ranges meet specifications during simulated operations. Key OQ requirements:

  • Empty chamber mapping — conducted for no less than 24 hours; sensors placed as defined in the test strategy; protocol must include a diagram showing sensor placement
  • Temperature study on the specified load — also no less than 24 hours
  • Open door testing (where applicable) — simulates routine access; results used to justify alarm delay settings
  • Power loss / temperature rise testing (where applicable) — how long until temperature exceedance after power failure
  • Quality-related alarm testing — confirm alert and action alarms trigger at set points
  • For liquid nitrogen or CO₂ freezers: confirm that the level of LN₂/CO₂ is maintained at set-point
  • Worst-case bracket approach: test at extreme conditions that cover all normal operating scenarios

Worst-Case Bracket Approach

In the OQ, select the least favourable combination of operating conditions (for example: empty chamber + summer ambient + highest expected door-opening frequency). If the chamber meets specification under these conditions, it covers normal operating scenarios, and every combination does not need to be tested individually.

7.3 Performance Qualification (PQ)

For CTC systems, ISPE states that a system PQ is typically not required. Any operational testing required may be addressed in OQ. However, PQ may be warranted when:

  • Product-specific evidence is needed (e.g., temperature-sensitive biologics with narrow stability ranges)
  • Representative loading configurations must be demonstrated for multiple product types
  • Regulatory requirements or customer contracts explicitly call for PQ documentation
  • The system stores particularly high-value or critical intermediates where product loss risk is significant

Where PQ is conducted:

  • Use representative loading patterns — not necessarily full load, but covering the range of expected usage
  • Conduct loaded chamber mapping with thermal mass representative of actual product containers
  • Document the relationship between PQ results and the URS acceptance criteria
  • PQ protocols and reports follow the same GMP documentation structure as IQ/OQ

8.0 Independent Monitoring Principle

It is considered good practice to use an independent system to monitor critical parameters. Calibration of monitoring probes can be performed without impacting system operation. The location of probes is determined during mapping.

Monitoring systems used in practice include:

  • Conventional chart recorders
  • Datalogging chart recorders
  • Building Management Systems (BMS) / Building Automation Systems (BAS)
  • Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems

The choice depends on the facility, the number of CTCs to monitor, and regulatory expectations. BMS systems used for GMP monitoring must comply with data integrity requirements, including audit trails and access control.

Comparison of Monitoring System Options & 21 CFR Part 11 Applicability

Minimum 21 CFR Part 11 Requirements for a BMS (FDA Electronic Records)

  • Audit trail: Every creation, modification and deletion of a record must leave a trail, including the time stamp and the identity of the operator.
  • Access control: Restrict read and modify permissions by job function, with unique user IDs.
  • Data integrity: Electronic records must not be easily altered or deleted; backup and disaster recovery mechanisms are required.
  • Electronic signatures: If used to sign off formal QC records, they must meet the electronic signature requirements.
  • Validation: BMS/SCADA software requires CSV (computerized system validation).

8.1 Temperature Sensor Types

Three main types are used in GMP monitoring:

1. Thermocouples
Operate via the Seebeck effect — a temperature gradient across a conductor generates a voltage. Two dissimilar metals form a circuit where the voltage difference is measurable (1–70 µV/°C for standard combinations). Cables and joints can be prone to damage. A common protocol allowance is up to 1% sensor failure without invalidating the test run, provided failed sensors are not in critical regions.

2. Resistance Temperature Detectors (RTDs) and Thermistors
Based on resistance change with temperature. RTDs use metal (resistance increases with temperature); thermistors use ceramic material (resistance decreases with temperature). Measured using a Wheatstone bridge with a small current. High-quality RTDs may last >8 years in accelerated life tests. Wiring configurations (3-wire, 4-wire) minimise cable resistance effects.

RTD accuracy classes (IEC 751):

  • Class A: ± 0.15°C at 0°C (± 0.27°F at 32°F)
  • Class B: ± 0.30°C at 0°C (± 0.54°F at 32°F)

3. Chemical Indicators
Chemicals that change colour with temperature. Used only to record minimum/maximum temperatures. Not considered accurate; no time record associated with temperature change. Not suitable for continuous GMP monitoring.

Sensor Accuracy and Selection Guide

Selection Decision Principles

For 2–8°C refrigerated equipment:

  • The acceptance range is narrow (a span of only 6°C), so an RTD Class A (±0.15°C) should be used to ensure that sensor accuracy does not consume too much of the acceptance range margin.
  • Thermocouples suit wide-area mapping (an accuracy of ±1.5°C is still relatively large for the 2–8°C range) and need to be field-calibrated against a high-accuracy reference sensor.
  • Chemical indicators are suitable only for confirming the minimum/maximum temperature accompanying a consignment; they must not be used for equipment qualification or continuous monitoring.

8.2–8.3 Number and Location of Monitoring Probes

Monitoring sensors should be placed where stored product will experience the maximum and minimum temperatures and, where applicable, more extreme humidity during normal CTC operation. At-risk (worst-case) locations are identified during mapping.

General sizing guidance:

  • For units <2 m³: one monitoring sensor in the worst-case location (typically the hottest location for a below-ambient unit) may be acceptable. A single sensor provides robust detection of operational or performance issues.
  • For larger systems or those with multiple cooling units: more sensors are justified to ensure adequate coverage of positions where stored product experiences maximum and minimum temperatures (and/or humidity).

Permanent sensor placement considerations:

  • Risk-based worst-case storage locations from mapping results
  • Single vs. dual temperature control unit placement
  • Volume of controlled space; density of materials and obstructions
  • High/low storage locations (warm air rises)
  • Independent heat sources (production equipment) within the CTC
  • Door areas and ingress/egress locations
  • Frequency of personnel and forklift ingress/egress during working hours
  • Operations outside working hours (lower traffic, different thermal dynamics)
  • Air conditioning of loading area — humidity and frost on evaporator coils
  • Sensors must not interfere with product movement or storage, and must be positioned in multiple geometric planes

The mapping results and data analysis are summarised in a report recommending optimum sensor locations for approval by the Quality Unit and System Owner.

Security cameras are not installed in every corner, but at the places where problems are "most likely": entrances and exits, blind spots, next to valuable items. The logic for CTC monitoring probes is the same: use the mapping to identify the risk locations and place probes there deliberately, rather than distributing them evenly.

8.4 Thermal Dampening Fluids

Monitoring probes may have alarm delay times or be placed in thermal dampening fluids to avoid nuisance alarms from short-term temperature variances due to loading/unloading.

Air Probes with time delays:

  • Delay times must be justified as having no product impact
  • Must consider the monitoring system sample frequency: if product has an acceptable excursion limit of 30 minutes and the system samples every 15 minutes, every alarm is potentially an OOS situation
  • Standard approach: alarm delay of 15 minutes is commonly used as a default for units with a wide range of operational loads

Probes in thermal dampening fluid:

  • The dampening fluid must simulate the storage of product in primary containers
  • The fluid must react to CTC temperature changes quicker than the primary container/packaging
  • Justification must consider: container size and material of construction; fluid selection (glycol, glycerin, Paratherm™); location of probe within the container
  • Probe must be suitable for immersion; procedures confirm correct liquid level and probe positioning
  • For a variety of containers, a conservative (worst-case smallest container) approach guides the probe medium design
  • Account for both primary containers and secondary packaging thermal mass

Decision Guide: Thermal Dampening Fluid vs Time Delay

Scientific Basis for the 15-Minute Delay Setting

ISPE explains why the standard 15-minute delay is justified:

  • After the door of a low-temperature unit is opened, denser cold air flows out from the bottom of the chamber and is replaced by warmer ambient air, and it takes time for the chamber to cool down again.
  • A uniform delay setting (15 min) reduces the risk of errors caused by inconsistent settings between individual units.
  • A short temperature rise within 15 minutes does not usually indicate a system abnormality; beyond 15 minutes it may point to an equipment problem or a door left open by personnel.
  • Warning: If the product stability limit allows an acceptance time of < 15 minutes, this standard setting does not apply and a customised evaluation is required.

8.5 Alarm Set Points

Good practice is to define a clear alarm strategy for the CTC with two distinct categories:

8.5.1 Engineering Alarms

Generated by the temperature conditioning system and its control system. Include: system fault, high/low pressure, high/low temperature, temperature sensor fault (open/closed circuit). These require an engineering action.

Temperature alarm set points are defined based on system "as commissioned" performance — typically just outside the ± 3 standard deviation range to avoid nuisance alarms while ensuring early detection of potential problems.

Rationale: for a normal distribution, 99.7% of results lie within ±3σ. Using ±3σ set points creates very few false alarms while reliably detecting operational drift.

8.5.2 Quality Alarms

Alarms from the independent monitoring system, configured based on the defined temperature storage limits. Two levels are typically defined:

  • Alert: Tighter than the specification; triggers investigation. Typically ±1°C inside the specification band.
  • Action/Alarm: At or just inside the specification limits; triggers immediate action and investigation.

Example: For a 2–8°C unit with ±0.5°C sensor calibration accuracy:

  • Alert limits: 3°C and 7°C (37°F and 45°F)
  • Action limits: 2°C and 8°C (36°F and 46°F)

Alert vs Alarm Set Point Example: 2–8°C Refrigerator

Engineering Alarm Set Point Calculation

Based on ≥24 hours of operational data at steady state:

Upper engineering alarm = Mean + 3σ
Lower engineering alarm = Mean − 3σ

Example: If the steady-state mean is 4.5°C and σ = 0.3°C:
Upper engineering alarm = 4.5 + 3(0.3) = 5.4°C
Lower engineering alarm = 4.5 − 3(0.3) = 3.6°C

This means engineering alarms are narrower than quality alerts, providing the earliest possible warning before product risk occurs.

9.0 Periodic Assessment Framework

CTCs should be periodically assessed to determine the need for re-validation (re-mapping). Control systems, equipment, monitoring, and procedures should be part of this periodic evaluation to ensure the unit still operates in a verified/qualified manner.

The evaluation should include, but is not limited to, a review of:

  • Changes made to the system
  • Changes made to storage locations (rack/bin configuration)
  • Calibration history
  • Equipment use logs
  • Deviation investigations
  • Maintenance and repair history
  • Temperature mapping studies
  • Operational procedures
  • Existing verification/qualification documentation
  • Ambient temperature profiles (seasonal extremes)

Data collected from CTCs equipped with multiple permanently installed monitoring probes may be an acceptable substitute for requalification temperature mapping studies, provided the probes have a demonstrated history of successful operation including calibration and maintenance.

Decision Logic: Periodic Assessment vs Re-mapping

Two Different Evaluation Actions

Periodic assessment: A document-review activity that looks back over calibration records, alarm history, SOP changes, deviations and maintenance records. It is the minimum requirement of every periodic assessment and does not necessarily trigger re-mapping.

Re-mapping: A field-measurement activity in which temperature probes are placed in the chamber again and run for ≥24 hours to produce new temperature distribution data. Whether it is needed is decided by a risk assessment or a trigger event.

Analogy: Health Check vs Surgery

A periodic assessment is like an annual health check (blood tests, X-rays): it is routine. Re-mapping is like the pre-operative assessment before precision surgery: it is carried out only when there is a clinical indication (a trigger), but the frequency and the indications should be clearly defined in writing in advance.

Prerequisites for Using Continuous Monitoring Data to Replace Re-mapping

  • The probes are installed at the worst-case locations identified by mapping.
  • The probes have a complete calibration history (consistently no OOT / OOS).
  • Maintenance records are complete, with no unexplained data gaps.
  • The monitoring system itself has passed CSV (computerized system validation).
  • The alternative approach is clearly defined in the VMP (validation master plan).

9.1 Re-mapping Triggers

Mapping exercises should be repeated according to risk assessment results or whenever significant modifications are made to the facility or temperature-controlling equipment.

System / Equipment triggers:

  • Equipment repair affecting temperature performance (e.g., compressor replacement, refrigerant recharge)
  • Relocation of the CTC to a different area
  • HVAC changes affecting the space surrounding the CTC
  • Changes to racking, bin configuration, or storage organisation patterns
  • Addition or removal of de-stratification fans
  • Changes to door configuration or door seals

Performance / Trend triggers:

  • Increasing frequency of temperature alerts or alarms (negative trend)
  • Temperature deviations outside specification
  • Seasonal extremes exceeding historical data range
  • Monitoring data showing increased variability or drift

Regulatory / Administrative triggers:

  • Regulatory inspection observations or internal audit findings
  • Number of SOP changes beyond a threshold defined in the VMP
  • Number of change controls to the area, temperature control system, or monitoring system
  • Scheduled requalification frequency defined in the VMP (e.g., every 3 years)

9.2 Risk-Based Frequency

A Risk Assessment is recommended to determine the frequency of periodic review and the need for repeating mapping. The frequency should be aligned with the site's Validation Master Plan (VMP) and based on risk factors including:

  • Local regulations and regulatory expectations for maintaining a validated state
  • Regulatory inspection observations and internal audit observations
  • History of performance in maintaining the desired temperature range
  • Number of temperature deviations (number of alerts and alarms)
  • Number of SOP changes
  • Number of change controls to the area, temperature control system, or monitoring system

Typical industry practice:

  • Remapping frequency: every 3 to 5 years — for standard storage units with stable performance history
  • Critical product storage: annually — for high-risk, temperature-sensitive, or high-value products
  • The rationale for the chosen frequency must be documented for the facility and/or equipment

Risk factors that increase frequency:

  • High exposure to environmental extremes (refrigerated storage directly exposed to seasonal fluctuations)
  • Narrow operating range (e.g., -20°C ± 5°C vs. 15–25°C)
  • High product value or supply-chain criticality
  • Old equipment with degrading performance history

The storage of high-value and/or temperature-sensitive products may warrant more frequent re-verification/re-qualification. Maintaining product availability in the supply chain is a critical quality and customer-focused consideration.

Periodic Assessment Decision Matrix for a Multi-Product CDMO

Periodic Maintenance of a CRT Warehouse: Specific Checks (ISPE Recommendation)

  • Visually inspect the building envelope (walls, door seals, wind curtains) to confirm there is no obvious deterioration.
  • Review the alarm history of the monitoring system and the HVAC system to identify negative performance trends.
  • Confirm that the HVAC balancing dampers are still at the positions set during commissioning.
  • Clean the heating and cooling coils (if needed).
  • Confirm that control valves are not operating at 100% open (if they are, the system is reaching its performance limit).

9.3 Continuous Performance Verification

An alternative to periodic re-mapping: continuous performance verification using a sufficient number of permanently installed monitoring sensors. This strategy has become more feasible due to the reduced cost of radio transmitter sensors.

How it works:

  • Additional sensors are installed beyond the minimum monitoring requirement, placed at positions that would detect performance degradation from any direction (unit cooler failure, structural degradation, loading pattern changes)
  • Storage locations are formally defined and any revisions addressed through change control, allowing reassessment of probe number/location
  • The ongoing monitoring data provides continuous evidence that the system operates within specification

Design requirements for continuous verification:

  • The number and location of sensors must be determined based on ability to detect any impact of the conditioning system performance and system construction issues
  • Sensors must cover all zones where a degradation event would be detectable — including corners, near unit coolers, door areas, and stratification zones
  • For a walk-in unit in use, mapping may be performed with the unit loaded, using sensor locations from the initial mapping where possible

ISPE provides Figures 9.1–9.4 as examples of cold room sensor layout designs for both initial mapping (sparse sensors) and continuous verification (denser sensor arrays).

Comparison: Continuous Verification Strategy vs Periodic Re-mapping

Key Design Points for CDMO Continuous Verification

A continuous verification strategy is particularly valuable for a multi-product CDMO cold store:

  • Loading patterns differ between clients' products; re-mapping at every product change would be extremely costly.
  • By installing permanent sensors in sufficient density at worst-case locations, a variety of loading scenarios can be covered.
  • Each change in loading is assessed through change control; if the new loading pattern does not change the worst-case locations, the existing monitoring can continue.
  • Key: the sensor array design must include a location that can detect degradation in the performance of the refrigeration unit.

9.4 Handling Excursion Documentation

A point person or department should be formally identified to coordinate the response to temperature excursions outside specified limits.

Excursion response protocol elements:

  • Immediate containment — assess whether product has been compromised; initiate product quarantine if needed
  • Root cause investigation — equipment failure, human error, systemic issue, or environmental factor
  • Impact assessment — review product's temperature stability data; determine if the excursion duration × magnitude is within the product's known stability budget
  • Deviation report — formal GMP documentation of the event, investigation, and disposition decision
  • CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Actions) — address root cause to prevent recurrence
  • Trend review — incorporate excursion data into the next periodic evaluation to assess whether re-mapping is warranted

Walk-in freezer / refrigerator specific checks (periodic maintenance):

  • Envelope inspection: door seals, moisture/ice buildup as indicator of air leakage
  • Alarm history review for negative performance trends
  • Verify refrigeration set point is being maintained (e.g., compressor suction pressure)
  • Confirm coils are cleaned, fan operation checked, control valves and thermal expansion valves verified, defrost settings and operation verified

Why Both Are Required

Qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) establishes that a CTC was installed correctly and did perform within specification under defined test conditions — typically a 24-hour or defined-duration snapshot. It answers the question: "Is this system capable?"

Continuous monitoring establishes that the system continues to perform within specification across every hour of every operational day, under real-world loading patterns, seasonal temperature swings, door-opening events, and equipment aging. It answers: "Is this system actually in control right now?"

Periodic evaluation bridges the two — it reviews monitoring data trends, maintenance history, and change history to determine whether the qualification status remains valid, or whether a new mapping study is needed to re-establish the snapshot.

In the ISPE framework, a CTC is considered to be in a state of control only when all three elements are in place:

  1. Valid, current qualification documentation (IQ + OQ)
  2. Functioning continuous monitoring with calibrated probes in worst-case locations
  3. Regular periodic evaluation with documented risk assessment for re-mapping decisions

Section 10.1 — Challenging the Area with Operational Activities

ISPE Source Text

10.1 Challenging the Area with Operational Activities

The area being mapped should be challenged with specific known operational activities during the thermal mapping process. These activities should simulate normal processes and functions that would occur for routine operations, such as opening interior doors, opening exterior doors (Receiving Dock Doors, Shipping Dock Doors), running of equipment (e.g., thermo-formers, stretch wrappers, forklifts, conveyors) etc. where there is a potential impact to the conditions.

By performing these functions and operating equipment during the execution of the mapping studies, cause and effect relationships can be identified for the thermal profile.

Operational activities to simulate during mapping include:

  • Opening interior and exterior doors (dock doors, personnel doors)
  • Running warehouse equipment: forklifts, conveyors, stretch wrappers
  • Simulating loading and unloading cycles
  • Normal personnel traffic through the space

Note on Cause and Effect

The primary purpose of operational challenges is to capture the actual thermal profile experienced during real production — not an idealized empty-room baseline. A mapping study that omits these activities may underestimate temperature variability and produce specifications that cannot be met during routine operation.

Commentary & CDMO Application

Operational Challenges and Thermal Mapping

Core Concept: Challenging the Area with Operational Activities

A thermal mapping study is not just a matter of placing sensors in an empty, static unit. ISPE expects mapping to be performed under simulated real production conditions so that potential sources of temperature variation are exposed.

This amounts to verifying equipment performance under the conditions closest to actual use.

Loading Risk Scenarios in a Shared CDMO Cold Room

In a CDMO, a cold room usually holds products from several clients at the same time, which makes loading risks more complex:

  • Scenario 1: Simultaneous access by multiple clients — operators on different shifts go in and out frequently; without a vinyl curtain, every door opening causes heat ingress
  • Scenario 2: Large-volume loading — loading many pallets at once can temporarily block cold air circulation and create local hot spots
  • Scenario 3: Forklift entry disturbing airflow — forklift engine heat and an open door occur together, amplifying the temperature impact
  • Scenario 4: Lowest-frequency door openings outside working hours — access patterns on the early-morning shift differ, so the worst case is not necessarily during the day

Section 10.2 — Open Door Recovery Test

ISPE Source Text

10.2 Open Door Recovery Test

An example of a typical operational activity is to examine recovery time for the internal chamber temperature after opening the access door and simulating loading/unloading. It is recommended to determine the time that the chamber door may remain fully open before the monitoring sensors are no longer within the acceptable range.

This test can also help to determine how quickly the monitoring sensor recovers and returns to the normal operating temperature range.

Recommended Test Conditions

  • Use a minimum (simulated) load or no load — worst case with no thermal mass
  • Locate temperature sensors as recommended for thermal mapping, with special attention to sensors nearest the door
  • Set data acquisition rate to 1-minute intervals due to uncertainty in test duration

Acceptance Criteria

Generally, there are no specific acceptance criteria for this test unless a recovery time is specified in the unit manufacturer's specifications. The key output is a data-supported understanding of the chamber's tolerance for door-open events.

Special Case: Walk-in with Vinyl Curtains

For larger walk-in CTCs fitted with vinyl curtains, the units may be able to maintain internal conditions indefinitely, as the impact of passing through curtains is short-term and negligible. Vinyl curtains should be well-fitted, rest on the unit walls, and sit just clear of the floor.

Commentary & CDMO Application

Open Door Recovery Test in Detail

Why Is Minimum/No Load the Worst Case?

The greater the thermal mass, the better a unit resists temperature swings. If the test is run with an empty chamber (minimum thermal mass), any heat ingress is amplified quickly, which represents the worst case.

This is consistent with the logic of factory and site acceptance testing (FAT/SAT): verify under the conditions most likely to fail, because only then does a pass give real quality assurance.

Door Buffering Time: Operational Meaning

The key output of the open door recovery test is the maximum permitted door-open time. This figure must be translated into:

  • A clearly stated "maximum door-open time" in the SOP
  • An assessment item in operator training
  • A deviation trigger condition for CAPA tracking

If the SOP only says "close the door as soon as possible" without a specific time limit, it is effectively no control at all.

10.3 Power Outage / Thermal Degradation Test

Another type of test that may be helpful from a business continuity, disaster recovery, and operational perspective is a simulated Power Outage Test. This type of test would measure the amount of time the Controlled Temperature Chamber remained within the desired temperature range once electrical power was turned off.

Note: although it is expected the Controlled Temperature Chamber will have redundant and backup power supplies, the results from this test would provide data to define how quickly materials need to be moved to another location if the backup power systems failed. Doors to the storage chamber should remain closed during this test.

Recommended Test Setup

  • Use a minimum (simulated) load or no load in the CTC (worst case)
  • Perform as commissioning activity referenced in OQ before the storage unit is put into use, if possible
  • Locate temperature sensors as recommended for thermal mapping
  • Data acquisition rate: 1-minute intervals

Additional Operational Value

Once power is restored, the amount of time required to return to the appropriate required temperature can be of value for disaster recovery planning — specifically, the elapsed time from when power is restored until the last temperature sensor returns to the acceptable range.

ISPE Source Text

Calibration and Calibration Strategies

There are benefits to be derived from a considered calibration strategy:

  • Refrigeration system instruments: Many can only be calibrated when the system is emptied for major maintenance — therefore, they are calibrated following manufacturer recommendations
  • Control sensor: Can be verified at a single point, as the control functions are usually based on whether the system is above or below the operating set point
  • Monitoring sensors: Should be calibrated across the operating range, as they provide the data to support quality determinations in support of product quality

Access Control

Areas where product is stored should have a system to prevent unauthorized access. This system can be part of a site keycard system, or use a lock and key with procedural controls which identify who holds, and is entitled to use, the key.

Dehumidification

Dehumidification should be considered for a cold room or freezer. The life of the equipment can be extended where there are fewer defrost cycles required (reduced expansion/contraction). Dehumidification can also reduce the risk of ice forming on a freezer floor, which presents a safety hazard.

For large distribution facilities it may be worth considering the layout, and providing access to a walk-in freezer through a cold room, in order to reduce moisture in the freezer.

Equipment Durability

When specifying a new facility, it is worthwhile defining the system design life — the expected operating lifespan without significant maintenance. High quality refrigeration systems can have an expected life of 12 to 15 years. Standby systems are usually installed for an installation holding material of a significant commercial value.

Commentary & CDMO Application

Calibration Strategies and Access Control in Depth

Three-Tier Calibration Strategy Framework

Key principle: monitoring sensors are held to a higher calibration standard than control sensors, because monitoring data are the basis for product release.

Calibration Recall Tracking System

An effective calibration recall system should include:

  • Asset register: every sensor has a unique ID, location, calibration interval and last calibration date
  • Due-date reminders: a warning is sent to the calibration engineer and QA 30 days in advance
  • Overdue lock-out: sensors past their calibration validity are flagged automatically, and their data must not be used for release decisions
  • Retrospective assessment: if a sensor is found out of tolerance after its calibration period has lapsed, the impact on all product stored during that period must be assessed
  • Verification after replacement: after calibration, a reinstalled sensor must be checked for agreement with the other sensors at the same location

GMP Significance of Access Control

Access control is not only a security matter but also a GMP compliance matter. Unauthorized entry may lead to:

  • Product being removed in an undocumented way (affecting inventory integrity)
  • Improper operation causing temperature deviations (door held open for a long time)
  • Visitors coming into contact with unlabeled product (cross-contamination risk)

FDA 21 CFR Part 211.68 and EU GMP Annex 15 both require appropriate access control for storage areas.

11.1 Active Shipping Containers: Unit Load Devices

Currently there are two types of Unit Load Devices (ULD) systems suitable for the shipping of temperature sensitive product, designed for use in aircraft. They are powered to either cool or heat the air within the container.

System 1 (TCCU) Key Features

  • Maintains 5°C (41°F) for 72 hours at 30°C (86°F) ambient
  • Handles brief ambient excursions from -40°C to +60°C
  • GPS/GSM module for real-time location and temperature transmission
  • Two modes: recharge mode (connected to AC) and transport mode (24VDC battery)
  • Interior designed for 40×48 inch pallet, up to 500 kg product

System 2 Key Features

  • Maintains 5°C for 30 hours at -10°C to +30°C ambient
  • NiMH batteries; three cooling compressors with heaters
  • Supports US and EURO pallet sizes, up to 953 kg
  • LED alarm indicators: system on (green), charge (blue), alarm (red), alert (yellow)
  • Alert triggers: battery <30%, inside temp out of spec, ambient temp out of spec, compressor failure

11.1.3 Qualification Approaches

Two possible approaches for active shipping containers:

  • Unit Qualification: Map each individual unit; confirm satisfactory operation; qualify service supplier for maintenance and calibration quality systems
  • Supplier Assessment: (1) Qualify the manufacturer (design + factory testing); (2) Qualify service supplier (maintenance + calibration quality systems). Usually paired with an independent temperature logger per shipment.

Regardless of approach, loading and unloading methods must be qualified and procedurally defined. When unloading, shipment should be placed into quarantine until data logger confirms conditions were maintained. If out-of-conditions, a Non-Conformance must be raised and the implications investigated.

Commentary & CDMO Application

Qualification of Active ULD Shipping Containers

Unit-by-Unit Qualification vs. Supplier Assessment: Selection Logic

  • Choose unit-by-unit qualification: when ULDs are leased long term from the same supplier in a fixed quantity, so that separate qualification documentation can be built for each unit. Suitable for high-value/high-risk products.
  • Choose supplier assessment: when the ULD fleet is large or units are replaced frequently, so individual mapping is impractical. Audit the manufacturer's design and the service provider's maintenance quality, supplemented by data loggers accompanying each shipment as additional assurance.
  • Key supplementary measure: whichever approach is used, every shipment should carry an independent temperature recorder, which serves as the last line of confirmation.

Cold Chain Hand-off Point Risks

In the cold chain from the facility to the final destination, the most vulnerable links are usually the hand-off points:

  • Facility warehouse → courier pickup: product exposure time at the loading dock
  • Airport ground handling: transfer time from the cargo warehouse to the aircraft hold
  • Transit points: waiting time between two flight legs
  • Destination airport → customer facility: last-mile delivery

Each hand-off point should have a clear temperature confirmation procedure and a division of responsibilities set out in a quality agreement.

Sections 11.2–11.5 — Temperature Controlled Trailers: Design, Insulation & Validation

ISPE Source Text

11.2 Temperature Controlled Trailers

Temperature controlled over-the-road trailers can be used to move significant amounts of temperature sensitive pharmaceutical product. Trailers have a different set of environmental and usage profiles when compared to in situ custom-built CTCs:

  • Loading and unloading operations
  • Defrost cycles
  • Loading patterns

Moisture, as a degradation element, is much more prevalent in trailers due to the operational profile — continuous running for several days, followed by periods of downtime when the trailer is empty. Rigorous inspection of each trailer should be performed prior to loading.

11.3 Trailer Design

Product loaded into the trailer should be equilibrated to the appropriate temperature before loading. During transit, as heat or cold penetrates the walls, roof, and floor, circulating airflows capture the environmental heat or cold and condition it to the desired temperature in the HVAC unit on the front of the trailer.

Depending on the geographical area, manufacturers build and test trailers to meet standards of one of two organizations:

  • North America: American Trucking Association (ATA) Technology & Maintenance Council (TMC)
  • Europe: ATP Agreement on International Carriage of Perishable Foodstuffs

TMC / ATP Classification Table

11.4 Insulation Degradation

Polyurethane foam insulation gradually loses its blowing agent gas (CO₂ and other gases) over time, which is replaced with air and water vapor. This transition occurs slowly over years and is normal. The temperature control unit is sized to assure a useful life of 15 years with timely maintenance.

11.5 Trailer Validation Approach

The validation exercise is focused on loading patterns and assuring that proper airflow can be achieved under conditions of heat and cold. Loading practices that block airflow impede heat addition/removal and result in product temperatures that may not be controlled within the required range. A written protocol should document temperatures nearest the product during seasonal extremes.

Commentary & CDMO Application

Temperature Controlled Trailer Validation Strategy Compared with CTCs

Analogy: Why Does Success with an F-Class Trailer Also Mean Success with a DF-Class Trailer?

ISPE notes that a validation study successfully completed on an F-class trailer also applies to a DF-class (deep frozen) trailer.

It is like car safety testing: if a car with standard safety equipment passes a crash test, a higher-spec model with more safety features will naturally pass as well. F-class is the mainstream market choice and has lower insulation capability, so passing means the higher-standard DF class can also meet the requirement.

Practical Meaning of Insulation Degradation

The insulating performance of polyurethane foam declines over time (blowing agent gas escapes and is replaced by air and water vapor), which means:

  • The initial performance of a new trailer ≠ its performance after 5–10 years of use
  • Validation studies should consider equipment age and usage history
  • Periodic thermal scanning can detect insulation deterioration early
  • The quality agreement should require the carrier to provide trailer maintenance records regularly

Section 11.6 — Trailer Loading Patterns & Inspectional Criteria

ISPE Source Text

11.6 Trailer Loading Patterns

Loading patterns in a well maintained truck are the key determinant in assuring even product temperature within the trailer. Key requirements:

  • Product should be loaded on pallets that allow free airflow the length of the trailer
  • Product should not be in contact with the walls of the trailer
  • Double stacking of refrigerated goods should be carefully considered, as airflow distribution in the rear of the trailer may not be sufficient to maintain uniform temperature under extreme conditions
  • Product should be equilibrated to the appropriate temperature before loading

11.6.1 Inspectional Criteria

The Quality Agreement between the carrier and the company should define the shared responsibilities of inspection. The following items should be inspected for each use:

  • Air distribution chute intact
  • Air supply, air returns and floor drains clear of debris
  • Repairs to interior and exterior surfaces (damage from forklifts etc.)
  • Door seals intact
  • General cleanliness (visual inspection)
Commentary & CDMO Application

Critical Effect of Loading Pattern on Temperature Uniformity

Three Principles of Loading Patterns

  • Principle 1: Clear airflow — leave enough space between pallets for cold air to circulate; blocking airflow is the same as blocking temperature control capacity
  • Principle 2: Keep clear of the side walls — the trailer side walls are where heat exchange is strongest (especially in summer), so product touching the side walls is the most likely to go out of temperature range
  • Principle 3: Pre-cool before loading — loading product that has not reached target temperature introduces a heat source into the trailer and adds load on the refrigeration unit

Pre-Use Trailer Inspection Checklist

  • Air distribution chute intact and undamaged
  • Supply air vents, return air vents and floor drain holes not blocked
  • No forklift punctures or dents in the interior and exterior walls
  • Door seals intact, with no cracks or damage
  • Overall cleanliness acceptable (no foreign matter, no residue from a previous load)
  • Refrigeration (reefer) unit operating normally, with the correct set point
  • Trailer interior temperature has reached the target range
  • TMC/ATP certification plate visible and within its validity period

Chapter 12 Maintenance — Overview

The supplier of the refrigeration equipment should provide general recommendations for the maintenance of the equipment. These recommendations may not recognize the high value of the stored goods in this application. The potential impact on the maintenance program should be assessed.

12.1 Cleaning of the Coils

The coils (unit cooler and air cooled condenser) should be cleaned regularly and checked for any damage to ensure that performance levels are maintained. Damaged or distorted fins should be straightened with a suitable comb. This inspection should also ensure that there is no corrosion of the fins.

Regular inspection should also confirm that the defrost cycle is effective (where applicable), especially if the cycle is adjusted to account for seasonal variation.

General Cleaning: Dry cleaning is preferred, in order to minimize risk of contamination. Regular inspection is recommended to ensure that there is no unseen microbial/fungicidal growth.

12.2 Check the Condensate Pipework

The condensate pipework should be checked to ensure that it is not damaged and that it has the correct fall and flows freely. Trace heating or insulation also should be checked. Periodic verification during operation is also recommended; this should ensure that the pipe supports are adequate and have not loosened.

12.3 Functional Testing of Unit Coolers / Defrost Elements

There is usually no feedback from the solenoid valve on the unit coolers, so the only way of checking their correct operation is to carry out, periodically, a functional check to verify the performance of each unit cooler.

12.4 Leak Testing

  • Periodically check pipe runs and equipment for leaks using electronic test devices
  • Check refrigerant fluid level and pressure against system specification — early detection may help avoid system failure
  • Keep a record of refrigerant used for the system (may be mandatory depending on region)
  • Equipment rooms may be fitted with leakage detectors/alarms — these should be calibrated and checked regularly
  • End Users should be aware of local regulatory requirements
Commentary & CDMO Application

Coils, Condensate Lines and Leak Testing in Detail

Key Insight: Manufacturer Recommendations ≠ GMP Requirements

ISPE states clearly that an equipment manufacturer's maintenance recommendations usually do not take into account the special needs of high-value pharmaceutical storage. This means:

  • Manufacturer recommendations may be based on food or general industrial applications
  • In a GMP environment, maintenance frequency and standards must be evaluated and set by the user (the CDMO)
  • The maintenance program must consider "if this equipment fails, what is the impact on product quality?"

This is the foundation of a risk-based PM program: equipment criticality → determines maintenance frequency and depth.

Microbial Risk in Coil Cleaning

ISPE recommends dry cleaning rather than wet cleaning, for these reasons:

  • Wet cleaning introduces moisture, which can create a breeding environment for biofilm on the coil fins
  • The low temperature of a cold room does not make it sterile: some psychrotrophic bacteria and molds can still grow slowly at 2–8°C
  • A microbially contaminated coil can spread spores through the airflow into the environment where product is stored

Regulatory Requirements for Refrigerant Leak Records

Many jurisdictions have mandatory record requirements for refrigerant use and leakage:

  • EU F-Gas Regulation (EU 517/2014): fluorinated refrigerants above a certain quantity require periodic leak checks and records
  • EPA Section 608 (US): leaks of ODS refrigerants above the threshold must be repaired and recorded
  • GMP perspective: a refrigerant leak may contaminate product, and a leak event may affect equipment performance and in turn storage temperature

The refrigerant log should be part of the PM documentation system.

Practice Question

Q: When were the coils on your refrigeration equipment last cleaned? Does the cleaning record include the visual inspection results before and after cleaning, and the cleaning method? If dry cleaning was not used, was the microbial contamination risk assessed?

ISPE Source Text

12.5 Thermal Scanning

The panels, doors, door seals, and sealing of utility penetrations can degrade over time (foamed panels may be better in this respect than panels filled with mineral wool). A thermal scan of the installation will allow any degradation to be seen and appropriate action taken.

As a minimum, the penetrations should be visually inspected to ensure that they are sound with no leakage/ingress of condensation. For conduit penetrations, the internal sealing also should be checked to ensure that condensation is not leaking inside the conduit.

12.6 System Alarms

The entire alarm system should be periodically tested by direct or simulated temperature change against the specified alarm set points and proper functionality of audible and visual alarms at local and/or remote alarm stations (e.g., emails, phone calls, or text based alerts) should be confirmed and documented.

Regardless of the root cause, product losses from cold storage-related failures can be prevented with the proper design and operation of the alarm system, and proper End User response to an alarm event. Periodic testing of the alarm system should help to ensure that it will work when needed.

The system maintenance procedures can be used to provide a confirmation that the system performance remains satisfactory, supporting a reduced requirement for remapping where appropriate. The data from the monitoring system should be reviewed periodically, to look for any negative performance trends.

Commentary & CDMO Application

Thermal Scanning, Alarm Systems and Remapping Triggers

Thermal Scanning: Non-Destructive Insulation Inspection

Infrared thermography can identify areas of insulation deterioration from temperature differences without removing panels:

  • Normal insulation: uniform surface temperature, no obvious thermal bridges
  • Degraded insulation: a "hot spot" appears in an area of the panel, indicating a cold bridge or damaged insulation material
  • Door seal leakage: abnormal temperature gradients around the door frame
  • Pipe wall penetrations: if poorly sealed, condensation will seep in along the pipe

Recommendation: perform thermal scanning while the unit is running fully loaded, when the temperature difference between inside and outside is greatest and problems are easiest to detect.

GMP Requirements for Alarm Testing

"Test alarms periodically" is not only a technical requirement but also a GMP compliance requirement. An alarm system that has never been tested may fail completely when it is actually needed.

  • Test method: a direct temperature change (simulating a real deviation) or a controlled simulation (injecting a test signal)
  • Test scope: local audible/visual alarm + remote notification (email/SMS/phone)
  • Documentation requirement: test results must be recorded, including the test time, trigger condition and notification arrival time
  • Response time target: there should be a clearly defined expected time from alarm trigger to someone acknowledging it

Maintenance Data → Reduced Remapping Need

ISPE provides an important lifecycle management principle: if the system's maintenance records are good and monitoring data show stable performance, the frequency of thermal remapping can be reduced.

This requires periodic review by an SME (subject matter expert) of:

  • Monitoring data trends vs. the baseline data from qualification
  • Alarm history (are new deviation patterns appearing?)
  • Maintenance history (have there been major repairs that affect performance?)
  • Equipment age and insulation degradation assessment

Conversely, remapping must be triggered in the following cases: major repair or replacement (compressor, condenser), relocation of the unit, or performance data showing a clear trend shift.

Practice Question

Q: When was your facility's alarm system last tested? Does the test record cover confirmation of response through the remote notification channels (SMS/email)? If on-duty staff change their phone numbers, does the alarm notification system update automatically?

Decision Tree for Product Impact Assessment of a Temperature Excursion
1. Alarm triggered or monitoring data show a temperature excursion: have the deviation time and duration been recorded?
No → immediately record the time of discovery and estimate the maximum possible exposure time (worst case)
Yes → go to step 2
2. Are the excursion temperature and duration within the cumulative exposure allowed in the product information (SmPC)?
Yes → record the assessment conclusion, confirm enhanced follow-up monitoring, and open a minor deviation report
No → go to step 3 (product quarantine)
3. Immediately quarantine the affected batches, notify QA and the product SME, and open a major deviation report
4. Is there approved out-of-storage stability data supporting product usability?
Yes → QA reviews the data; if supportive → conditional release or shortened shelf life
No → go to step 5
5. Can additional stability testing be performed to assess product quality?
Yes → send samples for testing; if results are supportive, conditional release, otherwise discard
No → discard the affected batches, perform a full CAPA analysis and root cause investigation
6. Whatever the final decision, the root cause must be identified and CAPA implemented, and whether remapping needs to be triggered must be assessed

URS Structure & Purpose

What a URS must contain and why it drives the entire qualification lifecycle

13.1.1 Purpose (Source Text)

Purpose: The purpose of this document is to provide the system developer with the user's requirements for the x°C freezer.

Overview

Text goes here [facility-specific context to be completed by the user].

Background

Text goes here [product types, storage history, regulatory context].

Key Objectives

The system will store GMP material at x°C to x°C.

Main Functions

The system shall be capable of storing material at x°C to x°C and provide a general system fault alarm and high and low alarm set points that can interface with the monitoring system. The monitoring system will provide the quality system alarms and provide operator paging.

A complete URS typically includes:

  • Purpose and scope
  • Definitions and abbreviations
  • References (regulations, standards)
  • System description
  • User requirements (capacity, process, automation)
  • Design and construction constraints
  • Operations and maintenance

Commentary & CDMO Application

Why Is the URS So Critical?

The URS is the starting point of the whole equipment qualification effort. Every IQ/OQ/PQ test item should trace back to a specific requirement in the URS. If the URS is vague, later validation cannot be traced.

The Three Tiers of URS Requirements

  • Regulatory requirements (Regulatory): 21 CFR Part 11, EU GMP Annex 11 — must be met, non-negotiable
  • Business requirements (Business): capacity, electrical specifications — derived from operational reality
  • Operational requirements (Operational): shift patterns, cleaning cycles — determine day-to-day usage constraints

The CDMO Multi-Client Scenario

In a CDMO, the same cold room may store GMP materials for several clients, and each client has different requirements for temperature range, access frequency and identification systems. The URS must reflect the "strictest common standard" rather than the requirements of a single client.

Common Pitfall

"The system shall maintain proper temperature" is not a requirement but an expectation. The correct wording: "The system shall maintain the internal temperature at 2°C to 8°C, with a stabilization time of no more than 30 minutes under maximum load conditions."

Practice Questions

  1. The "Purpose" field of a URS says "Provide GMP storage space." Identify at least two problems and rewrite it.
  2. A CDMO serves two clients at the same time: client A requires 2–8°C and client B requires -20°C. How would you handle this conflict in a single URS?
  3. The "Main Functions" section mentions that the monitoring system provides paging. Which tier (regulatory/business/operational) does this requirement belong to, and why?

Do Not Just List Regulation Names

Simply citing "21 CFR Part 11" is not a requirement. You must state which specific requirement applies to this system. For example: "Electronic temperature records shall not be modifiable, and any modification must leave an audit trail."

Translate regulations into business language. "Storage locations identified by barcode labels" is more specific and more testable than "compliant with inventory management system requirements." Capacity

  • System maximum single shipment loaded into the unit is 4 pallets (Note: product is at storage temperature)
  • System load removed in single pallet units

  • Internal emergency lighting
  • A mechanism to allow door opening from the inside of the unit regardless of the lock status

Commentary & CDMO Application

The Calculation Logic of Capacity Requirements

"12 Euro pallets" must be tied to actual dimensions (X×Y×Z). If the URS states only the quantity and no dimensions, the supplier may design a space whose shape cannot fit any standard pallet.

Why the Single-Delivery Quantity Matters

"Maximum single delivery of 4 pallets" is a key thermal load parameter. This figure directly shapes the design of the temperature recovery time test in OQ. If the URS does not define it, validation cannot be designed around the worst case.

Practical Requirements for GMP-Grade Pallet Racking

"Crevice free" and "resistant to sporicidal agents" are specific, testable material specifications. PVC-coated steel racking or stainless steel is acceptable, but material certification must be provided at the DQ stage.

Safety Requirements Are Not Only an OSHA Issue

A mechanism to "open the door from the inside" is a regulatory requirement, but at a CDMO site there is more to consider: is there a personnel tracking system when an operator enters the cold room? Does the alarm connected to the emergency button cover 24 hours?

Process, Monitoring & Automation Requirements

Temperature control, alarms, data integrity, and system interfaces

13.1.2 Process & System Requirements (Source Text)

Process Requirements

  • Include allowable excursions (e.g., specific product may be exposed to high temps up to x°C for y minutes if MKT does not exceed z°C)
  • Maintain operating rate of x°C to x°C — expected results
  • Maintain operating range of x°C to x°C — acceptance criteria
  • Factory installed access port for installation of temperature mapping sensors

Note: MKT is not generally used for cold rooms or freezers, but is commonly applied to controlled room temperature storage.

Independent Temperature Monitoring System

  • High and low temperature alarm and paging to alert staff in the event of an excursion

Automation System Functions

  • Local display of operational status, temperature, and alarm conditions
  • Access control security to the panel and set points
  • Local audible/visual alarms for high, low, high-high, and low-low temperatures and general system fault
  • Factory installed access port for installation of temperature mapping sensors
  • Critical alarms displayed at X; Non-critical alarms displayed at Y

Alarms Classification

Data and Security

  • Data storage capacity requirements
  • Security access requirements
  • Archive requirements
  • Data Security and Integrity — recorded data cannot be modified

Commentary & CDMO Application

The Difference Between "Operating Rate" and "Acceptance Criteria"

The URS must clearly distinguish between the operating rate (the equipment's target operating temperature) and the operating range (the acceptance criteria/specification upper and lower limits). These correspond respectively to normal-operation verification in OQ and worst-case verification in PQ.

GMP Significance of the Alarm System Architecture

  • Level 1 (Low/High): alerts the operator to a trend
  • Level 2 (Low-Low/High-High): a GMP deviation that requires immediate action
  • DCS integration: ensures alarm records enter the audit trail governed by 21 CFR Part 11

Why an Independent Monitoring System Matters

The URS specifically requires an "independent" temperature monitoring system. This means the monitoring sensors must not share circuits with the equipment's control sensors, and the two systems must be able to cross-check each other. This is a common point of inspection for FDA inspectors.

Sensor Access Ports

Requiring "sensor access ports pre-installed at the factory" is a smart design choice. If ports are drilled after the cold room has been built, the seal integrity must be revalidated, which is both time-consuming and risky.

Data Integrity: How Do You Verify "Not Modifiable"?

The requirement "recorded data shall not be modifiable" must be supplemented in the URS with: (1) how the system prevents modification (technical controls), and (2) how an audit record is left when a correction is needed (21 CFR Part 11 §11.10(e)). Both points must be tested in OQ.

  2. Do the "independent monitoring system" and the equipment control system need to be validated separately? What documentation does each need?
  3. If a CDMO client requires a data access log, which URS subsection should it go in? Which tier of requirement is it?

How to write the front matter of a URS that will survive regulatory scrutiny

Section 1 — General: Purpose and Scope (Source Text)

Commentary & CDMO Application

"Describe What Is to Be Achieved, Not How"

Section 1 of the sample URS states clearly that the document does not prescribe how the requirements are to be met. This is the core difference between a URS and a DS (Design Specification). If the URS starts to specify brands, models or engineering methods, it has overstepped.

The Living Document Principle

The URS should be updated over the equipment lifecycle. A common CDMO problem: after 5 years of use the functionality has been expanded, but the URS was never updated, so later validation loses its traceability basis. It is advisable to include a "Change Summary" section at the end of the URS (the sample already includes this section).

The Role of the Definitions Section

CDMOs and clients often misunderstand each other because of inconsistent terminology. Defining the composition of the "SME" in the URS (QA, engineering, validation, production) can avoid disputes over responsibility at later acceptance. The definition of RTD is critical for choosing the type of monitoring sensor.

The References Section Must Not Be Left Empty

Many companies enter "N/A" here. In practice it should list the applicable FDA guidance, EU GMP annexes, site SOP numbers and the equipment supplier's technical standards. These documents are core review material during DQ.

The Validation Function of the System Description

Sample URS — Section 5: Capacity & Product Requirements

Translating business needs into traceable, numbered requirements

5.1 Process Requirements: Capacity (Source Text)

5.2 Process Requirements: Product Physical Properties

[To be completed: product types, weight per pallet, temperature excursion limits for each product category stored in this unit.]

Commentary & CDMO Application

The Value of Numbered Requirements

The numbering in URS 5.1, 5.2, 5.3 and so on is the basis of the validation traceability matrix. Every IQ/OQ/PQ test item must point to at least one URS ID. If requirements are not numbered, traceability cannot be established.

"Should" vs "Shall" vs "Must"

  • Shall / Must: mandatory requirement; validation must confirm it
  • Should: recommendation/target; exceptions are acceptable but the rationale must be documented

5.2 uses "should" (a design condition) while 5.1 uses "must" (a hard requirement). This choice of language is deliberate, and CDMO engineers need to understand the difference.

External Design Conditions

The external temperature range of -2.8°C to 36.7°C comes from ASHRAE standards and represents the climatic extremes of the region where the equipment is located (for example, the US). This figure affects the calculation of the refrigeration capacity of the cooling system and is a fundamental technical parameter for IQ.

The GMP Significance of "No Material Stored on the Floor"

Keeping material off the floor is a GMP precaution (against moisture and contamination, and to facilitate cleaning). But the URS must go further and specify: "The underside of all shelving shall be at least X cm above the floor, leaving space for cleaning tools to reach." Only then is it a testable requirement.

Commentary & Qualification Link

Why an Independent Monitoring System Matters (6.1-6.3)

The URS explicitly requires that the control system and the monitoring system be separate and independent: the control system drives the compressors to hold temperature, while the monitoring system records GMP data and triggers alarms.

The two monitoring probes are placed in the warmest zone and the coldest zone identified by temperature mapping. This shows how mapping results directly shape equipment configuration. The 0.5°C accuracy requirement must be verified through calibration during OQ.

Exam focus: monitoring probe locations are determined by mapping, not chosen arbitrarily by the supplier.

BMS Integration Requirement (6.8): the Core of GMP Data Integrity

Requirement 6.8 states that temperature data must be archived to the site BMS (building management system). This means:

  • Data cannot reside only in the equipment's local memory; it must be transferred to a controlled BMS
  • The BMS itself must meet 21 CFR Part 11 or equivalent requirements (audit trail, electronic signatures)
  • IQ must confirm the BMS communication link; OQ must confirm the integrity of data transfer
  • PQ must confirm that the BMS records continuously, without interruption, under actual operating conditions

Analogy: The Alarm Delay Timer (6.13)

Picture a "grace period" on every alarm. A door open for 5 seconds should not trigger an alarm immediately, because an operator may simply be passing in and out quickly. The delay timer lets the system alarm only after a genuine excursion persists, which reduces nuisance alarms.

This delay setting must be recorded and tested in OQ: "Open the door for X seconds and confirm that the alarm triggers after Y seconds."

Scope of 21 CFR Part 11 (Note to 6.6)

The URS explicitly states that the cold room control system (which does not provide records) need not comply with 21 CFR Part 11, whereas the independent monitoring system (which provides GMP records) must. This is a deliberate scoping decision: it avoids over-compliance that adds cost while still protecting quality-critical data.

When writing a URS, the CDMO needs to define clearly which subsystem falls within the scope of Part 11.

Redundant Design (Lead-Lag Mode) (6.10, 6.16)

Lead-Lag mode: two compressor units alternate as the lead and the lag (standby) unit, which equalizes wear and service life. When the lead unit fails or the temperature goes out of range, the system switches automatically to the lag unit. This is a business continuity requirement: the configuration is confirmed in IQ, and failover is tested by simulating a fault in OQ.

Hygienic Design for GMP (7.1, 7.2, 8.9)

Material compatibility and pest-proof design are fundamental to GMP facility design. The Section 7 requirements are verified in IQ: after installation, visually inspect that all seams are sealed and that the materials meet specification.

Spor-Klenz® is a hydrogen peroxide-based disinfectant that corrodes materials other than 316L stainless steel (for example, ordinary carbon steel and some plastics). The URS names the disinfectant explicitly so that the supplier can select the correct materials.

Dual Standards for Temperature Control Accuracy (8.1)

URS 8.1 specifies a control sensor accuracy of 0.25°C (measurement uncertainty) / 0.5°C (accuracy). Note:

  • Control sensor (8.1): 0.25°C. It drives the compressors and determines how well the equipment can actually control temperature
  • Monitoring sensor (6.3): 0.5°C. It records GMP data and triggers alarms

The two systems have different accuracy specifications because their functions differ. OQ must calibrate and verify the two systems separately.

Analogy: Door Curtains and Warm Air Infiltration (8.8)

Think of the cold room door as a gap through which hot air can invade. Each time the door opens, warm, humid outside air rushes in; the compressors need extra power to pull the temperature back, and frost builds up faster. A vinyl curtain is the simplest effective solution: it needs no power, cannot fail, yet significantly reduces the heat load.

In PQ, temperature recovery time is tested under operating conditions with frequent door openings, which verifies the design effect of 8.8.

  • Confirm that the door curtain or an equivalent device is installed (8.8)
  • Confirm that the drainage design prevents condensate from dripping (8.7)
  • Confirm the installation position of the evaporator RTD (8.2, record the serial number)
  • Confirm the utility connection specifications (9.1, condensate pressure and temperature)

Commentary

Power Redundancy Strategy (10.4-10.5)

Together, these two requirements ensure redundancy of the power supply:

  • 10.4: the cold room is connected to standby generator power, so the compressors keep running during a power outage
  • 10.5: the two compressor units are connected to different MCCs (motor control centers), so the failure of one MCC does not affect the other compressor

IQ must confirm that the power circuits of the two compressors really come from different MCCs and record the MCC numbers. OQ must simulate a power loss on a single MCC and confirm that the other compressor takes over automatically.

The Boundary Between EHS Requirements and GMP Qualification

The Section 11 requirements (noise, emergency exit, refrigerant monitoring) are EHS type requirements, but this does not mean they can be ignored in pharmaceutical qualification:

  • Noise (11.1): IQ visually confirms the sound-insulation design; OQ measures the actual dB value
  • Emergency exit (11.2): IQ confirms that the internal emergency release handle is installed; the functional test is completed in IQ
  • Emergency button (11.3): IQ confirms installation; OQ functional test (button pressed, external indicator light comes on, BMS receives the alarm)
  • Refrigerant leak monitoring (11.4): IQ confirms sensor installation; OQ functional test (simulate a trigger and check the fire alarm system response)

Analogy: Electrical Standards (10.1)

NEC/UL/CE are like building code permits: they ensure minimum safety standards. In GMP qualification they are the "entry ticket". If the equipment does not comply with NEC/UL, IQ fails outright, because if even basic safety compliance is not met, pharmaceutical quality compliance is out of the question.

Refrigerant Leaks and GMP (11.4)

Modern cold rooms often use HFC or low-GWP refrigerants. A refrigerant leak:

  • Endangers personnel safety (asphyxiation or poisoning at high concentrations)
  • Affects product integrity (compressor shutdown, then temperature excursion, then an OOS event)
  • Requires environmental compliance reporting (refrigerant emission regulations)

Therefore, although 11.4 is labeled EHS, it is in practice also directly linked to GMP data integrity and product protection.

Commentary

SOP Requirements in the URS (13.2-13.3)

The URS regulates not only the equipment itself but also operating documentation. Requirements 13.2 and 13.3 explicitly require the SOP to cover:

  • Permitted storage locations (to avoid blocking airflow and affecting temperature uniformity)
  • Maximum storage quantity (overloading may exceed the equipment design load)
  • Alarm response procedure (temperature alarm, then assessment, handling and recording)

These SOPs are a prerequisite for the PQ phase: PQ must be run under SOP guidance to verify system performance under actual use conditions.

Impact of Maintenance Access (13.1) on Qualification

Requirement 13.1 is the basis for IQ and the maintenance plan. In IQ, confirm:

  • Whether all access routes needed for maintenance (1 m minimum clearance) have been preserved
  • Whether instrument calibration points are accessible (6.2 already requires sensors to be designed for ease of calibration)
  • Whether, in an emergency repair, technicians can reach critical components without removing product

If inadequate maintenance access is discovered after IQ, it is a Design Qualification (DQ) failure and requires an engineering change notice (ECN).

GMP Significance of Door Gaskets (13.4)

The door gasket is a weak point of a cold room: as it ages its sealing performance declines, warm air infiltration increases, and the risk of temperature excursions rises. A gasket designed for easy replacement means that:

  • The PM plan can replace it on schedule without stopping operations
  • A seal integrity test (such as a smoke test or a temperature stability check) must be performed after replacement
  • Whether change control applies depends on whether the replacement part is of the "same specification"

Matrix

URS-to-Qualification Traceability Matrix

Traceability Matrix — Cold Room URS Example

What Is a Traceability Matrix?

A traceability matrix is a document that links every URS requirement to the specific qualification test(s) that verify it was met. Without this linkage, there is no objective evidence that your qualification program actually tested what the URS demanded.

In regulatory inspections (FDA 483, EMA), auditors specifically ask: "Show me where in your OQ you tested requirement 6.3." If you cannot point to the test, the requirement is, in effect, untested.

Traceability Commentary

Three Core Principles of the Matrix

  • Coverage: every URS requirement is linked to at least one qualification test. If a requirement has no corresponding test, this is a gap in the qualification plan; it must be filled or recorded as "N/A (not applicable) with the reason".
  • Bidirectional traceability: you can find the tests starting from the URS (forward traceability), and you can trace from a test back to the URS requirement (backward traceability).
  • No double counting: one OQ test can verify several URS requirements, but every URS requirement must have at least one test.

Common Audit Findings: Matrix Gaps

  • URS 5.6 (full-load test): PQ was performed only under partial load. Finding: worst case not tested
  • URS 6.16 (automatic changeover): no fault simulation test in OQ. Finding: function not verified
  • BMS integration (6.8): connection confirmed in IQ, but PQ did not confirm long-term continuous recording. Finding: no evidence of sustained performance
  • URS requirement listed in the matrix, but the test references the wrong document number. Finding: traceability broken

Analogy: The Matrix as an Exam Answer Key

Imagine the URS is the exam questions and the qualification tests are the answers. The traceability matrix is the "question-to-answer cross-reference". If a question has no corresponding answer, the examiner (auditor) spots it immediately: however well the equipment actually works, a gap in the documentation is a gap.

CDMO operators often say, "But our equipment really does that!" Without documented evidence linked through the matrix, that statement carries no weight in the GMP world.

Practice Questions

  1. URS 6.11 requires three alarms (fan failure, door open too long, refrigeration system failure). Do you need three separate OQ tests or one test covering all of them? What is your reasoning?
  2. URS 5.6 requires testing in three states: empty, half-full and full. If the CDMO's PQ tested only a "typical operating load", is that compliant? How would you handle it?
  3. Your traceability matrix shows that URS 8.3 (FM fire protection requirement) is referenced only in IQ. The auditor asks, "How do you ensure the fire protection system remains effective throughout the life of the equipment?" How do you answer?

BMS Integration Checklist

BMS Integration Requirements & Technical Specifications

What BMS Integration Means in Practice

The Building Management System (BMS) — sometimes called SCADA, EMS, or EMCS — is the central nervous system of a pharmaceutical facility. For CTCs, BMS integration means:

  • Continuous temperature data transmitted from local sensor to BMS server
  • BMS generates time-stamped electronic records (21 CFR Part 11 compliant)
  • Remote alarm annunciation: phone/pager/email notification to on-call staff
  • Centralized alarm management: acknowledge, escalate, close with reason codes
  • Data export in validated format (CSV, PDF reports, API) for GMP batch records
  • Audit trail: who changed what alarm setpoint and when

IQ Phase — BMS Connection Verification

  • BMS communication protocol confirmed (Modbus RTU / BACnet / OPC-UA / 4–20 mA)
  • Physical wiring / network connection from CTC panel to BMS gateway verified
  • BMS tag names match URS and P&ID nomenclature
  • CTC sensor signals visible in BMS engineering workstation
  • Data historian (e.g., OSIsoft PI, Wonderware) configured for CTC tags
  • Alarm setpoints entered in BMS per approved alarm setpoint table
  • BMS server backup and disaster recovery procedures in place
  • 21 CFR Part 11 assessment of BMS software version completed
  • BMS user access levels configured and documented
  • Network firewall rules documented (cybersecurity review)

OQ Phase — BMS Functional Testing

  • Simulate temperature above action limit: confirm BMS alarm generated within defined time
  • Confirm alarm transmitted to on-call pager/phone within SLA (e.g., <5 min)
  • Confirm audible alarm at CTC panel (6.14) AND remote BMS notification both triggered
  • Acknowledge alarm at BMS: confirm local annunciation silenced, BMS record updated
  • Simulate BMS network outage: confirm CTC local logger continues recording
  • Restore BMS connection: confirm data gap identified and backfilled or documented
  • Test alarm escalation: if unacknowledged for X minutes, escalate to supervisor
  • Test data export: generate CSV / PDF temperature report for one week of data
  • Verify electronic audit trail captures setpoint change with user ID and timestamp
  • Test automatic log-off (URS 6.7) in BMS interface

PQ Phase — BMS Continuous Performance

  • Confirm BMS records uninterrupted data throughout full PQ duration (14–30 days typical)
  • Verify no unexplained data gaps in historian
  • Confirm all PQ temperature exceedances (if any) were detected and alarmed by BMS
  • Verify BMS report format matches GMP batch record requirements
  • Confirm periodic BMS backup executed during PQ without data loss

BMS Integration Commentary

Regulatory Basis for BMS Integration in GMP

The URS 6.8 requirement for "BMS archiving" sits at the intersection of several regulations:

  • 21 CFR Part 211.68: computerized systems must have accurate input and output, with backup and recovery capability
  • 21 CFR Part 11: electronic records need audit trails and electronic signatures (if they replace paper)
  • EU Annex 11: computerized system validation, including data integrity, access control and backup
  • ISPE GAMP 5: a BMS is usually classified as Category 4 (configured software) or Category 5 (custom software) and requires full validation

CDMO-Specific Challenge: BMS Management for Multiple Clients and Products

In a CDMO, the same cold room may store raw materials or finished products for different clients, so BMS data management faces:

  • Data segregation: Client A's temperature records must not be mixed with Client B's
  • Multiple reports: each batch needs a temperature report for the corresponding client (with different format requirements)
  • Alarm notification routing: should alarm notifications for a given cold room go to that client's responsible person or to the CDMO?
  • Access control: how much historical BMS data may a client auditor access?

These questions must be settled with clients during URS drafting; an inadequate design must not be discovered only after qualification.

Data Formats and API Integration

Current BMS integration trends include:

  • CSV export: a standard format that can be imported directly into GMP document systems (MES/LIMS)
  • OPC-UA: an industrial IoT standard protocol, securely encrypted, suited to cross-system integration
  • REST API: allows the quality system to query real-time temperature data directly and automatically trigger deviation investigations
  • MQTT: lightweight real-time data transmission, suited to monitoring multiple devices

The URS should specify the data format explicitly to avoid integration difficulties after the supplier delivers.

Analogy: The BMS Is the Equipment's "Black Box"

An aircraft black box records all flight data so that the sequence of events can be reconstructed after an accident. The BMS plays the same role for a CTC. When a product has an OOS (out of specification) problem, the inspector's first question is, "Please provide the temperature record for the 72 hours before and after the event." If the BMS record is complete, the investigation has evidence to work from; if BMS data is missing, the release decision for the whole batch rests on an incomplete foundation.

Practice Questions

  1. In an OQ test you simulate a 30-minute BMS network outage. The CTC local recorder keeps recording. After the network is restored, how do you confirm that the 30-minute data gap has been correctly recorded and handled? What type of GMP document does this belong to?
  2. A CDMO has 3 cold rooms storing APIs for different clients. Client A requires a weekly temperature report, Client B requires a report for every batch, and Client C requires real-time API access. Describe the factors to consider in the BMS configuration.
  3. FDA 483 observation: "The CTC temperature alarm setpoint in the BMS is inconsistent with the alarm setpoint specified in the URS." Describe your CAPA plan and preventive measures.

Supplier Documentation Management Commentary

Supplier Qualification and the URS

Before issuing the URS in a request for quotation, the pharmaceutical company should first perform supplier qualification (also called vendor qualification) on potential suppliers:

  • Questionnaire assessment: the supplier's quality system (ISO 9001?), GMP experience, and customer references
  • Audit: visit the supplier's plant to confirm manufacturing capability and quality management
  • Sample evaluation: ask the supplier for FAT records or qualification reports for the same equipment model
  • Quality Agreement: confirm the supplier's commitment to GMP requirements

Only suppliers that pass qualification enter the approved supplier list (ASL), and only then can the URS be sent to them for quotation.

FAT vs SAT: Overlapping Functions, Different Settings

A successful FAT reduces the risk at SAT and IQ: problems are found early, and the supplier is responsible for correcting them before shipment.

GMP Importance of As-Built Drawings

As-built drawings record the true state of the equipment after actual installation. They differ from design drawings: design drawings show what was "planned to be built", while as-built drawings show what was "actually built".

In IQ you must confirm that the as-built drawings have been updated: if any design change occurred during installation (such as pipe routing or the location of electrical connections), it must be reflected in the as-built drawing, otherwise the drawing is an inaccurate GMP record.

Subsequent maintenance, change control and requalification all rely on the as-built drawings as the baseline document.

Analogy: Supplier Documents Are the Equipment's "Birth Certificate"

Every document for the equipment (calibration certificates, material certificates, as-built drawings) together makes up its complete "identity". Like a person's birth certificate, education record and medical history, the lack of any one of them will cause a problem at some important moment:

  • No calibration certificate: IQ cannot be closed
  • No material COC: the supplier audit fails
  • No as-built drawings: no baseline for change control after maintenance
  • No O&M manual: operators have no basis for GMP training

CDMO Fleet Management Considerations

Managing Multiple CTCs as a Fleet

The Fleet Management Challenge

A typical pharma CDMO (aseptic fill-finish) operates 5–20+ controlled temperature chambers simultaneously: walk-in cold rooms (2–8°C), freezers (–20°C, –80°C), incubators (25°C/60%RH, 40°C/75%RH). Each requires its own URS, qualification records, calibration schedule, alarm response SOP, and PM plan. Managing this as independent silos is unsustainable at scale.

Fleet management treats similar CTCs as a standardized program, reducing documentation redundancy while maintaining compliance integrity.

Strategies for Fleet Qualification

  • Master Validation Plan (MVP) for CTCs: One MVP covers all CTCs, defining the common qualification strategy, acceptance criteria template, and periodic re-qualification schedule.
  • Generic Protocol Templates: One IQ/OQ template covers all cold rooms of the same model. Site-specific data (serial number, location, BMS tag) filled in per unit.
  • Lead Unit / Matrix Approach: Full IQ+OQ+PQ on the most challenging unit; reduced protocol for identical units (reference lead unit data).
  • Centralized Calibration Schedule: CMMS (Computerized Maintenance Management System) tracks all CTC calibration due dates fleet-wide.
  • Common SOP with Unit-Specific Appendix: One SOP for alarm response; Appendix A lists alarm setpoints for each CTC by room number.

Re-qualification Triggers (Fleet-Wide)

Any of the following events triggers a re-qualification assessment for the affected CTC(s):

  • Compressor replacement or major refrigeration repair
  • Significant change in loading pattern (new product type, new storage layout)
  • Temperature exceedance event (OOS — Out of Specification temperature)
  • Building renovation affecting HVAC or electrical supply to CTC
  • Periodic re-qualification due date (typically every 1–3 years per site policy)
  • Alarm setpoint change (requires change control + at minimum OQ re-test)
  • BMS system upgrade (requires IT validation + re-qualification of affected functions)

CDMO Scenario Commentary

A Single URS vs the Reality of Multiple Units

The example URS in Appendix 1 is for a single cold room. In CDMO practice, you may be managing at the same time:

  • 3 walk-in cold rooms from Supplier A (same model)
  • 2 walk-in cold rooms from Supplier B (different models)
  • 4 –20°C freezers from different suppliers
  • 2 –80°C ultra-low temperature freezers

Best practice: prepare one generic URS for each "equipment family" (same supplier, same model), and attach the data specific to individual units as an "Appendix".

VMP Design for a CDMO

A VMP for a CTC fleet should include:

  • A list of all CTCs (model, location, temperature range, use)
  • Shared IQ/OQ/PQ acceptance criteria templates
  • A periodic requalification schedule (next due date for each unit)
  • A seasonal re-mapping policy (once in summer and once in winter, or once a year?)
  • An SOP for requalification after a temperature excursion event
  • The scientific rationale for the matrix approach (if adopted)

Analogy: Fleet Management Is Like Quality Standards in a Hotel Chain

All hotels in the same chain share service standards (SOPs) and fit-out specifications (URS), but each hotel has its own room numbers (equipment serial numbers) and address (installation location). Customers (clients/auditors) can expect the same quality of service at any branch.

Managing a CDMO's CTC fleet works the same way: standardization does not lower requirements; it makes compliance management more efficient and more consistent.

  3. No BMS integration specification: Leaving BMS as "supplier shall connect to plant BMS" without protocol, data format, or tag naming convention.
  4. No documentation list: Expecting suppliers to know what GMP documents you need — be explicit.
  5. Treating the URS as a shopping list: Writing requirements without a plan for how each will be verified during qualification.

Introduction & Purpose

This appendix provides a sample document defining the number and location of temperature mapping sensors, together with the load test rationale. It uses a Client's Warehouse as the worked example, but the principles apply to any controlled temperature chamber (CTC).

The mapping study serves two purposes:

  • Characterise the spatial temperature distribution within the chamber under simulated normal use
  • Identify the maximum and minimum temperature locations, which then govern where permanent monitoring sensors are installed

Where the stored product has no critical humidity requirements, humidity need not be mapped or monitored — decisions about scope should be driven by product CQAs, not by convention.

Overview and Purpose

Appendix 2 uses warehouse mapping as an example to show how to select the number and location of sensors rationally. The core logic is that mapping serves the permanent monitoring that follows: first use dense mapping to find the worst-case locations, then use those results to decide where to install the permanent sensors.

Core Concept: Mapping vs. Monitoring

  • Temperature Mapping: a validation activity, performed once or periodically. Its purpose is to find the hottest and coldest points
  • Temperature Monitoring: routine operation, running continuously. Monitoring sensor locations are determined by the mapping results
  • Together they form the complete temperature control strategy of a CTC

Analogy: A City Weather Station

Before a city is built, temporary weather sensors are deployed densely (mapping) to find the hottest and coldest spots, and permanent weather stations (monitoring) are then set up at those representative locations. CTC mapping follows exactly the same logic.

Appendix 2 — Heat Gain & Loss Analysis

Heat Gain/Loss Analysis: Identifying External and Internal Heat Sources

Identifying Heat Sources Before Sensor Placement

Before selecting sensor locations, the engineer must analyse all external and internal heat gains and losses. This analysis directly determines which zones are likely to show temperature extremes.

External Heat Gains and Losses

  • North wall and ceiling — primary conductive heat exchange paths with the outdoor environment
  • Other perimeter walls — typically surrounded by air-conditioned spaces, so influence is smaller
  • Loading dock doors — during normal operation, doors open for loading/unloading; vinyl curtains with inflatable truck seals mitigate but do not eliminate warm air ingress

Internal Heat Gains and Losses

  • Lighting: low-energy fluorescent lamps left on during the working day — small but constant load
  • Product: supplied at storage temperature — negligible thermal load
  • Equipment: stacking forklift — small, intermittent load
  • Personnel: approximately two people — small transient load
  • Air conditioning outlets: single system with equally spaced duct outlets in aisles

Heat Source Analysis: The Scientific Premise for Sensor Placement

Sensors cannot be placed arbitrarily. You must first understand which locations are likely to show temperature extremes so that sensors can be concentrated there. Heat source analysis is the starting point of this chain of reasoning.

Key Principle: Heat Source = Potential Mapping Hot Spot

  • Exterior walls (especially north walls/roof) → summer hot spots, winter cold spots
  • Near loading dock doors → the area with the greatest temperature impact when the door is opened
  • Near air supply outlets → potentially the coldest area (direct blast from the cooling outlet)
  • Equipment heat sources (forklift charging stations, lighting, etc.) → local hot spots

Analogy: Finding the Hottest/Coldest Spot in an Office

Before choosing a seat in an office, observe first: next to the window (hot in summer), under the air-conditioning vent (coldest), beside the copier (warm). The logic of mapping sensor placement is exactly the same: decide where to put sensors based on heat source analysis, not by spreading them evenly.

CDMO Sterile Drug Product Warehouse Applications

  • WFI tank room → heat dissipation from insulated piping is an internal heat source
  • Freeze dryer room → condenser heat dissipation is the main heat source
  • 2–8°C drug product cold room → the effect of the temperature difference at the corridor-side wall must be evaluated
  • Door seal integrity at entrances and exits → must be confirmed before each mapping study

Appendix 2 — Sensor Placement Rationale

Sensor Placement Principles: Corners, Center, High/Low Points, Doorways

Sensor Location Selection Principles

The Guide references the French Standard NF X15-140 (October 2002) — "Measurement of air moisture – Climatic and thermostatic chambers – Characterization and verification" — as guidance for determining sensor locations.

The chamber is first divided into zones, each served by one air conditioning vent. Key placement rules:

  • A sensor at the lowest and highest product storage points in each zone
  • A sensor at each corner of the room on the nearest racking/product position
  • One sensor in the centre of the space at mid-level
  • Additional sensors near external walls and doors — where heat exchange is greatest

The symmetrical nature of central zones allowed a reduced sensor count in those areas compared to the end zones (which have door and end-wall influences). Sensor reduction must be scientifically justified, not assumed.

For permanent monitoring system sensor placement, the mapping data is used to identify the locations of maximum and minimum temperature — the monitoring sensors go there, providing the earliest warning of an out-of-limit event.

Sensor Placement Principles in Detail

Standard Sensor Placement Logic (NF X15-140 Principles)

  • Zone: use the air supply outlets as dividing lines and consider each zone independently
  • High and low points: one sensor each at the highest and lowest product storage position in each zone
  • Corners: one sensor at each of the four corners of the space (the corner effect is most pronounced)
  • Center: one sensor at the midpoint of the space at mid-height (reference baseline)
  • Near doorways: the most direct path for warm air infiltration
  • Exterior wall side: where heat conduction has the greatest effect
  • Near the evaporator coil: usually the coldest area

The core principle for sensor count: enough is enough; more than the design needs is wasteful, and fewer than required is risky. A symmetrical layout can justify reducing the number of sensors, but the end zones (near the door) must not be reduced.

Key Point: Why Not Use Just One Sensor?

A single sensor can only tell you the temperature at its own location. If product is stored in a corner while the sensor is in the center, the actual maximum temperature in the corner may exceed the PAR without the alarm ever being triggered. This is a patient safety risk, not a technical debate.

Appendix 2 — Load Testing & Seasonal Variation

Load Testing Strategy and Seasonal Condition Coverage

Load Test Strategy

The warehouse must be mapped under two distinct load scenarios. In both cases, minimum thermal mass is used so that temperature changes propagate quickly and worst-case conditions are observable:

  • "Empty" condition: No product in chamber. No airflow obstruction; lowest average air velocity throughout the space. This reveals the equipment's fundamental temperature distribution.
  • "Full" condition (simulated): The front 1.5 zones are filled with empty boxes simulating the largest stored product case. Empty boxes provide minimum thermal mass but maximum airflow interruption — showing the worst-case impact of racking on temperature distribution.

The rationale for focusing on front zones: the warehouse is symmetrical, so understanding the door-adjacent zone under load is sufficient to characterise the most at-risk area.

Seasonal Variation Coverage

The initial mapping should be conducted in summer (worst-case heat load). The mapping is not considered complete until seasonal variation exceeding the 90% design conditions has been experienced. Local meteorological data provides the 90%/95% summer and winter boundary conditions used in design.

Data from the summer campaign may justify a reduced sensor array for the subsequent winter mapping, based on demonstrated symmetry and stable performance.

Load Testing and Seasonal Conditions in Detail

Analogy: Why Use Empty Boxes Instead of Real Product?

Real product has substantial thermal mass, which buffers temperature shocks and makes worst-case temperature excursions look smaller. Empty boxes have volume (obstructing airflow) but almost no thermal mass, forcing the system to be exposed under the least favorable conditions. This means actively looking for the worst case rather than masking it.

The Logic of the Two Load Conditions

  • Empty：
    • Airflow is least obstructed → shows the temperature distribution capability of the equipment itself
    • Usually more uniform, but dead spots can still be found
  • Simulated Full：
    • Empty boxes block airflow → may create local hot/cold spots
    • Minimal thermal mass → temperature shocks propagate quickly and the worst case is clearly visible

Seasonal Verification: It Cannot Be Done Only Once

A single summer mapping cannot represent the worst case across the whole year. In winter, heat loss through exterior walls/roof may cause cold corners, which would not be seen in a summer study. A complete mapping study must cover both the summer and winter extremes.

Regulators (including WHO GDP and EU GDP) increasingly expect seasonal mapping as part of complete documentation.

Seasonal Mapping Practice at a CDMO

  • Taiwan sites: summer (June–September) heat and humidity is the worst-case external heat load
  • In winter (January–February), the northeast monsoon may increase cold loss through the north wall
  • Planned mapping timing: perform the initial mapping immediately after completion in summer; perform the winter confirmation in January–February of the following year
  • If a customer requires annual requalification, it can be aligned with the summer peak

Appendix 3 — Measurement Uncertainty: Overview & Concepts

Appendix 3: Measurement Uncertainty Overview and Core Concepts  |  §15 · Source pp. 77–84

Why Measurement Uncertainty Matters

The maximum and minimum temperature locations are identified during mapping. However, the data recorded is not the "true" temperature — it is a measurement subject to uncertainty from multiple sources:

  • Sensor calibration uncertainty
  • Data logger/input module uncertainty
  • Sensor placement effects (thermal contact, convective environment)

For monitoring sensor alarm limits, the Guide distinguishes two scenarios:

  1. Monitoring sensor located at the max/min spot: Use only the sensor's own measurement uncertainty (COD/MU) to set alarm limits
  2. Monitoring sensor NOT at the max/min spot: Add an offset representing the temperature difference between the monitoring location and the true max/min, in addition to the COD/MU

In both cases, the measurement uncertainty of the mapping sensors used to determine the offset must also be included in the calculation.

Why Measurement Uncertainty Matters

Every temperature measuring instrument has error. The question is not whether error exists (it always does), but how large it is and how to take it into account when designing acceptance criteria rather than ignoring it.

Three Sources of Measurement Uncertainty

  • Sensor calibration uncertainty: even immediately after calibration, there is an accuracy range of ±X°C
  • Data logger uncertainty: error from the signal conversion (analog → digital) process
  • Location effect: systematic error caused by the spatial offset between the sensor and the actual worst-case temperature location

Analogy: Thermometer Uncertainty

A thermometer reads 37.2°C, but the manufacturer states an accuracy of ±0.2°C, so the "true" body temperature may be 37.0–37.4°C. If you use this thermometer to decide whether a patient has a fever (threshold 37.5°C), then at a reading of 37.4°C you cannot be sure whether the patient truly has a fever. Measurement uncertainty lets us be honest about the limits of measurement and leave a margin of protection in the decision criteria.

Different Calculations for Two Monitoring Sensor Locations

  • Placed at the worst-case location (ideal): only the MU of this one sensor needs to be considered
  • Placed at another location (common): a location offset must be added, plus the combined MU of the two sensors

The larger the offset, the narrower the permitted alarm range, sometimes so narrow that the monitoring scheme becomes impractical. In that case, the best solution is to reselect the sensor location.

Appendix 3 — Guard-Band Formula & Decision Limits

Guard-Band Formula and Derivation of Decision Limits

The Guard-Band Formula

The ISPE Guide provides explicit formulae for calculating decision limits (DL) from the Proven Acceptable Range (PAR) and measurement uncertainty:

For the LOW limit: DL_low ≥ PAR_low + [GB – Usr] For the HIGH limit: DL_high ≤ PAR_high – [GB – Usr] Where: DL = Decision Limit (alarm trigger point) PAR = Proven Acceptable Range GB = Guard Band = k × u_system k = coverage factor (minimum 2.00, ≥97.7% confidence) u_sys = combined measurement uncertainty of the system Usr = Required Uncertainty of Limits (usually = 0)

When Usr = 0 (no allowable excursion from PAR), the formula simplifies to:

DL_high = PAR_high – GB DL_low = PAR_low + GB GB = k × u_system (k ≥ 2)

When a sensor is located at a position that is offset from the max/min temperature location, the offset is incorporated:

GB_with_offset = offset + k × √(u_T1² + u_T2²)

where u_T1 and u_T2 are the uncertainties of the two sensors involved (the mapping sensor at the extreme and the monitoring sensor).

The Guard-Band Formula in Detail

Explanation of Core Terms

  • PAR (Proven Acceptable Range): the temperature range within which the product can be stored safely, for example 2–8°C. It is derived from stability studies.
  • Guard Band (GB): a safety buffer moved inward from the PAR boundary, equal to k × the system measurement uncertainty
  • Decision Limit (DL): the value at which the alarm actually triggers. The DL is tighter than the PAR, leaving room for MU
  • k = 2: corresponds to a 97.7% (one-sided) confidence level, meaning the probability that the true value exceeds the PAR is only 2.3%

Appendix 3 — Worked Examples A, B & C

Worked Examples: Sensor Location and Alarm Limit Setting

Shared Given Conditions for All Examples

u_T1 = u_T2 = u_T3 = u_T4 = u_T5 = u_T6 = 0.125°C Usr = 0°C (no allowable excursion from PAR) k = 2 (≥97.7% confidence, single-sided) PAR = 2°C to 8°C GB = u_sys × k = 0.125 × 2 = 0.25°C

Example A — All Sensors Within Guard Band

Condition: All mapping sensors show readings within 0.25°C (= GB) of each other. The temperature differences between locations can be attributed entirely to measurement uncertainty — there is no true spatial gradient.

Conclusion: The monitoring sensor can be placed at any mapping sensor location, since all are equivalent.

Alarm limits:

DL_high = PAR_high – (GB – Usr) = 8 – (0.25 – 0) = 7.75°C DL_low = PAR_low + (GB – Usr) = 2 + (0.25 – 0) = 2.25°C

Example B — Sensor at Non-Extreme Location

Condition: Sensor 1 = 7.0°C (highest), Sensor 4 = 6.5°C (lowest), at the same time. Difference = 0.5°C > GB. Monitoring sensor 5 is co-located with Sensor 4.

Option B(a) — Leave monitoring sensor at Sensor 4 position:

DL_high = 8 – (0.5 + 2√(uT1² + uT4²) – 0) = 8 – (0.5 + 2×0.177) = 8 – 0.854 = 7.15°C DL_low = 2 + (GB – Usr) = 2.25°C (unchanged)

Option B(b) — Relocate monitoring sensor to Sensor 1 (high temp) position:

DL_high = 8 – (GB – Usr) = 7.75°C (unchanged) DL_low = 2 + (0.5 + 2×0.177) = 2.85°C

Example A Analysis

Key Takeaway from Example A

When the differences among all sensor readings are ≤ GB (that is, the differences can be fully explained by measurement uncertainty), the spatial temperature distribution is considered "uniform" and the monitoring sensor can be placed anywhere. The alarm limits are then reduced by only one GB:

Acceptance interval: 2.25°C to 7.75°C (each side narrowed by 0.25°C)

This is the simplest case, and in practice many small cold rooms may meet this condition.

Example B Analysis

Intuitive Understanding: The Trade-Off in Sensor Location Selection

Imagine you are in an unevenly heated room, with a hottest corner (7.0°C) and the spot where you are sitting (6.5°C).

  • Option B(a) stay where you are: you feel 6.5°C, but the hottest corner is 7.0°C. Your alarm system has to sound earlier (at 7.15°C instead of 7.75°C), because it must compensate for the 0.5°C gap you cannot see
  • Option B(b) move to the hot spot: you monitor directly at the hottest location, and the high-temperature alarm returns to the looser 7.75°C, but you now need to be more sensitive on the low-temperature alarm (2.85°C instead of 2.25°C)

Engineering Decision Principle

If the offset is large (for example >0.5°C), staying at a non-worst-case location will narrow the DL greatly, and alarms will be too frequent in practice. In that case, relocating the sensor is the better engineering solution.

Example C — Two Extremes, One Monitor

Condition: Sensor 1 = 7.0°C (high), Sensor 2 = 6.0°C (low), Sensor 4 = 6.5°C (monitoring position). High-low spread = 1.0°C.

C(a) Monitor stays at Sensor 4:

DL_high = 8–(0.5+2√(uT1²+uT4²)) = 7.15°C DL_low = 2+(0.5+2√(uT2²+uT4²)) = 2.85°C

C(b) Monitor to high temp location (Sensor 1):

DL_high = 7.75°C (unchanged) DL_low = 2+(1.0+2√(uT2²+uT1²)) = 3.35°C

C(c) Monitor to low temp location (Sensor 2):

DL_high = 8–(1.0+2√(uT1²+uT2²)) = 6.65°C DL_low = 2.25°C (unchanged)

C(d) Two monitors — one at high, one at low:

DL_high = 8–(GB–0) = 7.75°C DL_low = 2+(GB–0) = 2.25°C (No offset; each sensor monitors only its side)

Example C Analysis and Strategy Selection

Engineering Significance of Option C(d)

When the offset between the high and low temperature points is large (1.0°C in this example), any single sensor must bear the entire uncertainty burden of the side it "cannot see", which narrows the alarm limits greatly.

Best solution: install one monitoring sensor each at the hottest point and the coldest point. The high-temperature sensor handles only the high-temperature alarm, and the low-temperature sensor handles only the low-temperature alarm. The offset drops to zero, the guard band equals a single GB (0.25°C), and the alarm range is widest.

Regulatory Review Perspective

A DL_high of 6.65°C in Option C(c) is exceptionally tight for a 2–8°C chamber, and any brief temperature fluctuation could trigger an alarm. A reviewer will ask: "Does your alarm system generate so many false alarms that operators end up ignoring alarms?" This is a core question in ISPE risk management.

Example Protocol: Complete Application of Measurement Uncertainty to a -20°C Freezer

Step-by-Step Protocol

System Description: -20°C freezer, 20 ft × 20 ft × 10 ft, with redundant compressor/evaporator systems in a CRT warehouse. Single door opens to warehouse; one wall exposed to external temperatures.

PAR: -17°C to -3°C. Usr = 0 (product fails if outside these limits per stability studies).

Step 1 — Define sensor locations (32 sensors justified):

  • Sensors 1–9: Basic layout — high/low at each corner + centre
  • Sensors 10–25: High, middle, and low on fixed racks (airflow restriction)
  • Sensor 26: External door to unconditioned space
  • Sensors 27–29: External wall (>10°C differential from set point)
  • Sensor 30: Potential defrost cycle heat exposure
  • Sensor 31: External conditions (information only; no acceptance criteria)
  • Sensor 32: Control/monitoring sensor location

Step 2 — Equipment: Validator 2000 Data Logger with T-Type Thermocouples. Accuracy = ±0.125°C. Calibration confirmed valid.

Step 3 — Calculate GB and DL:

GB = k × u_sys = 2 × 0.125 = 0.25°C DL_high = –3 – 0.25 = –3.25°C DL_low = –17 + 0.25 = –16.75°C

Step 4 — Setup: Sample rate: every 5 minutes. Test duration: minimum 24 hours (to capture defrost cycles and redundant system switchover).

Step 5 — Evaluate: Record min/max per sensor. If all readings within DL, uniformity acceptable. Compare min/max to monitoring device; determine if relocation or offset adjustment is required.

Example Protocol Analysis

Why Are 32 Sensors Needed?

The Guide explicitly states that this is an "excessive for demonstration purposes" example, intended to show the rationale for various location choices. In actual practice, the number can reasonably be reduced according to scale and complexity, but every sensor needs a documented rationale for its location.

-20°C Freezer Calculation Summary

PAR: –17°C to –3°C u_sys: 0.125°C k: 2 GB: 0.25°C DL_high: –3.25°C (0.25°C tighter than the PAR upper limit) DL_low: –16.75°C (0.25°C tighter than the PAR lower limit)

Why Test for at Least 24 Hours?

Freezers have a defrost cycle: the system periodically stops cooling to melt the frost on the evaporator. During defrost, the chamber temperature rises briefly. If testing lasts only a few hours and does not cover a defrost cycle, the mapping data cannot represent the worst case of normal operation. Likewise, if there is a redundant system (Cooling System A/B alternating), the changeover event must be covered.

CDMO -80°C Ultra-Low Temperature Freezer Applications

  • Defrost events in ultra-low temperature freezers may be more pronounced; confirm that the mapping duration is long enough to capture them
  • Sensor calibration range must cover -80°C down to at least -90°C (sufficient margin)
  • Freezers usually do not allow the door to be opened during mapping; a special cable entry design (sealed feed-through port) is required
  • Power failure simulation test: confirm the temperature response when the UPS backup power or standby generator switches over

Appendix 5 — Science & Risk-Based Development of Testing

Appendix 5: Science- and Risk-Based Test Design  |  §17 · Source pp. 87–91

Purpose and Framework

Appendix 5 describes a structured risk assessment process to determine the risks to product quality from CTC equipment and define associated risk controls. The output of the risk assessment drives the scope and depth of qualification testing — including how many sensors, which scenarios, and how long to test.

Key assumptions in this approach:

  • The output of the system = the Product
  • The inputs to the system are Qualified (meet specification)
  • The review covers aspects within the system boundary
  • Focus is on product quality (patient safety)
  • Maintenance and calibration quality systems are assumed to be in place and effective (to avoid FMEA becoming unmanageably detailed)

Information used for the assessment:

  • Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs)
  • Critical Process Parameters (CPPs)
  • Potential risks to product quality
  • Source of each risk
  • Risk controls (design or procedural)
  • Means of detection if controls fail

Risk-Based Test Design Framework

Not every CTC requires the same degree of validation testing. The core principle of ICH Q9 is that the depth and frequency of testing should be proportionate to the level of risk. Low-risk equipment receives basic validation; high-risk equipment (storing high-value, highly sensitive products) receives more rigorous and more frequent validation.

Application of ICH Q9 in CTC Validation

  • Q9 principle: quality risk management should be based on scientific knowledge, with patient safety as the highest consideration
  • CQA identification: for example, protein activity of a biologic (temperature-sensitive) determines how strict the PAR is
  • CPP identification: chamber temperature (the most critical control parameter)
  • Risk control tools: alarm systems, backup refrigeration, UPS, SOPs (limits on door-opening frequency/duration)

Analogy: Insurance Premium Pricing

An insurance company charges higher premiums to high-risk drivers and lower premiums to low-risk drivers. CTC risk assessment follows a similar logic: a high-risk chamber (a -80°C freezer storing expensive biologics) needs a "more expensive" validation program (more sensors, more scenarios, more frequent requalification); a low-risk chamber (a CRT warehouse storing general consumables) can use a leaner program.

Relationship to IQ/OQ

The scope of the risk assessment in this appendix is aligned with traditional IQ/OQ or IOQ. Risk assessment does not replace validation; it is the rational basis for deciding what the validation should do, how deeply, and for how long. This gives validation activities a purpose, rather than being a mechanical execution of a checklist.

Appendix 5 — Defining Scope & Risk Assessment Process

Risk Assessment Process and Scope Definition

Risk Assessment Process Structure

The ISPE Guide recommends a two-stage process for efficiency:

  1. Initial draft by a small number of Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) — generates the system description, control strategy, and preliminary risk list
  2. Review and scoring by a larger cross-functional team including Quality/Validation, Operations, and Engineering representatives, led by an experienced facilitator

Pre-defined action levels:

  • Low risk: Accepted as-is
  • Medium risk: Can be accepted by team, or additional controls may be added
  • High risk: Must have additional controls added to reduce to Medium or Low before acceptance

Scoring uses the three-dimensional model:

  • Severity (S) — impact of failure on product CQA
  • Occurrence (O) — likelihood the failure will happen
  • Detection (D) — ability of existing controls to detect the failure

The risk assessment report must contain: system description and control strategy; CQAs; CPPs; Critical Aspects (CAs) where applicable; methodology; scope; results summary; mitigation plans; communication to senior management where required; and conclusions.

Risk Assessment Process Analysis

Practical Wisdom of the Two-Stage Process

  • Stage 1 (drafting by a small team): fast, avoiding large meetings getting bogged down in detail. SMEs first form the framework of the risk list
  • Stage 2 (cross-functional review and scoring): brings in shop-floor operating knowledge and avoids the gap of "engineers design, operators execute".

This structure also meets the ICH Q9 expectation of "diverse perspectives": a risk assessment cannot be completed by one person alone.

FMEA vs. the Lightweight Version in This Guide

A full FMEA (Failure Mode and Effects Analysis) analyzes every possible failure mode and may generate hundreds of lines of analysis. This Guide deliberately treats the maintenance and calibration quality systems as "in place and effective" to avoid excessive detail. This keeps the risk assessment focused on the inherent risks at the system design level rather than the details at the procedural execution level.

List of Common CTC Risk Factors (CDMO Perspective)

  • Product temperature sensitivity (protein formulations > small-molecule drugs > consumables)
  • Chamber equipment age and reliability history
  • Load criticality (difference in temperature uniformity between full and partial loads)
  • Product value (financial impact of a batch failure)
  • Severity of seasonal environmental extremes
  • Single point of failure risk (no redundant refrigeration system)
  • Frequency of power outages and coverage by UPS/standby generator
  • Daily door-opening frequency (intensity of logistics operations)

Appendix 5 — CDMO Multi-Product Storage Risk Analysis

CDMO Multi-Product Storage Risk Analysis and Practical Application

Risk Factors Unique to CDMO Cold Storage

A Contract Development and Manufacturing Organization (CDMO) faces unique CTC risk factors compared to a single-product manufacturer:

  • Multiple clients, multiple PAR ranges: The same cold room may need to satisfy different temperature acceptance criteria for different clients simultaneously (e.g., 2–8°C for Client A at ±1°C, 2–8°C for Client B at ±0.5°C)
  • Variable loading patterns: Different clients have different batch sizes, different pallet configurations, and different schedules. The "full" condition may represent a mixture of product types with varying thermal mass
  • Change control complexity: Any change to one client's storage arrangement may affect temperature distribution for other clients — triggering a re-evaluation of mapping data validity
  • Audit frequency: Each client may conduct their own supplier audit, potentially with different expectations for mapping depth and frequency
  • Regulatory diversity: Clients may have products approved in different jurisdictions (FDA, EMA, PMDA), each with different interpretations of mapping requirements

CDMO Multi-Product Storage Risk Analysis

The Most Stringent PAR Principle

When a cold room stores products from several customers at the same time and the customers' PARs differ, the most stringent PAR should be used as the acceptance criterion for the whole chamber. This protects the most sensitive product, but it also means that the temperature control accuracy of the entire room must meet the specification of the most demanding customer.

Practical Case: CDMO Sterile Filling Site

Scenario: a 2–8°C cold room simultaneously stores:

  • Customer A: insulin bulk solution (PAR 2–8°C, extremely temperature-sensitive)
  • Customer B: small-molecule API (PAR 5–25°C, high tolerance)
  • Customer C: empty filling tubes (no temperature requirement; they are just habitually stored here)

Risk assessment conclusion:

  • Customer A's PAR is the most stringent → manage the whole chamber to Customer A's standard
  • Customer C's items should be moved out or clearly segregated, to avoid affecting the logic of the overall risk assessment
  • Mapping must be performed under the least favorable mixed-load condition

Analogy: Fire Standards in a Multi-Tenant Office Building

If one tenant of an office building is a chemical laboratory (high hazard), the fire standards for the whole building must be designed to the laboratory's highest requirements, not to those of an ordinary office. The multi-customer management logic for a cold room is exactly the same.

Example 1 — Mapping Strategy for a Walk-in CRT Space

App 6, §18.1 | Mapping Strategy for a Walk-in Controlled Room Temperature Storage Space

English Source

Scenario

A 20 ft × 20 ft × 10 ft (6 m × 6 m × 3 m) CRT space storing up to ten double-stacked pallets. No process steps that could cause variable heat loads.

Step-by-step Probe Logic

  1. Purpose: Storage only — no rolling heated material, no variable heat loads. No additional probes beyond baseline.
  2. Three planes: Divide 3D space into upper / middle / lower levels.
  3. Volume ≤ 70 ft³ (2 m³): Probes at 4 corners of upper and lower planes + 1 center (Fig. 18.1). Total = 9 probes.
  4. Volume ≤ 700 ft³ (20 m³): Add probes at center of each wall face, ceiling, and floor (Fig. 18.2).
  5. Volume > 700 ft³ (20 m³): Use Fig. 18.2 as starting point; add supplementary probes so horizontal and vertical spacing does not exceed 30 ft (9 m).
  6. Airflow: Ceiling diffuser, 4 corner returns, no shelving obstructions. Pallets max 6 ft — no significant flow blockage. No extra probes needed.
  7. Envelope: Inside a building already conditioned to 20°C year-round — no concern for seasonal thermal penetration.
  8. Heat sources: Lights present but no product stored near fixtures; no motors or exothermic materials. No extra probes needed.
  9. HVAC: Single dedicated recirculation unit, no redundancy, no defrost cycles. Failure is acceptable since surrounding building is already conditioned — space temperature would not be impacted.

This example volume is 4,000 ft³ (113 m³) — configuration of Fig. 18.2 is appropriate; no sensors are >30 ft apart horizontally or vertically.

Commentary & CDMO Context

Core Concept

Three Volume Thresholds Determine the Base Probe Count

  • ≤ 70 ft³: 9 probes (4 corners top and bottom + center)
  • ≤ 700 ft³: add the center point of each face
  • > 700 ft³: build on the above, with spacing not exceeding 30 ft (9 m) as a rule

Analogy: Mapping Is Like the Blind Men and the Elephant

The larger the space and the more obstructions, the more "sensing points" are needed to confirm overall temperature uniformity. The 30 ft spacing rule is an industry consensus, not a hard regulatory requirement, but any deviation needs a documented rationale.

CDMO Practical Points

Common issues in a CDMO CRT raw material warehouse: heat load from forklift traffic routes and temperature fluctuations at the receiving area door. This example involves simple storage with no special loads, which is the best-case scenario; in a real environment, evaluate whether steps 6–9 have any exceptions.

Example 2 — Mapping Strategy for a Refrigerator

App 6, §18.2 | Refrigerator Mapping Strategy

English Source

Key Principles

Same development steps as a walk-in unit. Critical difference: shelves obstruct airflow, so each shelf level is treated as its own mapping zone.

Probe Density Options

  • 5 probes/shelf — poor circulation (solid shelves, densely packed)
  • 3 probes/shelf — intermediate circulation (wire shelves, fully packed); alternating front/back corners each level
  • Corners + middle only — good circulation (wire shelves, lots of air space)

Load Temperature vs. Air Temperature

Monitoring probes may be placed in the load or a suitable thermal buffer rather than measuring air, when short-term air fluctuations (defrost cycles, door openings) are acceptable but load temperature must remain within range.

Simulated Product

If simulated product is used, document what is used and why its thermal properties are comparable (e.g., "20 mL water approximates insulin because thermal properties are identical and this is the smallest container stored").

Empty vs. Full State

Map both empty and full states. Full = maximum anticipated load per URS and manufacturer recommendations. Also check under conditions of normal use after full-state mapping.

Shelf Position Flexibility

Define min/max shelf positions. Document limiting parameters on a label on the unit door — clearances (e.g., 3 in side/front/rear, 4 in bottom, 4 in top) and maximum room-temperature load (e.g., 20 lb / 9 kg).

Commentary & CDMO Context

Key Differences from a Walk-in Space

Layers = Zones (Each Shelf = Zone)

Refrigerator shelves block vertical airflow, so each shelf is treated as an independent mapping zone. Probe density is determined by shelf type and loading density; this is the biggest difference from an open warehouse.

Effect of the Defrost Cycle

During a refrigerator defrost, the air temperature rises briefly (possibly beyond the set range), but the physical drug product does not heat up immediately because of its thermal mass. ISPE allows monitoring probes to be placed in the product or in a thermal buffer solution rather than measuring the air, but this requires a documented rationale and validation.

Analogy: A Refrigerator Is Like a Multi-Level Parking Garage

Each level is independent, and warm air from an upper level does not automatically flow into the lower level. "Adding vented grille shelves" is equivalent to opening up passages between levels, so probe density can be reduced, but this needs reassessment.

Example 3 — Warehouse Mapping Layout & Rationale

App 6, §18.3 | Warehouse Mapping Layout and Rationale

English Source

Basic Configuration

Start with basic room probes ensuring ≤30 ft (9 m) vertical and horizontal separation. Then add probes at areas of restricted airflow from racks, drums, tanks, or HVAC direction.

Empty vs. Full State

Warehouses require both empty and full mapping. In warehouses, volume (not mass) is the primary factor affecting airflow patterns. Full state may be simulated with empty cartons/boxes to obstruct airflow as actual product would. Exception: where central heating/cooling relies on wall-side racking as insulation, thermal mass matters and a representative section should be loaded with simulated thermal mass.

18.3.1 Load Testing Considerations

  • Is the area used to condition material, or does material arrive already within spec?
  • Worst case — empty store receiving max load at most extreme permitted condition, or fully loaded store where thermal mass/volume impedes airflow?
  • Doors held open for maximum specified time in extreme environmental conditions?
  • Testing during simulated cooling equipment component failures is encouraged to support continued-use decisions during operational failures.

18.3.2 Summer / Winter Seasonal Testing

Organizations may use risk assessment to determine if seasonal testing is required. Risk mitigation factors that can justify single-season testing include:

  • Robust HVAC design software used to calculate performance
  • Commissioning confirmed component performance (fan volumes, coil capacities)
  • Monitoring sensor density provides "continuous mapping" — deviations would be visible
  • External temperature extremes <±6°C (±10°F) above normal for <3 hours

Unless in a temperate climate not subject to extremes, mapping should be performed in both summer and winter. If initial mapping is in one extreme, supplement within year 1 for the opposite season. Cold rooms (controlled environment) may be mapped once if monitoring provides adequate assurance of internal conditions.

The rationale for load and seasonal testing must be reviewed and approved by the Quality Unit and area/System Owner.

Commentary & CDMO Context

Key Differences Between Warehouse and Refrigerator Mapping

Effect of Volume vs. Mass

In a warehouse, the volume of racks/boxes (obstructing airflow) matters more than their mass (thermal buffering). Empty cartons can therefore serve as a simulated load, reducing cost. But if the HVAC relies on the insulation of wall-side racking, thermal mass becomes important.

Seasonal Mapping Judgment

Taiwan has a significant gap between summer heat (>35°C) and winter cold (below 10°C in the north), exceeding the ±6°C / 3-hour threshold. A CDMO in Taiwan usually needs mapping in both summer and winter, unless the facility is 100% within a temperature-controlled building year-round.

Analogy: The "Continuous Mapping" Effect

If the density of permanent monitoring sensors is high enough, routine monitoring data is equivalent to continuous mapping. Anomalies are detected immediately, with no need for separate seasonal mapping. This is the basis for a risk-based waiver given sufficient monitoring density.

  1. Your CDMO warehouse is located inside a heated and air-conditioned building, and the exterior walls have no direct sun exposure. How would you justify in the IQ/OQ protocol that only one seasonal mapping is needed? What documents are required?
  2. Which represents the more severe "worst case": an empty warehouse fully loaded with empty cartons, or one actually fully loaded with drug product raw materials? Why?
  3. If, after mapping, the data from a monitoring point shows that one corner is consistently close to the high-temperature alarm limit, what is your first action?

Required Protocol Elements (A–K)

Appendix 7 — Periodic Review of Controlled Temperature Chambers

App 7, §19.1 | Periodic Review of Controlled Temperature Chambers

English Source

Categorization System

Systems are categorized based on operating temperature range and material stored:

  • Material Type A: API, drug product, stability samples, cool/hot packs (unverified before use)
  • Material Type B: WIP, reference standards, critical reagents, raw materials
  • Material Type C: EM supplies, reagents, media, test materials

Review Frequency

  • Category 1: Periodic review ≤3 years from last review
  • Category 2: No formal periodic review; system performance verification via defined maintenance job plan
  • Category 3: No performance verification or periodic review required

Method 1 — Risk-Based Report

Reviews three aspects: (1) Internal layout & loading, (2) Insulation, (3) Cooling system. Each aspect has defined review criteria and documented conclusions. Key sub-checks include:

  • Racking/storage locations unchanged vs. C&Q file
  • Stored material size/mass unchanged vs. test strategy
  • Annual thermal scan of shell (no hot/cold spots vs. baseline)
  • Utility penetrations — no leakage or condensation
  • Door, seals, and vinyl curtain condition
  • Alarm history review — frequency/severity acceptable
  • PM confirms duty/standby function and refrigeration parameters within tolerance
  • Independent monitoring data shows no degradation trend
  • Alarm set points and delay periods verified at correct values

Report conclusion must state if the system is suitable for continued use. If not, issue a nonconformance. Must be approved by System Owner (or delegate) and Quality Unit.

Method 2 — Remapping

Alternative to Method 1. Remapping must consider: loading/unloading impact, load effect on temperature distribution, and defrost impact. Results used to confirm or modify monitoring probe locations. Same approval requirements apply.

Commentary & CDMO Context

Interpreting the Classification Logic

Classification Determines the Review Burden

Most refrigerators (2–8°C) at a CDMO storing APIs or drug products are Type A + Precision Unit = Category 1, the most stringent, and must be reviewed every 3 years. A CRT raw material warehouse storing WIP is Type B + CRT = Category 3, with no periodic review required. Correct classification can save substantial compliance resources.

Choosing Between Method 1 and Method 2

Method 1 (risk assessment report): suited to cases where the system is stable, there are no major changes, and monitoring data trends are normal. Low cost and short time.
Method 2 (remapping): suited to cases with major changes (such as shelf rearrangement or a new load type) or where Method 1 cannot rule out concerns. The most complete data, but high cost.

Analogy: Health Check Models

The most important acronym groups

The four qualification steps: IQ → OQ → PQ (+ FAT/SAT). Knowing these acronyms, their order and their meaning is basic knowledge for CTC qualification.

Easily confused acronyms

  • BAS vs. BMS: BAS (building automation system) is the broader concept; BMS (building management system) is the term pharmaceutical plants habitually use, and the two are often used interchangeably. The key point: any system used to record GMP data must comply with 21 CFR Part 11 / Annex 11.
  • RTD vs. thermocouple: An RTD (Pt100 platinum resistance) has an accuracy of about ±0.1–0.3°C; a thermocouple is less accurate (±0.5–2°C). For GMP temperature monitoring, RTDs are the preferred choice.
  • CPP vs. CQA: A CQA is a quality attribute of the product (such as potency stability); a CPP is a process parameter that affects a CQA (such as temperature). The two have a cause-and-effect relationship.
  • MU vs. Accuracy: MU (measurement uncertainty) is a statistical concept that combines sensor error, calibration error and environmental influences; accuracy is only the specification of the sensor itself. MU, not accuracy, is the basis for calculating guard-banding.

The difference between FAT and SAT

FAT (Factory Acceptance Test) is performed at the manufacturer's plant, like inspecting a new car at the car factory to confirm that its basic functions work.

SAT (Site Acceptance Test) is performed at the user's site, like driving the new car to your own parking lot and checking it again to confirm that it still works properly in the real environment.

In CTC qualification, FAT verifies the equipment's as-shipped specification and SAT verifies its on-site performance after installation; both are indispensable.

Guard-Banding Concept

Guard-banding is the practice of tightening acceptance criteria to account for Measurement Uncertainty (MU). If USP requires 2–8°C for Cold storage, and MU = ±0.3°C, then the guard-banded acceptance criteria become: 2.3°C ≤ T ≤ 7.7°C.

Top 10 Must-Know Principles

The essential knowledge framework for CTC mapping and monitoring — applicable from first qualification to lifecycle management

Principles 1–5

1. URS Drives Everything

Every qualification decision — acceptance criteria, sensor placement, alarm setpoints, test conditions — must trace back to the User Requirements Specification. If a requirement is not in the URS, it cannot be a qualification failure. If it is in the URS, it must be tested. The URS is the bridge between business/product needs and engineering/QA execution.

Commentary: Every qualification decision must be traceable to the URS. The URS is the bridge between requirements and execution: a requirement not written into the URS cannot be grounds for a qualification failure, and a requirement written into the URS must be tested and verified.

2. Worst-Case Location Is Non-Negotiable

Mapping studies must identify the worst-case locations (hottest and coldest points) under all relevant conditions: empty chamber, loaded chamber, door-open conditions, and seasonal extremes. The monitoring sensor must be placed at the worst-case location(s) identified in mapping — not at the most convenient or accessible point.

Commentary: Temperature mapping must identify the worst-case locations (the hottest and coldest points) and be performed under all relevant conditions, including empty chamber, fully loaded and door-open. Monitoring sensors must be placed at the worst-case locations confirmed by mapping, not at the most convenient locations.

3. Measurement Uncertainty Must Be Accounted For

Every temperature measurement has uncertainty. MU must be calculated for the complete measurement chain (sensor + cable + data acquisition + calibration) and applied to acceptance criteria via guard-banding. Ignoring MU means your qualification data may not actually demonstrate that the product temperature was within specification.

Commentary: Every temperature measurement has uncertainty. MU must be calculated for the complete measurement chain (sensor + cabling + data acquisition + calibration) and applied to the acceptance criteria through guard-banding. Ignoring MU means your qualification data may not truly demonstrate that the product temperature is within specification.

4. Alert ≠ Alarm — Both Are Required

Alert levels are early warning signals that allow corrective action before product is at risk. Alarm levels indicate the temperature has reached a point where product quality may be compromised. Setting only one level (as many companies do) is insufficient. The gap between alert and alarm must be scientifically justified based on MU, chamber response time, and product stability data.

Commentary: An alert is an early warning that lets operators act before the product is threatened; an alarm indicates that the temperature has reached a level that may affect product quality. Setting only one level is not enough, and the margin between the two must be scientifically justified based on MU, chamber response time and product stability data.

5. FAT + SAT + IQ + OQ + PQ Is the Minimum

A complete qualification lifecycle requires all five stages. FAT validates the equipment meets specification at the vendor site. SAT confirms it still meets specification after shipping and installation. IQ verifies correct installation. OQ verifies correct operation (empty, worst-case). PQ verifies performance under actual or simulated operating conditions (loaded). Skipping any stage creates qualification gaps that may be cited during inspections.

Commentary: A complete qualification lifecycle has five stages: FAT (factory acceptance test) → SAT (site acceptance test) → IQ (installation qualification) → OQ (operational qualification) → PQ (performance qualification). Skipping any stage creates a qualification gap that may be cited in an inspection.

Principles 6–10

6. Load Configuration Changes Invalidate Mapping

Temperature distribution in a loaded chamber is fundamentally different from an empty chamber. PQ mapping must be conducted with a representative product load. Significant changes to load configuration (e.g. changing from 50% to 100% capacity, changing packaging format, adding new shelving) should trigger an assessment of whether requalification is required.

Commentary: The temperature distribution of a fully loaded chamber is fundamentally different from that of an empty chamber. PQ mapping must be performed under a representative product load. A significant change in loading configuration (for example, capacity changing from 50% to 100%, a change of packaging format, or added shelving) should trigger an assessment of whether requalification is needed.

7. Monitoring System Data Must Be 21 CFR Part 11 / Annex 11 Compliant

All electronic temperature monitoring data used for GMP decisions (product release, deviation investigation, batch records) must meet data integrity requirements: audit trails, access controls, backup procedures, and system validation. This applies to BMS, data loggers, and any standalone monitoring software. Non-compliant data may be considered unreliable during FDA or MHRA inspections.

Commentary: All electronic temperature monitoring data used for GMP decisions (product release, deviation investigations, batch records) must meet data integrity requirements: audit trails, access control, backup procedures and system validation. Non-compliant data may be regarded as unreliable in an FDA or MHRA inspection.

8. Excursions Require MKT Assessment Before Product Disposition

Every temperature excursion must be investigated, documented, and assessed for product impact before making a release or rejection decision. MKT calculation is the scientific basis for excursion impact assessment — it accounts for the non-linear relationship between temperature and degradation rate. A short excursion to a moderately elevated temperature may not require rejection if MKT remains within specification.

Commentary: Every temperature excursion must be investigated, documented and assessed for product impact before a release or rejection decision is made. The MKT calculation is the scientific basis for excursion impact assessment because it accounts for the non-linear relationship between temperature and degradation rate. If the MKT remains within the specification range, a brief, minor excursion may not require rejection.

9. Periodic Review Maintains Qualification State

Qualification is not a one-time event — it is a lifecycle programme. Annual (or frequency-based) periodic reviews must verify: no unreported changes, calibration compliance, alarm response performance, trend analysis of temperature deviations, and continuing fitness for purpose. The result of a periodic review should be a formal documented conclusion: "qualification status maintained" or "requalification required."

Commentary: Qualification is not a one-time event; it is a lifecycle programme. The annual periodic review must verify: no unreported changes, calibration compliance, alarm response performance, temperature excursion trend analysis and continued suitability. The outcome of a periodic review should be a formally documented conclusion: "qualified state maintained" or "requalification required".

10. Risk-Based Approach Is Not an Excuse to Do Less

The guide's risk-based framework allows scientific justification for qualification scope decisions — fewer sensors, shorter study duration, reduced testing frequency for low-risk chambers. However, this flexibility requires documented risk assessment (FMEA or equivalent) with explicit rationale. "Risk-based" without documented risk assessment is simply "doing less" — which will not survive inspection scrutiny.

Commentary: A risk-based approach allows scientific justification of decisions on qualification scope (fewer sensors, shorter studies, less testing for low-risk chambers). But this flexibility requires a documented risk assessment (FMEA or equivalent) and a clear rationale. "Risk-based" without a documented risk assessment is simply "doing less", which cannot be defended in an inspection.

Short Answer Keys

  • Q4.1: Cold = ≤8°C (refrigerator: 2–8°C); CCT = maintained at 2–8°C but allows 0–15°C excursions if MKT ≤8°C. CCT is more relevant for distribution scenarios (loading docks, shipping legs) where brief excursions are unavoidable but product integrity can still be demonstrated via MKT.
  • Q4.2: The controller sensor is placed for equipment control accuracy (near the evaporator or return air), not to represent the worst product temperature. If the monitoring sensor is co-located with the controller, it will show the best-case temperature rather than the temperature the product actually experiences. This could mask excursions at the worst-case location where product is stored.
  • Q4.3: The tighter criteria reflect application of Measurement Uncertainty (MU) guard-banding per ISO/IEC Guide 98-3 (GUM). Our mapping sensors have a combined MU of ±0.5°C. To ensure that any measurement within our acceptance window guarantees the true product temperature is within USP limits, we apply guard-banding: if MU = ±0.5°C, acceptance criteria = [2 + 0.5] to [8 − 0.5] = 2.5°C to 7.5°C.
  • Q4.4: Requalification triggers: (1) Major equipment repair (e.g. compressor replacement); (2) Relocation of the CTC; (3) Significant change to load configuration or storage capacity; (4) Replacement of control or monitoring system; (5) Recurring or unresolved temperature excursions; (6) Failed periodic review; (7) Significant facility modification affecting ambient temperature or air circulation; (8) Change of product type with different temperature sensitivity stored in the chamber.
