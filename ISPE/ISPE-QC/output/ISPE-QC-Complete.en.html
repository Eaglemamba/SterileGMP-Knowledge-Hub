Background & Purpose

Original Text

ISPE-PDA 2019

Background

Quality Culture has always been important within pharmaceutical manufacturing operations. Strong companies know this and have invested resources in systems and personnel to support and promote a focus on quality processes, product quality, and meeting patient needs. More recently, health authorities have placed additional emphasis on quality culture by including it in guidance documents and inspection protocols like the PIC/S Data Integrity Guidance, the FDA New Inspection Protocol Project (NIPP), and the MHRA Data Integrity Guideline.

Both ISPE and PDA have developed information and resources to help pharmaceutical companies better understand why quality culture is important and how to assess the current situation within a site or organization. The ISPE Cultural Excellence Report shares the vision of quality culture improvements across six dimensions, utilizes a 19-behavior cultural maturity assessment, outlines a series of practices and tools to support implementation, and provides a practical frame of reference for companies that want to build healthy quality cultures within their organizations. The PDA Quality Culture Assessment Program is based on a Maturity Model containing 21 quality culture metrics and offers training for assessors, a PDA-administered all employee survey, and access to composite benchmarking results for comparison with peer sites.

Members of the ISPE and PDA culture task forces have come together to develop this reference guide that identifies specific aspects of quality systems and culture and recommendations for tools, techniques, and processes. The purpose of this guide is not to provide complete training or implementation of any tools or to be an all-inclusive list; instead, the guide is intended to serve as a resource with links to additional information for the most commonly used tools. The tools included in this reference guide are accessible without fee.

The guidance is divided into two sections: "Root Cause Analysis Tools and Approaches" and "Human Error Analysis Tools and Approaches."

"In closing, while the focus of many CAPA systems in practice today is on major compliance breaches, consideration should also be given to applying these techniques proactively to smaller non-conformance or out-of-trend events that often present risks to product quality, waste time and money, or more importantly, a missed opportunity for learning. Moreover, nothing guarantees that some of these more minor events will not recur with more severe consequences under unfavourable circumstances at some time in the future."

Author Team: Tami Frederick (Perrigo, Lead), Denyse Baker (AstraZeneca), Nuala Calnan (Biopharm Excel), Steven Mendivil (Amgen), Matt Pearson (Genentech Roche), Brianna Peterson (Boehringer-Ingelheim)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Why Are ISPE and PDA Collaborating?

Quality Culture: not merely compliance in documents and processes, but the values, behavior patterns and habits of thought about quality held by an organization from top to bottom.

ISPE brings depth in engineering and facility design; PDA brings expertise in aseptic processing and regulatory science. Their collaboration means the guide covers both the "hardware/process" and the "people/culture" dimensions, which makes it well suited to CDMO shop-floor use.

Key Point: Regulatory Trend - Inspections Have Extended to "Culture"

The FDA NIPP (New Inspection Protocol Project), the MHRA data integrity guidance and PIC/S PI 041-1: these three regulators have explicitly included "quality culture" in inspection assessments. This means:

  • On-site inspectors look not only at SOP documents, but also observe employees' actual behavior and attitudes
  • The quality of a CAPA (whether the true root cause was actually found) directly affects 483 observations and warning letters
  • A CDMO that cannot demonstrate systematic RCA capability will lose points in client audits

Analogy: Quality Culture Is Like a Hospital's Safety Culture

Imagine a hospital with every rule and regulation in place, but where nurses are afraid of being scolded and do not report medication errors, and doctors believe they never make mistakes and reject advice. This is "having a system but no culture." By contrast, a hospital that encourages proactive reporting and systematic learning can truly reduce patient harm. The same applies to a pharmaceutical plant: regulatory documents are a necessary condition; quality culture is the sufficient condition.

Introduction to RCA: Root Cause Analysis Introductory Remarks

Original Text

"An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure." — Benjamin Franklin

The mark of a mature culture within an organization is how it deals with ever-increasing complexity as failures and deviations arise. Also important is whether it proactively evaluates potential risks to prevent failures or recurring deviations or reactively detects and corrects failures after they have occurred. Twenty-first century Pharmaceutical Quality Systems (PQS) require a more balanced approach to prevent and cure.

Corrective and Preventive Action (CAPA) systems are designed to implement and record both corrective and preventative actions. ICH Q10 goes further about effective CAPA processes by recommending:

"A structured approach to the investigation process should be used with the objective of determining the root cause. The level of effort, formality, and documentation of the investigation should be commensurate with the level of risk, in line with ICH Q9. CAPA methodology should result in product and process improvements and enhanced product and process understanding."

A common problem encountered within CAPA systems is the lack of rigor applied to identifying the true root cause of failure events. This leads to situations where "effects" of a given failure or "causal factors" (those factors that could potentially have prevented the incident or mitigated its consequences) are identified and corrected, rather than the underlying cause. These symptoms of failure are often more visible and easily reported, while the actual root causes of the problem may be submerged, unknown, and therefore not addressed. Not surprisingly, this often leads to recurrence of the failure at another time or in another context in the organization.

RCA (Root Cause Analysis) is a systematic problem-solving process of unearthing these root causes to apply lasting solutions. Effective RCA requires the application of critical thinking, a multi-disciplinary knowledge seeking approach applied across the organization, site, and even shift rotation.

Different approaches or tools may be better suited for some problems or situations. In many situations, a combination of RCA tools will provide the most comprehensive results. Another best practice is for a firm to select a set of tools, not more than six, that are used repeatedly to build expertise and consistency of results.

Accompanying each RCA tool are recommendations for best applications "✓" and cautions or special considerations for use "⚠".

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The Three Levels of RCA Logic

Root Cause Analysis (RCA): a systematic method that aims to find the "deepest cause" of a problem rather than merely treating the surface symptoms.

Distinguishing the three levels:

  • Effect/Symptom: the visible problem, e.g., a media fill (APS) failure
  • Causal Factor: a condition that directly contributes to the problem, e.g., the operator did not follow the gowning procedure
  • Root Cause: the underlying cause, e.g., the gowning procedure is not designed with human factors in mind, so steps are easily skipped

Key Point: The Most Common Reason CAPAs Fail

"Ineffective CAPA" ranks among the top three FDA 483 observations. The underlying problem is almost always the same: only the effect was corrected; the cause was not resolved.

Typical case: environmental monitoring results on a sterile filling line exceed limits → the CAPA is "intensify cleaning and retrain operators" → the same problem recurs three months later → the true root cause was an incorrectly set HVAC filter replacement frequency.

Analogy: The Iceberg Theory

A problem is like an iceberg: what floats above the water is the symptom (visible and easy to report), while what lies below the surface is the root cause (which takes tools to dig out). Most CAPA systems deal only with the part above the waterline, which is why problems keep recurring.

Practical Application: How a CDMO Builds a Standard Toolbox of "No More Than Six Tools"

The guide recommends selecting no more than six RCA tools and using them repeatedly to build expertise. For a sterile fill-finish CDMO, the recommended core toolbox is:

  • 5 Whys - quick analysis of routine deviations
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) - complex microbial/equipment-related deviations
  • FMEA - risk assessment for new products/processes
  • Barrier Analysis - multi-department process gaps
  • Process Mapping - data integrity lifecycle
  • Poka-Yoke - engineered error-proofing for human error

Practice Questions

  1. ICH Q10 requires the depth of investigation to be commensurate with the level of risk. Does a minor deviation require a Fishbone? When should you escalate to a more complex tool?
  2. Why is "rotating multiple shifts" a difficulty for RCA investigations? How would you ensure that night-shift knowledge is included in the root cause analysis?
Part 1 - RCA Tools: Low Complexity Tools

Original Text

LOW COMPLEXITY

"If you are unable to understand the cause of a problem, it is impossible to solve it." — Naoto Kan

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Positioning of the Three Low Complexity Tools

Affinity Diagram: diverge first, then converge. Cross-functional members each write down the problems they have observed, and the notes are then grouped by similarity. Suited to the brainstorming stage.

5 Whys: the most commonly used and most easily misused tool. By asking "why" five times in succession, it forces the investigator to drill down from the symptom toward the cause.

Barrier Analysis: analysis based on the "layers of protection" mindset. Which existing barriers should have prevented the problem, and why did they fail? Suited to cross-department processes (such as handovers and handwritten records).

Key Point: The Three Most Common Misuses of 5 Whys

  1. Stopping at a person: "Why? Because the operator was not paying attention." → This is blame, not a root cause
  2. Following only one line: real problems usually have multiple causal chains; 5 Whys follows just one and may miss systemic issues
  3. Reconstruction afterward in a meeting room: the guide specifically advises doing it "at the location of the event, immediately after the event", because memory fades quickly

Conclusion: 5 Whys is suited to "quick initial analysis"; complex events must be paired with other tools.

Analogy: Barrier Analysis Is Like Layered Insurance Protection

Imagine your home has three layers of protection: a door lock, a burglar alarm and security cameras. A thief gets in → you investigate which layer of protection failed, why it failed, and whether it can be strengthened or a new layer added. Barrier Analysis in a pharmaceutical plant follows the same logic: the SOP is one barrier, the IPC is one barrier, visual verification is one barrier. When a deviation occurs, it means multiple barriers failed at the same time, and each must be reviewed in turn.

Practical Application: A 5 Whys Example for a Sterile Filling Line

Problem: one unit in a media fill (APS) has a low fill volume

  • Why 1: Why was the fill volume low? → The filling pump output was unstable during one cycle
  • Why 2: Why was it unstable? → The pump's peristaltic tubing had localized wear
  • Why 3: Why was the tubing worn? → Its hours of use exceeded the preventive maintenance interval
  • Why 4: Why was the interval exceeded? → The line change record did not track cumulative hours of use for each tubing section
  • Why 5: Why did the record not track this? → Tubing tracking is not a mandatory field in the BPR

Root cause: a gap in BPR design, with no mechanism for tracking consumable lifetime → CAPA: revise the BPR form and add a field for cumulative tubing hours

Practice Questions

  1. When you carry out 5 Whys, how do you avoid the investigation "drifting toward blaming individuals" instead of "discovering system problems"?
  2. Barrier Analysis presupposes that "a process flow chart is available." If the process in which a deviation occurred currently has no flow chart, what should you do first?
Part 1 - RCA Tools: Medium Complexity Tools

Original Text

MEDIUM COMPLEXITY

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Common Characteristics of Medium Complexity Tools

These four tools all require more preparation up front (flow charts, a cross-functional team, numerical scoring systems), but they can cover "multi-department, multi-causal-chain" scenarios and are a natural extension of the low complexity tools.

  • Process Mapping: visualizes the whole process to find the "white space" (gaps for which no one is responsible)
  • C&E Matrix: quantifies the degree to which each input factor affects the output, suited to screening for the "causes most worth investigating"
  • Causal Circle: reveals circular feedback relationships in a system, suited to complex system problems
  • Causal Factor Tree: presents all necessary and sufficient causal factors in a tree structure

Key Point: "Flow Charts Drawn in a Meeting Room Are Rarely Accurate"

The guide specifically warns that flow charts drawn from memory in a meeting room are almost always inaccurate.

The right approach: go to the site (Gemba Walk), observe the actual operating process, and verify each step together with the operators. Actual practice may differ between shifts and between operators, and that difference may itself be one of the root causes.

Formula and Calculation: C&E Matrix Scoring Logic

Output importance score (Y): 1–10
Correlation of input to output (X→Y): 0, 1, 3, 9

Weighted score for a single input =
    Σ (output importance × correlation score)

Higher score = input factor more worth investigating first

Example:
Fill volume deviation (Y importance = 9)
- Peristaltic tubing wear (correlation = 9) → weighted 81
- Operator skill (correlation = 3) → weighted 27
- Temperature fluctuation (correlation = 1) → weighted 9
Priority for investigation: tubing wear

Analogy: A Causal Circle Is Like the Butterfly Effect of a Traffic Jam

A road is blocked → drivers detour → other roads jam too → more people detour → the whole city is gridlocked. This is circular causation with no single "starting point", and any entry point may be an opportunity to intervene. In a pharmaceutical plant, "too few staff → operators rush to keep to schedule → errors increase → CAPAs increase → everyone is busier → even fewer people to handle routine work" is a similar vicious circle, and the causal circle tool helps you see this system clearly.

  1. Process Mapping mentions "white space." Try to give an example: in the BPR recording process on a sterile filling line, what white spaces might exist?
  2. The C&E Matrix is widely used in Six Sigma projects. What is the main difference between it and FMEA?
Part 1 - RCA Tools: High Complexity Tools

Original Text

HIGH COMPLEXITY

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The 6M Framework of the Fishbone

Ishikawa / Fishbone Diagram: presents the causes of a problem in the shape of a "fish bone"; the spine represents the problem (the fish head), and each rib represents one of the 6M categories.

  • Manpower (people): training, skills, fatigue, shift differences
  • Machine (equipment): equipment calibration, wear, maintenance status
  • Method: SOP design, process parameters, sequence of steps
  • Material: raw material specifications, supplier changes, component lot differences
  • Measurement: instrument accuracy, sampling method, analytical method validation
  • Milieu (environment): temperature and humidity, ISO cleanroom classification, HVAC, shift culture

Key Point: The Core Difference Between FMEA (Preventive) and Fault Tree (Reactive)

FMEA is proactive: "before" a design or process is established, it systematically lists all possible failure modes and their consequences, calculates the RPN score (severity × occurrence × detectability), and prioritizes high-risk items for action.

Fault Tree is reactive but can also be preventive: starting from a known top-level failure event, it works backward to derive all the lower-level causes that could lead to that failure, presented as a logic tree (AND/OR gates). It is often used for risk assessment before equipment is introduced.

Key difference: FMEA = working from the bottom up, asking "what happens if this element fails?"; Fault Tree = working from the top down, asking "how was this failure caused?"

Analogy: A Fishbone Is Like the Differential Diagnosis in a Medical Record

When diagnosing a complex symptom, a doctor does not look at just one cause, but rules out possibilities one by one by organ system (heart, lungs, nervous system...). A Fishbone does the same for a process problem: classifying by 6M ensures that every aspect is checked systematically and no corner is overlooked.

Practical Application: A Fishbone for a Microbial Contamination Investigation on a Sterile Filling Line

Hypothetical problem statement: "In a Q1 2026 batch, environmental monitoring (EM) at filling area point A exceeded the microbial limit"

  • Manpower: gowning qualification, APS training records, behavior observation of the operators on shift
  • Machine: RABS glove integrity, HVAC differential pressure records, automatic stoppering machine fault records
  • Method: EM sampling locations and frequency, UDAF airflow verification timing, cleaning and disinfection frequency
  • Material: culture media lot numbers, stopper supplier lots, disinfectant preparation records
  • Measurement: EM culture media expiry, incubation conditions, positive control results
  • Milieu: the preceding batch produced, first batch after equipment maintenance, seasonal climate changes

Practice Questions

  1. A frequently criticized weakness of the RPN (Risk Priority Number) calculation in FMEA is that RPN = severity × occurrence × detectability, so a failure with extremely high severity but very low occurrence and detectability scores may end up with a low RPN. How can this problem be improved?
  2. The Fault Tree warning is that "simple fault trees are usually incomplete and inaccurate." How would you ensure the completeness of a Fault Tree?
Part 2 - Human Error Analysis Tools

Original Text

When investigating root causes of a problem or deviation, the analysis will frequently point to human error. While it is tempting to stop there and insert countermeasures such as "retrain the operator" or "update the SOP," human performance is inherently variable and dependent on the conditions of the environment, which means these tools and approaches alone are unlikely to lead to long-term, stable solutions.

A more in-depth analysis of the human factors behind the errors in any given process or problem should be conducted. Human error should not be the conclusion of a root cause investigation but the starting point. This analysis should also include a thorough understanding or assessment of the culture of a sub-team, working group, or organization. Research has shown the frequency of mistakes is increased by the stress or complexity of the environment. Once the human factors are well understood, actions such as error proofing, poka-yoke, or related engineering solutions can be put in place to reduce the potential for human error.

MEDIUM COMPLEXITY

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: Why "Retraining the Operator" Is Almost Never the True Root Cause

This is one of the most important insights in the guide.

The guide states clearly: human error should be the starting point of an investigation, not the end point.

When we say "the operator made an error", we should actually ask:

  • What environmental conditions made the error more likely to occur? (pressure, distraction, fatigue)
  • Is the SOP designed so that correct behavior is easy to perform and incorrect behavior is difficult?
  • Is there any confusing design in the equipment/tools?
  • Did the operator have what was needed to perform the task (time, tools, clear instructions)?

"Retraining" addresses only one hypothesis, "insufficient knowledge", yet most human errors do not originate from insufficient knowledge.

Key Point: Selection Logic for the Three Human Error Tools

Poka-Yoke (error-proofing): an engineering-level solution that makes the correct method the only possible method. Examples: a reagent container whose connector is designed to fit only the correct line (physical error-proofing); a system that locks the next step until the previous step is completed (software error-proofing). Low cost, lasting effect, and not dependent on personnel memory.

Human Factors Optimization: systematically evaluates the factors in the work environment that affect personnel performance (lighting, noise, fatigue, information load, tool design, etc.) and provides strategies to reduce the baseline human error rate.

ABC Model: a behavioral science framework. It analyzes the chain "Antecedent → Behavior → Consequence" to identify which antecedents trigger undesirable behavior, and which consequences reinforce or weaken that behavior.

Analogy: Poka-Yoke Is Like the Design Evolution of the USB Connector

First-generation USB: the right way up was unclear, so you had to try twice every time you plugged it in (an opportunity for human error). USB-C: no right side up, and it goes in from either direction (Poka-Yoke: the possibility of plugging it in wrongly is eliminated). Poka-Yoke in a pharmaceutical plant should pursue the same goal: make it "impossible" for the operator to get it wrong, rather than relying on training to make them "remember not to get it wrong."

ABC Model Analysis Framework

Antecedent → Behavior → Consequence

Example: operator skips the double-check step
- Antecedent: time pressure + batch delay alert + supervisor present urging them on
- Behavior: omits the BPR double-verification signature step
- Consequence: short term → schedule is caught up (positive reinforcement)
         long term → a deviation event occurs (delayed negative consequence)

Points of intervention:
- Modify the antecedent: eliminate the systemic source of time pressure
- Modify the consequence: give skipping a step an immediate, clear consequence
              (system lock, alarm triggered)

Practical Application: Poka-Yoke Design Thinking for Sterile Filling at a CDMO

Common human error scenarios and the corresponding engineered error-proofing solutions:

  • Scenario: the operator forgets to change the peristaltic tubing → Error-proofing: the BMS tracks cumulative hours of use and automatically locks the filling pump when the limit is exceeded; the change must be confirmed before it can be unlocked
  • Scenario: components are mixed up (caps from batch A used for batch B) → Error-proofing: a barcode scanning and matching system, so the equipment does not start when the scan does not match
  • Scenario: a glove breach goes undetected → Error-proofing: a scheduled glove integrity test system that automatically triggers the test procedure before operations
  • Scenario: an environmental monitoring sampling point is missed → Error-proofing: an electronic EM system in which the QR code of every sampling point must be scanned before the batch record can be closed

Practice Questions

  1. The ABC Model analysis shows that the "consequence" of the operator skipping a step is a "short-term feeling of success" (catching up with the schedule) rather than "punishment." How would you redesign the consequence so that it is immediate and specific?
  2. Poka-Yoke emphasizes being simple, cheap and effective. Within your existing resource constraints, can you design a Poka-Yoke solution for a high-risk step on a sterile filling line?
Tool Selection Matrix: RCA Tool Selection Guide for CDMO

Quick Reference Matrix

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concepts: The "Golden Triangle" Core Toolbox for a CDMO

The guide recommends that each company select "no more than six tools" and practice them repeatedly. For a sterile fill-finish CDMO, the most valuable core triangle is:

  • 5 Whys - fast, with a low barrier to entry, suited to immediate handling of routine deviations
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa) - the 6M framework ensures comprehensiveness, particularly suited to microbial and equipment events
  • FMEA - proactively identifies risks and is an essential tool for new product introduction, process transfer and equipment validation

Together these three tools cover the complete cycle of "rapid response → in-depth investigation → preventive front-loading."

Key Point: Combining Tools Is Best Practice

The guide states clearly: "In many cases, a combination of RCA tools provides the most comprehensive results."

Typical combined workflow:

  1. Immediately after the event: 5 Whys (on site, quick)
  2. Cross-functional team: Fishbone (comprehensive analysis)
  3. Quantified prioritization: C&E Matrix (screen for the most important factors)
  4. CAPA design: if human error is involved → Poka-Yoke; if equipment is involved → update the FMEA
  5. Effectiveness verification: Barrier Analysis (confirm whether the new barrier has been established)

Analogy: Tools Are Like Surgical Instruments

A surgeon does not use a single knife for every operation, but neither brings every instrument in the hospital to each one. They have a "core toolbox" from which they can choose and combine flexibly in different situations. The same logic applies to RCA tools: mastering six or fewer is far better than dabbling in fifteen.

Scenario: During a client's third-party audit of a sterile fill-finish CDMO, the inspector points out: "In the past 18 months, your environmental monitoring excursions occurred four times, and each CAPA was 'intensify cleaning' or 'retrain operators', yet the events keep recurring."

Full Guide Summary

  • Quality culture has become an explicit assessment item in FDA, MHRA and PIC/S inspections, and CAPA quality directly affects regulatory standing
  • The core logic of RCA: effect (symptom) ≠ root cause; if the true root cause is not found, the problem will inevitably recur
  • Low complexity tools (5 Whys, Affinity Diagram, Barrier Analysis) suit quick initial analysis but are insufficient for complex events
  • Medium complexity tools (Process Mapping, C&E Matrix, Causal Circle/Tree) suit multi-department, multi-causal-chain scenarios
  • High complexity tools (Fishbone 6M, FMEA, Fault Tree) suit complex equipment/microbial events and preventive risk management
  • Human error is the starting point of an investigation, not the end point; "retraining" is rarely the true root cause; engineered Poka-Yoke error-proofing is more durable than administrative controls
  • Recommended CDMO golden toolbox: 5 Whys + Fishbone + FMEA, covering three levels: rapid response, in-depth investigation and proactive prevention
  • Using tools in combination is better than using a single tool; select six or fewer tools and practice them repeatedly to build team expertise
