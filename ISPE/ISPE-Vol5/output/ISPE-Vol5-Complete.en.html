1.1 Background

Original Text

It is a universal regulatory expectation that pharmaceutical manufacturing facilities, systems, utilities, and equipment are designed, constructed and qualified to be suitable for the intended purpose. Regulatory authorities have indicated in their guidances and regulations that organizations must build quality into their processes and products. This Guide intends to offer a compliant, integrated commissioning and qualification process that is based on a science and risk-based approach. This process supports Quality by Design (QbD) and ensures that risks to product quality from the systems are adequately addressed.

It is recognized that the terminologies for the commissioning and qualification process are varied. The overall process has traditionally been called commissioning and qualification; ASTM E2500-13 refers to the process as verification. This second edition of the Baseline® Guide focuses on the approach; the terminology used is not as important as the approach. For the purposes of this Guide, the term Commissioning and Qualification (C&Q) is used to describe the process for establishing that facilities, systems, utilities, and equipment are suitable for the intended purpose. The term verification is used to describe an activity performed during the C&Q process to establish suitability for the intended purpose.

Commissioning and qualification differ only in that the qualification process has oversight by the Quality Unit, focused on the system risk to product quality. The Quality Unit ultimately decides whether systems are suitable for the intended purpose, based on the identification, testing, and documentation of the system features deemed critical to product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Essence of C&Q

Commissioning: the process by which engineers confirm that a system is installed as designed and operates properly. It emphasizes technical conformity and does not require QA approval.

Qualification: building on Commissioning, and under Quality Unit oversight, focuses on product quality risk to demonstrate that the system is "fit for intended use".

The key difference between the two is just one thing: the degree of Quality Unit involvement. This insight is one of the most important messages of this edition of the ISPE Guide.

Analogy: Installing a Plant Is Like Building a New Kitchen

Imagine you open a restaurant and install brand-new kitchen equipment.
Commissioning = the technician confirms that the gas stove ignites properly and the refrigerator holds the right temperature. This is engineering acceptance.
Qualification = the health department inspector confirms that the equipment meets food safety standards and issues a permit. This is the regulatory compliance layer.
C&Q in a pharmaceutical plant integrates "engineering acceptance" and "regulatory compliance" into one efficient process that avoids repeat testing.

Key Point: The Method Matters More Than the Names

ISPE deliberately says that terminology matters less than method, because companies and national regulations use very different names for IQ/OQ/PQ/FAT/SAT. ISPE Vol. 5 2nd Ed. provides a methodological framework that lets you achieve compliant qualification regardless of the terms you use.

ASTM E2500 calls the entire process "verification", which differs from the wording of ICH Q10. This difference often causes confusion during inspections, and this Guide explicitly standardizes on the C&Q framework.

1.2 Purpose and Objectives

Original Text

This Guide intends to offer a compliant, integrated C&Q approach to making the qualification process efficient and cost-effective. The approach includes a System Risk Assessment process that identifies the Critical Aspects (CAs) and/or Critical Design Elements (CDEs). CDEs are system functions and features identified as having the potential to control risks to product quality and therefore ensure patient safety. Qualification confirms that the CDEs and design risk controls identified through the System Risk Assessment have all been checked, tested, and found to be installed and operating as specified.

This Guide recognizes industry best practices regarding C&Q and presents concepts that can assist organizations in meeting current regulatory expectations for system qualification by:

  • Providing guidance on how to apply Quality Risk Management (QRM) to C&Q to establish CDEs
  • Defining the focus of an integrated C&Q effort to be the CAs/CDEs and Critical Process Parameters (CPPs) which provide the ability to deliver the product Critical Quality Attributes (CQAs)
  • Defining best practices for User Requirements Specification (URS), Design Review (DR), Design Qualification (DQ), and acceptance and release of the system
  • Clarifying the change management processes required to support the C&Q process
  • Providing Good Engineering Practice (GEP) documentation standards
  • Describing where testing should be duplicated and where it is acceptable to test once
  • Addressing integration of equipment automation systems
  • Providing examples of appropriate C&Q deliverables

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The CQA → CPP → CA → CDE Hierarchy

This is the logical backbone of the entire ISPE Vol. 5 framework and must be fully understood:

  • CQA (Critical Quality Attribute): a quality characteristic the drug product must meet, such as sterility, assay, or pH
  • CPP (Critical Process Parameter): a process parameter that affects a CQA, such as sterilization temperature or fill weight
  • CA (Critical Aspect): a function or capability that must be ensured in the process (from ASTM E2500)
  • CDE (Critical Design Element): a specific design function or physical feature of the equipment/system that directly affects a CA

The core task of qualification is to verify that all CDEs are installed as designed and operate properly.

Analogy: Focusing on CDEs Is Like an Electric Vehicle Safety Audit

To audit the safety of an electric vehicle, you do not need to test every screw. You only need to confirm that the components that truly affect driving safety (the braking system, battery overheating protection, the steering mechanism) are installed to specification and operate properly.

CDEs are the pharmaceutical plant version of these "critical components": test only what really matters and avoid pointless repeat testing.

Practical Application: The Benefits of C&Q for a CDMO

For a CDMO, the direct business benefits of adopting the ISPE C&Q framework are:

  • Shorter qualification time: FAT data can be used directly as IQ evidence, with no need to repeat the work on site
  • Lower cost: focusing only on CDEs avoids excessive testing of non-critical components
  • Faster onboarding of new clients: a standardized URS → DQ → C&Q process makes client audits smoother
  • Stronger regulatory defensibility: System Risk Assessment documentation is the best defense against an FDA 483

1.3 Scope

Original Text

This Guide applies to systems used by pharmaceutical manufacturers of human and veterinary drugs, including biological drug products.

Medical devices have separate regulatory requirements and are outside of the scope of this Guide. For combination devices, this Guide can be applied to the manufacturing equipment for the combination device, but not to the device itself.

The following areas are not in the scope of the C&Q process as described in this Guide:

  • Product and process development, including identification of any process requirements that influence system design
  • Computerized systems software development and testing (refer to ISPE GAMP® 5)
  • Process validation, including Process Performance Qualification (PPQ) and Continued/Ongoing Process Verification (refer to ISPE Good Practice Guide: Practical Implementation of the Lifecycle Approach to Process Validation)
  • Cleaning validation
  • Sanitization and sterilization validation
  • Analytical procedures and methods validation
  • Decommissioning activities (refer to ISPE Good Practice Guide: Decommissioning of Pharmaceutical Equipment and Facilities)

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Drawing the Boundary of C&Q

The most important lesson of this section: PPQ begins where C&Q ends.

C&Q confirms that "the system meets design specifications and is fit for use", while PPQ (Process Performance Qualification) confirms that "this system can consistently produce product that meets specifications". The two have different subjects: the former is the equipment/system, the latter is the process/product.

Key Point: Computer System Software Development Is Out of Scope

Software V&V (Validation & Verification) falls under ISPE GAMP 5, not this Guide. This boundary is very important. When you qualify a SCADA system:

  • Hardware (installation of sensors, valves, control panels) → ISPE Vol. 5 C&Q
  • Software (control logic, recipe management, audit trail) → GAMP 5

In practice, the two often need integrated planning to avoid duplicate testing or gaps.

Analogy: C&Q Is the Handover Inspection of a House, PPQ Is Living in It

C&Q is like the handover inspection when you buy a new house: confirming that the plumbing, electrical work, walls, and windows were built as drawn and work properly.

PPQ is like "living there for three months": confirming that it is draft-free in winter, not stuffy in summer, and that there is enough water for daily use.

Both are necessary, but they are completely different in nature and must not be confused.

1.4 Process Overview and Structure of the Guide

Original Text

An overview of the integrated C&Q approach is shown in Figure 1.1 (Science and Risk-Based C&Q Process Map).

The inputs to the integrated C&Q process include product and process CQAs/CPPs (established through research and development and/or the technology transfer process) and the application of QRM to define the control strategy.

The steps in the integrated C&Q process are:

  • URS (Chapter 2)
  • System Classification (Chapter 3)
  • System Risk Assessment (Chapter 4)
  • DR/DQ (Chapter 5)
  • C&Q Planning (Chapter 6)
  • C&Q Testing and Documentation (Chapter 7)
  • Acceptance and Release (Chapter 8)
  • Periodic Review (Chapter 9)

Also included are support programs:

  • Vendor Assessment for C&Q Documentation Purposes (Chapter 10)
  • Engineering Quality Process (Chapter 11)
  • Change Management (Chapter 12)
  • Good Documentation Practices for C&Q (Chapter 13)
  • Strategies for Implementation (Chapter 14)

The process begins with the development of the URS. The next step is system classification — categorizing systems as direct impact or not direct impact. Direct impact systems are commissioned and qualified while systems that are not direct impact are commissioned only.

C&Q testing may include pre-delivery inspection, Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT), construction quality document verification, and Site Acceptance Testing (SAT). C&Q planning should identify aspects of the FAT and SAT that will be utilized to satisfy qualification requirements.

Completion of testing leads to acceptance and release, the last phase of C&Q. Systems then go through a Periodic Review to ensure they remain suitable for the intended purpose.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Reading the Integrated C&Q Process Map

The whole ISPE C&Q process is a requirements-driven, risk-focused, iterative structure:

  • URS (Chapter 2) → defines "what I want"
  • System Classification (Chapter 3) → determines "how important this system is"
  • System Risk Assessment (Chapter 4) → identifies "where things could go wrong"
  • DR/DQ (Chapter 5) → confirms "whether the design meets the requirements"
  • C&Q Planning (Chapter 6) → plans "how to verify"
  • Testing (Chapters 7-8) → executes verification and releases the system
  • Periodic Review (Chapter 9) → ensures continued effectiveness

Key Point: FAT Can Serve as IQ Evidence!

This is one of the most practically valuable concepts in ISPE Vol. 5: FAT (Factory Acceptance Testing) completed at the supplier's facility can be used directly as part of the evidence for IQ (Installation Qualification), with no need to repeat the tests on site.

The prerequisites are:

  • The FAT is executed with clear QA oversight at the supplier
  • The FAT protocol is written in accordance with the GEP requirements of the C&Q plan
  • The C&Q plan clearly documents which FAT results are accepted as qualification evidence

Analogy: Direct Impact Systems Are Like Operating Room Equipment

Direct Impact System: a system that directly affects drug product quality, such as a purified water system, autoclave, or filling machine. It must undergo full Commissioning + Qualification.

Not Direct Impact System: for example office air conditioning, utility mains water piping, or lighting. It needs only Commissioning, with no Qualification documentation.

Just as in a hospital only the operating rooms need laminar-flow HVAC audits, the ventilation in an office corridor does not need the same rigorous acceptance process.

Practical Application: Integrating FAT/SAT Saves 30-50% of Qualification Time

When a CDMO plans the introduction of new equipment, if it requires the supplier at the time of the purchase contract to write the FAT protocol according to the ISPE C&Q framework, and sends QA personnel to attend the FAT, the data can be incorporated directly into the IQ report.

Actual cases show that, compared with the traditional approach (a full IQ on site after FAT), this method can save 30-50% of qualification labor hours and significantly shorten equipment start-up time.

1.5 Rationale for this Second Edition

Original Text

This Guide, the second edition of the ISPE Baseline® Guide: Commissioning and Qualification, has been designed to present industry best practices for C&Q. It also combines concepts from regulatory guidances (e.g., from EMA, FDA, ICH) and the following ISPE documents that are superseded with this revision:

  • ISPE Baseline® Guide: Volume 5 – Commissioning and Qualification (First Edition)
  • ISPE Guide: Science and Risk-Based Approach for the Delivery of Facilities, Systems, and Equipment
  • ISPE Good Practice Guide: Applied Risk Management for Commissioning and Qualification

Certain aspects of the C&Q approach described in the previous edition are retired and replaced:

  • Component Criticality Assessment — Since CDEs are identified as an output of the System Risk Assessment, assessment of the criticality of each system component is unnecessary.
  • Enhanced Commissioning — This Guide describes how the level of testing can be aligned with criticality and intended purpose of the system.
  • Enhanced Design Review — This Guide describes an approach for performing effective DRs using the System Risk Assessment to achieve DQ.
  • Enhanced Documentation — Good Documentation Practices are defined in the Guide.
  • Indirect Impact — The term is not used. The Guide describes C&Q for systems based on whether a system has direct product impact or not.
  • V-Model — The integrated C&Q approach provides a lifecycle C&Q approach and is represented by a different process map. The V-model appropriately represented the links between the design process and testing, but did not incorporate a risk assessment process based on product quality.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The End of the V-Model and the Birth of a New Framework

The V-Model was the gold standard of validation from the 1990s to the 2010s, mapping design-phase documents to test-phase documents in a V shape:

  • URS ↔ PQ
  • Functional Spec ↔ OQ
  • Design Spec ↔ IQ

The reason the second edition of ISPE abandons the V-Model: the V-Model emphasizes document correspondence but does not integrate "product quality risk assessment", so non-critical systems also underwent extensive qualification testing, which was highly inefficient.

The new framework has QRM (Quality Risk Management) at its core and qualifies only the CDEs that truly affect product quality.

Key Point: The Term "Indirect Impact" Is Abolished

The first edition had three classifications: Direct Impact / Indirect Impact / No Impact. The second edition simplifies this to two: Direct Impact / Not Direct Impact.

Reason: "Indirect Impact" was vaguely defined in practice and often caused disputes among validation engineers: is cleaning water Indirect or Direct? What about facility HVAC?

The new classification is clearer: "Does it directly affect product quality?" There are only two answers.

Analogy: From a "Full-Body Checkup" to a "Targeted Precision Examination"

Traditional V-Model qualification is like a full-body checkup every year: brain, heart, digestive tract, bone density... every organ is scanned whether or not you have symptoms. It is time-consuming and expensive.

The new ISPE framework is like a targeted precision examination based on your personal health risks: a family history of heart disease means a focused cardiac check; a risk of diabetes means close blood glucose monitoring. For areas with no risk, a routine checkup is enough.

This is the essence of "Science and Risk-Based C&Q": focusing on real risk.

1.6 Key Concepts

1.6.1 Efficient Approach without Redundant Testing

The foundation of this Guide is based on the expectation that engineers and system vendors ensure systems are installed and operate to meet the design specifications and user requirements. This is commonly referred to as commissioning. Best industry practice is to utilize such an approach when installing any new system.

When a system has direct impact on product quality (direct impact system), the CDEs of the system can be verified by the approach described in this Guide. This approach ensures that identified CPPs are maintained; CPPs ensure that CQAs are attained. Testing of system CDEs is utilized to support qualification of the system. Thus, the documentation of the testing performed in commissioning needs to be acceptable to be presented in a regulatory audit or inspection.

1.6.2 Terminology for Product Impacting Attributes

There is a recognition that critical utilities, such as Water for Injection (WFI) systems, may not have product CQAs but do have compendial requirements and CPPs. These parameters can directly influence the system's ability to deliver product meeting those attributes.

The authors continue to use the terminology of CPPs and CQAs for all systems, including WFI and upstream equipment. It is important for individual organizations/sites to define what is critical for their products, processes, and systems.

1.6.3 Quality Unit Oversight

The Quality Unit is expected to have oversight of the entire validation lifecycle including qualification. Because the Quality Unit is not expected to have expertise in all aspects of pharmaceutical manufacturing systems, identified SMEs (Subject Matter Experts) should be responsible for determining how best to design and test systems to ensure they are suitable for the intended purpose.

The Quality Unit needs to be engaged in the process, be considered part of the C&Q team, approve the appropriate testing plans and documents for systems that directly affect product quality, and approve the final acceptance of the qualified state. The authors see this as a Quality Assurance (QA) role, not a documentation quality control role.

Tutorial Commentary

1.6.1 Core Concept: Commissioning Documents = Quality Documents That Can Be Submitted to Inspectors

This is the most commonly misunderstood point. Many engineers believe commissioning documents are "engineering documents" that only need to be technically correct and need not be written to GMP standards.

ISPE's position is very clear: when commissioning tests are used to support qualification, these documents must withstand regulatory inspection. This means:

  • Data must be recorded truthfully and contemporaneously (no backdating)
  • Discrepancies must be documented and handled
  • The sign-off process must be complete

The quality of the commissioning reports completed by engineers directly determines qualification efficiency: a good FAT report can eliminate a great deal of repeat testing.

1.6.2 Key Point: How Are the CPPs of a WFI System Defined?

A WFI system does not itself manufacture drug product, but the quality of its output water affects every process that uses WFI. Therefore:

  • The CQAs of WFI = the endotoxin limit, conductivity, and TOC of WFI (these are "intermediate quality attributes")
  • The CPPs of WFI = distillation temperature, circulation flow rate, circulation pressure
  • The CDEs of WFI = temperature sensor accuracy, heat exchanger integrity, loop material (316L stainless steel)

Understanding this extended application helps validation engineers avoid being at a loss when facing utility systems.

1.6.3 Core Concept: QA Is a Quality Assurance Role, Not a Document Review Machine

ISPE specifically stresses that the role of the Quality Unit is Quality Assurance, not Documentation Quality Control.

Practical implications:

  • QA does not need to review every technical detail word by word (that is the SME's responsibility)
  • QA needs to confirm that the C&Q plan covers all product quality risks
  • QA is responsible for the final release decision, based on the results of the risk assessment, not on the thickness of the documentation

This shift in mindset is an important cultural challenge for the QA departments of many Taiwanese CDMOs.

Analogy: QA Is the Judge, the SME Is the Expert Witness

In court, the judge (QA) does not need to run DNA analysis personally, but must assess whether the report submitted by the expert witness (SME) is credible and sufficient to support a ruling.

The final release decision is made by the judge (QA), with clear accountability. The expert witness (SME) provides technical judgment and should not be asked to go beyond their own area of expertise.

Tutorial Commentary

Term Clarification: The Relationship Between IQ / OQ / PQ and C&Q

The second edition of ISPE retains the definitions of IQ/OQ/PQ, but in actual execution they are output documents of the C&Q process, not independent steps.

Traditional thinking: do IQ first → then OQ → then PQ (three sequential stages)
ISPE new thinking: FAT, SAT, and on-site tests are planned logically, and the IQ/OQ/PQ documents are a summary of the results of these tests, not a mandatory execution sequence.

Key Point: The Critical Difference Between Deviation and Discrepancy

Discrepancy: any result that does not meet expectations. It may be minor and may be corrected directly.

Deviation: specifically defined by ISPE as "a significant difference affecting a CA/CDE that cannot be directly corrected". It must be approved by the Quality Unit before it can be closed.

Mixing up these two terms in qualification reports is a common mistake and easily causes unnecessary problems during inspections.

Chapter 2: User Requirements Specification  |  pp. 19–22

2.1 Introduction

The User Requirements Specification (URS) brings together system requirements from multidisciplinary sources to support system design, C&Q, operations, and maintenance. Within the C&Q process, it is a foundational document that identifies the product and process requirements for the system. These product quality related user requirements are based on product knowledge (CQAs), process knowledge (CPPs), regulatory requirements, and organization/site quality requirements. The product and process requirements in the URS are inputs to the C&Q process; they provide the science-based knowledge that forms the basis for applying QRM to C&Q.

It is recommended that a URS is developed for each system that has the potential to impact product quality, or a direct impact system (as determined through system classification described in Chapter 3). A formal URS document is not always necessary. A statement of the URS may be contained in various forms, e.g., purchase request, functional specification, change request, or data sheet. For standard off-the-shelf or simple catalog systems, a statement of the URS may be a purchase order, vendor cut sheet, or catalog data. The system would be verified against this document.

Commentary

Core Concept: What Is a URS?

The URS (User Requirements Specification) is the starting document of the whole C&Q process. It is developed by a multidisciplinary team and consolidates system requirements from quality, engineering, regulatory, HSE and other functions.

  • CQAs (critical quality attributes) → come from product knowledge and define the quality standards the final product must meet
  • CPPs (critical process parameters) → come from process knowledge and define the process variables that must be controlled
  • QRM (quality risk management) → the URS provides the scientific basis that makes the risk assessment well founded

The core value of a URS: it tells the system "what to do", not "how to do it".

Everyday Analogy: A Requirements List for Building a House

A URS is like the list an owner gives the architect before design starts: "I need 4 bedrooms, 2 bathrooms, an open kitchen and an accessible ramp." The list says what is wanted but not "which brick to use for the walls" or "which route the pipes take". That is the designer's job (Basis of Design, BOD).

Key Point: A URS Does Not Have to Be a Standalone Document

For standard off-the-shelf items or simple equipment, the URS can be a purchase requisition, a vendor catalogue page, a functional specification, or a change request. What matters is that the system is ultimately verified against that document.

2.2 Development of the User Requirements Specification

The URS defines the requirements that must be met for a system to be suitable for the intended purpose; it should not detail how those requirements are met. The URS is developed by a project team that includes SMEs (refer to Section 6.4 for an example approval matrix). It can be revised throughout the system lifecycle.

The URS document SHOULD include:

  • Describe the intended purpose of the system
  • State the requirements in verifiable terms without describing how the requirements will be met
  • State the category and source of each requirement
    • Categories may include, for example:
      • Quality
      • Business
      • Health, Safety, and Environmental (HSE)
    • Sources may include, for example:
      • Product and process requirements derived from:
        • Product and process knowledge (CQAs, CPPs, CAs)
        • GMP regulatory requirements
        • Organization/site quality requirements
      • System Risk Assessment (includes CAs and associated CDEs)
      • Range and accuracy for any controlled parameters
      • National, local and site requirements, as applicable
      • Business, HSE, system owner, and SME requirements
      • Engineering specifications and industry standards (e.g., ASME, ASTM, ISO)
      • Project requirements document or project charter
      • Legacy system evaluations

The URS should also include the following:

  • Data integrity requirements
  • Data storage/display requirements
  • Alarm requirements, with identification of the critical alarms
  • System automation requirements
    • If there are any connections (e.g., data archiving) and interactions (e.g., alarm initiation) to a larger automation control system, then that system can have a separate URS. There needs to be clearly defined boundaries between the systems.
Note: System software development and testing is outside of the scope of this Guide. Refer to ISPE GAMP® 5: A Risk-Based Approach to Compliant GxP Computerized Systems.

The URS, which may be further detailed during early project phases, provides the foundation for the Basis of Design (BOD) and becomes a reference used to establish that a system is suitable for the intended purpose. To be suitable for the intended purpose, systems need to meet their URS.

The URS should NOT include the following information as requirements:

  • How a requirement is to be met
  • Detailed design specifications
  • Sequence of operations
  • Generic statements (e.g., must comply with local codes, must meet all GxPs)
  • General references to government/national standards (e.g., nonspecific reference to ASME code, ISO standards)
  • Contractual terms and deliverables
  • Unverifiable parameters
  • Standard functionality of the equipment type

Refer to Chapter 16 (Appendix 2) for an example URS template. It is the responsibility of the organization/site to designate quality impacting elements of their URS in a manner that is most appropriate for their integrated C&Q process.

Commentary

Core Concept: Three Categories of URS Requirements

Each requirement should state its Category and its Source:

  • Quality: requirements related to GMP regulations, CQAs and CPPs
  • Business: requirements related to capacity, efficiency and cost
  • HSE: requirements related to health, safety and environment

Why source tagging matters: if the Quality Unit (QU) has already approved the CQA/CPP source documents, the URS does not need to be approved again by the QU (see Section 2.3).

Core Concept: What Are CDEs and CAs?

CA (Critical Attribute): a key property or characteristic of the system whose deviation could affect product quality.

CDE (Critical Design Element): the specific design element in the system used to control or achieve a CA. CDEs come from the system risk assessment (SRA).

The URS should reference the output of the SRA (including CAs and the related CDEs) so that design requirements have a science- and risk-based rationale.

Everyday Analogy: A Restaurant Order vs. the Kitchen Recipe

The URS is the customer's order: "I want a steak, no onions, medium." That is a requirement, and it can be verified. How the chef prepares the ingredients, which pan is used and how many minutes it is marinated belong to the kitchen's design specification (DS), not the URS. Writing "pan-fry in a cast-iron pan" on the customer's order blurs the boundary between URS and DS.

CDMO Practice: The Challenges of Writing a URS

In a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization) environment, writing a URS is especially complex because:

  • Limited client knowledge: the client may not yet have fully established the CPPs for its product (early clinical phase), so the URS must leave room for flexibility.
  • Multi-product shared equipment: the same system serves different molecules for several clients, so the URS must cover the most stringent requirements (worst case).
  • Confidentiality: a client's CQAs/CPPs are trade secrets, and the CDMO must obtain enough information under an NDA to write a correct URS.
  • Practical advice: build a standard URS template (with a master list of quality requirements) and adjust it for each client/product. This greatly improves efficiency and ensures consistency.

  • Any change that may affect product quality → assess the impact through the system risk assessment (SRA)
  • All changes → follow the change management process (see Chapter 12)

This ensures the URS always reflects the actual design intent of the system and stays linked to QRM.

3.1 Introduction

This Guide presents an approach for classifying systems into two categories depending on the system capability to impact product quality. System classification establishes whether a system is commissioned and qualified or only commissioned, as follows:

  • Direct impact systems are commissioned and qualified
  • Not direct impact systems are commissioned
Note: In the previous edition of this Baseline® Guide, component classification could be carried out as a subset of system classification. Component classification is no longer necessary since the System Risk Assessment (as described in Chapter 4) identifies CDEs for the system in a more efficient manner.

For systems that are not direct impact, the scope of commissioning is determined by SMEs and is based on the business and HSE criticality of the system. For example, a chilled water system serving an administrative building (not direct impact) may be balanced only. Meanwhile, a chilled water system (not direct impact) serving direct impact process equipment may be balanced, with the balancing confirmed at the minimum and maximum design flow rates to ensure reliable system performance during operation.

Commentary

Important Change: Component Classification Has Been Eliminated

In the first edition of ISPE Vol. 5, components within a system could be classified further (Critical / Non-critical) after the system classification.

The second edition has dropped this practice, because the system risk assessment (SRA, Chapter 4) identifies CDEs (Critical Design Elements) more efficiently and directly replaces the function of component classification.

In other words: the SRA is more detailed and more scientifically grounded, so the extra intermediate step of component classification is no longer needed.

Everyday Analogy: Airport Control vs. the Car Park

The runway lighting system at an airport (which directly affects the safety of takeoffs and landings) = Direct Impact, requiring rigorous commissioning + validation. The lighting in the airport car park (which does not affect aircraft safety) = Not Direct Impact, requiring only basic commissioning (the lights come on). Both are "lighting systems", but because their level of impact differs, the regulatory requirements are completely different.

Defining System Boundaries  |  pp. 23–24

3.2 System Boundaries

Projects are commonly divided up into systems to facilitate construction management, document collation, turnover, and C&Q. System boundaries are markings on design drawings to distinguish systems from one another; this includes all components and piping to logically define what is included and what is not included within the system. System boundaries should be defined and documented early on in the project (at the BOD phase) to enable development of the systems list and subsequent execution of System Risk Assessments. The systems are usually listed in the C&Q Plan (refer to Chapter 6). System boundaries should be defined such that the systems cover the entirety of the project scope; this is typically documented in the system Process and Instrumentation Diagram (P&ID) or Process Flow Diagram (PFD).

The scope of supply from vendors is often used as the basis for defining system boundaries; however, these boundaries should be carefully reviewed as there may be benefits in defining the boundaries differently. For example, boundary considerations for a direct impact compressed air system are as follows:

  • A Point of Use (POU) filter is typically outside the vendor scope of supply for a compressed air system but may or may not be included in the system boundaries.
  • The POU filter could be included within the boundaries for the compressed air distribution system. All compressed air testing and verification activities would be part of the compressed air system.
  • Alternatively, the POU filter could be included in the process system for which the air is being supplied to. All compressed air testing and verification activities for the POU filter would then be part of the process system.

During the early stages of a project, system boundaries may also be subdivided to facilitate development of C&Q documentation by different suppliers. For example, the URS for a compressed air system can include generation and distribution sections, each of which may have its own set of C&Q documentation.

The optimum approach for the project should be evaluated and then defined in the C&Q Plan. In the compressed air system example discussed earlier, it may be simpler to provide a URS/C&Q package for the generation system from one supplier and a separate URS/C&Q package for the storage and distribution system that is built on-site by a separate supplier.

Regardless of the C&Q documentation strategy, systems need to meet their URS.

Commentary

Core Concept: Defining System Boundaries

System boundaries are the limits marked on design drawings (P&ID or PFD) that clearly define:

  • Which equipment, piping and instruments belong to this system (included)
  • Which do not belong to this system (excluded)

Boundaries should be clearly defined at the BOD (Basis of Design) stage and listed in the systems list of the C&Q Plan.

The boundary definition directly affects:

  • The scope of the system risk assessment
  • The scope of IQ/OQ testing
  • Responsibility for the C&Q document package

Core Concept: P&ID vs. PFD

P&ID (Piping and Instrumentation Diagram): shows all piping, instruments, control valves and equipment connections. It is the main drawing on which system boundaries are marked and has a high level of detail.

PFD (Process Flow Diagram): shows material flow and major equipment. It is more conceptual and is used in the early design stage.

System boundaries are usually marked on the P&ID with a revision cloud (bubble) or a boundary line.

Everyday Analogy: Who Owns the POU Filter?

A point-of-use (POU) filter on compressed air is like the electricity meter in an apartment building: it can belong to the "building common system" or to the "individual unit system". Either approach is reasonable, but one must be chosen explicitly and not left vague. ISPE's core message: where the boundary is drawn matters less than that it is clearly stated and recorded in the C&Q Plan.

Practical Warning: Vendor Scope of Supply ≠ System Boundary

Many engineers simply use the vendor's scope of supply as the system boundary, which can cause problems:

  • POU filters, pipe fittings, control panels and the like may fall outside the scope of supply but functionally belong to the same system
  • If the boundary is poorly defined, the qualification documentation will have "gaps": equipment for which nobody is responsible

Recommendation: define the boundary on the principle of functional completeness, then adjust it against the vendor's scope of supply.

CDMO Practice: Boundary Management for Multi-Supplier Systems

In a new CDMO facility or an expansion project, the compressed air system is often built in sections by several suppliers (compressor package + in-plant piping + POU filters). Recommended approach:

  • List the boundary of each subsystem explicitly in the C&Q Plan and mark it on the P&ID
  • If the system is split into several URSs/C&Q packages, make sure there are clear handover verification requirements where the package boundaries meet
  • The main system URS should state how the subsystem URSs relate to one another (for example: "see DOC-001 for the compressed air compressor package URS and DOC-002 for the distribution piping URS")
  • However the system is split, every system must ultimately comply with its URS

Core Concept: The Q1–Q8 Classification Logic

  • Q8: Does the system provide or verify product identity information? (product identification)

Key Point: Classification Depends on Professional Judgment

ISPE states explicitly that Q1–Q8 are "tools to support judgment", not a "mechanical checklist". Classification must be based on a thorough understanding of the product, the process and the nature of the system.

For example: a chilled water system serving an administration building → Not Direct Impact; one serving the cooling jacket of a bioreactor → Q1 (CPP temperature control) must be evaluated → may be Direct Impact.

Classification should be performed by "appropriately qualified personnel": engineers alone are not enough, and quality and process experts must be involved.

Only if all answers are "no" does the patient go to the general waiting area (= Not Direct Impact, requiring only commissioning). The triage questionnaire is an aid, and in the end a physician's judgment is still needed.

  • The URS is the foundation document of C&Q. It consolidates CQAs, CPPs, regulatory requirements and the organization's quality requirements, and states "what the system must do" rather than "how it does it".

4.1 Introduction

The System Risk Assessment is the application of QRM (Quality Risk Management) to examine the product quality risk controls for direct impact systems. This assessment identifies the critical design controls (CAs/CDEs) and procedural controls that are required to mitigate system risks to an acceptable level. It is important for the project team performing the System Risk Assessment to include technical SMEs that understand the science behind the process and the risks associated with the CQAs.

System Risk Assessments should be developed for direct impact systems (as determined through system classification described in Chapter 3). For standard off-the-shelf or simple catalog systems, some organizations may consider it unnecessary to perform the System Risk Assessment; a simple protocol or report (e.g., Installation and Operational Verification) could be used to provide the documented evidence that the installation and operation meet the system requirements.

Figure 4.1 shows the relationship between the activities and documents in the C&Q process for direct impact systems. C&Q inspection and testing activities can take place during fabrication and construction as well as during the commissioning/testing activities.

After the System Risk Assessment is completed, the URS should be checked to ensure that the CAs/CDEs have been included; if they are not included, then the URS should be revised to add them. Procedural control elements should be added to the C&Q scope.

Acceptance and release of the system (described in more detail in Chapter 8) involves confirming that the controls identified in the System Risk Assessment are in place as follows:

  • The design controls (CAs/CDEs) have been shown to operate as specified
  • The procedural controls are in place
Commentary

📚 Core Concept: What Is an SRA?

A System Risk Assessment (SRA) is a structured document that applies the principles of ICH Q9 Quality Risk Management (QRM) specifically to a direct impact system.

It answers one core question: "Which design features and operating procedures of this system are the critical lines of defense protecting product quality?"

  • CAs (Critical Aspects): system attributes that directly affect a CQA (e.g., sterilization temperature, filter pore size)
  • CDEs (Critical Design Elements): design elements used to control a CA (e.g., temperature sensor location, choice of membrane material)
  • Procedural Controls: managing risk through SOPs/work procedures (e.g., periodic calibration, operator training)

💡 Analogy: An SRA is like the structural calculation of a building

Before a house is built, the structural engineer calculates which beams and columns are load-bearing (= CAs/CDEs) and which are merely decorative (= indirect impact). The SRA is the "structural calculation" of a pharmaceutical facility and its equipment: only the parts identified as critical require full qualification testing.

⚠️ Key Point: The Two-Way Relationship with the URS

Once the SRA is complete, you must go back, review and update the URS to make sure all CAs/CDEs are captured in the requirements document. This is an iterative "assess first, then supplement the requirements" process, unlike the traditional linear "write requirements, then test" mindset.

4.1.1 Rationale

The traditional approach to qualification did not directly utilize commissioning documentation; instead, it often relied on an additional set of documents, the Installation Qualification (IQ) and Operational Qualification (OQ) protocols. These protocols provided the documented evidence that the system was installed and operating according to its specification. The scope of the IQ/OQ was determined by various elements such as organization/site established guidelines, vendor specifications, user requirements, and regulatory and guidance documents.

This traditional approach to qualification often resulted in IQ/OQ activities covering more scope than was necessary and repeating successful tests already performed during engineering start-up and commissioning. Furthermore, commissioning activities were commonly not robust enough or well documented since engineering teams anticipated that additional or repeat testing would be performed during qualification activities.

This Guide proposes a more streamlined approach to C&Q. Engineering documentation (i.e., commissioning inspection and testing documentation) is used to provide the documented evidence that the system has been installed and operates to meet the specifications. The CQAs and related CPPs are documented for the system, and a System Risk Assessment is developed to define potential risks to the CQAs and identify design (CAs/CDEs) and procedural controls used to mitigate these risks. Qualification confirms the system is suitable for the intended purpose and that the risk controls (design and procedural controls) are in place and functioning as specified.

Commentary

⚠️ Three Major Problems with the Traditional Approach

  1. Duplicated testing: IQ/OQ repeats tests that were already performed at the factory, wasting time and resources.
  2. Lack of rigor in commissioning documents: Engineers know that "QA will retest it anyway," so commissioning documents are written carelessly and without enough detail.
  3. Unfocused scope: What IQ/OQ does and does not test lacks a risk-based rationale, and templates often lead to "test everything."

📚 The Core Logic of the New Approach

The reform spirit of ISPE Vol. 5:

  • Engineering documents = qualification documents: if the documentation is of sufficient quality, commissioning results can be used directly as the basis for qualification
  • The SRA defines the scope: only the parts identified by the SRA as CAs/CDEs require qualification testing
  • Redefinition of qualification: the focus of qualification is whether "risk control measures are in place and effective," not whether "all specifications are met"

💡 Analogy: A chef's mise en place vs. a food safety inspection

The traditional approach is like a food safety officer repeating every step from start to finish after the chef has cooked the dish. The new approach is like the officer specifying in advance which steps are critical to food safety (= the SRA) and inspecting only those steps, while the chef's preparation records can be cited directly.

4.1.2 Purpose and Benefits

The System Risk Assessment has multiple purposes and is a means to:

  • Identify, assess, and record the quality risks to CQAs and associated controls
  • Identify and document the design and procedural controls that are required to control and manage the identified risks
    • The rationale for these controls can also be documented in the System Risk Assessment
    • Testing of the design controls (CAs/CDEs) and verification of procedural controls provides the documentation used to support system qualification
  • Provide guidance for the scope of the DQ, i.e., the scope covers the design of the CAs/CDEs
  • Provide the information that is required for the basis of acceptance and release
  • Provide a focus for Quality Unit oversight

The System Risk Assessment establishes (if not available in other documents) and documents the CAs/CDEs of the system operation and design that can impact product quality. It identifies the design and procedural controls that would mitigate those risks to product quality to an acceptable level. By verifying these controls, the system is confirmed to match that risk profile.

Commentary

📚 What Is a "Risk Profile"?

The Risk Profile is the overall description, produced once the SRA is complete, of a system's residual risk status after all control measures are in place.

Important: the risk profile is dynamic. It counts as a "confirmed risk profile" only after all CAs/CDEs have been verified; until then it is called a Provisional Risk Assessment.

🏛 CDMO Application Scenario

In a CDMO (contract development and manufacturing organization) environment, the SRA serves multiple clients at the same time:

  • The first client's product uses a bioreactor, so the SRA must identify dissolved oxygen control as a CA
  • The second client's product is more temperature-sensitive, so the SRA must additionally identify the jacket temperature control CDEs
  • QA can use the SRA to tell each client clearly which design features of the system protect their product

4.2 System Risk Assessment Process

As a prerequisite, the CQAs and associated CPPs should be identified and documented in the URS.

This Guide presents the following approach to performing the System Risk Assessment:

  • A small team develops an initial baseline draft that includes development of the process description and identification of design and procedural controls
  • A broader team reviews this initial baseline draft, making any necessary revisions to ensure the information is correct and no risks or controls have been omitted, and assigns the system risk profile

As a general guide, the initial session by the small team and the review session by the broader team should not exceed more than a few hours each if the preparation work is complete and teams with the correct level of expertise are used.

An example System Risk Assessment format is presented in Section 4.4.

Commentary

⚠️ Prerequisites Matter

The guide explicitly requires that CQAs and CPPs be documented in the URS first before the SRA begins. If a CQA turns out to be unclear during the SRA, work must go back for clarification first; otherwise the quality of the assessment cannot be assured.

💡 Analogy: A two-stage review is like a design review process

Baseline Draft = the designer sketches the first draft (well prepared and efficient). Broader Review = a cross-functional review meeting (more perspectives, finds omissions). Doing them separately is far more efficient than gathering 10 people at the outset to speculate in the room.

  • Make sure every operating step is reviewed systematically
  • Steer technical discussion back to the "risk and control measure" framework

  • Be approved and signed by high-level management
  • Be tracked under change management

This reflects the spirit of ICH Q9: risk management does not mean eliminating all risk, but managing risk on the basis that it is understood and accepted.

📚 The Role of the Traceability Matrix

A Traceability Matrix is a management tool that links three things: URS requirements ↔ SRA risk controls ↔ test documents.

The guide offers a flexible option: if a company uses a traceability matrix, CDEs can be added to the matrix only, without updating the URS, reducing the document maintenance burden.

Practical advice: large cross-functional projects (such as building a new facility) suit a traceability matrix; for small equipment modifications, updating the URS directly is simpler.

Therefore a CDMO's SRA must be performed separately for each process/client, and the same risk level conclusion cannot be shared.

4.2.3 Using Completed Assessments as a Starting Point for New System Baseline Drafts

The experience and documentation gained from completing System Risk Assessments can be applied to future systems. In addition, many systems used for pharmaceutical manufacturing and packaging are standard systems that have been in use for a reasonable period of time, have built-in control strategies, and are developed by experienced vendors with established engineering quality systems. The small team that develops the baseline draft (as described in Section 4.2.1) should assess the new system against the completed System Risk Assessment for the existing system that is identical/similar. The SMEs should determine if the system under review has different functionality and/or components. If the SMEs determine that these differences may introduce additional or different risks, then the baseline draft needs to be updated to include the risks and associated risk controls.

The remainder of the System Risk Assessment process would proceed as described in Section 4.2.2. The broader team reviews the baseline draft and determines the risk profile for the new system.

The use of completed assessments as a starting point for new system baseline drafts can improve the efficiency and consistency of the process.

Commentary

📚 The Process Logic of a "Templated SRA"

This section provides a very practical efficiency mechanism:

  1. Take an existing (completed and approved) SRA as the starting point
  2. The team compares the differences between the old and new systems (function, components, configuration)
  3. Update risk identification and control measures only for the "points of difference"
  4. Carry over the unchanged parts directly
  5. The full broader review is still required to confirm that the overall assessment is correct

Important: this is not "copy and paste." Each SRA must still be independently reviewed and approved.

⚠️ Criteria for Judging "Identical" vs. "Similar"

The following differences may introduce new risks and require the SRA to be updated:

  • Expanded process parameter range (e.g., higher temperature or pressure)
  • Different manufacturer or model (even if the function is the same)
  • Control loops added or removed (e.g., changed from manual to automatic)
  • Different installation location (e.g., a room of a different cleanliness classification)
  • Used for a different CQA or product type

🏛 Major Practical Benefits for CDMOs

One of a CDMO's competitive advantages is its ability to standardize. A "templated SRA" lets a CDMO:

  • Line 1 of identical equipment: SRA needs full resources (assume 3 days)
  • Lines 2, 3 and 4 (identical equipment): mainly a gap/difference analysis (assume 0.5 days)
  • Introducing similar equipment from a different brand: build on the existing SRA and focus the review on the differences (assume 1 to 1.5 days)

This not only saves time but also ensures consistency of risk identification across multiple lines, a clear advantage in FDA/EMA inspections.

4.3 Timing of System Risk Assessments

4.3.1 Development of the System Risk Assessment

The System Risk Assessment can be developed after completion of concept design and prior to the start of detailed engineering. It can be updated later as required. The System Risk Assessment can be completed when the Issued for Construction (IFC) design drawings and specifications are available, whether for new or modified systems. These drawings and specifications represent what will be built including any incorporated changes made through the DR/DQ process. Therefore, the System Risk Assessment can be confirmed and approved at this stage.

Note: Depending on the level of product and process knowledge, some of the CDEs associated with CAs may be known while the balance of the CDEs will be identified during the engineering design lifecycle (and can be documented as part of the DQ). All CDEs should be listed in the System Risk Assessment.

The System Risk Assessment defines the system risk profile, i.e., what it will be after the risk controls have been verified to be in place and operating as specified. Until the verification is completed, it is considered a provisional risk assessment.

For novel systems, starting the System Risk Assessment earlier in the design process can be beneficial since this allows for any identified risks to be controlled by design. Most risk control is achieved through system design; a procedural control is not considered as robust.

For standard or catalog systems where the potential risks are well understood and design controls are built in where possible, the System Risk Assessment could be performed at any time before the final DR of the system. Alternatively, an Installation and Operational Verification document could be used. DQ can also confirm that the system risk controls are adequate.

4.3.2 Lifecycle of the System Risk Assessment

Depending on the project size, timing, and complexity, additional product/process or system knowledge may become available after completion of the System Risk Assessment. If this additional knowledge impacts the system risk profile, the System Risk Assessment needs to be reviewed and updated.

System Risk Assessments are not subject to time-based periodic review; it is more efficient to reassess the risk profile in the event of a deviation related to the system performance and determine if an update is required. The System Risk Assessment can also be used as a reference document for assessing potential changes to a system.

For any changes to the system, the URS and System Risk Assessment should both be reviewed as part of the change management process (refer to Chapter 12). If the proposed change introduces additional risks or impacts existing risk controls, the System Risk Assessment (and associated URS) needs to be reviewed and updated.

Commentary

📚 Key Term: IFC (Issued for Construction)

IFC (Issued for Construction) is a key milestone in the engineering design process: it indicates that the design drawings have completed all reviews (including DR/DQ) and are the final version to be used for construction.

ISPE chose IFC as the point for final SRA approval because at this stage "what we are going to build" is fully determined and the risk control measures can be mapped accurately to specific design elements.

📚 The Meaning of "Provisional Risk Assessment"

Provisional Risk Assessment: the interim state after the SRA is approved and before verification is complete.

This is an important regulatory concept: SRA "approval" means "this is how we plan to control the risk," but it cannot be considered the "realized risk profile" until all CAs/CDEs have been verified as passing. Until then, the system cannot be formally released for commercial production.

⚠️ A Major Shift in Industry Thinking: Event-Driven, Not Periodic Review!

This is one of the most important management concepts in this chapter:

Traditional thinking (wrong): "The SRA must be reviewed again every year"

ISPE guide (correct): the SRA is an event-triggered document, reviewed and updated only in the following situations:

  • The system has a deviation related to system performance
  • A system change is proposed (change control)
  • New product/process knowledge is gained that affects the risk judgment

This shift greatly reduces unnecessary documentation burden and lets QA resources focus on where review is really needed.

💡 Analogy: Car warranty vs. accident report

Traditional "periodic review" is like mandating every year that a car go into the shop for a full "system-wide risk reassessment" even when nothing is wrong. ISPE's "event-triggered" approach is like reassessing risk only when the car has an accident or a major modification, and otherwise simply keeping to the existing maintenance plan. This is not "lowering the standard"; it is "putting attention where it is really needed."

🏛 CDMO Application Scenario: Change Management Triggering SRA Updates

Common situations that trigger an SRA review at a CDMO:

  • A client requests a change to the process parameter range (e.g., a higher upper temperature limit)
  • Upgrade of the equipment control system (software version update that may affect alarm settings)
  • A microbiological deviation investigation finds a potential problem in the design of a system
  • Introduction of similar but larger equipment (scale-up)

Every time change control is initiated, an assessment step should be triggered automatically: "Does this change affect an existing SRA?"

Each row in the SRA table follows a structured logic flow:

Commentary

📚 The Logical Structure of the 13 Columns of Table 4.2

The 13 columns can be divided into four functional groups:

  • Columns 1-3 (context): What is this step doing?
  • Columns 4-7 (quality link): Which CQA is it related to? How does it have an effect?
  • Columns 8-10 (control measures): How is it controlled by design (CAs/CDEs)? How is it controlled procedurally (SOPs)?
  • Columns 11-13 (risk decision): What is the overall risk level? What is the rationale? What items remain open?

⚠️ The Practical Meaning of Column 6, "Direct/Indirect Impact"

"Direct": the CPP directly determines the CQA value, e.g., filling speed directly affects fill volume accuracy.

"Indirect": the CPP affects the CQA through an intermediate link, e.g., compressed air pressure affects valve actuation speed, which in turn affects fill volume.

This distinction affects alarm classification: alarms for direct impact are usually designed as high-priority (critical) alarms.

💡 Analogy: The SRA table is like a detective's case sheet

Each row is like a case: columns 2-3 are the crime scene, columns 4-7 are the victim and the method, columns 8-10 are the protections already in place, columns 11-12 are the final judgment of overall severity, and column 13 is the follow-up investigation still to be done.

5.1

Introduction

DR and DQ: purposes, scope, and the critical distinction

5.1 Introduction (p.35)

This chapter provides guidance for performing and documenting Design Reviews (DRs) and Design Qualification (DQ). This Guide emphasizes the importance of DRs leading to DQ as a continual process throughout all phases in the design of new or modified systems.

DRs provide assurance that the design deliverables are consistent with the URS and the mitigation control strategies developed during the System Risk Assessment. When properly performed, DRs reduce the need for costly design-related change orders.

DQ is performed only for direct impact systems (as determined through system classification described in Chapter 3). DQ involves more formal documentation, with Quality Unit approval, to show traceability of the CAs and relevant CDEs in the design to the CPPs and CQAs.

DR and DQ are not intended to be separate activities, but rather separate documentation in which DQ is focused on CAs/CDEs and involves the Quality Unit as an approver. There should be minimal duplication of work; the final report from DR is a key input into the DQ process.

Commentary

🔑 Core Concept: The Fundamental Difference Between DR and DQ

Design Review (DR): covers all design aspects, including engineering, operations, safety and quality, and applies to all systems.

Design Qualification (DQ): applies only to "direct impact systems" and focuses on CAs (critical aspects) and CDEs (critical design elements). It requires Quality Unit approval and is a formal qualification document.

💡 Memory aid: DR looks at the whole picture; DQ looks at the quality-critical points, and only for direct impact systems.

⚠ Key point: "Not separate activities, but separate documents"

What this sentence in the source means in practice: DR and DQ can overlap in time, and engineers do not need to "finish DR before starting DQ".

In practice: DR meeting minutes → CAs/CDEs are identified → the DQ report refers directly to the DR results, avoiding duplicate records. DQ is not a fresh start; it is a focusing and upgrading of the DR (with QA approval added).

📚 Analogy: Building Review vs. Fire Safety Certification

DR is like a full building review: structure, utilities, fire protection and aesthetics are all examined together. DQ is like a fire safety certification: only the fire-related design (escape exits, sprinkler system) is formally approved, and the authority (the Quality Unit) stamps it. Both use the same set of building drawings, so there is no need to draw them twice.

5.2

Design Process

Three phases: Concept → BOD → Detailed Design; GEP and risk-based approach

5.2 Design Process (p.35–36)

The design process can be divided into several phases to ensure the design deliverables are sufficiently developed, the risks are identified and mitigated, and the project (as defined) meets financial objectives. The phases of the design process are:

  1. Concept
  2. Basis of Design (BOD)
  3. Detailed Design

The concept and BOD phases define the Scope of Work (SOW) for the project.

In the concept phase, several approaches (scope options) to meeting the URS may be evaluated. These alternatives should be evaluated for their impact on the business objective, project cost, and risk to quality. The project stakeholders should select the preferred scope option; the project then progresses to the BOD phase.

In the BOD phase, additional engineering work provides a more developed SOW. The BOD design package contains Issued for Design (IFD) documents; it is the basis for the detailed design phase which delivers Issued for Construction (IFC) documents.

5.2.1 Importance of Good Engineering Practice

During the design process, it is important to follow GEP to support the integrity of related data and documentation. DR activities include evaluating deliverables against engineering standards and business, operations, and quality requirements as well as identifying gaps and proposing required corrective actions. Documenting these activities using Good Documentation Practice concepts (as described in Chapter 13) supports the overall effort to ensure the system is suitable for the intended purpose.

5.2.2 Science and Risk-Based Approach to Design Reviews

A quality risk assessment of the manufacturing process (including the environmental conditions supporting the process and/or critical systems) is a significant activity outside of the C&Q scope that can influence the early phase of design. Design stage System Risk Assessment typically uses knowledge from process risk assessments and other process knowledge to evaluate system design with respect to process control failure modes. These assessments are an integral part of the science and risk-based C&Q approach.

Design stage quality risk reviews are an iterative process that is linked to the design development of the process system. The review of the System Risk Assessments and how the results affect the proposed design should be included in the scope of DRs.

Commentary

🕐 The Three-Stage Design Process

1. Concept stage: evaluate several options and select the one that best balances the URS, cost and quality risk.

2. Basis of design (BOD) stage: produces IFD (Issued for Design) drawings and establishes the scope of work (SOW).

3. Detailed design stage: produces IFC (Issued for Construction) drawings, which can be used directly for construction.

DR should be performed at all three stages; DQ is normally performed formally after detailed design is complete.

🔧 Terminology: IFD vs. IFC

  • IFD (Issued for Design): still at the design stage, used internally by engineers and for review, and may still be revised.
  • IFC (Issued for Construction): design review completed and the version frozen, ready to be released to the contractor for construction.

IFD is the main subject of DR; IFC is the final subject of DQ.

📚 The Role of GEP in Design Review

GEP (Good Engineering Practice) is not a regulatory document but a set of best-practice standards recognized across the engineering profession. One function of DR is to confirm that the design complies with GEP: compare against industry standards (such as ASME, ASTM and ISO standards), identify gaps and propose corrective actions.

GEP plus good documentation practice (GDP) = the foundation for ensuring that a system is "fit for its intended use".

🧠 5.2.2 The Importance of Iterative Risk Assessment

Risk assessment during design is not a "one and done" exercise. As the design evolves from concept to detailed design, new failure modes may emerge. DR should regularly incorporate the latest system risk assessment (SRA) results to keep design decisions consistent with the risk control strategy.

💡 Practical tip: list the SRA version number in the scope of each DR to ensure traceability.

Design Review

Objectives, SME roles, process, and outcome including Figure 5.1

5.3.1 Objectives of Design Review (p.36)

DRs are defined in ASTM E2500-13 [5] as follows:

"Planned and systematic reviews of specifications, design, and design development and continuous improvement changes performed as appropriate throughout the life-cycle of the manufacturing system. Design reviews evaluate deliverables against standards and requirements, identify problems, and propose required corrective actions."

DRs include both quality critical and non-critical aspects of the system design.

The documented results of the DR should be the basis for any design modifications/additions performed to correct any deficiencies in the design under review.

5.3.2 Role of Subject Matter Experts and Quality Unit

Throughout the design process, a cross-functional project team (refer to Section 6.4 for example approval matrix) reviews the design of the system to ensure that the product and process user requirements, GMP, and regulatory requirements are included. Thus, it is important that this team includes technical SMEs that have the appropriate experience and correct knowledge of the process, process equipment, control strategies, and facilities to ensure that the design under review will meet the requirements of the URS, function efficiently, and consistently produce quality product.

If the system being reviewed is direct impact, then the team should also include a Quality Unit representative to ensure that all quality requirements are met. This can help to facilitate the review and approval of the DQ.

5.3.3 Design Review Process (p.36–37)

Prior to the DR, design documents should be circulated to the DR project team for their review to ensure a comprehensive review process. It is the responsibility of the project manager and the design leads to ensure that the appropriate participants are part of the DR. Senior management from the Quality Unit, operations, and production are responsible for ensuring the proper representatives with the appropriate experience are present during the DR.

The project team should define the method for conducting DR according to the system type, size, and risk. This method should be documented and approved in applicable plans (e.g., Project Execution Plan, C&Q Plan).

The effort, formality, and documentation of DRs should follow the ICH Q9 [3] principles of being commensurate with the level of risk. DR documentation may take the form of engineering meeting minutes or notes. For highly critical novel systems, DRs may be more focused and detailed.

For off-the-shelf equipment, DR should consider the complexity and/or risk to system or facility operation and to quality. Vendor documents are typically used to conduct these reviews and, if necessary, a technical vendor representative could attend the DR. The focus should be on the CPPs controlled by the equipment and the CAs/CDEs required for the product.

DRs focus on both engineering design and quality aspects of the design.

The engineering design focus is the technical aspects of the system; the objective is to verify the operability, maintainability, and safety aspects of the system. Technical SMEs, system owners, and any other stakeholders need to be present. It is optional for the Quality Unit to attend these reviews that focus on the technical aspects; however, their presence is beneficial and can simplify the DQ step.

The quality focus of the DRs is on compliance elements and the CAs/CDEs of the system design that affect product quality; the objective is to verify that all regulatory requirements are addressed by the design. It is strongly recommended the Quality Unit attends these reviews with quality focus, and that these reviews are documented.

5.3.4 Design Review Outcome (p.37)

The DR project team should have appropriate methods for recording and distributing the results of the DRs and managing any consequent changes. The results should confirm the continuing suitability of the design and confirm that the design is suitable for the intended purpose.

The team is responsible for communicating to their management any issues affecting design.

Commentary

📚 The ASTM E2500-13 Definition Explained

ASTM E2500-13 is the ASTM International standard for the specification, design and verification of pharmaceutical manufacturing systems. Its definition of DR stresses three key words:

  • Planned: not ad hoc; scheduled in advance with a clear scope.
  • Systematic: a structured review method, not a casual skim of documents.
  • Throughout the life-cycle: includes new construction, modification and continual improvement.

Key point: DR covers both quality-critical and non-critical design aspects, in contrast to DQ (which looks only at CAs/CDEs).

👥 Division of Roles Between SMEs and the Quality Unit

SMEs (subject matter experts): drawn from engineering, process, equipment, facilities and other areas, ensuring that the design is technically feasible and meets operational needs.

Quality Unit:

  • Engineering-oriented DR → attendance is optional (but beneficial)
  • Quality/compliance-oriented DR → attendance is strongly recommended and should be documented
  • DR of a direct impact system → a QA representative must participate

💡 Early QA involvement in DR can greatly simplify the later DQ approval process.

⚡ Applying ICH Q9 Principles in DR

The effort, formality and level of documentation of a DR should be commensurate with the risk level of the system:

  • Low-risk support system → meeting minutes are sufficient (engineering meeting minutes)
  • Highly critical new system → a more focused and detailed DR document is needed
  • Off-the-shelf equipment → review based on supplier documentation

This reflects the core ISPE Vol. 5 philosophy of being "science- and risk-based".

It is not a regulatory requirement, but it is a powerful project management tool that ensures every quality requirement has a corresponding design control and nothing is missed.

5.4

Design Qualification

Objectives, prerequisites, DQ process, and final DQ report

5.4.1 Objectives of Design Qualification (p.38)

DQ provides documented verification that the design of a new or modified direct impact system will result in a system that is suitable for the intended purpose. The objectives of DQ are to:

  • Verify the design will meet the quality user requirements as listed in the URS
  • Verify the design will adequately control risks to product quality and patient safety, as identified during the System Risk Assessment
  • Verify the CAs/CDEs necessary to implement these requirements and risk controls

5.4.2 Prerequisites

The prerequisites for DQ include:

  • Approved URS
  • Functional requirements (for automated systems)
  • System Risk Assessments with identification of design (CAs/CDEs) and procedural controls that will be used to mitigate the unacceptable risks
  • Completed DRs, with verification that all open design issues that affect CAs/CDEs, CPPs, and CQAs are closed and included in the final design

It is the responsibility of the project manager to ensure that all prerequisites have been completed and are made available to the participants prior to the DQ for their review. The DQ should be scheduled to allow adequate time for preliminary review of the material to be covered.

5.4.3 Design Qualification Process (p.38–39)

DQ is the documented process for the review and approval of the final system design to verify the following:

  • All process and product requirements in the system URS have been incorporated into the design
  • Quality and regulatory requirements in the system URS have been incorporated into the design
  • Any changes to the design approach for the CAs and any associated CDEs have been reviewed, as appropriate

The DR documentation should be reviewed to confirm the Quality Unit was included in the DR, as defined in the project plan, and has provided the appropriate input to the design.

The approach for performing DQ should be defined in planning documents. For small projects, there may be one DQ performed after all the design is completed. For larger projects, the design may be broken into several areas or functions, with the DQ performed when each design is completed.

A formal protocol document to check requirements against the design is not a regulatory requirement. However, the following information should be defined: plan for conducting the DQ, scope of the DQ, and Quality Unit approval of the DQ. This information could be documented for individual systems or for specified integrated systems.

A Traceability Matrix can be used to document traceability of the CAs and any associated CDEs, and CPPs to the CQAs. The CA and CDE acceptance criteria can also be documented in the Traceability Matrix.

5.4.4 Final Design Qualification Report (p.39)

The final DQ report should include or refer to the following, as applicable:

  • Any needed risk reviews to document the incorporation of design control elements to support quality product manufacturing
  • Identification of each CDE (critical aspect of the physical and functional design) that serves to control identified potential process failures
  • Description of each CA and any supporting CDEs in terms that are verifiable through inspections or testing (acceptance criteria)
  • The CDEs that serve to meet a product or process user requirement in terms that are verifiable (mainly through performance testing)

A final DQ report should be issued stating the design is suitable for the intended purpose; the report should be approved by representatives (from the appropriate level) in Engineering, Manufacturing and the Quality Unit.

Commentary

🎯 The Three DQ Objectives in Depth

The three objectives of DQ form a complete chain of qualification logic:

  • Objective 1 (compliance with the URS): links the URS to the design, ensuring that every quality requirement has been "designed in".
  • Objective 2 (risk control): links the risks identified in the SRA to the control measures in the design (CAs/CDEs), ensuring nothing is omitted.
  • Objective 3 (confirmation of CAs/CDEs): explicitly lists which are the critical aspects/critical design elements, laying the foundation for the subsequent IQ/OQ/PQ test plans.

💡 DQ is the "bridge between design and qualification": it ensures that quality requirements have been fully designed in before construction/procurement begins.

📋 DQ Prerequisites Checklist

Before DQ starts, the project manager must confirm that the following documents are complete and available:

  • ✅ Approved URS
  • ✅ Functional requirements (for automated systems)
  • ✅ System risk assessment (SRA) with CAs/CDEs identified
  • ✅ Completed DRs, with all open issues affecting CAs/CDEs/CPPs/CQAs closed

💡 Practical tip: create a DQ entry checklist to confirm all prerequisites in advance, so that DQ is not forced to stop midway.

📄 Using the Traceability Matrix in DQ

The traceability matrix is a core tool in DQ. Typical columns include:

  • URS requirement number & description
  • Corresponding CA (critical aspect)
  • Corresponding CDE (critical design element)
  • CPP (critical process parameter)
  • CQA (critical quality attribute)
  • Acceptance criteria
  • Subsequent test references (IQ/OQ/PQ protocol numbers)

To stress again: not a regulatory requirement, but strongly recommended. It lets an auditor see the complete traceability chain of requirement → design → qualification at a glance.

🖊 Three-Party Approval of the Final DQ Report

The DQ report must be approved by representatives of three departments at the appropriate level:

  • Engineering: confirms the design is technically feasible and complete
  • Manufacturing: confirms the design meets operational needs
  • Quality Unit: confirms the design meets quality and regulatory requirements

Three-party approval makes the DQ report a formal "quality system document", unlike a DR report (which may be just engineering meeting minutes).

🏢 CDMO Application: Where DQ Sits in a Capital Project

In a CDMO capital project, DQ usually falls at the following points:

  • After IFC drawings are issued: the design is frozen and the final DQ review can be carried out.
  • Before procurement/long-lead equipment orders: confirm that the CAs/CDEs in the design are locked, avoiding costly change orders later.
  • Before FAT: DQ approval means the design aspects have been fully qualified, so FAT can focus on confirming manufacturing quality.

The DQ report is also one of the core components of the "design documentation package" during FDA/EMA inspections.

  • Construction schedule is forced to slip
  • May trigger re-procurement of equipment already ordered

Introduction

Why planning is essential — C&Q as a managed engineering activity from URS to acceptance

Original Text (English)

6.1 Introduction

C&Q is a planned, documented, and managed engineering activity that starts with the establishment of the URS and continues to start-up and acceptance and release of systems. A well-planned program is essential and leads to an efficient, "right the first time", and cost-effective C&Q effort; the result is the delivery of systems that operate in a safe manner, meet established design requirements and stakeholder expectations, and are suitable for the intended purpose.

Planning provides the ties between process understanding and system design; it focuses qualification efforts to enhance the likelihood that the system will deliver quality products while maintaining a controlled operating state. It also acts as a means for communication between members of the multidisciplinary project team.

This chapter focuses on the formal C&Q Plan. There are other plans that can be generated, such as overall project plans or cleanliness plans, which have great value but are not covered in this chapter. Refer to Chapter 21 (Appendix 7) for a listing of supporting plans and general concepts for each.

Regulatory expectations for C&Q planning are described in Chapter 15 (Appendix 1).

Commentary

The Nature of C&Q: A Planned Engineering Activity

ISPE stresses that C&Q (Commissioning and Qualification) is not a process that ends once the testing is done. It is a planned, documented, and managed engineering activity that runs from the day the URS is written until the system is formally accepted and released.

This perspective matters: it positions C&Q as part of the engineering project, rather than paperwork the quality unit scrambles to complete at the end.

Analogy: A Construction Plan for Building a House

C&Q Planning is like the construction plan a building contractor must prepare before breaking ground.

You cannot work it out as you go: "We pour concrete today and decide tomorrow whether to inspect it." Every work step must be scheduled, with clear ownership, a completion date, and acceptance criteria.

The C&Q Plan is that construction plan. It makes sure the whole team (engineering, QA, production, vendors) works from the same script.

Note: This Chapter Focuses on the Formal C&Q Plan

The book reminds readers that many other plans exist (for example, cleaning plans and overall project plans), but this chapter covers only the formal C&Q Plan. For other plans, see Chapter 21 (Appendix 7).

6.1.1

Purpose and Benefits

Integrated schedule, critical path method, cross-team communication, and stakeholder expectations

Original Text (English)

6.1.1 Purpose and Benefits

A well-conceived and executed C&Q Plan can greatly facilitate a timely and cost-effective qualification effort. It is essential to have excellent communication, planning, and coordination between the Quality Unit, vendors, operations, automation, engineering, construction, commissioning, and qualification personnel, to name a few, to deliver a project on time, within budget, and meeting all expectations. A C&Q plan facilitates this communication.

An important secondary goal of the C&Q Plan is to foster an interdisciplinary team approach to C&Q. Well-planned project logistics are crucial to the success of every project. A well-coordinated effort leads to a high quality, effective, and efficient C&Q project.

A single, integrated, overall schedule should be developed with input from the entire project team. This schedule should cover all systems (utilizing defined system boundaries as discussed in Section 3.2) and phases of the projects lifecycle from construction to Process Validation/PPQ completion. This schedule should be updated and issued regularly. These updates should not drive a C&Q Plan revision, but the C&Q Plan should identify how the schedule updates will be captured and communicated.

An integrated schedule, using a critical path method, can increase the awareness of dependent activities and provide a clear understanding of the critical path. Including all project team members in the schedule development will engage the team members and foster better planning. All team members should comprehend the schedule format. Ordering long delivery time items early can help timelines, but consideration should be made to ensure the process requirements for those items are understood and included in the requisition. Understanding when to engage support for C&Q helps to support the success of the project.

A well-planned effort can ensure that the user, business, regulatory, and patient expectations are met. All efforts to ensure the system is suitable for the intended purpose (e.g., start-up, balancing, trial runs, establishing maintenance programs) should be included in the planning phase since this is where the formality of the documentation can be established. These expectations include:

  • Systems are delivered on time and within budget
  • Systems meet approved user requirements and are suitable for the intended purpose
  • Stakeholder needs, including speed to market, are met
  • Products produced from the systems meet defined quality standards
Commentary

The Three Main Benefits of a C&Q Plan

  • A communication bridge across functions: gives QA, vendors, production, automation, engineering, and construction a common plan document to follow
  • Integration of cross-disciplinary teams: breaks down departmental walls and builds one integrated C&Q project team
  • Management of stakeholder expectations: on-time delivery, within budget, fit for intended use, and assured product quality

Key Term: Critical Path Method (CPM)

CPM is a scheduling technique widely used in project management. It identifies the longest chain of dependent activities in a project; a delay in any activity on this chain directly delays the whole project.

What it means in C&Q: for example, HVAC commissioning must be completed before IQ of the aseptic filling area; IQ must be completed before OQ; and OQ before PQ. This chain is the critical path. Identifying it early lets you order long-lead equipment and arrange resources in time.

Analogy: The Consequence of an Uncontrolled Schedule

Imagine a filling line installation project where the owner wants PQ to start in October, but nobody mapped out the critical path in advance. In August it turns out that the approved-vendor evaluation (EP) for the water system has not been done and the equipment has not arrived, so OQ cannot start.

The purpose of the Critical Path Method is to make sure that this kind of "discovered at the last minute" problem is found and dealt with at the planning stage.

Key Point: A Schedule Update Is Not a Plan Revision

ISPE specifically points out that regular schedule updates are normal, but a schedule update should not trigger a revision of the C&Q Plan. The C&Q Plan should describe how schedule updates are recorded and communicated, rather than re-approving the plan every time the schedule changes. This is very practical advice.

6.2

C&Q Planning Approach

Level of planning based on project size, complexity, and risk — when a separate C&Q Plan is needed

Original Text (English)

6.2 C&Q Planning Approach

The amount of planning, documentation, reporting, and level of detail of the C&Q Plan should be based on expectations of the organization/site, size and complexity of the system or project, and the potential impact on product quality and patient safety.

A C&Q Plan should not repeat details that are in other documents but needs to capture the organization of those documents in order to facilitate an efficient project execution.

A separate C&Q Plan is not always necessary. Aspects of C&Q planning can be included with other documents, such as a validation plan or change control. For example, the simple change of adding a small system would not need a separate C&Q Plan; the requirements could be incorporated into a change control plan. More complex systems would benefit from a separate C&Q Plan, for example, the installation of a filling line with terminal sterilization and secondary packaging or the doubling in capacity of a manufacturing site including additional utilities to support the expansion.

Commentary

Plan Depth Should Be Proportionate to Project Size

ISPE takes a risk-proportionate approach: the level of detail in the plan depends on:

  • The expectations of the organization/site (company policy)
  • The size and complexity of the system or project
  • The potential impact on product quality and patient safety

When Is a Standalone C&Q Plan Needed?

No standalone plan needed (change control is sufficient):
Adding a small piece of auxiliary equipment (for example, replacing a peristaltic pump)

Standalone C&Q Plan needed:

  • A new filling line installation including terminal sterilization and secondary packaging
  • A site capacity doubling that includes utility expansion (WFI, pure steam, clean HVAC)

Rule of thumb: the more complex the project and the greater its impact, the more it needs a standalone formal plan document to coordinate it.

The C&Q Plan Should Not Duplicate Other Documents

The plan's function is to "organize the document structure", not to "copy document content". It tells the reader which document is where, who is responsible, and when it is due, rather than re-transcribing the contents of the URS or SRA.

6.2.1

Timing of C&Q Plan

The approval sequence rule: C&Q Plan approved BEFORE test instructions, test instructions approved BEFORE execution

Original Text (English)

6.2.1 Timing of C&Q Plan

In an overall scheme, the creation of the C&Q Plan falls in the process map as shown in Figure 1.1 (Chapter 1).

It is important to note that C&Q planning begins prior to the creation of any formal written plans. C&Q planning starts during the planning and design phase of a project and continues through project implementation. A documented strategy may be developed as a precursor to a C&Q Plan, to help align team members with the process and personnel who will be responsible for C&Q.

A C&Q Plan should begin early in a project in the concept or early basic engineering phase.

The C&Q Plan needs to be approved before the respective testing documents (i.e., test instructions) are approved, and such test instructions need to be approved before test execution starts to support qualification. This is recommended for commissioning plans and test instructions.

The C&Q Plan should address how existing test documentation and test results from commissioning (including FAT, SAT, etc.) are referenced in qualification documents, as appropriate.

Commentary

Approval Sequence Rule (One of the Rules Most Often Violated in Industry)

ISPE clearly specifies three approval steps that must happen in order:

  1. C&Q Plan approval completed (plan approved)
  2. Test Instructions approval completed (IQ/OQ/PQ protocols approved)
  3. Test execution begins

This sequence cannot be reversed, and certainly not skipped.

Why Does Industry Often Violate This Rule?

In real CDMO or equipment installation projects, common situations include:

  • The equipment has arrived and engineers are eager to "just run the IQ first" while the protocol is still in QA approval
  • The C&Q Plan has not been finally approved, but commissioning engineers have already started FAT
  • To meet the launch schedule, the protocol is approved only after OQ has been executed

In a regulatory inspection, these behaviors can all be judged procedural non-compliance (protocol not approved prior to execution). Once cited in an FDA 483 or Warning Letter, the cost of remediation is far greater than the few days of waiting for approval would have been.

Planning Should Begin in the Concept Phase or Early Basic Engineering

The C&Q plan is not a document to be written only a few weeks before the equipment arrives. ISPE recommends starting to plan in the Concept Phase or Early Basic Engineering.

This is because the quality requirements in the URS, the system classification results, and the CDE identification from the SRA must all be settled at the planning stage before the scope and acceptance criteria of testing can be defined.

6.2.2

Lifecycle of C&Q Plan

Kept up-to-date through the project; minor departures documented in Qualification Summary Report

Original Text (English)

6.2.2 Lifecycle of C&Q Plan

The C&Q Plan is not finalized until after the risk management process has reached a point to capture the impact of product quality decisions or of establishing the CDEs.

C&Q Plans are expected to be kept up-to-date as changes to the project are made. Revisions of the C&Q Plan may be necessary as new information is learned or as modifications to the design are made, e.g., changes in scope (additions or removals), timing, or identification of additional CAs/CDEs.

Approval is recommended for these early plans and for subsequent changes made by the revision process; this ensures agreement at various stages of the C&Q approach. Changes to the C&Q Plan should be reviewed and approved by the original approvers (or their designate) and a revision history should be maintained of significant changes to the Plan.

It should be noted that revisions may not always be necessary. Minor departures from the C&Q Plan may be documented in the Qualification Summary Report with appropriate justification and rationale, as deemed appropriate by the project team.

At the end of a project, a written document capturing the completion and closure of the project should be created. See Chapter 8 for more details regarding acceptance and release.

Changes to the C&Q Plan should be handled under change management (as discussed in Chapter 12) to ensure that as changes are implemented, the impact to C&Q efforts is addressed.

Commentary

The C&Q Plan Is a Living Document

The C&Q Plan is not a document that is written and then locked away. As the project progresses, the plan must be updated accordingly when design changes occur, the scope is adjusted, or new CDEs are identified.

Principles for revising the version:

  • Significant change → initiate a formal version revision, approved by the original approvers (or their delegates)
  • Minor deviation → may be recorded in the Qualification Summary Report with a justification, without necessarily revising the plan

Key Term: Qualification Summary Report

The Qualification Summary Report is the final report that, once all qualification activities are complete, consolidates all test results, deviation handling, and an execution summary.

ISPE allows minor deviations from the C&Q Plan to be recorded in this report (with an explanation), so the C&Q Plan need not be revised each time. This is a pragmatic and flexible provision.

Plan Changes Must Go Through Change Management

Any revision of the C&Q Plan should be handled through Change Management (Chapter 12), to ensure the impact of the change on C&Q work is properly assessed and documented.

This prevents the situation in which the plan is quietly changed without notifying QA.

6.2.3

Elements to be Addressed

Scope of testing, document source identification, coordination with overall project execution plan

Original Text (English)

6.2.3 Elements to be Addressed

This section is intended to be a discussion of the elements that need to be addressed as part of C&Q planning, not the documents where they are captured.

6.2.3.1 Scope of Testing

System classification (as described in Chapter 3) identifies which systems require qualification. The System Risk Assessment (as described in Chapter 4) identifies which elements in those systems require qualification. The URS (as described in Chapter 2) and System Risk Assessment establish the scope of testing for qualification. The equipment specifications establish the scope of testing for commissioning.

The testing approach is based on the most efficient method to test the system and should be captured in the C&Q Plan. If systems are tested separately, the C&Q Plan should include a final test verifying that all systems are able to work together prior to acceptance and release. If PPQ is included in a separate plan, the linkage to the execution of that critical phase should be captured.

6.2.3.2 Identification of Document Source

Engineering firms, vendors, contractors, quality, engineering, and owners provide the documentation necessary for design, construction, commissioning, qualification, operation, and technical support of the systems. It is important to identify the documentation source early in the process so the impact on the testing of the system is understood and thus the quality of the documentation understood. This understanding will allow for utilization of the documentation to support qualification.

For example, the decision should be made during planning if any FAT, construction, SAT, or commissioning testing results and documentation will be used to satisfy the requirements to declare the system qualified or suitable for the intended purpose. This decision is based on vendor assessments for C&Q documentation purposes (as described in Chapter 10) and the criticality of the testing. It is often desirable to minimize the amount of retesting performed.

The C&Q Plan may also address the expectations of the documentation. It should be noted that pristine documentation, i.e., documentation that has minimal errors or corrections, is not essential but the documentation needs to be easy to follow and understand.

6.2.3.3 Coordination with Overall Project Execution Plan

Another important aspect is working with the project manager to ensure that the planning of C&Q activities, whether a separate plan or part of another plan, fits seamlessly into the overall Project Execution Plan. C&Q activities should be properly reflected in the Project Execution Plan at an appropriate level of detail to be able to identify interdependencies between C&Q as well as to other project activities. Commissioning is normally occurring throughout construction and needs to occur when it is appropriate; e.g., commissioning the agitation system cannot be completed until it is energized.

Having these activities in the Project Execution Plan supports:

  • Coordination of test activities among C&Q to prepare for FAT or SAT events
  • Ability to schedule sequence of testing for multiple systems (e.g., electrical supply before energizing a system)
  • Schedule review cycles for qualification documents with Quality Unit representatives
Commentary

Logic for Determining the Scope of Testing

The Scope of Testing is determined by the following documents:

  • System Classification → determines which systems need qualification (direct impact vs not direct impact)
  • System Risk Assessment (SRA) → determines which elements of the system need qualification (identification of CDEs)
  • URS + SRA → together establish the scope of qualification testing
  • Equipment specifications → determine the scope of commissioning testing

Document Source Decision

At the planning stage you must decide: can FAT, SAT, or commissioning documents be used to support qualification conclusions?

The basis for this decision is:

  • The result of the Vendor Assessment → is the quality of the vendor's documentation sufficient?
  • The criticality of the test → does it involve a CDE?

The aim is to minimize retesting. If the FAT is already well documented, there is no need to repeat the same test on site.

Documentation Does Not Need to Be Perfect

ISPE states plainly: "Pristine documentation is NOT essential." Documents may contain recorded corrections; what matters is that they are easy to read and understand. This is a pragmatic position that avoids wasting resources in pursuit of "correction-free documents" or, worse, eliminating corrections rather than recording them formally.

Coordination with the Overall Project Execution Plan (PEP)

C&Q activities must be integrated into the overall PEP (Project Execution Plan), so that you can:

  • Identify the interdependencies between C&Q activities and construction, procurement, and operations
  • Sequence the testing of multiple systems sensibly (power must be available before startup)
  • Schedule time windows for QA document review (QA review cycle)

In CDMO practice, this means the C&Q planning engineer needs to attend PEP scheduling meetings, rather than discussing matters only within the QA department.

CDMO Application: Example C&Q Plan Structure for a New Filling Line

Taking a CDMO undertaking the installation of a new aseptic filling line as an example, the C&Q Plan might include:

  • Scope: filling machine, freeze dryer, depyrogenation oven, CIP/SIP system, isolator (direct impact); HVAC, purified water system, compressed air (direct impact); chilled water (not direct impact)
  • Strategy: freeze dryer uses FAT (at the vendor's site) + SAT + IQ/OQ/PQ; filling machine uses vendor FAT + on-site IQ/OQ/PQ
  • Document matrix: lists the documents for each system, the responsible department, and the expected completion date
  • Approval matrix: following the Table 6.1 format, clearly identifies the pre-approvers and post-approvers for each document

The acceptance criteria for CDE tests in the plan can be deferred to the individual test instructions, rather than being listed one by one in the C&Q Plan. This gives flexibility in practical operation.

6.3.1

Scope of the Effort

Both direct and not-direct impact systems; PQ for utilities; cycle development considerations

Original Text (English)

6.3.1 Scope of the Effort

The scope captures all systems that are covered within the scope of the project. This includes both direct impact and not direct impact systems (as described in Chapter 3). More details are provided below.

6.3.1.1 Additional Scope Considerations

PQ activities may be included in a C&Q Plan for pharmaceutical grade utilities (e.g., WFI, Purified Water), support systems (e.g., autoclaves, depyrogenation ovens, washers), environments (e.g., non-environmental monitoring in clean rooms), and non-product PQ (e.g., media fills for filling lines). Individual organizations/sites need to decide if these are in qualification or a separate validation program.

Cycle development for areas such as sterilization and cleaning also need to be considered. Cycle development can identify factors that require qualification or that modify the qualification. These are often executed after the equipment qualification effort. It is recommended to not perform the final qualification testing until after cycle development since changes identified by cycle development can be captured in the Engineering Change Management system (see Chapter 12). Individual organizations/sites need to decide if cycle development is part of the C&Q effort and capture the decision in the C&Q Plan.

Commentary

Scope Includes Both Direct Impact and Not Direct Impact Systems

The scope of the C&Q Plan cannot list only Direct Impact systems. Not Direct Impact systems (such as chilled water and general compressed air) do not require formal qualification, but they still fall within the management scope of the C&Q plan and need commissioning.

PQ Activities May Be Included in the C&Q Plan

The PQ (Performance Qualification) of the following items may (but need not) be managed within the C&Q Plan:

  • Pharmaceutical-grade utilities: WFI (water for injection), purified water (PW)
  • Support systems: autoclaves, depyrogenation ovens, washers
  • Environment: environmental qualification of the aseptic room (not environmental monitoring)
  • Non-product PQ: aseptic process simulation (media fill)

Each site must decide for itself whether to include these in the C&Q plan or to set up a separate Validation Program.

Cycle Development Should Be Completed Before Final Qualification Testing

ISPE specifically recommends: do not carry out final qualification testing before Cycle Development is complete. Cycle development (such as sterilization cycles and cleaning cycles) may uncover problems that require design changes. If qualification is done first and the design is changed afterwards, the qualification must be repeated, which is very costly.

Correct sequence: Cycle Development → confirm no design changes → final IQ/OQ/PQ

6.3.2

Strategy and Approach

Commissioned-only vs commissioned+qualified; vendor documentation criteria; testing dependencies

Original Text (English)

6.3.2 Strategy and Approach

The C&Q Plan needs to define C&Q strategy and approach to aid in overall project planning. The C&Q Plan captures whether systems will be commissioned only or commissioned and qualified. The system classification (as described in Chapter 3) and System Risk Assessment (as described in Chapter 4) have identified the CPPs and CDEs that need to be supported by the C&Q effort.

The rigor of testing depends on the user requirements, CDEs, and system characteristics (e.g., packaged system, modular, or installed system) as well as experiences with the vendor.

An aspect that needs to be captured in the C&Q Plan is the identification of the documentation source that will be utilized to satisfy the C&Q requirements. Appropriate prerequisites, means, and checks should be described in the plan to ensure reliable results, e.g., the pre-approval of vendor/manufacturer documents by an SME. If vendor-provided documentation or testing is to be accepted to support qualification, the criteria for determining if it can be utilized needs to be established in the Plan. Refer to Chapter 10 for details regarding vendor assessments for C&Q documentation purposes.

The C&Q Plan should also capture if individual pieces of equipment or systems will be tested separately because they are delivered and installed at different times by different vendors or if testing will be executed once all components are installed. Dependencies within the scope of work (e.g., if one system needs to be tested before another system is tested) should also be included.

Commentary

Strategy Decision: Commissioning Only, or Commissioning Plus Qualification?

The C&Q Plan must clearly define the strategy for each system:

  • Commissioned Only: applies to not direct impact systems (such as chilled water and general power)
  • Commissioned + Qualified: applies to direct impact systems (such as the aseptic filling line and the WFI system)

Basis for the decision: system classification (Chapter 3) + the CDE identification results of the SRA.

Rigor of Qualification Depends on Several Factors

The rigor of testing is influenced by the following factors:

  • User requirements (the requirements in the URS)
  • The number and criticality of CDEs
  • System characteristics (packaged system vs modular vs field-installed)
  • Prior experience working with the vendor (with an approved vendor, more of the documentation can be leveraged)

Standards for Using Vendor Documents: Define Them in the Plan in Advance

If the plan uses vendor documents (such as FAT reports or vendor IQ) to support qualification conclusions, the standards for their use must be defined in the plan beforehand, for example:

  • Vendor documents must be pre-approved by an SME
  • What standard the Vendor Assessment report must meet before the vendor's documents can be used
  • What level of vendor credentials is accepted (audit results, ISO certification, etc.)

These standards must be written into the plan, not remedied afterwards.

Test Dependencies Must Be Clearly Documented

For example, the OQ of the HVAC system must be completed, with the pressure differentials and air change rates of the aseptic room confirmed, before IQ of the filling line can begin. Such dependencies must be clearly recorded in the C&Q Plan to support correct test sequencing.

6.3.4

C&Q Plan Contents

System overview, installation phase, operational phase, discrepancy handling

Original Text (English)

6.3.4 C&Q Plan Contents

The C&Q Plan should define the systems that will be commissioned and qualified based on the system or area boundaries. The plan should include a description of systems including their means of automation. The system overview should be a high-level process narrative, including system boundaries, based on operational/functional requirements. The C&Q team representatives should provide input in defining system boundaries (which can be in a system description, marked on P&IDs, or highlighted on system drawings) in the early project phases to develop an executable plan. It is often possible to gather much of the system overview information from other project documentation.

The C&Q Plan should address the level of testing for each system. This may range from detailed descriptions to references to standard tests. An alternative is to provide only high-level descriptions in the C&Q Plan and leave the detailed descriptions to execution documents. The selected approach should increase the level of efficiency and effectiveness. The plan should also include project schedules by system (as defined by the system boundaries) that capture the dependencies between systems.

Commissioning is an important aspect that requires planning, documentation, and managed resources. Commissioning encompasses testing from FAT, testing during construction, SAT, installation testing, and much of the start-up activities of a project lifecycle including those that will be used to assess the qualification of a direct impact system.

Testing during commissioning should be used to support qualification. If commissioning documentation will be used to support qualification, it is critical to involve the leader of the qualification effort and the Quality Unit in the planning and coordination of commissioning activities and deliverables. A periodic commissioning planning and status meeting should be established.

The C&Q Plan needs to include C&Q steps. There are two typical phases:

  • Installation: Documentation supporting installation is successfully completed. This may include FAT and SAT documentation as well as site commissioning for systems built at the site.
  • Operational: Documentation supporting the functionality and performance of the system after the system has been mechanically completed. This may include testing performed in FAT and SAT.

The plan should address the steps to follow when test results do not meet the expected results or acceptance criteria (discrepancy). There should be a predefined methodology for how discrepancies should be handled (see Section 7.4).

Commentary

The Role of the System Overview

The System Overview is a high-level description of the system that explains its function, the System Boundary, and its means of automation.

It can be shown in:

  • A written description
  • A scope drawn on the P&ID (piping and instrumentation diagram)
  • Boundaries marked on system drawings

Defining the system boundary early is the key to making sure later IQ/OQ/PQ neither lets anything slip through the net nor tests more than the scope requires.

Prerequisites for Using Commissioning Documents for Qualification

If commissioning documents are to be used to support qualification conclusions, ISPE emphasizes:

  • The person responsible for qualification and the Quality Unit must be involved in planning and coordinating commissioning activities from the planning stage
  • Regular commissioning planning and progress meetings are needed

This is because the quality of commissioning documents (format, acceptance criteria, approval procedures) must meet qualification requirements, and this can only be agreed in advance, not fixed afterwards.

Discrepancy Handling

The C&Q Plan must define in advance the steps to follow when test results do not meet expectations or acceptance criteria.

This Discrepancy Handling Procedure must be written before execution (predefined), rather than decided on the spot after a deviation occurs. See Section 7.4 for details.

7.1.1

Approach and Scope

From design to release — static, dynamic, integrated system testing

Original Text (English)

C&Q testing and documentation starts as early as design and ends with the acceptance and release of the system to the end-user. The process includes planning, document review, and static and dynamic testing of the installation, function, and performance, including testing of any defined worst-case scenarios.

The requirements and stage of the testing/inspection should be predefined in the planning phase to avoid duplication of testing. Testing should only be repeated if necessary. For example, retesting may be necessary if there are changes that may impact a test result, e.g., differing utilities or disconnecting equipment and control systems after FAT to facilitate shipping.

For systems where the automation is an integral part of the system (typical for manufacturing equipment, e.g., WFI storage and distribution system with a dedicated control panel), the entire system is tested as an integrated system comprising the mechanical, electrical, and associated automation.

The scope of testing varies depending on the project scope, but for a new process area would include facilities, utilities, manufacturing equipment and their associated automation or process control systems.

Testing activities and rigor will depend on the type of system (e.g., manufacturing, automated, utilities), potential to impact product quality, and on the project (e.g., green field, brown field, relocation, stick-built, modular, off-the-shelf system).

Commentary

📐 Static vs Dynamic Testing

  • Static testing: document review, drawing verification and verification of material compliance, that is, checks made while the equipment is at rest.
  • Dynamic testing: the equipment is actually operated to test functional performance and worst-case conditions, that is, checks made while the equipment is running.

Both are part of C&Q, and neither should be neglected.

⚠️ Key Point: Avoid Repeat Testing

Defining test requirements in advance at the planning stage is the key to saving cost. However, retesting is required in the following cases:

  • The equipment is disassembled and shipped after FAT (control system disconnected)
  • The site utility specifications differ from the original design
  • Any subsequent change that could affect test results

🔗 Integrated System Testing

A WFI distribution system (mechanical piping + electrical + SCADA) must be tested as a whole. Verifying only the mechanical part is not enough, because the control logic is the core of actual operation. ISPE stresses this point because plants often subcontract the mechanical and automation work separately, which easily leads to "each part tested on its own, gaps at the integration".

💡 Green field vs Brown field

  • Green field: a brand-new site, built from scratch
  • Brown field: renovation or expansion of an existing site, where existing equipment and new construction coexist
  • Modular / Off-the-shelf: modular or ready-made equipment, where the depth of testing is relatively lower

The project type directly affects how much rigor is set for testing.

7.1.3

Quality Unit Oversight

Four methods for QA oversight of CDE testing documentation

Original Text (English)

To ensure that the deliverables meet quality expectations, it is good practice to ensure engagement of the Quality Unit as part of an integrated team during the generation of test documentation that will be used to support qualification.

The organization/site needs to determine how Quality Unit oversight will be provided for the testing and associated documentation of the CAs/CDEs. There are several appropriate methods, some of which are described below:

  • Procedures that define GEP and the approach that will be used can be approved by the Quality Unit
    • Standard test procedures (test scripts) and the associated results templates can be pre-approved by the Quality Unit
  • Test strategy and acceptance criteria can be defined in the C&Q Plan for the Quality Unit to approve; the Quality Unit can then review the results as part of the development of the C&Q summary report
  • Testing used to demonstrate that the CAs/CDEs identified in the System Risk Assessment can be covered in an Installation and Operational Qualification (IOQ) protocol for Quality Unit pre and post-execution review and approval
  • Inspection and test records can be marked up to show the tests that are used to demonstrate the CAs/CDEs identified in the System Risk Assessment, or these tests can be assembled in a subsection of the test document for Quality Unit pre and post-execution review and approval
Commentary

📊 Four Ways the Quality Unit Oversees CDE Testing

ISPE explicitly provides four QA involvement approaches of different depth, and a company can choose according to its size and system risk:

  1. Approach 1: Pre-approved procedures (GEP SOP + pre-approved test scripts)
    QA approves in advance the SOP that defines GEP, as well as the standard test procedures (test scripts) and results templates. QA does not need to attend each test and simply reviews the results afterward.
    Suitable for: mature sites with established standardized test procedures
  2. Approach 2: C&Q Plan approval + summary report review
    QA approves the C&Q Plan (including the test strategy and acceptance criteria) and later reviews the results in the C&Q summary report.
    Suitable for: mid-sized projects where QA resources are limited and QA involvement should be spread over several points in time
  3. Approach 3: Separate IOQ protocol (pre- and post-approval)
    For the CDEs identified by the SRA, a separate IOQ Protocol is written, which QA approves before execution and reviews after execution. This is the most traditional validation path.
    Suitable for: direct impact systems and situations with higher regulatory scrutiny risk
  4. Approach 4: Marking up
    On the engineering test records, mark which tests cover which CDE, or group the CDE-related tests into subsections, for QA pre- and post-approval. This is the lightest integrated approach.
    Suitable for: commercial vendor equipment whose test records are already quite complete

⚠️ Key Reminder

The four approaches are not mutually exclusive: different systems in the same project can use different approaches. The key is to state clearly in the C&Q Plan which approach is chosen and why.

The concept of an "integrated team" runs through this chapter: QA is not a "judge" who appears only after testing is complete, but a partner involved from the moment documentation is generated.

7.1.4

Roles and Responsibilities

The four C&Q team roles — coordinator, user, technical expert, quality unit

Original Text (English)

The planning, execution, and closeout of testing should be performed by a multidisciplinary team (which includes vendors, engineering, and users) with appropriate Quality Unit oversight. Refer to Section 6.4 (Example Approval Matrix) for an example of the roles and responsibilities for the review and approval of deliverables.

The following roles are typically included in a C&Q team:

  • Testing Coordinator
  • Production user (system user)
  • Technical experts (engineering, automation, validation, or other SME)
  • Quality Unit representative

The testing coordinator represents the project manager in this scope of work. In small projects it is possible that the testing coordinator and the system owner is the same person, whereas for large projects it may be reasonable to designate the role to another individual. The testing coordinator is responsible for the coordination of all testing and ensuring that all tests are completed to meet the schedule.

Production users represent the system owners who will operate the system. In large construction projects, this group may be the engineering representatives that designed the system, due to their knowledge of the planned operation and requirements. Production users are responsible for ensuring the system operates as expected and that documentation (such as operational procedures and batch records) are developed so that they are approved for acceptance and release.

Technical experts are part of or support the testing team, as appropriate. Where testing activities are performed by vendors or third parties, the responsibilities between both parties need to be defined and documented.

The Quality Unit representative provides oversight to ensure that all CAs/CDEs identified in the System Risk Assessment are tested.

Commentary

💡 Role Flexibility: Small vs Large Projects

ISPE notes specifically that in a small project the Testing Coordinator and the System Owner can be the same person. This reflects the pragmatic spirit of the guide: it emphasizes that responsibilities are fulfilled, not that separate people must be assigned.

In a CDMO setting, the testing coordinator is usually a Validation Engineer or a project engineer, while the Production User is represented by a process engineer from manufacturing.

⚠️ Points to Note When a Third Party Performs Testing

When a vendor or third party performs testing, the scope of responsibility on each side must be defined in writing (for example, who signs, who witnesses, and who owns the CAPA when a problem is found). This is usually written into the contract or into the Roles & Responsibilities section of the Test Plan.

7.2.1

Planning

PDI and FAT supporting on-site inspection; minimizing repeat testing

Original Text (English)

When C&Q testing is executed as a planned, documented, and managed engineering approach to the start-up and turnover of systems, the result is a safe and functional environment that meets established requirements and stakeholder expectations in a cost-effective way. Where such systems are direct impact (as determined through system classification described in Chapter 3), qualification is required.

The optimum test strategy can minimize repeat testing and help to reduce time in project schedules. For example, Pre-Delivery Inspection (PDI) and FAT may be used to support on-site inspection if appropriate approvals are obtained prior to execution. Components such as instruments can be verified (for aspects such as, e.g., manufacturer, model number, materials of construction) at the vendor site during a PDI or FAT. These checks can be used to support qualification activities, and do not need to be repeated provided there is justification that the functionality will not be affected by any subsequent activities prior to acceptance and release.

See Chapter 6 for details regarding C&Q planning.

Commentary

🎯 The Logic of the "Optimal Testing Strategy"

The goal of the planning stage is to design a strategy that minimizes repeat testing. The key tools are PDI and FAT: tests completed at the vendor's facility, with appropriate approvals, can serve as the basis for on-site qualification and need not be repeated on site.

⚠️ Preconditions for "No Repetition"

For PDI/FAT data to replace on-site testing, the following must be met:

  1. Appropriate approvals obtained prior to execution
  2. A documented rationale that subsequent activities (such as disassembly for shipping and reassembly on site) will not affect the functionality already tested

If the equipment is disassembled for shipping, functional testing usually has to be repeated on site.

🏭 Practical Application in CDMO Procurement

The purchase contract should explicitly require the vendor to provide a PDI opportunity and list which items can be verified during PDI. This saves on-site start-up time: a problem found at the vendor's facility costs far less to fix than one found after the equipment arrives on site.

Commentary

🎓 7.2.2 Training Requirements

"Appropriate training and experience" is a basic prerequisite for performing C&Q testing. Training covers:

  • Setup and operation of the specific system (equipment-related)
  • Safety
  • Documentation standards

In regulatory inspections, inspectors often ask to see the training records of the people who performed the testing. This is a basic documentation requirement and must not be overlooked.

💡 7.2.3 DR/DQ as the Baseline for Change Management

DR (Design Review) = design review (internal to engineering)
DQ (Design Qualification) = design qualification (with QA involvement, for direct impact systems)

The approved design documents are the "baseline": from then on, every change must be assessed against these documents and go through the engineering change management (ECM) process.

Fast Track risk: if procurement or construction starts before the design is approved (execution "at risk"), the organization must clearly define who bears the risk, and for direct impact systems QA approval is required.

⚠️ 7.2.4 Conditions for Accepting Vendor Documentation

The vendor may perform and document testing in its own format, provided that:

  1. The vendor's documentation standards are acceptable (assessed beforehand)
  2. The protocol/test plan is reviewed and approved by both the owner and the vendor before testing begins
  3. If necessary, QA also approves (determined by the four approaches in 7.1.3)

Signing after the fact ("retroactive signature") is a common inspection finding and should be avoided at all costs.

🔄 7.2.5 Change Review Before Testing

Before testing is executed, the following must be reviewed:

  • System Risk Assessment (SRA): confirm whether the CDE list has been updated
  • Change management records: confirm that the system as actually installed matches what the test documents describe

This step prevents the common problem of "test documents lagging behind the actual installed state".

7.3

Execution

Testing and inspection activities — scope, sequencing, and phase transitions

Original Text (English)

This section describes the testing and inspection activities. Each organization/project should dictate the testing and inspection required, when it should be performed, and the titles of the record documents.

Tests should be planned and performed so that the results remain valid throughout subsequent phases of the project. Where this is not possible, there should be an evaluation to identify necessary retesting that may be required.

It is not necessary to complete each stage before proceeding to the next (e.g., moving from installation testing to operational testing) as long as:

  • Any outstanding activities and critical discrepancies are noted and tracked with scheduled completion dates.
  • There is documented evidence that the outstanding activities and critical discrepancies will not impact the subsequent work. For direct impact systems, this justification should be approved by the Quality Unit.

See Chapter 8 for information on the review and closure of testing documentation.

Commentary

📋 Flexibility Principle for Phase Transitions

ISPE allows the next phase to start before the previous phase is fully complete (for example, installation testing has a few open items but operational testing can begin), provided that:

  1. All open items are recorded and given completion deadlines
  2. Written confirmation that these open items will not affect subsequent work
  3. Direct impact systems: the confirmation above requires QA approval

This reflects ISPE's risk-based thinking: not a dogmatic "it must be finished before we can proceed", but managing risk "with justification and documentation".

⚠️ "Punch List" Management

Open items are usually recorded in a "Punch List". A common CDMO practice is to split it into "Critical" and "Non-Critical" items: critical items must be resolved before the next phase starts, while non-critical items can be given a later completion date.

7.3.1.1

Turnover Packages (TOPs)

Purpose, typical contents (15 items), and format requirements

Original Text (English)

The purpose of the Turnover Package (TOP) is to provide complete documentation on system components, equipment, and setup. This documentation should also include as-built documentation (agreed upon data, drawings, software, etc.) turned over to the end-user. The TOP may be used throughout the system lifecycle, including ongoing maintenance, system change management, and retirement.

TOPs should be reviewed to ensure that they are complete and represent the installed system. Once completed, the TOP should be accepted and approved. Updates to the TOP should be included in the change management of the system where appropriate. Where documentation from the TOP is used to support operations or represent the basis for qualification, they should be stored in a suitable location that provides adequate control and access.

Contents of the TOP

The TOP contents are usually determined by factors such as organization/site procedures, end-user preferences, GEP, system type, and contracts with the vendors. Information included in a TOP should ensure controlled operation, maintenance, and change. A complex novel system would, therefore, require more types of information than a simple off-the-shelf pump. Due to the variability of systems, vendors, and documentation, the TOP contents need to be flexible.

A typical TOP may include the following lifecycle related documents:

  • Vendor-provided SOPs
  • Spare parts lists
  • Maintenance requirements
  • Maintenance records for work conducted prior to turnover
  • As-built drawings
  • Material of construction certificates
  • Construction test and inspection records (e.g., pressure tests)
  • Loop calibration documentation
  • Material safety data sheets
  • System start-up and shutdown information
  • Warranties
  • Records of adjustable settings
  • Any specific documentation required by local regulations

The TOP contents should be clearly set out and agreed upon by all parties prior to placement of orders. The delivery of the documentation requirements should not present a risk to the project at a later stage, from either content or timing aspects.

TOP Formats

System vendors typically have their own format for TOPs, but the owner organization should clearly define a standardized format that aligns with their document control processes. This can help to ensure consistency between the various TOPs that are submitted. The owner organization can propose a standard format or accept the vendor format if it meets internal requirements. The format might be system-based. The format of TOPs should ensure that they are user friendly with tabulated sections. TOPs should be obtained for all operating systems. To facilitate storage, electronic versions of these documents are often required by the owner organization.

Commentary

📦 What Is a TOP (Turnover Package)?

The TOP is the complete set of documents that accompanies the handover of a system from the contractor/vendor to the owner. It records the full state of the system at the moment of handover and is an important reference for the whole system life cycle (installation, operation, maintenance, change, decommissioning).

Core value of the TOP:

  • Basis for validation: as-built drawings are an important basis for IQ
  • Maintenance support: spare parts lists and maintenance requirements give the PM department something to follow
  • Change management: when equipment is modified, the TOP is the baseline document
  • Regulatory compliance: certificates of conformance (CoC) for materials support regulatory submissions

⚠️ Key Practice: Confirm the TOP Contents Before Placing the Order

The source text states explicitly that "TOP contents should be clearly defined and agreed by all parties before the order is placed". This is a very important procurement management principle:

  • If documents are requested from the vendor only when the equipment is almost ready for delivery, you often face an uncooperative vendor, incomplete documents or delayed delivery
  • Recommendation: list the TOP document list in the RFQ (request for quotation) as a contract requirement
  • Payment terms can be tied to the completeness of the TOP documents

💡 What Is Loop Calibration?

Loop = a complete measurement/control loop, for example: temperature sensor → transmitter → DCS signal input card → displayed value.

Loop calibration verifies the accuracy of the whole loop, not just the sensor itself. This is especially important for CDEs (such as WFI system temperature and autoclave pressure), because an error at any point can cause loss of process control.

7.3.2

Installation Testing

Acceptance Testing, Commissioning, and Qualification phases — mechanical, construction, automation scope

Original Text (English)

The verification of installation provides documentation that the system (or system components) is installed correctly, is ready to start-up, and meets the design requirements including those identified as CDEs.

Testing may be conducted within a single record/plan or separated into FAT, SAT, commissioning, and qualification phases.

The scope of installation testing includes all system installation requirements within the system boundaries, as defined in the specifications or C&Q Plan. Installation testing should include all mechanical, electrical, and automation components, and verify that they are in a state that as-built drawings and punch lists may be established.

Mechanical aspects may include, but are not limited to:

  • Connection to appropriate utilities, services, and IT infrastructure
  • Installation (e.g., pressure testing, slope verification, valve drain verification)
  • System walk down with results marked to record the current system status, redline drawings updated, and identified issues captured as discrepancies

  • Component details
  • Room finishes, e.g., flooring, coving, doors, windows, ceilings, drains, panels, penetrations (wall, ceiling, floor), etc. are installed as specified

  • Electrical drawing walk down, e.g., data communication, alarm systems connections, cable trays, and conduits are installed and labeled
  • Electrical cabinet construction and installation, e.g., are of appropriate waterproof classification
  • Software version and configuration settings identification and backup
  • Control panel wiring diagrams
  • Screen navigation testing
  • Hard-wired alarm and interlock testing

  • Communication loss and recovery testing
  • Loop testing, calibration, or tuning
  • Data integrity and audit trail testing
Commentary

🏗️ Three Areas of Installation Testing

Installation Testing covers three aspects, and none can be left out:

  • Mechanical: pipe connections, slope verification, pressure testing, confirming that it is "installed"
  • Construction Records: construction completion, welding records, material compliance, confirming that it is "installed correctly"
  • Automation & Electrical: software, wiring, alarms, data integrity, confirming that "the control system is correct"

⚠️ Key Points of the 10-Item Automation List

  • Software version and backup: the version number must be recorded and a backup made, a basic GAMP 5 requirement
  • Hard-wired alarm and interlock testing: hard-wired alarms (such as an over-temperature cut-out) must be tested independently of the software, to confirm they still work even if the PLC fails
  • Power/Communication loss and recovery: how the system recovers after a power outage or communication interruption is a key safety test for pharmaceutical equipment
  • Data integrity & audit trail: a key focus of FDA/EMA regulation; any manual entry or system event must have a tamper-proof record

💡 What Is an Audit Trail?

An audit trail is an operation log recorded automatically by the system, for example: "2026-01-15 14:32, Operator A changed the temperature set point from 121°C to 123°C".

Both FDA 21 CFR Part 11 and EMA Annex 11 require that:

  • Records cannot be deleted or modified (entries can only be added, never overwritten)
  • Records include: time stamp, user ID, old value, new value and reason for change (if any)
  • During testing, verify that the audit trail function works correctly and prevents tampering

7.3.2.1

Pre-Delivery Inspections (PDIs)

Construction checks at the vendor site before delivery — value and scope

Original Text (English)

PDIs are construction checks (inspection and testing) of systems or major system components before delivery to the owner site. These are typically done to verify construction progress and quality standards and to ensure delivery as scheduled. PDIs include inspection of parts (e.g., baffles in a heating jacket) that would not be able to be inspected during FAT or where a FAT cannot be performed for the system. This allows for remedy of any failings and avoids the delays to schedule (and associated costs) that would result from discovering problems later on-site.

Commentary

🔍 PDI (Pre-Delivery Inspection): Why Go to the Vendor's Factory?

The core logic of PDI is: "find problems where they are easiest to solve".

Defects found at the vendor's facility:

  • Low repair cost (the vendor has the equipment, tools and people)
  • No impact on site construction progress
  • Hidden components that "cannot be seen after delivery" can be inspected (such as baffles inside a heating jacket)

If problems are found only after the equipment reaches the site:

  • The equipment may have to be dismantled and returned (high cost)
  • It takes up site space and schedule
  • It may delay the whole plant start-up plan

💡 Analogy: PDI Is Like a Car's Factory Inspection

Just as a car undergoes a full quality inspection before leaving the assembly line, PDI gives the owner a chance to confirm quality before the equipment leaves the factory. The difference is that pharmaceutical equipment is often custom-made and the vendor does not stock spare units of the same model, so once a problem is found after shipment, the repair time and cost are far higher than in the automotive industry.

🤔 Discussion Question: When Is the Value of PDI Highest?

In which of the following situations is a PDI most worthwhile?

  1. Buying an off-the-shelf peristaltic pump
  2. Ordering a 10,000 L WFI still (multi-column still, custom design required)
  3. Buying standard 316L stainless steel fittings and valves

Answer: Case 2 is the one most worth a PDI. Custom, complex equipment carries a high risk of manufacturing deviations, and the cost of repair after delivery is extremely high. Standard off-the-shelf items (cases 1 and 3) carry relatively low risk, and the marginal benefit of a PDI is small.

7.3.2.2 Factory Acceptance Test (FAT): Installation Tests

p56 — Earliest verification at vendor site; shipping agreements; FAT vs SAT scope

Original Text — English

7.3.2.2 Factory Acceptance Test: Installation Tests

The FAT is inspection/testing of the system at the vendor (system manufacturer) site. The objective of the FAT is to provide testing and documentation at the earliest possible time, to verify that the vendor has fulfilled contractual responsibilities and the system meets the requirements. This allows for quicker and more efficient remedy of any failings and avoids the delays to schedule that would result from discovering problems later, on-site.

After completing the FAT, the owner representative agrees with the vendor whether the system is ready to be shipped or on pre-conditions for shipping. Agreements are documented and approved by the vendor and owner.

Where a system needs to be disassembled for shipping, the process should be documented. Details should include how the system will be disassembled, any marking or labeling of components, and how it will be reassembled and tested on-site. This information provides the rationale for:

  • Testing that can be accepted from the FAT
  • Testing that will be repeated as part of the SAT
Commentary

Core Concept: FAT (Factory Acceptance Test)

FAT (Factory Acceptance Test) is installation/functional testing performed at the vendor's premises, and its purpose is to find problems as early as possible. Defects found before the equipment ships are the cheapest to correct and have the least impact on the schedule.

Legal significance of FAT: passing FAT shows that the vendor has met its contractual obligations, and both parties sign the documentation as evidence.

Analogy: FAT = factory quality inspection

Imagine you order a custom-built piece of equipment. FAT is the acceptance check at the factory before it ships. If you only find a problem after shipping the equipment back to your site, you pay freight both ways and wait for the repair, which can be very costly in a pharmaceutical project schedule.

Key point: disassembly for shipping determines the FAT vs SAT test split

If the equipment must be disassembled for shipping (such as a large WFI system), some of the items the vendor has already tested must be re-tested at SAT, because reassembly may affect the original calibration results. Conversely, test results that are not affected by disassembly can be relied on from the FAT data.

This disassembly/reassembly plan must be documented in advance at FAT, as the basis for the SAT test scope.

CDMO practical application

When a client commissions a CDMO and asks to witness FAT (usually for Phase 3 or commercial manufacturing equipment), the CDMO must confirm in advance: observer qualifications, NDA protection, photography/documentation restrictions, and who owns the FAT punch list.

7.3.2.3 Receipt Inspection

Receipt Inspection (RI) provides documentation that delivered components, materials, or systems conform to the requirements, e.g., manufacturer, model, serial number as applicable with the required supporting documentation. 7.3.2.4 Site Acceptance Test: Installation Tests

The SAT is the testing of the system at the owner organization site. The objective of the SAT is to provide testing and documentation of the installed system, to verify that the vendor has fulfilled contractual responsibilities and that the system meets the requirements prior to contractually accepting the system.

The SAT test plan includes a review of the FAT punch list (items carried over from the FAT that did not meet the acceptance criteria/expected results) and the plan to resolve open items. The SAT also includes reconfirming FAT test results where required due to system disassembly and reassembly.

After completing SAT testing, the owner representative agrees with the vendor whether the system is ready to be turned over to the owner or on pre-conditions for system turnover. Agreements are documented and approved by the vendor and owner. Acceptance of a system with open discrepancies can be made with the approval of the Testing Coordinator and System Owner.

Commentary

Key Points of RI (Receipt Inspection)

Receipt Inspection (RI) is the first check once equipment/materials arrive on site, confirming that they are correct, complete and undamaged.

  • Confirm that make, model and serial number match the purchase specification
  • Confirm that the accompanying documents (material certificates, calibration reports, etc.) are complete
  • Confirm that the goods arrived in good condition and are correctly labeled
  • Nonconforming items must be quarantined and must not enter the installation process

Analogy: RI = unboxing an online order

Just as you inspect a parcel when it arrives from an online purchase, RI must be performed immediately on receipt. If you discover the wrong specification only after the item has been fitted into the system, the cost of removing and replacing it is much higher.

A system with unresolved discrepancies can still be accepted

After SAT is complete, if any discrepancies remain open, the system can still be accepted and handed over, provided the Testing Coordinator and System Owner jointly approve. This is common practice, but the open items and the plan for resolving them must be clearly recorded.

7.3.2.5 Installation Verification

Installation verification includes verifying quality requirements related to installation (CDEs as defined through the System Risk Assessment). Example activities include:

  • Verification that the product, ingredient and/or cleaning material contacting parts meet the specifications (materials of construction and finishes, as applicable)

7.3.3 System Start-Up

System start-up is a GEP approach to ensure that the system is energized and started up in a safe manner. This is also where adjustments to the system, settings to regulate the system, or any balancing needed are made. A prerequisite to system start-up is that the system is mechanically complete without open critical punch list items for safety. System start-up may be conducted both at the vendor facility and after installed on-site.

The vendor operation or start-up manual should be reviewed prior to energizing the system. For systems that include vendor-provided control systems, training should be completed prior to energizing the system. Training of system operators, engineers, and maintenance personnel should be conducted in association with system vendors and properly documented.

System start-up is a four-step process, consisting of:

  1. Verification that the system is complete with all valve switches in the correct position. For stick-built systems and modular systems, ensure that the integrated system (including automation) has been reviewed before it is energized.
  2. Pre-start-up safety review.
  3. Supervised energizing of the system utilities.
  4. Pre-functional check of the system, with focus on safety functions.

Note that Steps 1 and 2 above need to be completed before Step 3. Step 3 needs to be completed prior to Step 4.

Any issues identified during system start-up activities should be verified to not impact future testing and addressed during the following testing steps.

Installation Verification focuses on CDEs

Installation Verification is not a comprehensive installation check. It specifically targets the CDEs (Critical Design Elements) identified in the system risk assessment.

The two most important types of check:

  • Material compliance: whether parts in contact with the product or cleaning agents meet the specified materials (for example, 316L stainless steel, electropolished finish)
  • Critical instrument calibration: whether calibration of thermometers, pressure gauges, flow meters and similar instruments is complete and valid

Vendor training is a prerequisite for energization

For equipment with a vendor-supplied control system (such as PLC or SCADA), training of operators, engineers and maintenance staff must be completed and documented before power-up. This is not only a safety requirement but a basic principle of GEP.

Original Text — English

7.3.4 Operational/Functional Testing

Operational/functional testing verifies that the system functionality matches what has been approved in the specifications (including those identified as CAs) and is suitably documented in a test plan/record for the full desired range of system operations in the target environment.

Typically, the final step of functional testing is a full operational testing — a full load test of the system to encompass worst case conditions. This test may need to be deferred if it is not possible to provide the relevant conditions. Final tuning of control loops may need to be delayed until system balancing has been performed (e.g., air or water balancing) or until enough system loads are available.

Functional testing activities include, for example:

  • Testing integrated automation controls and equipment for the whole system — both normal and alarm/failure modes
  • Testing analog control loops at a range of values representing the intended system use — encompassing the anticipated range of normal operation, including upper and lower operating limits (worst case conditions) without inducing process failure
  • Confirming the system can handle predictable simultaneous use scenarios for utility systems
  • Determining and recording manual component settings (e.g., valve positions, limit switch/screw positions, control switch settings)

7.3.4.1 Factory Acceptance Test and Site Acceptance Test: Operational Tests

After installation testing and system start-up, the FAT and SAT test documents may include execution of operational testing.

7.3.4.2 Qualification Operational Tests

Operational tests that demonstrate the ability of the system to meet the CAs/CDE functionalities are considered part of the qualification of the system and require Quality Unit oversight. Complex or novel systems may require formal system integrated operational testing to document PQ/PPQ operational criteria/control strategy.

Commentary

Core principle of functional testing

The goal of functional testing is to verify that the system meets specification across the entire expected operating range, including normal conditions and worst-case scenarios.

Key elements:

  • Must begin only after installation testing and calibration are complete
  • Covers mechanical, electrical, automation (PLC/SCADA), HSE and other aspects
  • The final step is usually the Full Load Test

Analogy: worst-case testing = stress testing

Just as a car is road-tested in extreme weather, functional testing must cover the upper and lower operating limits. For example, the HVAC system must achieve the design air change rate on the hottest summer day and the coldest winter day. Testing only at "average" conditions is not enough.

7.3.4.2 QOT: a key distinction that requires QA involvement

The biggest difference between Qualification Operational Tests (QOT) and ordinary functional tests:

  • Functional tests of CAs/CDEs = qualification activities
  • They must be under Quality Unit (QA) oversight
  • Deviations in test results must go through the QA approval process

In other words, for the functional testing of a single piece of equipment, the part related to CAs is OQ (operational qualification) and QA must be involved; the part unrelated to CAs is only commissioning and can be completed by engineers on their own.

Exercise

A pharmaceutical plant has a steam piping system. Steam pressure is a CDE (it affects sterilization effectiveness). Question: is testing the interlock action of the automatic steam line switching valve at abnormal pressure a QOT or an ordinary functional test? How should it be handled?

Answer: Steam pressure is a CDE, and the valve interlock action related to it directly affects sterilization safety, so this is a QOT. It requires QA oversight, and deviations must be handled through the qualification deviation process.

7.3.5 Performance Qualification Testing

Original Text — English

7.3.5 Performance Qualification Testing

Performance Qualification (PQ) testing is the documented verification that manufacturing systems perform effectively and reproducibly, based on the approved process or analytical method and product specification, applying either original product or representative placebo. Cycle development and/or engineering tests are often necessary prior to performing PQ testing.

Completion of installation and operational testing (as applicable) to ensure the systems are operating correctly is required before performing cycle development; PQ testing can then be conducted. Operational SOPs should be used during PQ.

Examples of systems where PQ is usually required before acceptance and release include:

  • Autoclaves
  • Filling machines
  • Pharmaceutical water and steam systems
Commentary

Three Key Features of PQ (Performance Qualification)

PQ (Performance Qualification) is the last of the three qualification steps and the one closest to "real use":

  1. Uses actual product or a substitute (placebo), not just water or a simulated medium
  2. Demonstrates reproducibility, usually requiring multiple repeats (for example, three runs in sterilization validation)
  3. Performed under the operating SOP, verifying that specification is also met when personnel operate according to the SOP

The PQ Prerequisite Chain

The order specified by ISPE is very strict:

IQ complete → OQ complete → engineering tests/cycle development → operating SOP established → PQ executed

If an intermediate step is skipped (for example, running PQ without first completing cycle development), serious problems will arise at audit.

Analogy: PQ = the hands-on practical exam

IQ checks that the tools are installed correctly, OQ checks that the system does what the operating manual says, and PQ asks whether, in the real exam, a candidate following the SOP gets results that meet specification. If PQ fails, the system still cannot meet requirements under real operating conditions, and the entire release process must be paused.

Chapter 8: Acceptance and Release — Introduction (8.1)

Original Text — English

8 Acceptance and Release

8.1 Introduction

This chapter provides guidance on the acceptance and release phase which is where all testing is formally reviewed and accepted. The conclusion of the acceptance and release phase is that the system can be released for:

  • Manufacturing operations
  • The next stage of qualification (e.g., for a WFI system)
  • Use in the manufacture of PPQ or commercial batches

Acceptance and release represents the initiation of the "qualified state" and the beginning of QA-managed Quality Change Control (see Chapter 12), continuous performance monitoring, and periodic review. The project team transitions system ownership to operations/engineering.

The acceptance and release phase should occur as a cooperative review between the appropriate SMEs, including a representative from the department that will accept care, custody, and control of the system. The SMEs should review the results from an overall technical perspective.

If the system includes CAs/CDEs (direct impact), then the Quality Unit needs to be part of the acceptance and release review and approval, focusing on CAs/CDEs. Acceptance of a system with open discrepancies should be a Quality Unit decision. Typically, all discrepancies have to be addressed; however, non-product impacting discrepancies may remain open with an appropriate approved action plan.

The acceptance and release phase is not intended to be a catchall for closeout of all system or project documentation.

Commentary

Regulatory Significance of the Start of the "Qualified State"

The moment QA signs the acceptance and release documents, the system formally enters the Qualified State. From a regulatory standpoint, this point means the following:

  • Any later change to the system must go through QA-managed quality change control
  • Ongoing performance monitoring procedures start
  • The periodic review cycle starts (usually every year or every 3 years)
  • Ownership of the system is formally transferred from the project team to the operations/engineering department

Analogy: Qualified State = handover of a finished house

Before acceptance and release, the equipment is "under construction", and engineers can modify it freely. Once QA signs the release, it is like a house after handover: any alteration requires a "renovation permit" (change control). Changing the system without formal QA-approved change control is like renovating without declaring it, which is a major deficiency at audit.

Deviations that do "not affect product quality" can be released with the system

Not all deviations must be closed before release. If a deviation does not affect product quality, it can be left open temporarily on the following conditions:

  • A documented action plan for resolution is in place
  • A responsible person and completion deadline are assigned
  • QA formally approves the exception

8.2 Prerequisites for the Acceptance and Release Phase

8.2 Prerequisites for the Acceptance and Release Phase

The installation, operational, and performance (as required) testing activities that should be completed prior to the acceptance and release phase include:

Commentary

Entry Ticket to the A&R Phase (Gate Criteria)

Acceptance and release do not happen automatically. The following five items must be confirmed:

  1. All test results have been reviewed (not just "data exists" but "someone has looked at it and confirmed it")
  2. Unexpected results have been investigated (they cannot be ignored)
  3. Discrepancies have been addressed (not necessarily all closed, but each has an action plan)
  4. Engineering changes have been closed (or have a QA-approved exception)
  5. Discrepancies have been resolved through engineering change management or retesting

We recommend holding an "A&R Readiness Review" one to two weeks before formal acceptance, using a checklist to confirm the five prerequisites above one by one. The minutes of this meeting can serve as evidence at later audits that the pre-release review was carried out.

8.3 Requirements to Complete the Acceptance and Release Phase

Original Text — English

8.3 Requirements to Complete the Acceptance and Release Phase

In order to complete the acceptance and release phase, the following items are required to be addressed: completion of test documentation, closure of discrepancies and changes, and implementation of quality system elements.

8.3.1 Completion of Testing Documentation

All of the planned testing needs to be successfully executed and the documentation completed. At least one SME review should be documented within the executed tests.

The documentation for completion of testing should be approved by the team of SMEs and include the following information at a minimum:

  • Testing description (component verification, calibration verification, etc.)
  • Testing expected results and if met or not met
  • Testing acceptance criteria and if met or not met
  • Reference to test protocol and execution data (e.g., vendor documents, commissioning)
  • Summary and conclusion

8.3.2 Closure of Discrepancies

Discrepancies should be categorized by potential impact.

  • Discrepancies that do not impact product quality may remain open. The report should contain an indication of any open discrepancies along with potential manufacturing planning implications. These discrepancies should be assigned to a responsible individual with defined responsibility for resolution within a specific timeline.
  • Discrepancies that could impact product quality need to be closed.
  • All discrepancies should be listed with status (closed or open) within the summary report.

8.3.3 Closure of Engineering Changes

All changes should be handled in accordance with change management (as described in Chapter 12). The system changes that have occurred during the testing phase should be closed as part of the acceptance and release phase, unless there is agreement with the Quality Unit for these open changes to be carried forward. All engineering changes should be listed with status (closed or open) within the summary report.

8.3.4 Implementation of Quality System Elements

For systems that contain CAs/CDEs, the implementation of quality systems is required prior to releasing a system for manufacturing operations. These aspects are referred to as quality system elements and may include (as applicable), but are not limited to:

  • Availability of SOPs for operation of the system
  • Calibration/maintenance system and associated procedures
  • Mechanism for tracking system use and maintenance (e.g., logbook)
  • Vendor documentation (e.g., maintenance requirements, spare parts)
  • Operator training within the training quality system
  • Completion of any other organization requirements to add the system into the operating facility

A statement that all required quality system elements are in place should be included in the summary report for systems with CAs/CDEs. If a system is released with outstanding items (requires Quality Unit approval), a quality system should be in place to track these items to closure with an appropriate level of Quality Unit oversight.

Commentary

8.3.1 Minimum Content Requirements for Test Documentation

ISPE requires a Summary Report to contain five minimum elements:

  1. Description of the tests (what was done)
  2. Expected results (what was meant to be achieved) and whether they were achieved
  3. Acceptance criteria and whether they were met
  4. References to the corresponding test protocols and raw data
  5. Summary and conclusions

These five items are minimum requirements, not options. If a report lacks any one of them, the auditor will raise an observation.

8.3.4 Quality System Elements

This is easy to overlook: the equipment has passed testing, but if these supporting systems are not in place, the system still cannot be released:

  • SOPs: operating procedures (correct version, approved)
  • Calibration/maintenance system: the equipment is included in the calibration program
  • Logbooks: a mechanism for recording equipment use, cleaning and maintenance
  • Vendor documentation: manuals, parts lists, maintenance requirements
  • Personnel training: relevant personnel have completed and documented their training

CDMO application: Quality System Elements Checklist

We recommend creating a "quality system elements confirmation form" for equipment release, attached to the A&R report as an appendix, confirming item by item that each element is in place. Pay particular attention: the SOP must be the latest approved version; a draft version does not count as "in place".

The Transition Point from Engineering Change to Quality Change Control

Before A&R is complete, changes to the system can follow the "engineering change management" process (faster, led by engineers). Once QA signs the release, the system enters the Qualified State and all later changes must go through QA-managed quality change control (more rigorous, requiring assessment of the impact on the qualified state). This transition point must be clearly understood.

8.4 Summary Report Organization — 4 Example Options

p63–p66 — Commissioning CSR; Qualification QSR; Change Control; Large Projects A&R Report

Original Text — English

8.4 Summary Report Organization

The acceptance and release process is completed through a summary report. The contents and complexity of the report may vary, but it needs to include SME review and approval of: closure of testing documentation, discrepancies, engineering changes, implementation of quality system elements (as required), and documented formal release of the system.

The summary report can be accomplished in several different ways depending on the scale and complexity of the project. A separate document for the summary report is not always necessary.

8.4.1 Example Option #1: Commissioning Summary Report – Systems with No CAs/CDEs

For systems with no CAs/CDEs (not direct impact), the acceptance and release can be included within the Commissioning Summary Report, which includes:

  • Completion of testing documentation
  • Closure of punch list items and discrepancies
  • Closure of engineering changes
  • Confirmation of documentation in place to ensure the system can operate and be maintained properly
  • Clear statement of formal release of the system

8.4.2 Example Option #2: Qualification Summary Report – Systems with CAs/CDEs

For systems with CAs/CDEs, where all of the requirements are ready at about the same time, the acceptance and release can be included within a single C&Q Summary Report, which includes:

  • Completion of testing documentation
  • Closure of discrepancies
  • Closure of engineering changes
  • Quality System Elements implementation (or reference to change control record)
  • Clear statement of formal release of the system (or reference to change control record)

8.4.3 Example Option #3: Formal Release with Use of Change Control – Existing Facility

When new systems or changes to existing systems are implemented through QA-managed Quality Change Control, some or all of the requirements may be contained within the change control. For existing facilities, it is common to verify the implementation of quality system elements and document the formal release of the system in the change control rather than in the Qualification Summary Report. The use of the change control allows for closure of the testing phase in a timely manner.

8.4.4 Example Option #4: Acceptance and Release Report – Large Projects with Interconnected Systems

For large projects with interconnected systems, multiple reports may be used to complete the acceptance and release phase. An overall acceptance and release report would reference the completed summary reports for each of the interconnected systems, document completion of all quality system elements, and include the formal release statement.

One strategy may be to release per processing area (e.g., cell culture) after all testing is completed and closed out with separate summary reports.

Note: The grouping strategy may complicate future changes. The grouping would also require presenting information from all systems during regulatory audits even when only a single system is requested. The advantages are that qualification would more closely reflect the grouping created by the process risk assessment as well as the batch record structure and eliminate potential gaps with the integration of systems.

Commentary

The Four Release Options at a Glance (Quick Selection Guide)

Option 1 — Commissioning Summary Report

Applies to: systems without CAs/CDEs (indirect impact)

Documentation: Commissioning Summary Report (a single document)

QA: not required

Option 2 — Qualification Summary Report

Applies to: new systems with CAs/CDEs, where all requirements are ready at the same time

Documentation: C&Q Summary Report (covering all elements)

QA: required, signature needed

Option 3 — Release through Change Control

Applies to: adding or modifying a system in an existing facility that already has a qualified state

Documentation: QSR + change control record (quality system elements are documented in the CC)

QA: required (CC approval equals release)

Option 4 — Overall A&R Report

Applies to: large interconnected systems (such as a whole new facility)

Documentation: an independent SR for each system + an overall A&R Report

QA: required; QA signature on the overall A&R Report = release of all systems

Analogy: four options = four modes of building acceptance

Option 1: Tool shed acceptance (just fill in a form)
Option 2: Acceptance of a single new house (one complete acceptance report)
Option 3: Adding partitions in an existing building (building permit application/change management)
Option 4: Acceptance of a whole new housing development (each building accepted individually + overall handover documents for the development)

Two Sides of Option 4

Advantages: reflects the system grouping logic of the process risk assessment, eliminates gaps in cross-system integration, and gives a more consistent batch record structure.

Disadvantages: once grouped, a change to any single piece of equipment may require updating the documents of the entire group. If an auditor examines one piece of equipment, you may have to provide the qualification documents for every piece of equipment in the group, which is a huge volume of documentation.

CDMO decision tree: which Option should I use?

  1. Does this system have CAs/CDEs? → No → Option 1
  2. Does it have CAs/CDEs → is it in an existing facility with a CC process? → Yes → Option 3
  3. New facility / can all elements be ready at the same time? → Yes → Option 2
  4. Large interconnected system / multiple systems to be released in batches? → Option 4

8.6 Traceability Matrix

A Traceability Matrix may be used as a tool to identify CAs/CDEs, acceptance criteria, and includes reference to the testing document (planned or completed). The Regulatory Milestone: "QA Signature = Start of the Qualified State"

This is the most important point in the entire C&Q process:

  • Commercial significance: the CDMO can start manufacturing commercial batches on this system and charging clients

  • Operational significance: the system enters the maintenance/monitoring cycle, and responsibility passes to the operations department

Analogy: traceability matrix = construction management checklist

A traceability matrix is like a construction progress tracker when building a house. It helps the project manager confirm that every design requirement (URS) has a corresponding design (DR) and test (C&Q), with nothing missed. ISPE specifically stresses minimizing the amount of information in the traceability matrix. An overly large matrix becomes a burden at audit: every design change requires the matrix to be updated in step, and if the document is too large, the maintenance effort grows exponentially and auditors have more chances to find inconsistencies. Enough is enough; the goal is precise traceability.

Introduction

Regulatory requirement to maintain qualified state

Original Text (English) — pp. 69

There is a regulatory requirement to maintain qualified systems in a validated state, i.e., the qualification documents remain valid and the system remains suitable for the intended purpose (refer to Chapter 15 for the regulatory basis). Quality systems are in place to maintain that status and should be subject to internal audit to ensure robustness. These quality systems are:

  • Change control
  • Maintenance
  • Calibration
  • Deviation

It is regulatory expectation that there is also a periodic review process to ensure that systems can be considered qualified (refer to Chapter 15 for the regulatory basis).

This chapter describes an approach for periodic review where the frequency of review or requalification is established based on risk, the current level of process understanding and process performance, and regulatory requirements.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

Regulatory Requirements for the Validated / Qualified State

The regulations (ICH Q7, EU GMP Annex 15, FDA Process Validation Guidance) clearly require that validation is not a one-time event but must be maintained on an ongoing basis. Once a system has been validated and qualified, the quality system must ensure that it remains fit for use.

ISPE identifies four quality systems that maintain the validated state:

  • Change Control: any change to equipment, systems or processes must be assessed for its impact on the validated state
  • Maintenance: preventive and corrective maintenance to keep equipment functioning properly
  • Calibration: periodic calibration of measuring equipment to ensure the data can be trusted
  • Deviation: deviation investigations reveal system problems and lead to corrective action

Key Point: Periodic Review vs. Requalification

Periodic Review is the assessment activity that confirms a system remains in the validated state, whereas Requalification is the activity of re-executing qualification tests once a problem has been found. The purpose of periodic review is to "confirm that requalification is not needed" or to "identify the triggers for requalification".

Rationale

Regulatory expectations & science/risk-based approach

Certain systems have regulatory expectations to confirm ongoing system performance through periodic verification; such systems include sterilizing autoclaves, depyrogenation tunnels/ovens, and equipment with a Sterilize-in-Place (SIP) functionality (e.g., freeze dryer). For other types of systems, organizations have traditionally used an annual detailed review of the change history, maintenance, and operational records to provide this confirmation. However, this approach has typically provided a small return when compared to the considerable investment to conduct the review process. In addition, there is concern that, due to the scale of the effort involved, there is a significant risk that important performance signals may be missed.

This Guide proposes applying a science and risk-based approach to periodic review:

  • For a critical utilities system (such as HVAC, clean compressed air, pharmaceutical water and steam systems), routine monitoring includes:
    • Regular reviews of the results
    • Regular reporting of system performance trends, preventative maintenance and calibration metrics
    • Regular check of qualification drawings (typically biannually) and of system tagging (typically annually)

    If routine monitoring is performed, then no additional review is considered necessary. The monitoring confirms system performance and the drawing verification confirms that documentation is up-to-date.

  • For other systems (e.g., manufacturing systems), the relative potential risk to product quality for different system types is evaluated and the frequency of review is defined based on the risk.

This proposed approach is a triage process that looks for any unusual performance signals at a high level. If none are observed, then the review is complete. If there are signals observed, then a deeper review is conducted that may result in detailed investigation and testing.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

Interpreting Table 9.1: Which Systems Have Explicit Regulatory Requirements?

The systems listed in Table 9.1 are all critical, high-risk equipment in sterile manufacturing, so the regulations prescribe their periodic verification frequency directly, with no need for a categorization decision:

  • Autoclave / SIP / depyrogenation tunnel: annual revalidation (EU GMP Annex 1 [24])
  • Aseptic filling line: media fill (APS) every six months (FDA aseptic guidance [23])
  • HEPA filters in ISO 5/Grade A environments: leak testing every six months

Key note: if SIP is under continuous monitoring, revalidation is generally not needed. This is a concrete example of ISPE's "monitoring replaces review" logic.

Analogy: Traditional Annual Review = A Once-a-Year Big Clean-Up

The traditional approach is to spend a great deal of effort every year pulling out and reviewing all of the year's records, like a year-end big clean-up. The problems are:

  • The workload is enormous and reviewers get fatigued, so they may miss the signals that really matter
  • Most systems run well, so the annual review adds no value: low return, high effort

ISPE's science/risk-based approach is like "installing an alarm": monitor continuously in normal times and investigate in depth only when the alarm sounds. It is more efficient and equally compliant.

Core Logic of Cat 0: Continuous Monitoring = Periodic Review

Critical utilities such as WFI, purified water and HVAC already have rigorous routine monitoring programs (TOC, conductivity, microbiology, particle counts, etc.). ISPE holds that this continuous monitoring is itself the best periodic review. These systems are therefore classed as Cat 0 and need no additional formal periodic review.

This logic is very important: it allows an organization to focus its resources on the high-risk systems that genuinely need review.

Mature quality systems & 8 performance metrics

The 8 Quality System Performance Metrics

Computer Audit Trail Review: 8 Mandatory Items

  1. Generic/shared accounts: confirm that no one logs in with "admin" or a shared account
  2. User access management: whether accounts of departed or transferred employees have been disabled (User Access Management, UAM)
  3. Administrator activity audit trail: whether every administrator action is supported by a quality system record
  4. General user activity audit trail: where applicable, whether every action is supported by a record
  5. Changes to critical configuration settings: for critical settings identified in the data integrity assessment, any change must be supported by a quality system record
  6. Confirmation that audit trail reviews are performed: whether the reviews required by the system administration SOP and the operating SOP are actually carried out
  7. Confirmation by the quality unit reviewer: whether the quality unit reviewed the audit trail as the verifier
  8. Backup and archive: backup and archiving of GMP-critical data has been verified

Many CDMOs have multiple computerized systems (LIMS, MES, SCADA, EBR, etc.), and each one requires the 8 reviews above. Common deficiencies include:

  • No SOP defines the review frequency ("periodic" is not clearly defined)
  • IT performs the review but the quality unit does not act as the verifier
  • Shared account issues are not treated as GMP violations

Recommendation: build an inventory of computerized systems, assign an owner to each system, define the review frequency and document it in an SOP.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

Core Logic of System Categorization

Categorization is based on the potential impact on product quality, and the position in the process (early vs. late stage) is an important factor:

  • Early-stage processes (fermentation, crude purification): multiple downstream process steps and tests act as a screen, so equipment problems are less likely to affect the final product directly → lower risk
  • Late-stage processes (fill/finish, sterilization): these directly affect final product quality with no buffer downstream → higher risk

The Four Categories

  • Cat 0 No formal periodic review needed: continuous monitoring is in place, or an integrity test is performed with every use (such as the integrity test for sterilizing filtration). WFI, HVAC, compressed air and packaging systems all fall into this category.
  • Cat 1 Follow the regulatory frequency: for example, autoclaves are revalidated annually under EU GMP Annex 1, and no periodic review is added on top of that.
  • Cat 2 Review every 2 years: fill/finish filling systems, chromatography purification equipment, etc. These are important but have monitoring as a safety net.
  • Cat 3 Review every 3 years: complex systems such as tablet coaters, tablet presses and fermenters. Note: a lower frequency does not mean less important; it is because the system has sufficient process monitoring to provide early warning.

Common Misunderstanding: Lowest Frequency for Cat 3 = Least Important?

A bioreactor is classed as Cat 3 (every 3 years) not because it is unimportant, but because the process itself has extensive continuous monitoring (pH, DO, temperature, agitation, etc.), and this monitoring data is itself ongoing performance verification. It therefore does not need formal periodic review as frequently as a high-risk fill/finish system (Cat 2).

CDMO Application: How to Build the System Categorization List

CDMO implementation steps:

  1. Build a complete inventory of all validated equipment/systems (linked to the IQ/OQ/PQ validation documents)
  2. Categorize each system (Cat 0-3) and record the basis for categorization (SME assessment comments)
  3. Set the review schedule according to Table 9.3, and place Cat 2 and Cat 3 systems in a multi-year plan
  4. For Cat 1 systems, track the regulatory annual revalidation schedule, with no separate periodic review
  5. Re-review periodically (at least every two years) whether the categorization is still appropriate

Analogy: Car Servicing vs. Aircraft Overhaul

Cat 0 is like the warning light on a car's fuel gauge: monitor continuously and go to the workshop only when there is a problem. Cat 1 is like the statutory overhaul of an aircraft (MRO): the regulations fix the cycle and it is non-negotiable. Cat 2/3 are like high-speed rail periodic maintenance: the frequency is set according to complexity, with a comprehensive assessment at the scheduled time.

9.4

Phase 2: Periodic Review Execution — Overview

Three-tier triage across four quality systems

Original Text (English) — pp. 75–76

Using the frequency schedules established in Phase 1, the periodic reviews can be performed using a three-tier approach which involves reviewing the following four quality systems:

  • GMP compliance expectations
  • Change control system
  • Maintenance/calibration system
  • Deviation system

The three-tier approach is summarized as follows:

  • An initial assessment (Tier A) is done at a high level. If this initial assessment shows any signs that the system is not operating consistently, then the assessment progresses to the next level; if not, then the review is considered complete.
  • The second level assessment (Tier B) is more detailed. If this assessment shows that there are any issues, then the assessment progresses to the next level. If this assessment concludes that the system is still performing satisfactorily, then the review is complete.
  • The third level assessment (Tier C) involves defining and completing the actions required to determine the system to be suitable for the intended purpose, with the necessary supporting documentation updated as necessary.

The assessment period is the time period since the previous periodic review or since the original qualification.

Note: When a periodic review program is initially introduced, it is typical to cover the systems in each schedule class within the associated timeframe. For example, for systems with a two-year review period, the initial review of all systems in that schedule class can be completed in the first two years.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

The Three-Tier Triage Process: The Core Innovation

This is the core innovation of the ISPE periodic review approach. The traditional approach is to perform an annual review of the same depth for every system; ISPE's three-tier triage works as follows:

A
Initial Assessment (Tier A): high-level queries; most systems are completed here
↓ If an abnormal signal is found
B
Detailed Assessment (Tier B): SME evaluation; complete if there is no concern
↓ If there is concern about the validated state
C
Corrective Action (Tier C): develop an action plan and raise a non-conformance record

Key point: no signal = review complete. There is no need to carry out pointless deep reviews just to "look like a lot of work has been done".

Four Quality Systems × Three Tiers = 12 Assessment Modules

This matrix structure makes the review work modular: each quality system is assessed independently, only those with signals escalate to the next tier, and those without signals are complete. For example: if Tier A queries all four systems and finds a signal only in the "deviation system", only the deviation system escalates to Tier B, and the review of the other three systems is complete.

Analogy: Emergency Room Triage

ISPE uses the term "triage process" directly. The logic of emergency triage is that not every patient needs immediate surgery; medical resources are allocated by severity. Likewise, in periodic review not every system needs an in-depth investigation: investigate in depth when there is a signal, and finish when there is none, ensuring resources go where they are really needed.

The Importance of the "Assessment Period"

The Assessment Period is the time span from the end of the last periodic review (or completion of the original qualification) to the present. All queries cover records for this period, ensuring the review is comprehensive and does not miss problems from the intervening time.

When a periodic review program is first introduced, ISPE allows the initial reviews to be completed in batches within the time frame specified for each category (for example, Cat 2 systems can be completed one after another within the first two years); they do not all need to be completed immediately.

9.4.1

Initial Assessment — Tier A

High-level signal detection across four quality systems

Original Text (English) — pp. 76–77

To perform the initial assessment, the records from the four quality systems are reviewed to identify any concerns related to the qualified state of the system being assessed.

GMP Compliance Expectations (Tier A)

If there is an organization/site quality system that monitors changes in regulatory requirements/expectations and proactively addresses changes within the organization/site, then this category does not need to be reviewed as part of periodic review.

If this system is not in place, then the Quality Unit should:

  • Review changes in the relevant regulatory regulations and guidances for the assessment period, with the current scope of qualification reviewed to ensure it meets any changes in the requirements where necessary.
  • Review related audit observations and determine if there are any changes in regulatory expectations.

Change Control System (Tier A)

For the system being assessed:

  • Perform a query in the change control system database for the assessment period
  • Review the query results and determine if there have been any reportable change requests with qualification impact that can affect the system quality attributes

Note: Change records that have been determined to be low risk, and thus are not reportable to regulatory agencies, do not need to be reviewed as part of the periodic review process.

Maintenance/Calibration System (Tier A)

For the system being assessed:

  • Perform a query in the maintenance management system for the assessment period
  • Determine if there were repeated corrective maintenance and/or calibration records

Deviation System (Tier A)

For the system being assessed:

  • Perform a query of the deviations for the assessment period
  • Review the query results for deviations with potential product quality impact where the root cause has been attributed to the system being assessed

Note: Deviations that have already been determined to have little or no risk to product quality do not need to be reviewed as part of the periodic review process.

After this initial assessment of each quality system:

  • If any concerns (regarding the potential impact to the qualified state) within a quality system are identified, then a second level assessment needs to be performed. Note that the scope of the second level assessment is limited to the quality system(s) of concern.
  • If no concerns are identified, then the assessment for that quality system is complete.
Commentary and Key Learning Points

Tier A: Query Focus for the Four Quality Systems

Tier A is purely querying and screening. It requires no in-depth analysis, and its purpose is to find "signals":

  • GMP compliance: if the organization already has a system for proactively tracking regulatory changes, this item can be skipped. If not, check whether any regulatory revisions during the assessment period affect the current scope of validation.
  • Change control: query whether there were any reportable changes that affect the validated state during the assessment period. Low-risk/non-reportable changes do not need to be reviewed.
  • Maintenance/calibration: query for repeated corrective maintenance (CM), which is an early warning of equipment degradation.
  • Deviation: query for deviations whose root cause points to equipment and which have a product quality impact. Deviations already assessed as low risk do not need to be reviewed.

Key Terms: Corrective vs. Preventive Maintenance

  • Preventive Maintenance (PM): routine maintenance performed on a predetermined schedule, such as regular replacement of consumables and lubrication. Completing PM on time is an indicator of a mature quality system.
  • Corrective Maintenance (CM): repair performed after equipment failure. Repeated CM is a trigger signal in the Tier A review. It indicates that the equipment may have systematic degradation, and further assessment is needed to determine whether the validated state is affected.

Conditional Logic of the GMP Compliance Assessment (Important!)

ISPE allows an organization to replace the Tier A GMP compliance assessment with a "proactive regulatory tracking system". This means that if the company has already established a procedure for regularly tracking regulatory updates from the FDA, EMA and other health authorities and acting on them, the Tier A assessment for this quality system can be waived. This is a practical ISPE design to avoid duplicated work.

CDMO Practice: Building a Tier A Query Template

It is recommended to create a standardized query template for each system, containing:

  • System name and identifier
  • Assessment period (start date to current date)
  • Query results for each quality system (signal / no signal)
  • If there is a signal: describe the signal and decide whether to escalate to Tier B
  • Reviewer signature and date

Most systems should be able to complete the Tier A queries in 15-30 minutes (assuming the quality systems are well maintained and can be searched quickly).

9.4.2

Second Level Assessment — Tier B

Deeper SME review of flagged quality systems

Original Text (English) — pp. 77

The second level assessment should only be performed on each quality system category that was determined during the initial assessment to require further assessment. All second level assessment activities should be performed by representatives (SMEs) from the same departments as the initial assessment team.

GMP Compliance Expectations (Tier B)

Using the information from the initial assessment:

  • Evaluate the impact of the regulatory changes identified in the initial assessment
  • In the judgment of the assessment team, determine if the changes present concerns about the qualified state of the system

Change Control System (Tier B)

Using the information from the initial assessment:

  • Evaluate the changes identified in the initial assessment
  • In the judgment of the assessment team, determine if the number and/or type of changes identified present concerns about the qualified state of the system

Maintenance/Calibration System (Tier B)

Using the information from the initial assessment:

  • Review the maintenance and calibration records identified in the initial assessment (including corrective maintenance)
  • In the judgment of the assessment team, determine if the records present concerns about the qualified state of the system

Deviation System (Tier B)

Using the information from the initial assessment:

  • Review the deviations with potential product impact identified in the initial assessment
  • In the judgment of the assessment team, determine if the deviations indicate that the system performance is not consistent and thus may not be performing in a qualified state
Commentary and Key Learning Points

Tier B: Judging from "Signal" to "Concern"

The core question of Tier B is: "Does this signal affect the validated state of the system?"

Tier A only confirms that "a signal exists", whereas Tier B requires the SME team to make a quality judgment:

  • Do the number/types of changes indicate that the system has moved outside its validated scope?
  • Does repeated corrective maintenance mean the equipment can no longer perform its critical functions reliably?
  • Do the deviations show a systematic trend, indicating that the system is no longer in the validated state?

There are two Tier B conclusions: no concern → complete or concern → escalate to Tier C.

The "Scope Limitation" Principle of Tier B

Important: Tier B is performed only for the quality systems flagged in Tier A. If Tier A assesses four quality systems and finds a signal only in the "maintenance/calibration system", Tier B needs to assess only maintenance/calibration and does not need to reassess the other three.

This design ensures the review effort corresponds to the risk: investigate in depth only when there is a problem, which effectively controls the total workload.

Analogy: Step-by-Step Deepening in Medical Diagnosis

Tier A is the blood test report (quick screening), Tier B is the doctor's detailed history-taking and physical examination during the consultation (in-depth assessment), and Tier C is arranging further tests and treatment (corrective action). At Tier B the doctor (SME) makes a clinical judgment: does this abnormality need further action?

Documentation Requirements for the Tier B Assessment

Tier B requires a written assessment record documenting:

  • Reason for escalation (which signal from Tier A)
  • SME team members (system owner, maintenance representative, quality unit)
  • Basis of the assessment (specific record numbers or data reviewed)
  • Conclusion: whether there is a concern about the validated state
  • If no concern: record that the review is complete and the system remains in the validated state
  • If there is a concern: escalate to Tier C and initiate corrective action
9.4.3

Third Level Assessment — Tier C

Defining actions to restore qualified state

Original Text (English) — pp. 77–78

The third level assessment involves defining and completing the actions required to determine the system to be suitable for the intended purpose, with the necessary supporting documentation updated as necessary.

At this level:

  • Actions are defined to restore or confirm the qualified state of the system
  • A non-conformance record is generated to capture and track the required actions
  • Actions may include requalification testing, documentation updates, or other corrective measures as determined by the assessment team
  • All supporting documentation must be updated to reflect the current state of the system

Refer to Chapter 25 (Appendix 11) for an example periodic review form. Refer to Chapter 26 (Appendix 12) for information regarding periodic review of Controlled Temperature Chambers (CTCs).

Commentary and Key Learning Points

Tier C: Closing the Loop from "Concern" to "Action"

Tier C is the corrective loop of the quality system, ensuring that the problems found are formally tracked and resolved:

  • Raise a Non-Conformance Record: formally document the problem so that it is traceable
  • Define corrective actions: these may include requalification testing, document updates, equipment repair, etc.
  • Update documents: validation documents, drawings and SOPs must reflect the current state of the system
  • Track completion status: confirm through the CAPA system that actions are completed and effective

Types of Actions Tier C May Trigger

Depending on the severity of the problem, Tier C may trigger:

  • Document updates: update as-built drawings, SOPs, validation plans
  • Supplementary qualification testing: perform supplementary OQ or PQ tests for specific functions
  • Full Requalification: if the system has changed significantly or its performance has deviated seriously
  • Equipment replacement or upgrade: if corrective maintenance can no longer resolve the root problem
  • Process adjustment: if the problem comes from process parameters rather than the equipment itself

CDMO Application: Integrating Tier C with the CAPA System

It is recommended to integrate Tier C corrective actions directly into the company's CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action) system:

  • Use "periodic review" as a CAPA trigger source category
  • Record the root cause analysis in the CAPA (e.g. deviation/maintenance trends)
  • Set action completion deadlines and owners
  • Require an effectiveness check before the CAPA is closed
  • After closure, update the periodic review record to confirm the system has been restored to the validated state

Section Summary: Chapter 9: Periodic Review

This chapter introduced ISPE's science- and risk-based periodic review approach. Its main structure is as follows:

  • Regulatory basis: the regulations require the validated state to be maintained; the four quality systems (change control, maintenance, calibration, deviation) together with periodic review jointly support this requirement.
  • Key innovation: the logic of Cat 0: critical utilities such as WFI and HVAC already have continuous monitoring programs, and continuous monitoring is itself the best periodic review, so Cat 0 systems need no formal periodic review.
  • System categorization (Phase 1): systems are divided into Cat 0-3 by risk to product quality; Cat 2 is reviewed every 2 years and Cat 3 every 3 years; Cat 1 follows the fixed frequency set by regulation (e.g. annual autoclave revalidation).
  • Three-tier triage process (Phase 2): the progressive A → B → C design means most systems complete review at Tier A (no signals), and only systems with real problems need in-depth investigation, greatly improving efficiency.
  • Computer audit trail review: the 8 mandatory items listed in Section 9.1.2 (including user access management, administrator activity tracking and backup verification) are an important part of data integrity compliance and are often overlooked.
  • Prerequisites: a complete inventory of validated systems, current regulatory versions, as-built drawings and quality system history records are the foundation for applying this approach.

Implications for CDMOs: the priorities for adopting this approach are (1) build a system categorization list, (2) ensure the quality systems can be queried quickly, (3) establish a computer audit trail SOP, and (4) train a cross-functional review team. On first introduction, the initial reviews of all systems can be completed in batches within the Cat 2/3 time frames.

Chapter 10: Vendor Assessment for C&Q Documentation Purposes

10.1 Introduction & Purpose/Benefits & Principles — Pages 79–80

🌎 Original Text — English

10.1 Introduction

This chapter describes vendor assessments and controls for work where vendors follow their own procedures to perform testing and produce documentation that may be used to support system qualification. When vendor tests are used to directly support qualification where appropriate, the result is reduced on-site testing and reduced cost and time in the schedule without impacting quality. In addition, the SMEs on the project team can contribute to improved product quality by focusing their attention on other higher risk quality critical activities.

Vendor assessments (for C&Q documentation purposes) do not include the vendor selection and/or commercial assessment, though the results may impact those assessments. Vendor assessments for C&Q documentation purposes may be integrated into this broader vendor selection and qualification process or comprise a standalone activity for C&Q planning and execution purposes.

Regulatory expectations for vendor assessments are provided in Chapter 15 (Appendix 1). For detailed information on assessing vendors of automated systems, refer to ISPE GAMP® 5.

Note: It is not necessary to perform vendor assessments, as described in this Guide, for vendors that provide qualification services (sometimes referred to as secondary services) when those vendors are contracted to follow the regulated owner organization procedures and practices or applicable specialist standards, such as the US National Environmental Balancing Bureau (NEBB).

10.1.1 Purpose and Benefits

The objective of a vendor assessment is to establish confidence in the vendor and the vendor quality systems to perform testing, manage changes, and provide documentation that is accurate, complete, and unambiguous, with results matching what was expected. Vendor verification documentation assessments are highly recommended when documents may be used to support qualification. Purchaser and vendor cooperation is key to successfully achieving this goal.

Vendor assessments can minimize business/project risk associated with vendor performance, including project cost and schedule risk, by performing testing and completing documentation deliverables required to support verification of CAs (qualification) right the first time.

The use of vendor documentation can prevent repeating work that was appropriately performed and documented and can help to achieve GMP compliance at a reduced cost and in less time. Vendors are the experts in their particular systems and the use of vendor testing and documentation should be encouraged. As an example, consider verification of product contact materials. Accepting a certificate of conformity from a quality vendor can be an acceptable, low-risk approach for material verification; checking 100% of the related heat certificates would therefore be unnecessary.

10.1.2 Principles/Concepts

It is good engineering practice to assess vendor's capability for testing and documenting the systems they provide for conformance to requirements and specifications, based on the criticality of the system. The decision and justification to use vendor documentation to support the verification of CAs and associated CDEs should be based on the intended purpose of the manufacturing system and the suitability of the vendor quality systems. The decision and rationale should be documented in the C&Q Plan and approved by SMEs and the Quality Unit.

The Quality Unit and Engineering/Technical SME should agree on the type (e.g., checklist, test plan, execution report) and quality (Good Documentation Practices) of the deliverables. The Quality Unit should review the vendor assessment and confirm the extent to which the vendor's documentation package may be used to support qualification and verify suitability for the intended purpose. This review and confirmation should be conducted and documented as early as possible.

Quality Unit oversight in the use of vendor verification documentation is a risk-based decision, with consideration of risk to product quality. For example, there is less product quality risk in documenting pipe boroscoping or passivation activities as compared to an SAT for a sterile filling isolator.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

📚 Core Concept: What Is Vendor Assessment?

In this chapter, the purpose of "vendor assessment" is very specific: to confirm whether the vendor's own test records and documentation can be used directly to support the Qualification of equipment/systems.

The core question is: "Is the quality of this vendor's documentation good enough that we do not need to repeat the full set of tests on site?"

Key terms:

  • Vendor Assessment = evaluating the vendor's ability to produce documentation that supports C&Q
  • Vendor Quality System = the vendor's QMS
  • Certificate of Conformity / CoC = a statement issued by the vendor declaring that the material/specification requirements are met

⚠️ Key distinction: this is not a supplier audit!

Many people confuse two different things:

  • Vendor assessment for C&Q documentation purposes (the topic of this chapter): evaluates whether the vendor can produce C&Q documents that comply with GDocP (FAT/SAT reports, test records, etc.), so that the owner does not have to repeat the testing.
  • Commercial supplier qualification (Vendor Qualification / Supplier Audit): evaluates the GMP compliance of API, excipient and packaging material suppliers to ensure material quality. This is an entirely different purpose!

The chapter states explicitly that C&Q vendor assessment "does not include" commercial vendor selection assessment, even though the results of the two may inform each other.

💡 Analogy: a CoC is like a "certificate of origin"

Imagine you are buying 316L tubing from a reputable stainless steel tube supplier. If you have already assessed that the supplier's QMS is reliable, you can accept their CoC (Certificate of Conformity) to verify the material, rather than sending 100% of the tubing for a heat certificate check yourself.

This reflects the principle that "vendors are the experts on their own systems": let the experts take responsibility for their own work, so the owner can focus on the items that are truly high risk.

🔎 Summary of Principles/Concepts

The basis for deciding whether to accept vendor documentation:

  • The intended purpose of the manufacturing system
  • The suitability of the vendor QMS
  • The rationale for the decision must be documented in the C&Q Plan and approved by the SME and the QU

The level of QU oversight is risk-based:

  • Low risk: pipe borescope inspection (Boroscoping) and passivation documentation
  • High risk: SAT (Site Acceptance Testing) of an aseptic filling isolator

The higher the risk, the deeper the QU involvement. This is the core logic running through the whole of ISPE Vol.5.

📋 Waiver Situations

If the vendor provides a "minor service" (for example, an HVAC balancing contractor such as NEBB) and the contract requires it to work in accordance with the owner's procedures or applicable professional standards, a vendor assessment per this chapter is not required.

11.1 Introduction & Rationale — Pages 83–84

🌎 Original Text — English

11.1 Introduction

When the C&Q process is considered as a part of an organization-wide PQS, the result is an achievement of both GMP and GEP concepts. A separate quality system is not required for C&Q; instead, it can be part of the overall PQS or a subset of the PQS. For the purposes of this Guide, the term Engineering Quality Process is used to describe the process within the PQS that covers C&Q. This Guide recognizes that this function is referred to differently across organizations which have their own structure and assignment of roles and responsibilities. This chapter intends to focus on the concepts that should be addressed for this function.

The overall organization PQS program provides the quality practices for processes in order to consistently deliver quality products. The Engineering Quality Process, as a subset process of the PQS, addresses the quality practices for the C&Q process and is intended to support the overall PQS program.

Both the PQS and Engineering Quality Process need to emphasize quality (i.e., the product or service meeting the specified requirements) versus compliance (i.e., meeting current procedures or organization requirements). Good quality practices and methods through the PQS produce robust reliable systems and ensures compliance. The Engineering Quality Process needs to be established as part of the PQS; the Engineering Quality Process can then be used for developing sustainable, consistent, cost-effective, and practical engineering processes for project and operational lifecycle management.

11.1.1 Rationale

Current regulatory authority and ICH guidances emphasize a focus on building quality into the product and not by inspection. ICH Q10 augments the defined GMPs by describing specific quality system elements and management responsibilities that should be in place to achieve three main objectives; it states:

"Implementation of the Q10 model should result in achievement of three main objectives which complement or enhance regional GMP requirements:
  ▶ Achieve Product Realisation [Section 1.5.1]
  ▶ Establish and Maintain a State of Control [Section 1.5.2]
  ▶ Facilitate Continual Improvement [Section 1.5.3]"

The FDA 2006 Guidance for Industry: Quality Systems Approach to Pharmaceutical CGMP Regulations states the following as the goal of the guidance:

"This guidance describes a comprehensive quality systems model, which, if implemented, will allow manufacturers to support and sustain robust, modern quality systems that are consistent with CGMP regulations."

In alignment with ICH Q8, the FDA guidance states:

"The overarching philosophy articulated in both the CGMP regulations and in robust modern quality systems is: Quality should be built into the product, and testing alone cannot be relied on to ensure product quality."

Another useful resource in understanding Engineering Quality Process as a philosophy is the work of Dr. W. Edwards Deming. Deming believed in improving the process and in doing so using process measures to guide improvement efforts. In his book, Deming states:

"Conformance to specifications may be achieved in several ways:
1. By careful inspection, sorting the bad from the good. Dependence on inspection is hazardous and costly.
2. By work on the production process to shrink variation about the nominal value."

The approach presented in this Guide embraces regulatory guidance and Deming's principle that demonstration of conformance to specifications can be achieved by developing a process, which addresses causes and concerns for variability and reduces reliance on inspection. Thus, the focus of the Quality Unit should be to ensure adherence to a process that identifies CAs/CDEs and confirms that they have been satisfactorily tested.

Commentary and Key Learning Points

📚 Core Concept: Positioning of the Engineering Quality Process

The Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS) is the organization-level quality framework, covering all production, quality and engineering activities.

The Engineering Quality Process is a subset of the PQS, responsible specifically for quality practices within the C&Q process.

Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS)
↓
Engineering Quality Process
↓
C&Q procedures, tools, templates

Important distinction:

  • Quality = the product/service meets the Specified Requirements
  • Compliance = conformance with current procedures or organizational requirements (Procedures)

A process of good quality naturally brings compliance, but compliance alone does not necessarily mean quality. ISPE emphasizes pursuing the substance of quality, not merely formal compliance.

🌟 The Three ICH Q10 Objectives

  • ① Achieve Product Realisation: establish processes and systems that consistently produce products meeting specifications
  • ② Establish and maintain a State of Control: ensure systems continue to operate in a controlled state
  • ③ Facilitate Continual Improvement: continuously optimize through change management and CAPA mechanisms

These three objectives are fully consistent with the core mission of C&Q: confirming that a system achieves its intended purpose (Product Realisation), keeping it in a State of Control, and continually improving it throughout the lifecycle.

💡 The Deeper Meaning of Deming's Principle

Dr. W. Edwards Deming, the father of modern quality management, held as his core claim:

"Do not rely on screening to find defective products; improve the process so that defects are not produced."

The implication for C&Q is:

  • Old thinking: after the equipment goes online, run extensive IQ/OQ/PQ testing, and only find out whether there are problems once testing is finished
  • New thinking (ISPE Vol.5): build quality requirements into every step of design, procurement and installation, so that Critical Aspects (CA) are verified at the most appropriate point, instead of all being piled up at the final PQ

This is precisely the philosophical foundation of the Risk-Based C&Q promoted by ISPE Vol.5.

⚠️ Importance of the FDA 2006 Quality Systems Guidance

The FDA's 2006 "Quality Systems Approach to Pharmaceutical CGMP Regulations" established the six-system inspection model (detailed in Section 11.2) and stated explicitly that "quality should be built into the product, and testing alone cannot be relied on to assure product quality". This is the core philosophy of modern GMP and the foundation of the entire ISPE Vol.5 methodology.

11.3 Deliverables — Document Hierarchy

🌎 Original Text — English

The main deliverable from a quality system is to ensure that systems are operating as intended as captured in documentation. A document hierarchy within the Engineering Quality Process helps to identify where the requirements/expectations are derived. Figure 11.1 illustrates a typical document hierarchy for a quality system.

Figure 11.1: Typical Document Hierarchy for Quality System

On the top of the hierarchy are policies/directives, which are the high-level documents that capture the expectations of the organization. The expectations for C&Q may be that the systems used to manufacture products have been qualified to be suitable for the intended purpose. These expectations may be part of an overall validation policy or detailed in an independent policy.

The next level is standards, which identify the minimum deliverables that are required for implementation of the program. The intent is to avoid different parts of the organization interpreting the intent of the policies/directives differently. For C&Q, the deliverable may be a Qualification Summary Report. Standards should:

  • Reference any specific relevant regulatory authority guidance
  • Be used to implement a harmonized approach throughout the organization — this should be an organization-wide model with a process for sites to learn from each other and practice a wider continual improvement path
  • Limit special conditions — the standard should not make allowances for individual site preferences or commitments, but should include a process to grant exceptions after proper review and approval

The next level is procedures, which may establish steps to be followed for the implementation of standards. Procedures may be corporate or site-specific documents. Procedures may be used for multiple sites but should avoid the term "global" since not all sites in the organization may follow them. Examples of expected procedures are for:

  • URS
  • System Risk Assessment (identification of CAs/CDEs)
  • DR/DQ
  • Protocols/Plans
  • Engineering Change Management
  • Reports

The bottom level of the hierarchy is executable documents, which are the plans, test scripts, reports, etc. that document compliance to the established requirements.

In summary, the Engineering Quality Process should:

  • Define how oversight by the Quality Unit on the program or project will be accomplished
  • Define approvers of documentation — the Quality Unit does not need to approve every document
  • Establish the linkage to the QRM process for C&Q (System Risk Assessment as described in Chapter 4)
  • Address integrated C&Q efforts — e.g., integrate the C&Q process so that one set of testing and documentation can satisfy C&Q requirements
  • Address what can be done as part of start-up and how much shakedown is done before documentation starts
  • Identify the level of testing and expectations for documentation — this should be commensurate with the risk to product quality
  • Define the vendor selection process — based on the information available, decide on the level of oversight and use of vendors and their documentation
  • Provide a means for measuring progress and maintenance in the C&Q program — it should establish a metric control plan to measure progress
  • Ensure continual improvement — senior management should be responsible for the quality system governance through management review
Commentary and Key Learning Points

📋 Document Hierarchy Diagram

Level 1: Policies/Directives
↓ Define the expected goals
Level 2: Standards
↓ Define the minimum deliverable requirements
Level 3: Procedures / SOPs
↓ Define the execution steps
Level 4: Executable Documents

Executable documents = test plans, test scripts, execution reports, protocols, etc.

💡 Analogy: Building Codes vs. Construction Drawings

It is just like the construction industry:

  • Policy = building law (you must build a safe building)
  • Standard = structural design code (how thick a load-bearing wall must be at a minimum)
  • Procedure = construction work procedure (how to pour concrete)
  • Executable document = the specific construction drawings and inspection records

The higher the level, the more abstract; the lower, the more concrete. Each level takes its direction from the one above, and levels cannot be skipped or interpreted independently.

⚠️ Important Requirement for Standards: Avoid a Proliferation of Exceptions

Standards should "Limit Special Conditions"; individual sites should not make exceptions based on preference. If an exception is needed, there must be a formal review and approval mechanism.

This is especially important for large multi-site CDMOs: sites should not interpret validation standards on their own, but should adopt a single organizational standard and keep improving through a "site-to-site learning" mechanism.

⚠️ The QU Does Not Need to Approve All Documents

The Engineering Quality Process must clearly define "which documents require QU approval". The QU does not need to approve every document, as that would waste resources and create bottlenecks. Document approval requirements should match the risk level (see the approval matrix in Section 6.4).

💡 Cross-Functional Collaboration Is the Key to Success

📖 Original Text (English)

12.1 Introduction

Change management processes should be established and applied throughout the lifecycle of manufacturing systems. The risk-based application of SME-led change management is one of the biggest challenges in the implementation of QRM-based C&Q as it applies the principles of ICH Q9. This Guide presents an approach which involves two modes of change management:

  • Engineering Change Management (ECM)
  • Quality Change Control (QCC)

Project change management that covers the elements of project scope, cost, and schedule is outside the scope of this Guide.

A system is considered to be changed when it is modified, altered, added to, removed, or improved in a way that makes its functions, physical features, or performance different from what they were prior to the change.

Replacing an existing part of a system with an identical one (like-for-like) may not be considered as a change. Organizations should define and follow a robust assessment process for classifying changes as like-for-like.

12.1.1 Purpose and Benefits

Change management is a documented program that:

  • Defines what is subject to change
  • Determines the risk for product impact caused by the change
  • Establishes a process for initiation, review, and approval of the request for change
  • Defines when the Quality Unit should be involved
  • Establishes a process for evaluation of the actual impact of the change

Change management is needed for the following reasons:

  • Ensures control of the impact on the current state of the system
  • Provides a basis for peer (SME) review and approval
  • Is a regulatory requirement (e.g., 21 CFR 211.100 and EU GMP Volume 4 §5.23)
  • Is a good business practice

Change management processes should not be (or perceived to be) an obstruction to fostering innovation and continual improvement. Efficient use of change management adds value by delivering technically sound solutions in an efficient manner.

🔧 Core Concept: Two Change Management Models

ISPE Vol.5 divides change management into two distinct mechanisms, corresponding to different stages of the C&Q lifecycle:

  • ECM (Engineering Change Management): applies before the system has been accepted and released by QA. It is led by Engineering or Operations and focuses on controlling technical changes during design and construction.
  • QCC (Quality Change Control): applies after the system has been qualified and put into production. It is led by QA, and any change requires QA's prior approval before implementation.

🏗 Everyday Analogy: Building a House vs. Living in It

Imagine building a house:

  • Construction stage (ECM): engineers can decide to change wall positions or pipe routing, as long as the change is recorded on the construction drawings and the relevant people are notified.
  • After move-in (QCC): to knock down a wall or change the wiring, you must first apply to the homeowners' association (= QA), and work can begin only after approval; an inspection is also required once the work is finished.

The "move-in" moment in C&Q is QA Acceptance and Release.

⚠ Practical Point: Determining Like-for-Like

Like-for-like replacement may not constitute a "change", but this is not a free pass. The organization must establish a clear assessment procedure that covers:

  • Whether the part number, specification and manufacturer are exactly the same
  • Whether performance characteristics (e.g., flow, pressure) show no difference at all
  • If anything differs, however small, treat it as a change and start an assessment

One common FDA inspection deficiency: a company claims like-for-like but has no written assessment record.

🏭 CDMO Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, the boundary between ECM and QCC is especially important:

  • During construction of a new facility: use ECM, led by Engineering; changes related to CDEs must be notified to QA
  • Before and after PQ execution: even if the system is still in trial operation, once QA has formally released it, any design modification must go through QCC
  • During introduction of a new customer product: any modification to the parameters or hardware of existing qualified equipment falls under QCC

12.2 Engineering Change Management (ECM)

Pages 91–92

📖 Original Text (English)

ECM is a key component of the Engineering Quality Process. It provides the primary control mechanism and is fundamental in supporting the execution of regulatory/quality change control for CQAs. Appropriate SMEs, process owners, and the Quality Unit (where CAs/CDEs are impacted) should be involved in the evaluation, review, and approval.

There are three primary areas of application for ECM:

  • Provide lifecycle management for systems supporting both GMP and non-GMP regulated processes during operation
  • Provide the primary change management mechanism during a project phase including work intended to support C&Q (e.g., during DR/DQ, fabrication, FAT/SAT, and verification)
  • Manage and document technical changes for non-critical aspects of qualified systems

The review and approval method for the ECM system should enable effective and rapid change management that is sufficiently flexible to adapt to:

  • The stage of the project and related regulatory impact
  • The impact on manufacturing, maintenance, and system lifecycle

The level of documentation of the change should be commensurate with the level of risk related to the change.

12.2.1 Scope and Sequence of ECM

International GMPs require that changes that have the potential to impact product quality and patient safety be handled within the PQS (Pharmaceutical Quality System). This drives the need to establish a defined endpoint for application of ECM to GMP and non-GMP changes within the project lifecycle; from this point forward, full application of QCC to CAs/CDEs commences.

From a regulatory perspective, it is only after the acceptance and release of a system (for execution of PQ/Process Validation, if applicable, or for manufacturing for commercial use) that changes to systems with CAs/CDEs should go through QA-managed QCC.

During the operational phase of a system, ECM should continue to be applied to non-critical aspects of the system. Each change should be evaluated on whether CAs/CDEs are affected.

💬 Commentary

🧩 Terminology: The Three Application Areas of ECM

ECM does not exist only during construction; it applies in three situations:

  1. Lifecycle management during operation of GMP and non-GMP systems: long-term maintenance changes to facilities and equipment
  2. The main change mechanism during C&Q projects: applicable from DR/DQ through FAT/SAT
  3. Technical changes to non-critical aspects of qualified systems: for example, replacing a non-CDE component

📊 The Risk-Proportionate Principle

"Documentation should be proportionate to the risk of the change" is a consistent ISPE philosophy.

Low-risk changes (non-GMP pathway): a simple engineering record is sufficient

Medium-risk changes (GMP but not CDE): a complete ECM record is required, with notification of the relevant SMEs

High-risk changes (involving CDE/CA): ECM + QA approval, or go directly through QCC

This is consistent with the risk management principles of ICH Q9: the most burdensome procedure should not be applied to every change.

⚠ Common Confusion: ECM + QA Approval vs. QCC

Many practitioners confuse these two models:

  • ECM + QA approval: applies before release; QA approves the change within the engineering framework, and a formal change request with "prior approval" is not required
  • QCC: applies after release; change control within the formal pharmaceutical quality system (PQS), where QA prior approval must be obtained before implementation

The key differences are timing (before/after release) and who leads (Engineering/QA).

🏭 CDMO Decision Tree: ECM vs. QCC

When a change request arises, a CDMO engineer can decide as follows:

  1. Has the system been formally released by QA? → Yes → go through QCC
  2. Does the change affect a CA or CDE? → Yes → QA approval is required (ECM or QCC, depending on release status)
  3. Does the change involve only non-critical aspects and is it supported by a System Risk Assessment? → Yes → ECM is sufficient, no QA needed
  4. None of the above → assess jointly with QA

12.3 Change Management during C&Q Projects

Pages 92–93

📖 Original Text (English)

Before new systems are used for commercial or clinical operations, there is no risk to the patient associated with changes to design or function during the C&Q or project lifecycle. There is therefore no regulatory requirement for Quality Unit approval prior to implementation of a change.

There is, however, potential risk to the successful delivery of product and process user requirements and the risk control strategies determined as part of the approved System Risk Assessment. The Quality Unit should approve, prior to verification testing, any changes to the design or intended function or acceptance criteria associated with CAs/CDEs (including any new CAs/CDEs driven by changes in design).

In addition to maintaining the intended design after DR/DQ, ECM also plays a role in ensuring the integrity of data and evidence generated during C&Q. Fixes and repairs during C&Q have the potential to affect and invalidate testing and other evidence previously generated and compiled against the original configuration.

Test results from commissioning that do not meet acceptance criteria require:

  • Documented description of the incident
  • Description of corrective action(s) taken
  • Retest or re-verification of results following the correction
  • Assessment of the impact of the corrective action on any test results or other evidence already collected, and determination of any associated retesting requirements

A note should be made in the C&Q record and action taken to correct the item to meet the acceptance criteria. If the acceptance criteria cannot be met after corrective actions have been made, an SME should evaluate the issue and determine if it can be accepted as-is. A note should be made in the commissioning record to indicate why the item is acceptable as-is.

For more information on handling of discrepancies, refer to Section 7.4.

💬 Commentary

🚦 Key Principle: No QA Prior Approval Needed Before Release

ISPE states clearly: before a system is used for commercial or clinical production, changes to design or functionality pose no direct risk to patients, so regulations do not require QA to approve changes in advance of implementation.

This principle goes against many people's intuition. It does not mean changes can be made freely: ECM still requires all changes to be recorded, and changes involving CDEs must be approved by QA before verification testing.

⚠ Data Integrity Warning: The Obligation to Retest after Corrections

Fix and repair work during C&Q may invalidate test data that has already been completed. For example:

  • After an instrument calibration deviation is corrected, previous measurement results must be re-evaluated
  • After a pipe is re-welded, earlier pressure tests may need to be repeated
  • After software logic is modified, the related IQ/OQ test steps must be reconfirmed

Therefore, the ECM record must include "an assessment of the impact of this correction on existing test data", a step that is often overlooked.

🔧 The Correct Way to "Accept As-Is"

When a problem cannot be corrected (e.g., a design limitation), "accept as-is" is permitted, but it must be:

  1. Assessed for acceptability by a qualified SME
  2. Clearly justified in the commissioning record
  3. Evaluated for impact on CDE/CQA (if there is an impact, QA acknowledgment is required)

A blank acceptance rationale, or one that merely says "OK", is a non-compliant record.

12.4 Change Management during Operation

Page 93

📖 Original Text (English)

After acceptance and release and after the system is placed into production use, any changes to the qualified system has the potential to affect product quality and patient safety. GMP regulations usually require Quality Unit pre-approval (approval prior to non-emergency implementation) of changes with the potential to affect product quality. When using a risk-based approach, QA-managed QCC should be applied when the change impacts CAs/CDEs.

After acceptance and release, there are scenarios that allow for application of ECM without Quality Unit involvement. 💬 Commentary

🏭 Change Management Framework during Operation

Once the system is formally in production, change management enters its strictest stage:

  • Changes affecting CDE/CA: must go through QCC, and QA must approve in advance of implementation
  • Changes to non-critical aspects: may continue to use ECM, but must be supported by a System Risk Assessment
  • Emergencies: GMP generally allows emergency implementation, but the formal approval process must be completed afterwards

  • Any deviation from approved procedures must be investigated
  • Change control must assess the impact on the qualified state

This is the regulatory basis for QCC becoming mandatory after release.

  • Electronic change control systems (e.g., Veeva Vault QualityDocs, MasterControl)
  • The system should be able to provide a complete change record on demand
  • The audit trail function ensures that every change has a timestamp and a record of the approver

During an FDA inspection, the inspector may ask for the complete change history of specific equipment on the spot, so the system's search capability is critical.

12.5 Engineering Change Management Process

Pages 94–95

📖 Original Text (English)

ECM is defined as a process by which qualified representative(s) reviews proposed changes for its impact, approves or denies the requests, and manages and tracks their implementation. ECM is a good engineering practice to effectively manage project execution.

An ECM process may contain the following attributes:

  • Recorded name of the originator and date
  • Description of the change, including the affected system or area and intended purpose
  • Assessment of the potential impact of the change on:
    • Quality requirements
    • Project schedule
    • Project budget (relative to the latest approved budget)
    • System scope, design, or performance requirements (including safety, operability, reliability, construction, commissioning, operations, and maintenance)
    • Other systems

    • Qualification documents (including system classification)

  • Record of the approval or denial of the proposed change
  • Tracking through completion

ECM Application by Project Phase:

Project Conceptual Phase: There needs to be a clear definition of which documents are controlled by QA and where QA is only involved on request. Key product and process knowledge documents (such as the URS, pilot scale process reports, System Risk Assessment, and VMP) provide the basis for basic design and the requirements for CQAs and CPPs. Changes to these key documents should be addressed using ECM with Quality Unit approval.

Design Phase: Change management should be applied in alignment with the principles of GEP, with approval by SMEs. The mechanics of proposing, approving, implementing, and tracking changes should be simple since the design phase is very fluid. At the completion of the design phase, DR/DQ is completed. At this point, ECM is applied to GEP changes, and ECM with Quality Unit approval is applied to changes in CAs/CDEs.

Construction, C&Q Phase: For large projects, the site and project are primarily under the control of a contract organization. Changes should be implemented using the contract organization's change management policy with input by the owner. During construction, many changes are driven by the reality of the installed environment versus the design intent. Typical changes include:

  • Field inspections and testing performance — Controlled by ECM; may require retesting post-change
  • Departures from specification:
    • Readily corrected (e.g., pipe labels): Correct, reinspect, and document
    • Corrected later (or impacting other inspections/tests): Use discrepancy/punch list or change management
    • Cannot correct: Needs evaluation by the technical reviewer
  • Deviations — Only occur during performance testing when a product/process user requirement is not met. Steps:
    • Documentation and evaluation by Quality and Manufacturing Units
    • Determination of an assignable cause
    • Corrective action: definition, approval, implementation, test, confirmation, and documentation

Correction of deviations should be addressed in the following preferred order:

  1. Design change
  2. Installation change
  3. Operational change

Non-correctable deviations may require an SOP or other procedural change (procedural control) to compensate for the shortcoming.

💬 Commentary

📋 Required Elements of an ECM Form

A complete ECM change request record should contain the following, which is also the checklist an auditor will use:

  • Initiator's name and date (Attributability)
  • Description of change: affected systems, areas, purpose
  • Multi-dimensional impact assessment: quality, schedule, budget, other systems, documentation
  • Record of stakeholder notification
  • Approval/rejection record
  • Completion follow-up: confirmation that the change was implemented as approved

⚠ Order of Priority for Deviation Correction: Design > Installation > Procedure

ISPE clearly specifies the order of priority for correcting deviations:

  1. Design change (highest priority): resolves the problem at its root by modifying the design or specification
  2. Installation change: modifies the physical installation, such as pipe or equipment location
  3. Operating procedure change (last resort): uses an SOP to compensate for shortcomings in design/installation

This order of priority reflects an important principle: relying on operators to follow special procedures to compensate for hardware defects carries higher risk than modifying the hardware itself.

13.1 Introduction

The goal of Good Documentation Practice (GDocP) is to create clear, concise documents that a knowledgeable peer may review without questions. Good Documentation Practice supports data integrity. Documents should be:

  • Attributable
  • Legible
  • Contemporaneous
  • Original
  • Accurate

These five attributes form the acronym ALCOA.

13.1.1 Scope and Purpose

This chapter applies to all C&Q documentation, including test documents and reports. In this chapter, GEP documents are considered to be the engineering records used to support commissioning, and GMP records are considered to be the documentation supporting qualification.

13.1.2 Requirements for Vendor Documentation to Support Qualification

When the decision is made to use vendor documentation to support system qualification of CAs/CDEs, the justification should be documented and approved by SMEs and the Quality Unit. Considerations should include:

  • Vendor assessment
  • Acceptable vendor quality system
  • Vendor technical capability
  • Vendor application of GEP such that information obtained will be accurate and suitable for the purpose of qualification
💬 Commentary

📑 The ALCOA Principles Explained: The Cornerstone of Data Integrity

ALCOA is the core data integrity framework required by pharmaceutical regulators worldwide (FDA, EMA, WHO):

  • A – Attributable: clearly record who performed what action and when. Every record must carry identification (signature/initials + date).
  • L – Legible: records must be permanent and clearly readable. Illegible, faded or obscured records are non-compliant.
  • C – Contemporaneous: record immediately when the action is performed; backdating is not permitted.
  • O – Original: retain the first-hand record; if transcription is needed, the original data must be retained and identified.
  • A – Accurate: data must truthfully reflect the actual results and must not be fabricated or falsified.

⚠ The Extended Requirements of ALCOA+

Modern regulations (especially the FDA's 2018 data integrity guidance) add four "+" attributes to ALCOA:

  • Complete: no selective recording; all tests (including failures) must be recorded
  • Consistent: the recording method should be consistent across personnel and over time
  • Enduring: records must be kept in a durable form (e.g., no pencil, no erasable pens)
  • Available: can be provided immediately for review by the authorities during an audit

🧪 Key Differences between GEP and GMP Documents

An easy way to understand the comparison:

  • GEP (commissioning documents): like an engineer's working notes; trials can be repeated and the final result recorded; engineering judgment is sufficient
  • GMP (qualification documents): like evidence in court; must be executed according to plan, recorded step by step, and any deviation must be recorded and explained

This difference determines whether a commissioning report can be used as a qualification document: it must meet GMP documentation standards.

🏭 Risks of Using Vendor Documents for Qualification

Many CDMOs want to use the equipment vendor's FAT/SAT documents in place of their own IQ/OQ. ISPE allows this, provided that:

  • The vendor has been assessed (Vendor Qualification/Audit)
  • The vendor's QMS meets acceptable standards
  • The vendor's documents were executed in accordance with GDocP requirements
  • The rationale for use has been approved in writing by the SME and QA

If the quality of the vendor documents is inadequate, relying on them may undermine the credibility of the entire qualification package.

13.2 C&Q Document Development Basics

Pages 98–99

📖 Original Text (English)

Testing and verification documentation are usually written in the local language. Summary documents are generally in English to facilitate regulatory review.

The use of forms/templates is encouraged to promote consistency. Forms/templates can be approved once when created and then utilized multiple times without pre-approval if the relevant content is not changed. Where this approach is used, there should be a procedure to ensure that the form/template used for data entry is current at the time of use.

When forms/templates are used, the SME should review the form to ensure that it includes all of the required testing for the system being verified; if additional tests are required, the form/template should be supplemented with additional testing.

Test scripts may be approved individually and assembled into a test plan/report. If test data sheets are used to supplement test scripts, they should be approved in accordance with the requirements defined in the test plan.

The test procedures should be clear and unambiguous such that the results are repeatable and reproducible:

  • Repeatable: The same result would be achieved if the same procedure is repeated by the same trained and qualified personnel.
  • Reproducible: The same result would be achieved by different trained and qualified personnel following the same procedure.

The test document should indicate the order that the cases are to be executed. Acceptance criteria needs to be established and it should be clear that the tests demonstrate that the acceptance criteria have been met.

💬 Commentary

📝 Best Practices for Template Management

Using approved standard templates to execute tests is a smart way to improve efficiency. Key requirements:

  • A template is approved once and used many times, with no need for re-approval each time
  • However, it must be confirmed at the time of use that the template is the current version (i.e., document control is effective)
  • The SME must confirm that the template covers all necessary tests for the specific system; if not, it must be supplemented

🔄 Repeatability vs. Reproducibility

These two concepts originate from Measurement System Analysis (MSA):

  • Repeatability: the same person, same equipment and same conditions, repeating the test, obtain the same results. The test's "self-consistency".
  • Reproducibility: different people, locations and equipment, performing the same procedure, obtain the same results. The test's "consistency across personnel".

If a test procedure is written vaguely, different people may obtain different results, and the credibility of the qualification declines.

⚠ Language Choice and Regulatory Review

Execution documents may be written in the local language (e.g., Chinese, Japanese), but summary reports are recommended to be written in English, because:

  • During FDA/EMA inspections, inspectors can usually read only English
  • An English summary lets multinational regulators assess the work without translation
  • When a CDMO serves customers in multiple countries, an English summary reduces communication barriers

The typical requirements for recording a GEP document should include:

👥 Executor Qualifications and Signature Records

Executors must have appropriate education, training and experience. Key requirements:

  • Every executor must be registered in the Signature Log with printed full name, formal signature and initials
  • Self-review principle: no one may review or approve their own work; this is a basic GDocP principle and a safeguard against conflicts of interest

⏰ The Contemporaneous Principle: The Deficiency FDA Cites Most Often

Backdating is one of the most common GDocP deficiencies in FDA 483 observations and Warning Letters.

Compliance requirement: record immediately when a test step is executed, whether the result is good or bad.

Common violation scenarios:

  • Forgetting to record on the day of the test and filling it in the next day
  • Recording on a sticky note first and transcribing it into the formal document later
  • Filling in batch records all at once at the end of the day rather than at the time of the operation

The audit trail of an electronic record system can show when a record was made, making backdating easy to detect.

📄 Requirements for Transcribed Data

When data must be transcribed from an original record to another document:

  1. The transcriber must sign and date
  2. Include the date the original data was collected (not the transcription date)
  3. A second person must verify the accuracy of the transcription
  4. The original data must be retained, clearly marked, with initials and date

🖨 Special Handling of Thermal Paper Printouts

Instruments such as particle counters and balances often use thermal paper, which fades easily and violates the "Legible" and "Enduring" principles.

Correct practice: photocopy immediately; the executor signs the copy, marks it "True Copy" and dates it, and the original notes its storage location.

✏ 13.3.2 Summary of Core Requirements for Written Records

Ink requirements: permanent, non-fading, non-smearing; blue or black recommended; pencil prohibited (unless photocopied and filed immediately)

Date format: DD-MMM-YYYY (e.g., 30-MAY-2019) is best, to avoid ambiguity (01/02/03 could be 2 January or 1 February)

For quantitative results, do not write: Pass / Fail / OK / Yes / ✓; the actual measured value must be recorded

Handling blank fields: must be lined out and signed with initials + date, or marked N/A; they must not be left empty

Document identification: initials + date at least once on every page

📈 The Difference between N/A and N/AV

This detail is often overlooked but important:

  • N/A (Not Applicable): may be entered only before the document is approved for execution, indicating that the test item is simply not applicable to the system. Once the document has been approved and execution has begun, marking N/A afterwards usually requires an explanation.
  • N/AV (Not Available): indicates that the item could not be assessed at the time of execution, and usually requires a deviation record and explanation.

🏭 CDMO GDocP Training Priorities

The behavioral rules a CDMO should emphasize most in operator GDocP training:

  • A culture of "record as you execute"; "do first, record later" is not accepted
  • Record the actual observed values, not "opinions" (Pass/OK)
  • Blanks must be dealt with (N/A + initials + date)
  • Any change must be traceable (see Section 13.5)
  • Correction fluid (whiteout) must not be used

What matters is that the data date on the printout must be consistent with the time the test step was executed; this is what demonstrates contemporaneous recording.

13.5 Corrections

Pages 102–103

📖 Original Text (English)

Mistakes and errors happen. Do not erase, overwrite, or corrupt original recorded data. Correction fluid, correction tape, pencil, and erasable ink should be avoided. Data should not be altered, obliterated, erased, or overwritten.

Corrections are acceptable when:

  • Correcting test procedures or instructions that do not change the intent of the step
  • Correcting typographical errors
  • Correcting test/sampling instructions
  • Correcting test data with no impact on interpretation

Corrections can be handled in the following manner:

  • Mark a single line through the error
    • The cross-out should permit the original information to be read
    • Always cross out the full number/word rather than an individual digit/letter
  • Record the correct information near the error and initial and date when the correction was made
  • If the reason for the change is not obvious, add a unique alpha/numeric identifier to reference the change, and record the justification on the same page using the reference alpha/numeric identifier

Situations may arise where the person needed for making a correction is no longer available. Any corrections made by a different person than the one making the initial entry needs to be compliant with the document/protocol and/or site procedures.

💬 Commentary

✊ The Golden Rule of GDocP Corrections: Single-Line Strike-Through

This is the most basic and most frequently violated rule in GDocP:

23.5 24.2 [initials] [date]

Correct steps:

  1. Draw a single horizontal line through the error (the original data must remain legible)
  2. Write the correct value next to it
  3. Add your initials and the date of correction
  4. If the reason is not obvious, add a unique reference code and attach an explanation on the same page

Strictly prohibited: correction fluid (whiteout), correction tape, overwriting, erasers

🤔 Why Must the Original Data Remain Visible?

The requirement that "the original data remain legible" rests on regulatory logic:

  • If the original data cannot be seen, it is impossible to judge whether the correction is justified
  • If the error is completely obscured, it looks like data falsification
  • Auditors need to be able to trace the complete chain of "original record → reason for correction → final value"

Using correction fluid makes the original data invisible; even if the corrected value is right, it may be judged a data integrity violation.

🏭 CDMO Real-World Scenario: Correction by Another Person

When the original recorder has left the company or cannot be reached and someone else must make the correction, the following is required:

  • Confirm that this situation is permitted by the company/site SOP
  • The person making the correction must explain why they are correcting on the original recorder's behalf
  • Record the corrector's initials and date (not the original recorder's)
  • Supporting documentation or a deviation record is usually required

This situation is not uncommon in long-running C&Q projects, especially when temporary engineering staff or contractors are used.

📝 Exercise: Which of the Following Correction Methods Are Compliant?

  • Cover the error with white correction fluid and write the correct value over it → Non-compliant (original data cannot be read)
  • Strike through the complete incorrect number with a line, write the correct value beside it, and add initials and date → Compliant
  • Strike through only one digit of the incorrect number and keep the other digits → Non-compliant (the complete value should be struck through)
  • Strike out the incorrect value and add the corrected value, but with no date → Non-compliant (missing date, violating Attributable)
  • For a correction whose reason is not obvious, add a footnote explaining the reason → Compliant

✅ Responsibilities of Reviewers/Approvers

Approving a document is not just "stamping"; the reviewer must confirm four things:

This flexible design lets a CDMO's QA department focus its audit resources on the items that truly affect quality, improving efficiency.

13.7 Storage, Retrieval, and Retention of Documents

Methods of document storage should be established, as dictated by organization/site policies and procedures and in a manner that allows documents to be located and retrieved within a reasonable time period, as required. 💬 Commentary

📂 Three Requirements for Document Storage

  1. Timely retrieval: regulations require that documents can be found and provided within a reasonable time; in an inspection this usually means "immediately" or "within a few hours"
  2. Access control: the document room (or electronic system) must have access management to prevent unauthorized access or modification
  3. Secure storage: a storage environment protected against moisture, fire and damage

⚠ Regulatory Requirements for Electronic Archiving

Scanning paper documents and archiving them electronically is permitted, but the following must be met:

  • The electronic document management system (EDMS) must be a validated system
  • Paper originals may be destroyed after scanning, but a written procedure must govern the process
  • The specific retention periods under the applicable regulations must be identified and followed (they differ by country)
  • US FDA: GMP records generally must be retained for at least 2 years (after the expiry date of the drug product), or longer
  • EU EMA / PIC/S: similar requirements, but specifics depend on product type

📦 21 CFR Part 11 and Electronic Records

If an electronic system is used to store and manage qualification documents, the US regulation 21 CFR Part 11 (electronic records and electronic signatures) applies and requires:

  • Audit trail: automatically records who created, modified or deleted a record, and when
  • Electronic signatures: must be uniquely linked to the signatory and non-repudiable
  • System validation: the EDMS itself must undergo CSV (computerized system validation) under a framework such as GAMP 5
  • Data protection: prevent unauthorized modification; the integrity of electronic records must be protected

The EU equivalent is EU Annex 11 (Computerised Systems).

🚨 Disaster Recovery Procedure

The procedure to follow when documents are damaged (e.g., fire, flood, computer crash):

  1. First try to recover the original documents (e.g., from a backup system or other copies)
  2. If recovery is not possible, a copy of the original document may be accepted as the official record
  3. The copy must be approved by Management, confirming it as a valid replacement
  4. Create an event record explaining the cause and circumstances of the loss of the original document

Prevention is better than cure: a document storage strategy should be planned during C&Q execution to minimize the risk of loss.

🏭 Practical Recommendations for CDMO Document Retention

  • Multiple backups: for important documents (such as the qualification package), keep the original + a digital scan (EDMS) + an off-site backup
  • Retention period management: establish a document destruction schedule to ensure GMP records are not destroyed before the regulatory retention period ends
  • Management of customer deliverables: for qualification work a CDMO performs for customers, ownership of documents and responsibility for retention must be clearly agreed in the contract
  • Inspection readiness: ensure that any specified historical qualification document can be provided within 30 minutes during an inspection; this is a practical measure of the efficiency of the documentation system

Chapter 12: The Two Pillars of Change Management

  • ECM (Engineering Change Management) applies to C&Q and construction before system release, led by Engineering/Operations; CDE-related changes require QA approval but not prior approval; non-critical aspects may continue to use ECM after release.
  • QCC (Quality Change Control) applies to all CDE/CA-related changes after system release, led by QA; all changes must obtain QA prior approval before implementation; the regulatory basis is 21 CFR 211.100 and EU GMP §5.23.

  • ALCOA+ (Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate + Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available) is the cornerstone of documentation management and maps directly to FDA data integrity requirements.
  • Contemporaneous recording is one of the most frequently violated principles; backdating is the deficiency most often cited in FDA 483s.
  • Correction rules: single-line strike-through (original data remains legible) + correct value + initials + date; correction fluid, obliteration and overwriting are strictly prohibited.

14.1 Introduction

This chapter describes strategies how organizations can effectively transition to the science and risk-based C&Q process described in this Guide. Implementing change successfully often requires high-level sponsorship due to the numerous stakeholders impacted.

The key to a successful transition is to recognize that there are multiple ways that the regulatory expectations can be met; adopting the science and risk-based approach does not invalidate current work or existing documentation. It should be noted that any process used for C&Q is expected to be science and risk-based.

Qualification documents represent a picture of the system at a point in time; this information changes with time and there are quality systems in place to manage this change (including associated revision of the qualification and engineering record documents), such as change management, maintenance, calibration, and periodic review. Therefore, existing documentation can remain acceptable to provide the documented evidence that the system is suitable for the intended purpose.

Guided Reading and Analysis

Core Principle: No Need to Repeat Qualification

This is the most important sentence in Chapter 14, and the question many companies worry about most: "If we adopt the new ISPE approach, do all the IQ/OQ/PQ work we did in the past have to be redone?"

The answer is no. ISPE states clearly that adopting a science- and risk-based approach does not invalidate existing qualification documents. Qualification documents are a "snapshot of the system at a point in time"; as long as they are supported by the existing quality systems (change management, maintenance, calibration, periodic review), they remain valid evidence of the system's suitability.

Key Point: The Importance of Senior Sponsorship

ISPE notes that a successful transition "often requires senior sponsorship" because many stakeholders are involved. This is not only a technical issue but an organizational change management challenge:

  • R&D: must provide scientific and process knowledge
  • Quality: must accept different documentation deliverables
  • Engineering: must deliver documentation to GDocP standards
  • System owners: must be trained to carry out the required reviews

Analogy: Qualification Documents Are Like a Medical Check-up Report

Think of qualification documents as a medical check-up report: last year's report does not become "invalid" just because new check-up standards exist this year. The old report still records your health at that time. As long as health management has continued in the meantime (maintenance, calibration, periodic review), the system can be considered to remain in a state of control.

The new approach is like a better check-up questionnaire: it helps you identify the key indicators (CDEs) more precisely and verify them, but it does not require you to throw out and redo all the old reports.

14.2 Preparing for Implementation

The suggested steps for implementation preparation are described in more detail as follows:

  1. Define the organization approach:
    This Guide describes a suggested approach for organizations to adopt, but it may need to be tailored to fit the specific structure of the organization.
  2. Evaluate the organizational maturity:
    It is useful to perform a documented assessment of organizational maturity, considering the quality systems and GEP, to determine the degree and pace of organizational transition. The assessment should consider all relevant aspects (e.g., experience, personnel resources, organizational culture). Examples of criteria for relevant departments include:
    • Research and Development/Process Development: Does this department have robust documentation and clear communication channels to provide data to enable the process?
    • Quality: Is the Quality Unit open to an alternative approach to C&Q and to the different documentation deliverables?
    • Engineering: Is the engineering department ready to deliver documents per Good Documentation Practice? Do they have adequate change management systems in place? Is there adequate document storage?
    • System Owners: Are the system owners adequately trained to perform the necessary reviews?
  3. Develop an implementation plan:
    A critical requirement for successful implementation is to have a well-defined implementation strategy with support from senior management. The use of initial small-scale proof of concept projects can help to identify any program aspects that require augmentation for a specific organization.
  4. Develop supporting quality systems and associated procedures:
    The procedures defining the engineering, quality, and documentation standards, as covered in this Guide, should be developed.
  5. Train staff on the approach:
    An important aspect for implementation is to provide overview training and ensure there is a good understanding of the key concepts. Considerations should also be made for how to present the approach to regulatory inspectors.
  6. Train staff on the procedures:
    The relevant staff should be sufficiently trained on the new procedures and have a good understanding of the overall program and its goals.

Guided Reading and Analysis

The Six-Step Preparation Framework

ISPE proposes six implementation preparation steps, which are essentially a framework for assessing organizational maturity and developing a plan:

  • Step 1: Define the organizational approach (tailor the Guide)
  • Step 2: Assess organizational maturity (from the perspective of four functions)
  • Step 3: Develop the implementation plan (including a pilot project)
  • Step 4: Establish supporting quality systems and SOPs
  • Step 5: Train staff on the methodology
  • Step 6: Train staff on the specific procedures

Four Dimensions of the Organizational Maturity Assessment

The Step 2 maturity assessment must look at four functions at the same time; a weakness in any one of them will affect the whole implementation:

  • R&D / process development: Is scientific knowledge adequately documented? Do the channels for passing on information work smoothly?
  • Quality: Is it willing to accept "different documentation deliverables"? This requires a change of mindset in the quality function.
  • Engineering: Does it have sufficient GDocP documentation capability? Is the change management system sound?
  • System owners: Are they adequately trained to carry out reviews?

The assessment result determines the "extent and pace" of the transition. This is a very practical consideration, allowing organizations at different maturity levels to move forward at different speeds.

The Strategic Value of a Pilot Project

Step 3 places particular emphasis on an "initial small-scale proof-of-concept pilot project". This is like an MVP (minimum viable product) in software development:

  • Trial run first on a small, low-risk system
  • Identify process obstacles specific to your organization
  • Obtain real results as evidence to persuade senior management
  • Develop internal "early adopters" who become advocates of the methodology

We recommend choosing newly purchased equipment or the next small qualification project as the pilot, rather than starting with the most complex critical system.

14.3 Approaches for Implementation

For new projects or facilities, the use of a champion for the new approach is recommended. This individual helps with the transition and implementation of the new approach and should have a good understanding of the process concepts/goals.

While implementation is more straightforward for new systems (for which there are no existing procedures/documentation), organizations also need to determine how the approach can be applied to a change on an existing qualified system. The most cost-effective way for implementation of the risk-based approach is to supplement the legacy approach as gaps are identified or changes to the system are made, thus maximizing the use of existing documentation.

A best practice approach would be to first assess if the system requires qualification — does the system have the potential to impact product quality? (see Chapter 3 for system classification criteria)

  • If it does not have the potential to impact product quality, then recategorize the system and commission the changes.
  • If it has the potential to impact product quality, then it requires qualification:
    • Review the existing documentation to determine if it meets current expectations.
    • If it does not, then qualify the system using the new approach, and remediate any gaps identified.

Guided Reading and Analysis

Three Implementation Scenarios

ISPE distinguishes three scenarios, each with a different implementation path:

  • New system: The most straightforward; there is no legacy documentation, so the new approach is used in full from the start.
  • Existing system + minor change: The most common; build on the existing documentation and add the new-approach requirements only for the changed portion.
  • Existing system + major change: Requires a more comprehensive review of the existing qualification documents to identify gaps and supplement the qualification.

Core strategy: make maximum use of existing documentation, and add to it only when gaps are found or the system changes.

The Importance of the "Champion" Role

ISPE specifically mentions the role of the "champion" (an advocate of the methodology). This person is critical in an organizational transition:

  • Has a deep understanding of the concepts and objectives of ISPE C&Q
  • Helps resolve cross-functional resistance and misunderstandings
  • Acts as technical lead for the pilot project
  • Builds the organization's internal capability

This role is usually filled by a senior engineer or QA professional, ideally someone who has received ISPE training or has relevant practical experience.

Decision Tree for Legacy Systems

For legacy systems, ISPE provides a clear decision logic:

  1. Step one: Determine whether the system has the potential to impact product quality.
  2. If no: Reclassify (possibly down to Indirect/No Impact) and handle it through Commissioning; qualification is not required.
  3. If yes: Qualification is needed; review whether the existing documentation meets current requirements.
  4. Documentation is adequate: The existing documentation remains valid and is maintained through the existing quality systems.
  5. Documentation is not adequate: Qualify using the new approach and remediate the identified gaps.

This logic avoids a "one-size-fits-all" requalification while still ensuring regulatory compliance.

The Role of Appendix 1: A Regulatory "Cross-Reference Table"

Appendix 1 lists, chapter by chapter, the references that map the ISPE C&Q approach to the major regulations. Its core message is: the ISPE C&Q approach is not a more lenient substitute, but an expression of regulatory expectations.

The strategic value of this cross-reference is that when an inspector questions the compliance of the new approach, you can cite the original regulatory text directly as your basis.

Interpreting the Key Regulatory Messages

Several quotations in Appendix 1 deserve particular attention:

  • "Commensurate with the level of risk" (ICH Q9): The effort, formality and documentation of quality risk management should be commensurate with the level of risk. This directly supports ISPE's principle of proportionality: low-risk systems do not need to be over-qualified.
  • "A continuum, not binary" (FDA 2011): FDA states explicitly that criticality is a "continuum" rather than an "either/or binary state". This gives regulatory support to the risk grading of CDEs.
  • "Senior management support" (FDA 2011): FDA states directly in its guidance that "project plans and the full support of senior management are key elements for success".

Analogy: Regulations Are the Map, ISPE Is the Navigation

Think of the major regulations (FDA, EU GMP Annex 15, ICH) as a "map": they describe the destination you need to reach (ensuring quality, managing risk, lifecycle management) but do not explain in detail how to drive there.

The ISPE C&Q Guide is like a "navigation system": based on the map, it plans a specific, efficient route. Appendix 1 confirms that this route does lead to the destination the regulations require, with no shortcuts and no detours.

15.14 Chapter 14: Implementation of Science and Risk-Based C&Q Process

Regulatory guidance highlights the importance of product quality risk management and risk assessment to determine the scope and extent of qualification as described in the following regulatory text:

"A quality risk management approach should be applied throughout the lifecycle of a medicinal product. As part of a quality risk management system, decisions on the scope and extent of qualification and validation should be based on a justified and documented risk assessment of the facilities, equipment, utilities and processes." [EU GMP Annex 15]

The entire framework of Chapter 14's implementation strategy is underpinned by this single principle: the scope and extent of C&Q activities should be proportionate to risk, determined by a justified and documented risk assessment. This applies equally to the decision of how to implement the new approach — whether for new systems, existing systems with minor changes, or existing systems requiring major remediation.

Guided Reading and Analysis

Regulatory Authorization: Risk Determines the Scope and Extent of C&Q

The regulatory basis in Chapter 14 is only one core quotation, but it effectively authorizes the central logic of the entire ISPE methodology:

"The scope and extent of validation should be based on a justified and documented risk assessment."

This means:

  • Not every system needs a full IQ/OQ/PQ
  • Not every test needs the same level of rigor
  • This is not a relaxation of standards; it is a more accurate focusing of resources on what really matters

The Core Argument for Inspection Readiness

If, during an FDA or EMA inspection, you are asked "why does your qualification documentation differ from the traditional IQ/OQ/PQ format?", the right response is:

  1. We follow the requirements of EU GMP Annex 15 (or the FDA Process Validation Guidance)
  2. The regulations explicitly require that the scope and extent of validation be determined by a documented risk assessment
  3. Our approach (ISPE C&Q) is precisely an expression of that regulatory expectation
  4. Our SRA (system risk assessment) is the "justified and documented risk assessment"

This argument grounds the legitimacy of the methodology in the regulatory text itself, rather than relying solely on the authority of the ISPE Guide.

CDMO Implementation Roadmap: A 12-Month Plan from Zero to Full Adoption

We recommend that a mid-sized CDMO advance the ISPE 2nd Ed. implementation on the following timeline:

  • Months 1-2: Organizational preparation: appoint a Champion, complete the organizational maturity assessment (App. 14), brief senior management
