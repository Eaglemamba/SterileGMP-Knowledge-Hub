Preface

Purpose and legal context of the GAMP IT Infrastructure Guide

Original Text (English)

This document, the GAMP® Good Practice Guide: IT Infrastructure Control and Compliance, is intended as a supplement to the Guide for Validation of Automated Systems (GAMP® 4). It provides an approach to meeting current regulatory expectations for compliant IT Infrastructure platforms, including the need to identify, qualify, and control those aspects impacted by GxP.

This document has been designed so that it may be used in conjunction with guidance provided in GAMP® 4 and other ISPE publications, such as the ISPE Baseline® Guides.

Disclaimer

This Guide is meant to assist pharmaceutical companies in managing the validation of IT Infrastructure platforms. The GAMP Forum cannot ensure and does not warrant that a system managed in accordance with this Guide will be acceptable to regulatory authorities. This Guide does not replace the need for hiring professional engineers or technicians.

CDMO Practical Interpretation

What is this guide?

The GAMP IT Infrastructure Guide is a supplement to GAMP 4. It focuses on the underlying IT environment that supports GxP applications, including servers, networks, operating systems and database platforms.

In a pharmaceutical CDMO, IT infrastructure is the foundation of every digital manufacturing system. MES has a problem? Check the OS patches first. LIMS lost data? Check whether the backup strategy is compliant. This guide is the standard for controlling that "foundation layer".

Building analogy

Applications (MES, LIMS, QMS) are the building; IT infrastructure is the foundation and the reinforcing steel. Even if the building itself is perfectly designed, an unstable foundation puts the whole structure at risk of collapse. This guide ensures that the "foundation" itself meets GxP requirements.

1.1

Overview

Why IT Infrastructure control matters for GxP compliance

Original Text (English)

GxP regulated companies have an ever increasing dependency on computerized systems when conducting their day-to-day businesses.

The validated status of GxP applications that are dependent upon an underlying IT Infrastructure is compromised if that IT Infrastructure is not maintained in a demonstrable state of control and regulatory compliance.

The consequences of the IT Infrastructure being out of effective control can be significant. Depending on the nature of a failure, an entire site or geographic region of operations could be brought to a standstill while the problem is resolved.

The infrastructure should be brought into initial conformance with the company's established standards through a planned qualification process building upon acknowledged good IT practices. Once in conformance, this state should be maintained by documented standard processes and quality assurance activities, the effectiveness of which should be periodically verified.

Key aspects to consider include:

  • Installation and operational qualification of infrastructure components
  • Configuration management and change control in a highly dynamic environment
  • Management of risks to IT Infrastructure
  • Involvement of service providers in critical infrastructure processes
  • Security management — access controls, availability of services and data integrity
  • Backup, restore, and disaster recovery
  • Archiving
"IT Infrastructure" and "infrastructure" are used synonymously to indicate an aggregation of platforms and services including their associated processes, procedures, and personnel. — GAMP IT Infrastructure Guide, Footnote 1
CDMO Practical Interpretation

Key warning: cascading failure risk

The guide states clearly that if IT infrastructure is not under control, operations across an entire site or region may come to a halt. In a CDMO environment, this means:

  • MES down → batch records cannot be created → production stops
  • LIMS inaccessible → release testing cannot be completed → batch is held up
  • DCS/SCADA failure → process monitoring is interrupted → FDA 21 CFR Part 11 data integrity concerns

Scope of CDMO IT infrastructure

  • Server infrastructure: host servers for MES, LIMS and QMS
  • Network: the site internal network and isolated GxP network segments
  • OS/Database: Windows Server, Oracle DB, etc.
  • Virtualization: VMware environment (if used)
  • Storage/Backup: long-term retention systems for batch records

Key definition: IT Infrastructure

IT Infrastructure = the collection of platforms + services + the related processes, procedures and personnel.

This definition is important: IT infrastructure is not just hardware. It also includes the processes that operate around it and the people responsible for it.

Purpose

What this Guide aims to achieve

Original Text (English)

This Guide provides comprehensive guidance on meeting current regulatory expectations for compliant IT Infrastructure platforms, including the need to identify, qualify, and control those aspects impacted by GxP.

This Guide intends to satisfy the growing need for guidance on key IT Infrastructure subjects in relation to current international GxP regulations, and to align terminology and language with other GAMP® Good Practice Guides.

The Guide is intended primarily for regulated life science industries, including pharmaceutical, biological, and medical devices, but also provides valuable information for suppliers of systems, products, or services.

This Guide includes the description of a scaleable qualification framework which has been derived from key principles and practices. It describes how this framework can be applied to different platform types in order to determine the extent and scope of qualification efforts.

In addition, this Guide provides an overview of current best practices for the design, qualification, and operation of an IT Infrastructure with emphasis on the qualification requirements of the major components.

CDMO Practical Interpretation

Three core actions: Identify → Qualify → Control

These three verbs define the whole lifecycle of IT infrastructure compliance management:

  • Identify: Which IT components support GxP applications? Which are within GxP scope?
  • Qualify: Perform IQ/OQ to confirm that the components meet their specifications
  • Control: Ongoing change management, configuration management and periodic review

Scaleable Qualification Framework

The framework can be scaled according to the following factors:

  • The GxP impact of the platform (risk-driven)
  • Company size (small CDMO vs multinational manufacturer)
  • Platform type (stand-alone server vs virtualized environment vs cloud)

This means the framework is not one-size-fits-all: you can adjust the depth and breadth of qualification based on risk assessment.

Benefits

Advantages of the horizontal platform-based approach

Original Text (English)

This Guide applies a structured approach, including Risk Management, to the qualification, management, and control of IT Infrastructure platforms supporting GxP applications.

A vertical, or system based, approach to qualification and audit of IT Infrastructure would be inefficient and impractical, as overlapping and often identical platform elements would be dealt with repeatedly. To avoid unnecessary effort, this Guide describes a horizontal, or platform based, approach.

Benefits of a horizontal approach include:

  • Higher level of standardization throughout the entire life cycle
  • Minimal overlap in documentation
  • Minimal overlap in qualification
  • Minimal overlap in audits, inspections, and assessments

The approach also builds upon:

  • The relatively low residual risk to GxP applications attributable to IT Infrastructure platforms (in contrast to application software designed to create and manipulate data)
  • Industry standard components with built-in error detection and self-correction features
  • Current good IT practices and international standards for reliable network performance
  • Efficient, automatic, standardized IT monitoring and management software tools
  • Many similar platform components used in similar configurations across a company
CDMO Practical Interpretation

Horizontal vs vertical approach: the supermarket checkout analogy

Vertical approach (inefficient): Each checkout is audited separately, including the POS system's server, network and OS. But one server may serve 20 checkouts, so the same work is repeated 20 times.

Horizontal approach (efficient): First qualify the "shared checkout platform" once. Validation of each checkout application then only needs to confirm that "the platform is already compliant".

In a CDMO, if one server supports both MES and LIMS, the horizontal approach means the server only needs to be qualified once.

Why is the residual risk of platforms lower?

Platforms (OS, database) are designed to protect data integrity, whereas applications are designed to create and operate on data. Applications are more likely to introduce business logic errors that lead to data problems; the risk of a platform is usually availability rather than data correctness.

Horizontal approach in CDMO practice

  • Standard server build: Create a standardized server configuration package that can be replicated and deployed after being qualified once
  • Platform qualification package: One platform qualification document shared by multiple applications
  • One audit for the platform: The auditor only needs to review the platform documentation once, rather than checking the underlying infrastructure of every application

Discussion Questions

  1. If your site has five GxP applications running on the same virtualized server, how much qualification effort would the horizontal approach save?
  2. How does the characteristic that "standardized components have self-error-detection capability" support a lighter qualification strategy?
  3. In what situations might the horizontal approach not be applicable (for example, special configurations or highly customized environments)?

The information security standard ISO17799 (now evolved into ISO 27001) overlaps heavily with GxP IT security requirements, especially in access control, audit logs, incident management and backup procedures. Many CDMOs pursue ISO 27001 certification and GxP compliance at the same time, and the two can share a large number of controls.

Key Concepts

Horizontal approach, key terminology, qualification vs validation

Original Text (English)

1.7.1 Horizontal Platform Based Approach

The IT Infrastructure exists to support the primary business, by providing:

  • Platforms to run business applications (e.g., CDMS, IVRS, LIMS, or ERP)
  • IT Infrastructure processes that facilitate a capable and controlled IT environment
  • General IT services (e.g., email, office tools, intranet, file storage)

Where platform components support multiple applications, these components should be qualified separately from the applications to avoid unnecessary duplication of activities and effort.

Separate qualification of the IT Infrastructure means that adding or changing a business application would require only the validation of the application. Similarly, changing components in the IT Infrastructure may not require further validation of the business application; however, the risk to GxP applications of each controlled change should be assessed.

Standard, re-usable, qualified platform components are referred to as building blocks throughout this Guide.

1.7.2 Key Terminology

Requirements: Often specified in documents such as Service Level Agreements (SLAs). For infrastructures supporting real-time applications (such as process control), requirement specifications may be more specific.

Commissioning: A well planned, documented, managed engineering approach to start-up and handover to end-user, resulting in a safe and functional environment meeting established design requirements. In the context of IT Infrastructures, useful for aspects that do not support GxP activities directly.

Qualification: A process of demonstrating the ability of an entity to fulfill specified requirements. Demonstrates the ability of servers, clients, and peripherals to fulfill specified requirements. Basic requirements include: QA involvement, formally verified design solutions, test results compliant with GxP documentation requirements, traceability of actions.

Validation: In the context of an IT Infrastructure, validation applies to the GxP applications that run on the infrastructure, rather than the infrastructure platforms themselves. The focus for platforms is on qualification of components.

The "Building Blocks" concept

Standardized, reusable, qualified platform components are called "building blocks".

Practical example: If you have built and qualified a standard Windows Server configuration, the next time you deploy a server with the same configuration you only need to confirm that the configuration is identical. You do not need to repeat the full IQ/OQ from scratch, which greatly reduces duplicated work.

Qualification vs Validation: the key difference

Qualification applies to IT infrastructure platforms (servers, networks, OS, databases).

Validation applies to GxP applications (MES, LIMS) that run on the infrastructure.

When an auditor asks whether your LIMS server has been "validated", the correct answer is: "The server has been qualified, and the LIMS application has been validated."

Criteria for GxP impact

Whether an IT system is within GxP scope depends on whether it directly affects any of the following:

Interpreting the regulatory comparison

Core differences between the two regulations

  • 21 CFR Part 11: A US regulation that focuses on technical controls for electronic records and electronic signatures (mainly IT functional requirements)
  • EU Annex 11: An EU GMP annex that covers the whole lifecycle of computerized systems and has more explicit requirements for IT infrastructure

After its 2011 revision, EU Annex 11 has clearer provisions on supplier management, cloud/outsourcing and infrastructure qualification, covering IT infrastructure more comprehensively than Part 11.

CDMO compliance strategy: satisfying both regulations

A CDMO serving both the US and EU markets must meet both sets of requirements. In practice:

  • Audit trail: System-level, time-stamped audit logs (satisfies Part 11 §11.10(e) and Annex 11 clause 9)
  • Backup & restore: Regular backups with verified restore capability (satisfies Annex 11 clause 7.2)
  • Access control: Role-based access control with the principle of least privilege (satisfies both)
  • Change control SOP: Covers IT infrastructure changes (satisfies Annex 11 clause 10)

The importance of EU Annex 11 clause 4.7

EU Annex 11 explicitly requires "IT infrastructure qualification". It is one of the few regulatory provisions in history to name IT infrastructure (rather than only applications) as needing qualification. This directly echoes the core position of this GAMP guide.

Applicable scenarios: 21 CFR Part 11 vs Annex 11

  • FDA PAI (pre-approval inspection): focus on Part 11 compliance
  • EMA GMP inspection: focus on Annex 11 compliance
  • PIC/S inspections (Japan PMDA, Australia TGA, etc.): usually follow the Annex 11 framework
  • Dual filing (US and EU): both sets of requirements must be met

CDMO Practical Interpretation

GAMP category quick reference (IT infrastructure edition)

IT infrastructure platforms mainly fall into HW Cat 1 and SW Cat 1-2; applications are SW Cat 3-5.

Why is most IT infrastructure Cat 1?

The assumption for Cat 1 (infrastructure software) is that this type of software is widely tested and deployed by major vendors (Microsoft, Cisco, VMware), the industry has accumulated extensive experience with it, and it has strong self-error-detection capability. Therefore, you only need to confirm that it is installed correctly and configured to specification; full business-function testing as for applications is not required.

Practical reminder: special handling of firmware

Firmware is usually regarded as part of the hardware. But when firmware can be updated independently (such as server BIOS/UEFI or network device firmware), it needs to be managed like software:

  • Include it in the change management process
  • Classify it as SW Category 2
  • Record versions and update history

In a GxP environment, firmware updates require an assessment of their potential impact on the GxP applications above them.

Integrating the ITIL framework with GxP

If your IT department has already implemented ITIL: Most of the process framework can be used directly for GxP compliance; you only need to add GxP requirements (such as stricter documentation and QA review) to the existing processes.

Special GxP challenges in Change Management

In a GxP environment, IT "change management" is more complex than in a general enterprise: every OS patch, network device firmware update and server configuration adjustment must be assessed for impact on validated applications. This requires close collaboration between IT and QA, not decisions made by IT alone.

Personnel

Roles, responsibilities, training, and qualifications

Original Text (English)

Management should define roles and responsibilities (job descriptions) in terms of tasks to be undertaken, and the qualifications and experience needed. Individual job descriptions should be assigned to named individuals fulfilling those roles, permanently or on an ad-hoc basis.

Key roles that may be identified include:

  • Executive Management (Project Sponsorship)
  • Project Manager
  • Application (system/data) owner / administrator / application-oriented SMEs
  • Data owners (if not coinciding with application owner)
  • IT Infrastructure process owner / administrators / SMEs (e.g., IT support engineers)
  • Platform owner / administrator / SMEs (e.g., network engineers)
  • Independent QA in relation to information technology
  • IT Quality and Compliance

Technical and managerial staff with appropriate educational background and experience should be available. Provision of training should be planned to ensure the required skills are developed and maintained.

Staff should be made aware of any regulatory requirements that apply to their duties, trained in those procedures that are applicable to them, and re-trained as changes occur. Records of training should be maintained.

Appendix 1 of this Guide provides more detailed guidance on the various roles.

CDMO Practical Interpretation

GxP training requirements for IT personnel

IT personnel usually have a technical background but lack awareness of GxP regulations. Necessary training topics include:

  • Regulatory awareness: Basic requirements of 21 CFR Part 11 and EU Annex 11
  • Process training: GxP change management SOP and deviation handling procedure
  • Documentation requirements: ALCOA+ principles (Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate, Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available)
  • Qualification: Writing and executing IQ/OQ documents

The importance of "independent QA"

The guide specifically emphasizes "independent QA in relation to information technology": QA review of IT activities must be independent, and the IT department cannot review its own qualification documents.

In a small CDMO, QA may not have an IT background. In this case, consider:

  • Hiring an IT Quality Compliance specialist with both IT and GxP backgrounds
  • Engaging an external consultant to provide independent review
  • Establishing a cross-review mechanism

Application Owner vs Data Owner: not necessarily the same person

The guide clearly distinguishes between two roles:

  • Application Owner: The person responsible for the LIMS system (software), usually the Lab Manager
  • Data Owner: The person responsible for a specific data set. For example, the VP of Quality Assurance may be the Owner of "batch release data" even though she does not own the LIMS system

In a GxP data integrity investigation, it is very important to clarify who the Data Owner is.

  • Horizontal (platform-based) approach avoids duplication — qualify the platform once, validate applications separately

  • Platform components are mostly GAMP HW Cat 1, SW Cat 1–2 (lower risk than applications)

Key takeaways

Five core messages of Chapters 1–2

  1. IT infrastructure is the foundation of GxP systems: If the foundation is out of control, the validation of the applications above it is invalidated.
  2. The horizontal approach is the core strategy: Qualify the platform once and multiple applications benefit, avoiding duplicated work.
  3. All three elements are indispensable: Even if the platform is qualified, compliance is still incomplete without trained personnel and documented processes.
  4. Risk-driven and scaleable: A small company does not need the same depth of qualification as a large multinational manufacturer, but the framework logic is the same.
  5. IT and QA must work together: Without independent QA oversight, GxP compliance of IT infrastructure is an empty shell.

3.5 Testing Requirements

Test documentation generated as a result of the execution of test specifications during qualification should meet the following general requirements:

  • Traceable to the specification that required the documentation
  • Reviewed and approved by required units or individuals, usually including QA
  • Clear and objectively verifiable acceptance criteria
  • Each test step or test case traceable to the pertinent section in the applicable requirement or design specifications
  • Accurate records of observations made by identifiable and trained personnel

  • Hardcopies of screen dumps, or collation by computerized tool, should be added to support test results where appropriate
  • Entries should be made in legible, permanent handwriting, or using a suitable computerized tool
  • Corrections should be made without obscuring the original entry — the date, reason, and identity of the person making the correction should be clear

Overview

This section describes how a platform, or a major addition to an existing platform, may be brought into compliance with the company's established standards using a planned qualification process. Minor additions are usually managed via change control.

This section describes how a planned qualification process is used to bring a platform, or a significant new addition to an existing platform, to a state that meets the company's established standards. Minor additions are normally managed through change control.

A Platform Project and Qualification Plan, describing the life cycle activities to be undertaken to qualify each platform type, should be created. The plan should cover:

  • The approved and effective SOPs required
  • Deliverables that will be the output of the qualification process
  • Responsibilities and approvals required

The qualification strategy is typically based on one of two scenarios:

Strategy 1: Building-Block (Generic Platform)

Platform specifications are independent of any specific applications. Developed from generic requirements, bound by company policies. Application specifications take existing, standardized platform capabilities into account. Qualification commences with little interaction with application owners — qualified platforms are considered commodities.

Applies to standardized IT environments where the platform specification is independent of any specific application and the qualified platform is treated as a commodity.

Strategy 2: Case-by-Case

Platform requirements are mainly derived from system (application) specifications on a case-by-case basis. Building-block concept not fully usable; significant interaction with application owners required; platform qualification is largely a one-off exercise.

Applies to highly customized or specialized application environments that require close collaboration with the application owner.

Qualification Process Flow & Building-Block Strategy

Sequential Qualification Steps

  1. Risk Assessment → determines scope and intensity of qualification, which components and configurations are required, rigor of supplier assessment
  2. Procurement → supplier evaluation based on risk outcome
  3. IQ (Installation Qualification) → evidence that all components installed and configured as specified; certification evidence may support IQ
  4. OQ (Operational Qualification) → evidence that critical features of the platform perform as specified
  5. Qualification Report → summarizes results, formally concludes the qualification process

The Building-Block Advantage

The preferred qualification strategy is to qualify types of building blocks, then run abbreviated qualifications of individual instances. Benefits:

  • Reduces qualification effort for each new server or workstation
  • Standardizes the IT environment — reduces variability and risk
  • Enables faster deployment of new GxP-supporting systems
  • Qualification work is reusable — ROI improves over time

Once a building-block type has been qualified, each instance can use a reduced qualification, which lowers effort and speeds up deployment.

GAMP 5 Parallel

Strategy 1 (Building Block) parallels GAMP 5's approach to Category 1 infrastructure — leveraging vendor documentation and standardized configurations. Strategy 2 parallels custom application validation where system-specific requirements drive the scope. A mature CDMO should aim for Strategy 1 wherever possible.

Topology Design Considerations

  • Segregation of GxP regulated networks from administrative networks
  • Protection and control of different GxP regulated networks
  • Need for built-in redundant networks

Servers, Operating Systems & Storage

Servers, Operating Systems and Storage Systems | Hardware Specifications and OS Parameters

Server Hardware Specification

When server hardware is installed, it should be based on supplier recommendations, including environmental considerations. Configuration information should be included in the platform specifications and checked for compliance during IQ.

Redundancy in server building blocks, power supplies, and storage should be considered based on Risk Assessment results.

Operating System Parameter Categories

Most operating systems allow a large number of parameter settings that affect server behavior. These fall into three categories:

System Time Management

In global IT infrastructure, provision for effective time synchronization traceable to an international standard is essential. Key considerations:

  • Traceability to international standard: UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) or TAI (International Atomic Time)
  • Availability of a reliable time source and preferred dissemination method
  • Management of summer/winter time offsets
  • Management of local time offsets

Time management specifications must be verified during both IQ and OQ. Responsibilities for setting/resetting system time must be documented.

Storage Systems — SAN vs. NAS

Storage specifications must be verified during IQ and OQ. OQ of storage is practical only via a connected server.

Pharma IT Commentary

Why System Time Is a GxP Critical Parameter

21 CFR Part 11 and EU Annex 11 require that electronic records include accurate, reliable date/time stamps. A server with incorrect time can invalidate batch records, audit trails, and electronic signatures. FDA inspectors regularly check NTP configuration and time synchronization logs.

21 CFR Part 11 and EU Annex 11 require electronic records to contain accurate and reliable date/time stamps. A server with the wrong time can invalidate batch records, audit trails and electronic signatures. FDA inspectors routinely check NTP settings and time synchronization records.

Configuration Items List (CIL)

For server IQ, a CIL must be prepared. Hardware CIL includes:

  • Make and model of server
  • Type and amount of memory (RAM)
  • Number of CPUs and disks
  • Disk controller type
  • Additional rebuild information

Software CIL includes:

  • Operating system (name + version)
  • Service programs / middleware
  • Service packs, hot fixes, security patches
  • Access control settings
  • Network settings (IP, hostname, DNS)

All CIL items must be identified by version numbers. CIL enables efficient duplication of verified servers.

A server's CIL is like the Drug Master File (DMF) for a pharmaceutical ingredient — it precisely documents everything about the "composition" of your server so it can be reproduced identically. Just as a DMF enables a generic drug manufacturer to reference original data, a CIL enables your team to rebuild or duplicate a server to the exact same validated configuration.

A server's CIL is like a Drug Master File (DMF) for a pharmaceutical raw material: it records everything the server is "made of" precisely enough that it can be rebuilt identically. The CIL lets your team rebuild or replicate a server to exactly the same validated configuration.

Peripheral Equipment

Peripherals (label printers, barcode scanners, electronic signature pads, cameras) are typically standardized COTS with embedded firmware. Application specifications should not deviate from default factory/corporate standard settings unless specifically required, justified, and documented.

Risk Assessment & Qualification Test Planning

Risk Assessment and Qualification Test Planning | GAMP-Based Approach to Qualification Scope

Risk Assessment Process

Risk assessment is typically an iterative process, performed during planning and specification as more information becomes available. As the specification and design is completed, it becomes possible to perform more detailed assessments to establish the appropriate level of qualification testing.

GAMP software and hardware categories should be taken into account during these assessments. The design review should provide:

  • Assurance that a chosen design will deliver required results with an acceptable level of risk
  • Knowledge of critical components or parameters needing special attention in maintenance procedures
  • Guidance on the appropriate level of supplier assessment required
  • Identification of critical aspects to be covered by the qualification process
  • Assessment of IT infrastructure processes and their effectiveness to reduce likelihood of undetected harm
  • Scope of required qualification testing: what to test, how much to test, test result documentation, acceptance criteria
  • Level of QA involvement required
  • Indication of training required
  • List of new or changed SOPs required to mitigate identified risks

Building Block Qualification Strategy

Once a building block (e.g., a standard Windows Server 2022 image) has been qualified, duplicates of this building block may be configured with the main focus being to verify that a duplicate has been produced correctly. This dramatically reduces the testing burden for identical subsequent deployments.

Risk Assessment Outputs

  • Critical component list — components requiring special IQ/OQ attention
  • Supplier assessment level — which suppliers need audit vs. questionnaire

  • Training requirements — staff who must be trained before operation
  • SOP changes required — new or updated procedures

The same principle applies in IT: risk assessment identifies the "CPPs" of your IT infrastructure (e.g., NTP sync, access controls, audit trail settings) that need the most rigorous qualification testing.

Procurement & Supplier Evaluation

Procurement and Supplier Evaluation | Approved / Conditionally Approved / Not Approved

Procurement Principles

Components and services should only be procured from suppliers who can demonstrate:

  • An acceptable quality level
  • Effective support, commensurate with the expected lifetime of the item
  • Support level appropriate to the risk associated with the failure of the item

Upon receipt, goods should be:

  • Checked for compliance with order specifications
  • Labeled as needed
  • Stored in a safe place to facilitate subsequent safe installation

Options before site installation:

  • Brief power-on tests — verify basic operation before transporting to target site
  • Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) — stage entire assemblies at supplier's premises; run formal FAT before transport

Supplier Classification

✅ Approved

Delivery history or gathered information is satisfactory. Note: this classification is not always given following the first audit since there are often issues to rectify. Depending on the issues, such a scenario should not necessarily be seen as a problem.

Delivery records or the information collected are satisfactory. Note: this classification is not necessarily given immediately after the first audit, because there are often issues to be corrected; depending on the circumstances, this is not necessarily a problem.

⚠ Conditionally Approved

Delivery history or gathered information is not fully satisfactory. Mitigation may include closer follow-up, more intensive tests, and witnessing than otherwise justified.

Delivery records or the information collected are not fully satisfactory. Mitigation may include closer follow-up, more intensive testing and witnessing.

❌ Not Approved

Information or delivery history is inadequate or unsatisfactory. Procurement from this supplier is not permitted for GxP-relevant items until issues are resolved.

Information or delivery records are insufficient or unsatisfactory. Procurement of GxP-relevant items from this supplier is not permitted until the issues are resolved.

On-site supplier assessments performed or overseen by QA should be considered when deliveries support GxP regulated activities.

Supplier Assessment in Pharma IT Practice

COTS vs. Custom Procurement Strategy

COTS (Commercial Off-The-Shelf): Preferred for standard infrastructure components. Lower supplier assessment burden because vendor certification, testing evidence, and market history are available. Examples: Windows Server, Oracle DB, Cisco switches, VMware.

Custom/bespoke: Higher supplier assessment required. Vendor must demonstrate development process quality, testing capability, and regulatory knowledge. Factory Acceptance Tests (FAT) are strongly recommended.

COTS (commercial off-the-shelf): The preferred choice for standard infrastructure components. The supplier assessment burden is lower because supplier certifications, test evidence and market history are available.

Custom products: Require more demanding supplier assessment, and a factory acceptance test (FAT) is strongly recommended.

Supplier Assessment Criteria

A thorough supplier evaluation for GxP IT infrastructure typically covers:

  • Quality Management System: ISO 9001, ITIL, or equivalent; documented processes for development, testing, and change management
  • GxP/Regulatory knowledge: familiarity with 21 CFR Part 11, EU Annex 11, GAMP, FDA/EMA expectations
  • Technical capability: competency in the specific technology area; qualified technical staff
  • Delivery history: on-time delivery, defect rates, recall history
  • Support capability: SLA commitments; 24/7 support if required; patch/update frequency; end-of-life policy
  • Security posture: data security practices; vulnerability disclosure process; supply chain security
  • Financial stability: assurance of continued support over the product lifetime
  • References: other pharmaceutical or regulated industry customers

For IT suppliers, "facility" = their development and support processes; "batch records" = software development documentation and test evidence.

Installation & Installation Qualification (IQ)

Installation and Installation Qualification | Documentation, Environmental, Servers, Networks

IQ Scope and Principles

Installation and integration methods must comply with company standards, and be based on:

  • Supplier recommendations
  • Good Engineering Practice (GEP)
  • Good workmanship

IQ verifies that the required physical hardware and software components have been installed and configured correctly in accordance with the platform and design specifications.

5.6.2.1 Documentation Verification

During IQ, the adequacy of the following documentation should be verified:

  • Design specification
  • Hardware and software descriptions
  • System operation manuals
  • Technical manuals
  • System use SOPs (may be in draft at IQ; verified at OQ)
  • Network topology diagram (physical layout)
  • Network logical diagram (switching capability and resilience)
  • Landscape overview (depiction of environments for operation and maintenance)
  • System labeling convention (wiring closets, cables, equipment)
  • Cable lists as appropriate
  • Logical address lists (IP addresses, hostnames)
  • Supplier documentation (system configuration details and installation guides)

All critical items must be managed by the Configuration Management process, including change management.

5.6.2.2 Environmental Conditions

  • Temperature and humidity (especially in server rooms)
  • Electrical power and circuit protection documentation verification
  • Wiring, cabling, termination/connection documentation verification
  • Normal power up/power down verifications

5.6.2.5 Network IQ — Physical Layer

In new facilities, cables should be prepared, installed, inspected, and tested per recognized standards (e.g., ISO/IEC 11801 or ANSI/TIA/EIA 568B). Physical layer test considerations:

  • Environmental factors: heat, dust, moisture, Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC)
  • Physical strength of fiber cables
  • Connectors and terminations
  • Transmission performance: attenuation, refraction

Test results must be annotated with actual values recorded, and signed and dated per company procedures.

The building block concept maximizes qualification efficiency. Once a building block is qualified:

  • Prepare installation scripts in advance for producing duplicates (based on images)
  • Verify that the installation script produces an identical duplicate during initial qualification
  • For subsequent deployments, the main IQ focus is verifying that the duplicate was produced correctly
  • This approach is recognized by GAMP and reduces test burden without sacrificing compliance

IQ Documentation Package

  • IQ Protocol: approved before execution; specifies what to check and acceptance criteria
  • CIL (Configuration Items List): hardware and software inventory with versions
  • IQ Report: completed test records with actual values; deviations logged and resolved
  • Network topology diagrams: physical and logical layouts — verified at IQ
  • Environmental records: temperature, humidity, power verification logs
  • Cable test reports: physical layer test results (actual vs. spec)

OQ and Acceptance

Operational qualification testing for IT platforms — the final stage before handover

Original Text (ISPE GAMP)

The final stage of Qualification is to confirm that the IT Infrastructure component operates in an expected manner as defined in appropriate specifications.

Provided the platforms are all commercially available standard products, the focus of OQ should be to test connectivity and where applicable capacity using integrated or computerized tools, rather than core functionality.

At a minimum, OQ should consist of a set of instructions to be followed, which describe the necessary test steps, expected results, and evidence to be collected. OQ should be based on the outcome of the Risk Assessments already carried out to verify that the sum of requirements derived from serviced applications, if applicable, are met.

Typical tests to consider would include:

  • Verification of key Data Management Software (e.g., database connectivity)
  • Verification of all permanent IP-addresses, and response times under conditions specified by the Qualification Plan
  • Verification of the routing pattern under conditions required by the Qualification Plan (e.g., trace network packet to destination)
  • Verification of security settings across all platform components and checking that default passwords have been altered
  • Verification of critical firewall features — positive and negative testing
  • Verification of key Operating System (OS) functionality (e.g., accessibility to defined disk volumes)
  • Verification of time synchronization

Tests should be traceable to requirements where appropriate. In simple cases, IQ and OQ may be combined in one activity; however, the sequence of installation followed by operational testing should be maintained unless pre-qualified building blocks are used.

Commentary & CDMO Application

What is OQ for IT Infrastructure?

OQ (Operational Qualification) for IT platforms is NOT about testing software features. It proves that the platform operates as intended — connectivity works, security is configured, time is synchronized. Think of it as testing the "road" before allowing "cars" (applications) to drive on it.

OQ for an IT platform does not test application functionality. It confirms that the platform itself operates as intended: connectivity works, security settings are correct, and time synchronization is consistent. It is like checking that the road itself is sound before letting the application "drive" on it.

OQ Test Categories for IT

Key OQ Principles

  • Risk-based scope: OQ tests are derived from the Risk Assessment output — not everything needs testing, only what matters for GxP applications.
  • Firewall negative testing: You must prove the firewall BLOCKS what it should block, not just that it allows what it should allow.
  • Traceability: Each test should link back to a documented requirement.
  • IQ+OQ combined: Allowed for simple systems, but installation sequence must always precede operational testing.

CDMO Analogy

In sterile manufacturing, we test autoclave OQ by running temperature distribution studies at specified loads. For IT, we run OQ by testing network connectivity at specified loads, firewall rules with both valid and invalid traffic, and database response under concurrent users — same risk-based logic, different technology.

Just as autoclave OQ measures temperature distribution under defined load conditions, IT OQ tests network connectivity and firewall rules under defined conditions. The logic is the same; only the technology differs.

Change Management & Configuration Management

Sections 6.1–6.2: The inseparable pair that controls all platform evolution

6.1 Change Management

The change control process cannot be separated from Configuration Management. When changes are proposed, both change control and Configuration Management activities need to be considered in parallel, particularly when evaluating impact of changes.

Due to the simplicity, the frequency and occasionally the urgency of some changes (e.g., network port patching or security patching), change control procedures need to accommodate timely and effective, yet documented, updating methods.

Change management processes should define how changes to configuration items should be managed, and should include an assessment of the impact on supported GxP applications and the extent of re-qualification required, where applicable.

6.2 Configuration Management

Configuration Management (CM) covers the identification, recording, and reporting of components, including their version, constituent components, and relationships.

As a minimum, all items identified as critical for maintaining the qualified state of the platforms should be kept under CM — providing an approved baseline for further evolution and allowing safe restoration of a qualified baseline in case of problems.

Components are typically referred to as Configuration Items (CIs). CIs include hardware items, software items, critical parameter settings, documentation, and any other part of the IT Infrastructure that an organization wishes to control.

CIs are defined down to the lowest level at which a component can be independently installed, replaced, or modified.

Server Management & Client Management

Ensuring availability, performance, and security of servers and client devices

6.4 Server Management

The objective of the server management process is to ensure that specified requirements for operational availability, performance, and security are consistently fulfilled by the server. An important part of the process is to manage the server configuration and changes needed to meet the objectives.

The handling and qualification of changes depend on the potential impact that a given change may have on the served platforms and applications. If the change affects GxP applications, the extent of re-qualification required should be considered.

6.5 Client Management

The objective for the Client Management process is to ensure that specified requirements for operational availability, performance, and security are consistently fulfilled by the client. An important part of the process is to manage preparation, deliveries, adaptations, patching, and security issues for the multitude of stationary and mobile units in use across the organization.

The Client Management Group is advised to use computerized tools where possible to centrally manage updates, patches, and security scans to enforce corporate policies.

Analogy

Server management is like maintaining the central pharmaceutical manufacturing suite — high-stakes, tightly controlled, few but critical. Client management is like managing the gowning lockers and workstations throughout the facility — many, diverse, requiring centralized policy enforcement to be efficient.

6.6–7

Network Management & Problem Management

Maintaining network performance and proactively managing infrastructure problems

6.6 Network Management

The objective of Network Management is to ensure that specified requirements for operational availability, performance, and security are consistently fulfilled by the network. Fulfillment of this objective involves identification and use of effective and reliable computerized tools, applications, and devices to assist network staff in monitoring and maintaining network performance in support of other platforms and services.

Network diagrams should be updated when the topology changes during maintenance and the inventory list of components should be updated to reflect the actual network configuration.

6.7 Problem Management

Problem Management includes control and active management of both problems and errors. A 'problem' is an unknown underlying cause of one or more incidents, and a known error is a problem that is successfully diagnosed, and for which a workaround has been identified.

All filed problems should be tracked and resolved via approved channels; those that require changes should be input to the Change Management Group.

Problem Management should trend problem reports and strive to counteract escalation of problems by providing timely reports to the platform administration, e.g., to avoid severe service degradation caused by growing congestion in a given network segment.

Help Desk & Backup / Restore / Archiving

6.8 Help Desk

The Help Desk process has the goal of providing the day-to-day support to users of the IT Infrastructure with problems, questions, and general support needs. Staff employed in the Help Desk must be technically skilled with respect to the actual platforms and technology used in the IT Infrastructure to support the business processes in the company.

The Help Desk is typically contacted via a central point (e.g., telephone or using a web application), where the Help Desk case is either solved immediately, or registered in a Help Desk system that manages the Help Desk process.

6.9 Backup, Restore, and Archiving

The backup, restore, and archiving capability of data on any computer platform is essential to preserve the integrity of the information in case of system failures.

Whether a given application requires full or incremental types of backup, a Risk Assessment process should be applied to achieve an appropriate frequency, commensurate with the acceptable residual risk of losing data for a given period of time, and the resulting impact on business.

Archiving is a process that ensures long term availability of data by provisions of safe storage, indexing, and refresh activities.

When a request to perform a restoration is received, consideration should be given to whether the person requesting the restoration is authorized to do so, and whether they are authorized to view the data being restored. Procedures also should ensure that the restore process does not inadvertently overwrite data that must not be lost.

Disaster Recovery & Performance Monitoring

Business continuity planning and ongoing qualification evidence through monitoring

6.10 Disaster Recovery

Loss of vital parts of the IT Infrastructure and business applications may have serious impact on the business of a company. With the often complex configuration and interdependence of the infrastructure platforms and business applications, business becomes sensitive to even smaller incidents in the IT Infrastructure and business applications.

Therefore, Disaster Recovery is an important part of the company's Business Continuity Planning. The primary goal of Disaster Recovery is to reduce downtime of critical business applications to an acceptable level following an incident.

Disaster recovery is closely related to the identified Configuration Items (CIs) within the Configuration Management process as well as the backup, restore, and archiving process.

6.11 Performance Monitoring

The objective of performance monitoring is to monitor and record satisfactory operation of the IT Infrastructure as evidence in support of its continued qualification status, and secondarily, to ensure fulfillment of the Service Level Agreement (SLA) and any other stated or implied expectations.

The review of results or alarms based on this monitoring activity will trigger maintenance, update, support, or disaster recovery activities.

To assure that platform performance requirements are continuously met, surveillance procedures should be developed and alert and action limits defined.

A baseline should be created at the time of installation (or during the Performance Qualification (PQ) of a supported software application), and actual values should be considered based upon those readings.

1 Introduction

Companies are organized in a variety of ways that fit each individual company's mode of operation, size, objectives, geographic layout, culture, etc.

Companies typically identify roles and responsibilities in a similar way; this Appendix discusses their interrelationships and a possible way of grouping them together.

Core Concept: What "Owner" Means

An Owner is not just a job title but a form of business accountability. The guide stresses that real ownership can only be held by someone whose business objectives depend directly on the availability and performance of the system.

In other words, the Owner must be a user, not an IT operations person. Only if a system outage directly hits their business KPIs do they have the motivation to take responsibility.

Analogy

Think of the line leader on a factory production line. They may not understand the machines best, but they are the most anxious when a machine stops, because output is their responsibility. The Owner of an IT system should have the same mindset, rather than being an IT engineer who merely "looks after the system for someone else".

The GAMP IT guide defines 8 key roles. In a CDMO these 8 roles do not necessarily map to 8 people, since one person may hold several roles, but the responsibility boundaries of each role must be documented (job description + signature).

Appendix 2 — Example of Risk Assessment and Controls

Original Text

1 Background

Risk assessments should be performed at each major life cycle phase of any object or groups of objects (e.g., platforms or data) identified to be important or even critical for a company to fulfill its mission.

Risk Assessment may be taken to comprise two phases: risk analysis and risk evaluation. Note that NIST's Guide to Risk Management for Information Technology Systems suggests a further refinement into nine steps. GAMP 4, Appendix M3 describes a process for performing Risk Assessments of a computerized system to ensure design, specification, and test efforts are appropriately focused. This Appendix describes how a combined approach may be adopted for IT Infrastructures.

2 Risk Analysis

The purpose of risk analysis is to provide clarity of the boundaries of the infrastructure under analysis, and review the history of threats (hazards) and vulnerabilities in the light of its potential impact on the company's mission, which for GxP regulated companies includes considerations of public health.

Input to the process should include:

  • platform specifications (hardware and software)
  • architecture/topology diagrams
  • applied or planned security policies and requirements
  • requirements from application (system/data) owners
  • levels of staff training and experience
  • history of attacks, weaknesses, failures, flaws
  • any audit or self assessment comments
  • current or planned controls that may prevent, detect, or otherwise mitigate harm before irreparable damage occurs

Output from the analysis process would include:

  • identification of the infrastructure objects scoped by the process
  • the hazards threatening the infrastructure's obligation to meet critical requirements
  • the vulnerabilities that may warrant further consideration
  • a list of critical controls

The next step is to determine the likelihood that a given harm occurs, the magnitude of the impact of the harm, and the effect of any identified mitigation factors, e.g., early detection.

Each of the factors mentioned above should be rated in relation to each other as high, medium, or low in a two stage process:

Tutorial Commentary

The GAMP Two-Stage Risk Assessment Framework

Stage 1 (risk level):

Combine likelihood × impact to produce a Level 1/2/3 risk classification.

Stage 2 (risk priority):

Building on the Stage 1 result, add the probability of detection. If harm can be detected very early, the risk priority can be lowered; if it is hard to detect, the priority rises.

Risk Assessment Logic (Conceptual)

Stage 1:
  Likelihood (high/medium/low) × Impact (high/medium/low)
  → Risk Level: 1 (low) / 2 (medium) / 3 (high)

Stage 2:
  Risk Level + Detection Probability (high/medium/low)
  → Risk Priority: high priority / medium priority / low priority

High risk priority → stricter control measures needed
Low risk priority → existing controls or monitoring may be acceptable

Analogy: Factory Fire Risk

Stage 1: chemicals stored near the factory (high impact) × staff not skilled in handling them (medium likelihood) → Risk Level 2
Stage 2: are there fire sensors (detection)? With good sensors, the priority goes down; with old sensors, the priority stays high

IT security follows the same logic: a misconfigured firewall (high impact), combined with whether log monitoring exists (detection), determines the final priority.

Key Point: Subjectivity and the Quality of Personnel

The guide explicitly acknowledges that "the data input to this step is mostly subjective, as there is typically a lack of quantitative data to support the assessment." This is why the quality of the people involved in the risk assessment is critical: they must have a balanced technical background and experience, rather than being purely IT engineers or purely QA.

Key Concepts: The Three-Column Matrix Structure

Table A4.1 uses a three-column format that reflects the core logic of GMP document management:

The essence of this structure: it is not enough to have "done it"; you need documents showing "how it is done" (the SOP) and "what was done" (the records).

Key Note: Why IT Needs SOPs, Not Just Validation

Many pharmaceutical companies mistakenly believe IT SOPs are useful only during system "validation". This is a dangerous misunderstanding. IT infrastructure SOPs serve the following functions:

  • Continuity of regulatory compliance: FDA 21 CFR Part 11 and EU Annex 11 require electronic record systems to be controlled throughout their entire lifecycle, not just at go-live
  • Mitigation of staff turnover risk: SOPs let new IT staff work in a consistent way without relying on individual memory
  • Basis for deviation investigations: When a system fails or data is lost, the SOP plus records are the only objective standard for determining accountability
  • Building confidence in client audits: The pharmaceutical clients of a CDMO usually ask to see the list of IT SOPs and the execution records during audits

Analogy: The SOP Matrix Is Like a Hospital's Infection Control Program

A hospital's infection control program does not regulate just one action such as "operating room disinfection". It covers access control, staff training, equipment maintenance, waste handling and emergency response, and every element has a corresponding SOP and record requirement. IT infrastructure SOPs follow exactly the same logic: from "who may enter the server room" to "how to handle a deviation when a backup fails", everything needs to be formalized.

Security SOPs: The Direct Link to 21 CFR Part 11

The SOPs required in the "Logical Security" section of the matrix correspond directly to the provisions of FDA 21 CFR Part 11:

  • User account management: Corresponds to §11.10(d), limiting system access to authorized individuals
  • Password management: Corresponds to §11.300, ensuring the uniqueness and non-repudiation of electronic signatures
  • Digital signature certificate management: Corresponds to §11.100, the identity verification requirements for electronic signatures
  • Logs of creation, deletion, transfers (account creation/deletion/transfer of responsibilities): Corresponds to §11.10(e), a complete audit trail

Key point: unique user accounts plus an audit trail are the most basic requirements for 21 CFR Part 11 compliance. Any shared account used by several people, or any access that cannot be traced, is an immediate 483 observation.

Data Management SOPs: A Backup Is Not a Verified Backup

Many companies run backups but overlook the matrix requirement for "Restoration testing as part of disaster recovery testing". This is a common pain point in FDA 483 observations:

  • Backup plan exists ✓
  • Backups run daily ✓
  • Backup media stored off-site ✓
  • Periodic verification that backups can be restored successfully ✗ ← most often overlooked

The auditor will ask: "When did you last perform a successful restore test? Is there a record?" Without restoration test records, the effectiveness of the backup system cannot be demonstrated.

Medium risk (common but quickly remediated):

  7. Continuity Management — disaster recovery plan not tested regularly

Low risk (structural issues, rarely cited on their own):
  8. Job Scheduling — deviation reporting mechanism for batch jobs
  9. Networks — documentation of third-party network connections

Practical Application: Priority Order for Building CDMO SOPs

  1. First priority (directly required by regulation): Logical Security SOP package, Change Management SOP, Data Backup & Restore SOP (including restoration testing)
  2. Second priority (frequently asked about in audits): User Access Review procedure, Training Effectiveness Assessment, Configuration Management
  3. Third priority (operational resilience): Disaster Recovery Plan (including annual drills), Capacity Management, Incident Management

You do not need to build every SOP perfectly at once. Start with the high-risk core documents and fill in the rest step by step according to risk assessment.

Tutorial Commentary

GMP Implications of "Emergency Access Management"

The matrix specifically calls out "Management of emergency access" as requiring an SOP, a common compliance gap in the industry. When a system fails urgently, IT staff may use a shared emergency/root account to bypass normal access controls.

Compliance requirements:

  • Emergency access accounts must have an SOP defining activation conditions and authorization levels
  • Every use must be recorded, with authorization signed retrospectively and the use reviewed
  • The password must be reset after use to prevent credential leakage

An "emergency" must not become an excuse for bypassing controls. On the contrary, discipline during emergencies is exactly what regulators focus on.

Analogy: A Backup Is Like Insurance, and Restoration Testing Is the Claims Drill

Buying insurance but never confirming that the policy is valid is the equivalent of "having backups but no restoration testing". Performing Restoration Testing regularly is like having the insurer confirm every year that your claim conditions are still met. Without restoration test records, your "insurance" may be a worthless promise.

The Importance of "Authorization to Restore"

The matrix requires the restore process to include "authorization to restore". This is not just a technical operation but a quality decision. In a GxP-controlled environment, restoring data from backup can affect:

  • The integrity of batch records currently in progress
  • The data consistency of electronic batch records (eBR)
  • The continuity of the audit trail

Therefore "who is authorized to approve a restore" must be clearly defined in the SOP and cannot be left to IT staff to decide on their own.

Three Essential Elements of a Change Management SOP

The matrix requires Change Management to include the change SOP itself, a Risk Assessment, and Management approval/rejection. None of the three can be omitted:

  • Change SOP: Defines how changes are proposed, evaluated and implemented
  • Risk Assessment: Evaluates the potential impact of the change on GxP systems and determines whether revalidation is needed
  • Management approval/rejection: Ensures the change decision has appropriate organizational authorization and is not made by IT alone

Unapproved IT changes (even "minor" ones such as patch installation) are among the most common problems in FDA 483 observations.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Concepts

The essential difference between Backup and Archive:

  • Backup: short-term protection, used for rapid recovery after a system failure. Backups are temporary, and media are rotated and overwritten.
  • Archive: long-term preservation, used for retention of regulated records. Archives are permanent (for the period required by regulation) and must remain readable in the future.

The two have different procedures, media management and retention periods, and must not be confused.

Key Notes — Why Restoration Testing Is a Regulatory Expectation

Many companies perform backups but have never tested a restore. The periodic review explicitly requires that both the backup procedure and the restore procedure be formally tested, with records.

The reason is clear: a backup failure is usually discovered only when a restore is needed. If you cannot successfully restore after batch records are lost, then even with a backup it is as if you had none; this directly violates the 21 CFR 211.68 requirements for electronic records.

"Recover to point of failure": requires assessment of the RPO (Recovery Point Objective, the maximum acceptable period of data loss).

Key Notes — Archive Readability

Data migration following system upgrades is one of the most frequently overlooked risks. Archived data in an old format may not be directly readable on the new system. You must either:

  • retain hardware/software capable of reading the old format, or
  • convert the archived data to the new format at the time of the system upgrade (the completeness of the conversion must be validated)

This bears directly on the FDA expectation that electronic records remain readable throughout the legally required retention period.

Analogy

Doing backups without testing restores is like buying a safe but never testing whether the key opens it. If you discover in an emergency that the key is broken, the safe is useless. Regular restore drills are the only way to confirm that the protection mechanism really works.

Backup Compliance Framework

Backup frequency design principles:
  RPO (acceptable data loss) → determines backup frequency
  RTO (acceptable system downtime) → determines recovery approach

Typical pharmaceutical CDMO backup architecture:
  Full backup: weekly, stored off-site
  Incremental backup: daily, local + off-site
  Log backup: hourly (databases)

Periodic review must-checks:
  □ Records of the most recent restore test (including date, tester, result)
  □ Off-site storage confirmation records
  □ Backup media rotation log
  □ Periodic assessment records for archive media (media aging)

Key Concepts — In-Depth Analysis of the Disaster Recovery Clause

The practical meaning of "access to archives commensurate with recovery lag times":

This means that after a system disaster, the required archived data must be obtainable within a time consistent with the recovery time objective (RTO).

In practice, this means:

  • If your RTO is 4 hours, archived data must be retrievable and readable within 4 hours

1. Introduction

A defined level of infrastructure security is required to meet business purposes and to satisfy external regulations. Lack of security may compromise availability of applications and services, record integrity and confidentiality, reputation with stakeholders, and may lead to unauthorized use of systems that would impact on product quality.

A company should take cultural and practical aspects into consideration when defining the rigor of its approach. Companies may adopt different approaches for specific infrastructure elements, based, e.g., on criticality and risk.

2. Infrastructure Security Management

Infrastructure security management includes all the policies, procedures, requirements, training and audit programs, etc. that a company may define appropriate to safeguard the infrastructure, including information assets.

As a minimum, regulated companies need to satisfy the applicable GxP requirements. Relevant documents include the PIC/S Guide, which builds on ISO/IEC 17799:2000 Information Technology – Code of Practice for Information Security Management. The 21 CFR Part 11 contains security requirements focused on electronic records and signatures. RFC 2196, "Site Security Handbook," is another useful source.

Security controls, in general, are considerably cheaper and more effective if incorporated at the requirements specification and design stages.

2.1 ISO/IEC 17799 — Code of Practice for Information Security Management

Information security is characterized in the standard as the preservation of:

  • Confidentiality: ensuring that information is accessible only to those authorized to have access
  • Integrity: safeguarding the accuracy and completeness of information and processing methods
  • Availability: ensuring that authorized users have access to information and associated assets when required

The ISO/IEC 17799:2000 is a risk-based method for assessing, evaluating, and managing risks. It provides a framework for developing a security program with 10 sections:

  1. Security Policy
  2. Organizational Security
  3. Asset Classification and Control
  4. Personnel Security
  5. Physical and Environmental Security
  6. Communications and Operations Management
  7. Access Control
  8. System Development and Maintenance
  9. Business Continuity Management
  10. Compliance

Not all controls will be relevant to all infrastructures. The use of ISO/IEC 17799 as a guide should be adapted to the circumstances of each organization.

2.2 ISO/IEC 17799 and Regulations

Regulatory guidance recommends consideration of these standards, but does not require organizations to be formally accredited. Companies should define a list of ISO/IEC 17799 issues relevant to their infrastructure and map existing policies against applicable sections, using internal audits to demonstrate control to regulatory authorities.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The CIA Triad

Confidentiality: Data can be accessed only by authorized personnel. In a GxP system, batch records must not be readable or downloadable by unauthorized people.

Integrity: Data must be accurate and complete, and any change must leave an audit trail. This maps directly to the electronic record requirements of 21 CFR Part 11.

Availability: Authorized users must be able to access the system when they need it. If the production line cannot retrieve batch records because of an IT system outage, that is an availability failure.

Analogy

Think of the CIA triad as a hospital medical record system. Confidentiality = only the attending physician can see the full chart; integrity = every change to the chart is logged; availability = the chart can be pulled up at any moment in an emergency. You need all three, just like environmental control in sterile manufacturing: if any one link is missing, product safety is at risk.

Key Notes

ISO/IEC 17799 is a recommended framework. Regulators do not require formal certification, but during an inspection you must be able to show that "the company has mapped this framework to its own policies and completed internal audits". This is the basis of compliance evidence, not an optional extra.

"Building security controls in at the requirements specification stage costs least and works best." This principle is consistent with the GAMP V-model: fixing things late costs far more than designing them in early.

2.3 Technical versus Procedural Issues

Management should establish an administration obligated to define and monitor an infrastructure security program. Controls include:

  • Administrative controls: policies, standards, procedures, guidelines, security awareness training, incident management
  • Technical controls: access control mechanisms, password and resource management, identification and authentication methods, security devices
  • Physical controls: controlling individual access into facilities, locking systems, removing unnecessary floppy disk or CD-ROM drives, protecting facility perimeter, monitoring for intrusion

2.4 Security Incident Management

An incident management procedure should be established as effective communication channels are essential in the quick identification and mitigation of a specific threat. Seemingly isolated incidents may not receive an appropriate level of attention unless they are centrally reported. For instance, an account being locked due to a forgotten password may become more suspicious if the same event occurs repeatedly.

2.5 Intrusion Detection

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDSs) in the form of hardware or software enhance the security of the perimeter of internal networks. Determined intruders may penetrate firewalls, and attacks do not come only from external sources. By monitoring suspicious activity, alarms can be sounded earlier. A well-maintained pattern database and regular review of IDS summary reports ensure efficacy.

2.6 Vulnerability Management

Vulnerability scanners or adequate manual procedures should be applied to determine weaknesses and gaps in security configurations. Corrective actions include: application of patches, changes to security settings, harnessing the network topology.

A practical approach is to start with a complete inventory of systems, identify a small number of reliable sources of security alerts, assess and prioritize each threat. With numerous new threats identified each day, it is often more appropriate to patch during scheduled maintenance (weekly or monthly) rather than daily.

2.7 Anti-Virus Shield Update

Most anti-virus applications can initiate totally automatic updates, but some companies prefer controlled daily updates. Configuration Management practices are required, recording which updates were applied to which systems, and at what date and time.

Two options for remote/off-site clients: (1) controlled distribution from internal IT — allows testing but inhibits rapid response; (2) automatic updates from anti-virus supplier — poses challenges for Configuration Management. A Risk Assessment comparing untested updates vs. leaving clients unprotected may be warranted.

2.8 Public Key Infrastructure

Where a company needs features such as digital signatures, it may implement a Public Key Infrastructure (PKI), or use PKI services from external organizations. Digital signatures secure authenticity, non-repudiation, integrity, and confidentiality of external messaging. Users need certificates from a trusted third party or the company's own Certification Authority (CA).

2.9 Origin of Software

The origin of all software related to qualified infrastructure platforms should be known. If open source software is used, ensure a reputable supplier supports the product and accepts responsibility for maintaining it. Software from non-reputable or unknown sources should be avoided.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three-Layer Security Control Architecture

Administrative controls (policies/training) → technical controls (access rights/authentication) → physical controls (access gates/equipment): the three layers reinforce one another. In a GxP inspection, all three layers need documentary evidence; technical tools alone are not enough.

Analogy

An IDS (intrusion detection system) is like a particle counter in a cleanroom. The protective barrier (firewall) stops most threats, but the counter keeps monitoring in the background and raises an alarm the moment something abnormal appears. The counter does not block contamination itself, but it gives you time to act before the contamination spreads.

Key Notes

Repeated account lockouts are a security event, not a minor IT issue. A single lockout may just be a forgotten password, but repeated lockouts may indicate a brute-force attack. A central reporting mechanism turns isolated incidents into a visible pattern, which is the same principle as quality trend analysis in GMP.

The antivirus update dilemma: controlled updates can be tested but respond slowly; automatic updates respond quickly but are hard to track under configuration management. GxP systems should prefer controlled updates, with the rationale documented through a risk assessment.

Open-source software is not free of risk: if the software source is unknown or the vendor makes no maintenance commitment, using it in a GxP environment is equivalent to using an unqualified raw material, with very high compliance risk.

3. Upgrades and Patches — Balancing Qualification and Security

Changes to IT Infrastructure platforms require formal change control processes, documentation, and testing. These processes take time, especially where individual application tests are required, and particularly for GxP applications.

Where changes are required to address security vulnerabilities, applying the full change control and testing processes prospectively may present an unacceptable risk to the business in terms of security. However, direct application of any upgrades/patches without change control and testing may compromise the qualified status of the IT Infrastructure and present compliance issues.

First, the scope of the change should be understood (Network Operations, Clients, Servers, Firmware/Hardware, Data Management Software, Operating System, Infrastructure Utility Systems). Second, the urgency of the change should be established.

3.1 Urgency of Security Update

Generally, the urgency of application of a change falls into one of three criticality categories:

  1. Emergency Fix: should be applied as soon as possible, where the security vulnerability presents immediate and real threats to the business
  2. Urgent Update: probable threat, fix should be applied within a specified time scale
  3. Update: no immediate threat, consider including within next scheduled platform upgrade

3.2 Compatibility of Security Updates

Security patches are usually available only for a limited number of product versions; suppliers of interacting software may not provide compatible versions, creating a dilemma for system owners. Where critical security patches cannot be installed, system managers may have to resort to procedural controls or even isolate the system from other systems or from the corporate network.

Further guidance on managing security upgrades and patches is given in Appendix 7.

  • Isolation: disconnect the system from the corporate network to reduce the attack surface

Both options must be documented through a risk assessment, and a long-term solution must be tracked in a CAPA.

1. Fundamental Principles

Regardless of the criticality of an upgrade, the following aspects should be considered:

  • Change Control: Change control documentation should be raised to cover application of the upgrade/fix. For emergency changes, this may be retrospective (but within a minimum period of time following application).
  • Configuration Management: Records should be maintained; documentation recording versions and patch levels of platforms should be accurately maintained. For emergency changes, documentation may be retrospectively updated.
  • Communication: All owners of critical applications should be made aware of the requirement for an upgrade prior to its application.
  • Incident Monitoring: An incident monitoring process should be in place. Where emergency changes are applied with minimal or no specific application testing, particular attention should be given to incident monitoring and reporting.

2. Upgrade Strategy

The upgrade strategy should be based on a technical assessment and an impact assessment:

  • The IT group should perform a technical assessment: whether the upgrade is minor, medium, or major based on complexity and possible impact on applications.
  • All owners of critical applications should perform an impact assessment on their systems.

Issues can arise when implementing patches to platforms where the base application and/or operating system have not been upgraded for an extended period and lag behind the current version. Installation of an emergency upgrade may be compromised — resulting in either the application being unable to run or the patch being unable to be applied.

Systems that have been removed from operational use but retained as read-only systems for record/data retrieval (partially decommissioned) may present issues, particularly where product support is no longer available. Consideration should be given to isolating or segregating such systems.

3. Level of Application Testing

Four general levels of testing that may be applied to applications following a platform upgrade:

  1. No Testing
  2. Confirmation Testing: A minimal level of documented testing to verify that an application runs on an updated platform.
  3. Confidence Testing: Documented testing against pre-defined specifications that consist of a representative subset of functional tests on the updated platform.
  4. Full Testing: Full regression testing of the application on the updated platform.

For emergency changes (e.g., critical security update), it is likely that no prospective testing will be applied. For business critical and GxP applications, enhanced incident monitoring should be applied, and a documented justification made until retrospective testing or final impact evaluation is completed.

Where the update is not an emergency: minor updates → Confirmation Testing only may be appropriate; significant updates or highly critical GxP systems → Confidence Testing as minimum; major updates with likely application impact → Full regression testing.

The two-stage Risk Assessment process from GAMP 4 (assessing impact of failure, likelihood of failure, probability of detection) is a good tool for determining appropriate testing level.

4. Global/Multi-Site Systems

For systems used at multiple locations with worldwide standardization of platforms, an efficient approach is to perform testing at a single location and use this as the basis of reduced or no testing at other locations.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Four Application Testing Levels

Level 1 — No Testing: Only for emergencies, and must be accompanied by enhanced event monitoring and a post-implementation test plan. It is never "permanently exempt from testing".

Level 2 — Confirmation Testing: Minimal documented testing to verify that the application still runs on the updated platform. Applies to upgrades the technical assessment rates as "minor".

Level 3 — Confidence Testing: Testing of a representative subset of functions against predefined specifications. Applies to significant upgrades or highly critical GxP systems.

Level 4 — Full Testing (full regression testing): Comprehensive regression testing. Applies to major upgrades that could plausibly affect the application.

Decision Matrix for Selecting the Testing Level

Nature of upgrade | GxP/critical system? | Recommended testing level
------------------|----------------------|--------------------------
Emergency patch   | Yes/No               | Level 1 + enhanced monitoring
Minor upgrade     | No                   | Level 2
Minor upgrade     | Yes                  | Level 2~3
Significant upgrade | Yes/No             | Level 3 (minimum)
Major upgrade     | Yes                  | Level 4

Analogy

The four testing levels are like quality inspection grades before a car leaves the factory. Emergency release (Level 1) = just check the engine starts and hand over the car; confirmation testing (Level 2) = also test the brakes; confidence testing (Level 3) = run a few laps on the test track; full testing (Level 4) = complete safety certification testing. The more expensive the car and the more important the passengers, the less you can cut back on the testing level.

Key Notes

The version-gap trap: if a system has not been upgraded for a long time, an emergency patch may fail to install, or may crash the application after installation. This is a common technical debt at CDMOs: the upgrade and maintenance costs you saved come back at double the price in an emergency.

Partially decommissioned systems: for a read-only system that must remain available for retrieval, network isolation is a necessary risk-reduction measure, not an option, if the original vendor has stopped supporting it.

Multi-site systems: after full testing at one site, other sites may reduce their testing, but this must be explicitly specified in the strategy document and presupposes a standardized platform.

Appendix 8 — Outsourcing (p95–p99)

Original Text

1. Definition of Responsibilities

Outsourcing involves the transfer of management and operations of a company's IT Infrastructure to an external company. Scope includes: operation of a data center and/or networks; operation of systems and/or processes (e.g., Help Desk); hardware/software component build (e.g., workstation).

The contracted service provider may have staff located on the regulated company's premises or provide services from a remote location (if on a different continent, 'offshoring' is sometimes used).

"The regulated company remains responsible for the regulatory compliance of their IT operations regardless of whether they choose to outsource/offshore some or their entire IT Infrastructure processes to external service provider(s). Compliance oversight and approvals cannot be delegated to the outsource partner."

The external service provider should focus on: regulatory education (all staff understand compliance impact); creation and maintenance of SOPs and work instructions; creation and maintenance of quality records; QA and QC of their IT processes.

2. Special Considerations

The location from which the outsourcing company provides services should be assessed. Services on-site should be managed per the company's standard security processes. Services from outside the regulated company's boundaries may require additional controls (e.g., for remote management of a data centre or a Help Desk).

3. Contracts

The regulated company is responsible for interpreting GxP regulations and defining appropriate requirements for the IT Infrastructure, provided to the external service provider in IT industry best practice terminology.

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and acceptance criteria should be defined by the regulated company. Contracts should handle ownership and retention of documents and records — if a contract is ended, the regulated company needs access to those documents or records at a later date.

Contracts should clearly state conditions under which the regulated company or regulators can undertake on-site audits to verify conformance. Expert support required on site to facilitate inspections, agreed notifications, and alert levels also should be specified.

4. Service Level Agreements

Where services are requested by one organization and provided by another, a formal SLA should be established. An SLA will usually be included as part of a contract and clearly state KPIs and associated fees.

A typical SLA will specify:

  • contacts on either side
  • duration of validity and circumstances triggering reviews
  • pre-requisites and customer deliverables or involvement
  • scope and nature of required services
  • metrics in the form of KPIs
  • records demonstrating fulfillment of specified service levels
  • pricing arrangements, including penalties for shortcomings
  • reports, scope, frequency, distribution
  • audit provisions, including readiness to facilitate inspections from regulatory authorities

5. Audits

Before entering into an outsourcing arrangement, a supplier audit should be considered. Staff from the regulated company's QA should be allowed to repeat initial audits at suitable intervals or if conditions warrant investigation.

The regulated company is responsible for authorizing appropriate resolution or mitigation of non-compliance areas arising from audits. The regulated company should include tracking of these within its CAPA program.

A non-disclosure agreement should be entered with the supplier to specify what information may and may not be shared.

6. Training Requirements

Regulations require that personnel have the appropriate combination of education, training, and experience. This applies to external supplier personnel as well. The service provider should ensure their staff is trained in regulatory compliance. Such training should be documented.

Tutorial Commentary

Key Note: Compliance Oversight Responsibility Cannot Be Outsourced

This is the most important principle of this appendix. No matter which supplier manages the IT system, regulatory responsibility always remains with the regulated company. It is like outsourced drug manufacturing: the contract manufacturer (CMO) carries out the manufacture, but compliance responsibility stays with the contract giver (MAH).

Practical implications for a CDMO: even if the IT infrastructure is fully outsourced to AWS or a local IT services company, at an FDA/EMA inspection you must still be able to demonstrate effective oversight of the supplier, including audit records, KPI tracking and CAPA execution. "The supplier said they did it" is not an acceptable answer.

Analogy

Outsourcing IT is like a restaurant outsourcing its ingredient supply. The chef (the regulated company) is responsible for the safety of the dishes, even if the ingredients are delivered by a supplier. The contract defines the ingredient specifications (KPIs), the supplier's kitchen is audited regularly (supplier audit), and ingredients are checked on arrival (incoming verification). If the ingredients cause a problem, the customer sues the restaurant, not the supplier.

SLA Essential Elements

Compliance-relevant essential items (inspection focus):
1. KPIs and acceptance criteria (quantitative performance indicators)
2. Audit rights clause (including on-site inspection by regulatory authorities)
3. Ownership of record retention (records remain obtainable after the contract ends)
4. Incident notification levels and timelines (Alert Levels)
5. Training requirements (supplier personnel must receive GxP training)
6. Procedures and penalties for non-compliance

Core Concept: Ownership of Record Retention

The contract must state explicitly that, if it is terminated, the regulated company can still access all GxP-relevant records. This is often overlooked in practice, until the supplier goes out of business or the contract expires and the records turn out to be "locked", so they cannot be produced for an inspection. This is a serious compliance risk.

Practical Application

When a CDMO outsources cloud infrastructure to AWS / Azure, the contract must state clearly:

  • Can FDA inspectors require an on-site inspection of the cloud provider's data center? (This must be written into the contract.)
  • When the CDMO leaves the cloud platform, how are all GxP records migrated? In what format? Within what time limit?
  • What are the cloud provider's security incident notification obligations (within how many hours must customers be told)?

These must all be confirmed during contract negotiation, not worked out after go-live.

1. Introduction

The objective of the Server Management process is to ensure that specified requirements for operational availability, performance, and security are consistently fulfilled by the server. An important part of the process is to manage server configuration and changes needed to meet the objectives.

All quantitative measures provided in this Appendix are derived from actual situations and are meant to assist companies, not to prescribe absolutes.

2. Backup and Restore

Failures of computer systems may result in a loss of data. The backup of electronic data is consequently essential to preserve the integrity and availability of the data.

2.1 Backup

There are two main types of backup:

  • Full backup: a copy of all data on a given system
  • Incremental/differential backup: a copy of data that has changed since the last backup cycle

The following minimum requirements are recommended:

  • Full backup: once per week, one day per week
  • Incremental or differential: all other days

Backups should be monitored to ensure successful completion:

  • review of backup logs to ensure successful completion
  • proactive assessments conducted regularly to ensure all required systems are included
  • backup media management

2.2 Verification

An extensive verification of the backup should be made when data is considered critical for the business, although the quantity of data may prevent full verification from being performed.

2.3 Schedules

The type of data should largely dictate backup schedules. Enterprise-level data actively in use can be considered to be changing each working day, with backup normally taking place overnight. Defining the schedule for global systems requires careful consideration, as access by users from other locations may create conflicts. Certain application data files cannot usually be backed up while in use — backup schedules may require augmented processes coordinating with deactivation of applications.

2.4 Restore

When a restore request is received, consideration should be given to whether the person requesting the restore is authorized, and that they are authorized to view the data being restored. When restoring data, checks should be made to ensure that more recent versions of files being restored are not overwritten, unless specifically requested. Consideration should be given to enable recovery to the point of failure.

Restoration beyond clearly defined single records or files may pose a challenge in assuring that all related records are brought back to the same state or point in time to retain overall integrity. This can be achieved only through carefully planned interactions with the system/data owner representatives.

2.5 Storage

A process for protecting backup data should be established to prevent loss, damage, and unauthorized access. Controls include:

  • secure storage facilities with proper access controls and environmental conditions
  • indexing and labeling capabilities and means to timely retrieval during inspections
  • detecting the end of retention requirements and notifying data owner
  • ensuring changes are carried out under change control
  • securely destroying data given necessary authorization

Although manufacturers give an expected lifetime for media, the expected lifetime when containing data is considerably less. Provisions should be made to refresh data after a defined period.

To ensure ability to recover data in a disaster, backup media should be stored at a secure 'off-site' facility — geographically diverse from the location where the system is housed.

2.6 Testing

The requirement to test the backup process should encompass any off-site storage solutions. The test should aim to restore data from historical backups, which may require handling of off-site backups. Restoring actual backups and comparing against the unchanged original file, where possible, gives the opportunity to check that the whole data path maintains data integrity. This periodic activity may certify both the backup and restore procedures.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Full vs. Incremental Backup Strategy

Full Backup: Once a week, backing up all data. Advantage: simple restore. Disadvantage: takes a long time and a lot of space.

Incremental Backup: Daily, backing up only data changed since the last backup. Advantage: fast and space-saving. Disadvantage: restore requires merging several backups in sequence.

GxP minimum requirement: full backup once a week + incremental backup daily. This is not a recommendation; it is the bottom line.

Analogy

A backup strategy is like a factory's photo record: once a month you take a group photo of the whole plant (full backup), and every day you record what has changed today (incremental backup). When something goes wrong, you find the latest group photo first, then apply the daily changes one after another, and you can restore the state to the day of the incident.

Off-site storage is like keeping a copy of the group photo in another building: if one building burns down, the backup survives.

Key Notes

Reviewing backup records is not the same as testing a restore: a backup log showing "success" is not enough. GxP requires restore tests to be actually performed periodically, with the restored data verified as identical to the original. "Can back up" and "can restore" are two different things.

Restore authorization control: a data restore operation must verify the requester's identity and their authorization to view the data. In a 21 CFR Part 11 system, an unauthorized restore is equivalent to unauthorized data access.

The media lifetime trap: the media lifetime stated by the manufacturer is that of blank media; the actual lifetime once data is written is much shorter. A periodic data refresh plan must be established to prevent long-term backup data from degrading naturally.

Analogy — Thick vs. Thin Client

A thick client is like a private dining room with its own kitchen: ingredients and cooking are all on site, so changes easily affect the whole. A thin client is like a takeaway pickup counter with only tables and chairs: the core processing happens entirely in the kitchen (the server side), and there is almost nothing local to tamper with, so the GxP risk is naturally lower.

Key Point — Why EMC/RFI Matters in a Pharmaceutical Plant

Production environments in pharmaceutical plants often contain large motors and high-frequency equipment that generate electromagnetic interference. A PC that does not meet EMC standards may produce data errors or crash, directly affecting the integrity of batch records.

Original Text — App. 10 §6–7 Images & Patch Management

6 Images or Installation Scripts

Client management often includes the creation and maintenance of images or standard installation scripts or both. This ensures easier management of client preparation processes and demonstration of control.

Besides the operating system and standard office packages, an image could include the company's most used client software rendering subsequent requests for installations unnecessary. This approach would improve reinstallation time, but require more time supporting the image, and although user accounts would not be created on a default basis, security policies could be violated.

Before an image can be released for use, it should be tested against the relevant applications and hardware used in the company.

7 Patch Management

Security patches to be installed on clients should be tested for compatibility with the operating system, office software packages, and client software. A test lab with instances of the hardware and software used in the company should be available, and pilot installations in a no-impact live environment should be made as part of the qualification plan. This should discover potential problems with the patch in the live environment before general rollout.

Besides test requirements, security patch information from the supplier should be made available to the organization. This will ensure awareness of the process and allow owners of critical business applications outside the scope of the PC management group to conduct tests before rollout (see Appendix 6 of this Guide).

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Compliance Advantages of Image Deployment

An image is a snapshot of a complete system that has already been tested. When it is deployed to a new PC, every computer starts from an identical state, greatly reducing the effort of qualifying each PC individually. This is the concept of qualification reuse: a single set of IQ/OQ records can cover all PCs built from the same image.

Key Point — GxP Risks of Patch Management

Security patches can sometimes change system behavior and cause problems for validated GxP applications. The correct process is:
Test lab verification → Pilot deployment → Full rollout
Skipping any step can put a validated system out of its validated state and trigger revalidation.

Analogy — Image vs. Installing PC by PC

Image deployment is like output from a restaurant chain's central kitchen: every branch has exactly the same sauce recipe, so quality is predictable. Manual installation PC by PC is like each branch simmering its own sauce: consistency is hard to guarantee, and during an audit it is hard to say clearly "which PC has what installed".

4 Network Provisioning and Installation

Network provisioning involves the following tasks:

  • Network planning and design is the responsibility of the network engineering group which keeps track of new technologies and introduces them as needed in conformance with applicable standards and company requirements.
  • Identification of bottlenecks through analysis of traffic and performance data provided by the Network Operation Center (NOC) and introduction of modifications to the network to optimize the use of the equipment.
  • Keeping track of new network management tools and introducing them where they would be efficient for gathering statistics and showing trends of traffic patterns for tuning and planning purposes.

5 Network Operation Center (NOC)

The function of the NOC, typically headed by the overall network administrator, is to assume responsibility for the daily operation of the network. The responsibility of the NOC is to provide network services in conformance with SLAs and other applicable requirements and standards, this includes:

  • establishing a network management system, e.g., based on SNMP
  • ensuring that the gathering of data for performance, tuning, and accounting purposes is functioning and kept current
  • restoration of faulty services
  • analyzing logs and assuring that all manageable network devices respond as required, e.g., via Syslog
  • assuring that there are current and easily available configuration backups of all network equipment as well as a documented trace of all configuration changes
  • providing management with reports and statistics (KPIs) to ensure that the network is performing optimally and the SLAs are within the agreed limits
  • ensuring that approved security procedures are followed by tracking all network accesses and by securing that only permitted access to the network is allowed
  • applying tools and procedures to ensure that the availability (defined by different parameters such as latency, bandwidth, and availability) is maintained by analyzing trends, planning upgrades, performing preventive maintenance, and fault correction

Core Concept: The Six Core Functions of the NOC

  • Monitoring: real-time awareness of network status
  • Log Analysis: Syslog aggregation to track anomalies
  • Config Backup: all device configurations are backed up, with change records
  • KPI Reporting: regular reporting of SLA achievement to management
  • Access Control: tracking of all network access
  • Fault Recovery: rapid restoration of service to reduce downtime

Original Text — App. 11 §6 Common Network Protocols

6.1 Simple Network Management Protocol (SNMP/RFC1157)

Network management system contains two primary elements:

  1. Manager
  2. Agents

The manager is the console through which the network administrator performs network management functions, often accompanied by data management systems to store configuration information, logs of alarms/alerts ('traps' in SNMP context), and performance data history.

Agents are the entities that interface to the actual device being managed. Bridges, hubs, routers, or network servers are examples of managed devices that contain managed objects.

SNMP allows managers and agents to communicate for the purpose of accessing these objects. The SNMP has become the de facto standard for internet work management. Because it is a simple solution, requiring little code to implement, suppliers can easily build SNMP agents into their products. SNMP also separates the management architecture from the architecture of the hardware devices, which broadens the base of multi-supplier support.

6.2 Syslog (RFC3164)

This protocol has been used for the transmission of event notification messages across networks for many years. Its value to operations and management has led it to be ported to many other operating systems as well as being embedded into many other networked devices.

In its most simplistic terms, the Syslog protocol provides a transport to allow a machine to send event notification messages across IP networks to event message collectors, also known as Syslog servers.

One of the fundamental tenets of the Syslog protocol and process is its simplicity. No stringent coordination is required between the transmitters and the receivers. There are usually many devices sending messages to relatively fewer collectors. This compilation process allows an administrator to aggregate messages into relatively few repositories.

6.3 Remote Monitoring (RMON) (RFC 1757)

Remote network monitoring devices, often called monitors or probes, are instruments that exist for the purpose of managing and/or monitoring a network. A company may employ many of these devices, up to one per network segment, to manage its networks.

RMON also appears as a software capability that is added to the software of certain network equipment, as well as software applications that could run on servers or clients. Despite the variety of these approaches, the RMON capability serves as a dedicated network management resource available for activities ranging from long-term data collection and analysis or for ad-hoc fire fighting.

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: The Three Main Network Protocols at a Glance

SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol): actively queries device status. The Manager sends queries to Agents to obtain operating parameters (CPU utilization, error counts, etc.) from routers, switches and other devices. When a device is abnormal, the Agent proactively sends a "Trap" (alert) to notify the Manager.

Syslog (system log protocol): passively collects event messages. Each device sends its own event log to a central Syslog server, so administrators can review the anomaly records of all devices in one place.

RMON (Remote Monitoring): in-depth analysis of the network traffic itself. It can be deployed on every network segment to collect packet-level statistics over the long term, for trend analysis and troubleshooting.

Functional Comparison of the Three Protocols

SNMP   → Device status management (Manager ↔ Agent)
         Active polling + Trap alerts
         RFC 1157

Syslog → Event log transport (device → Syslog Server)
         Passive aggregation, centralized storage
         RFC 3164

RMON   → Network traffic analysis (Probe → analysis platform)
         One probe per network segment
         RFC 1757

Key Point — Choosing Protocols for GxP Compliance

In a GxP network environment, Syslog most directly supports the audit trail requirement: all network events are timestamped and stored centrally for auditors to review. SNMP supports the real-time performance data needed for SLA monitoring. RMON is suited to in-depth investigation when a network performance problem is suspected.

Analogy

SNMP is like a nurse taking blood pressure at regular intervals (actively checking devices); Syslog is like the ward's event logbook (any anomaly is written in automatically); RMON is like an ECG machine (continuously recording fine waveform detail for playback and analysis when needed).

Appendix 12 — Glossary

Original Text — App. 12 §1 Definitions

Tutorial Commentary

Core Concept: Three Pairs of Easily Confused Terms

Assessment vs. Audit
The key difference is independence: an assessment can be performed by internal subject matter experts (independence is not required); an audit must be systematic, independent and documented. A department cannot audit itself, but it can self-assess.

Qualification vs. Validation
Qualification addresses whether equipment/a system can achieve its intended function; validation addresses whether a process can consistently produce product that meets specifications. Qualification (IQ/OQ/PQ) is usually a prerequisite for validation.

Building Block vs. Computerized System
A building block is a group of standardized components designed for maximum reuse; a computerized system is a complete system that meets specific user requirements. Standard infrastructure components such as routers and switches are not regarded as computerized systems, so their compliance requirements are lighter.

Key Point — Periodic Review

The frequency of periodic review depends on three factors: complexity, criticality and rate of change. GxP application systems are usually reviewed annually; infrastructure systems may be reviewed every two years. The purpose of the review is to confirm that the system remains in its validated state; if a deviation is found, revalidation must be initiated.

Key Point — Privileged Access

Privileged access (system administrator accounts) is a high-risk control point in GxP systems. Every privileged access action should be recorded and approved, and included in periodic review. 21 CFR Part 11 requires audit trails to capture who did what and when, so the activity records of privileged accounts are especially important.

Analogy — Assessment vs. Audit

An assessment is like a head chef tasting the dishes in their own restaurant and noting improvements; an audit is like a Michelin inspector arriving anonymously and judging objectively against standards. The former can be done by yourself; the latter must come from an external or independent person.
