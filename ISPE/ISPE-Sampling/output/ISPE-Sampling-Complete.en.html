1.1

Background

Why effective sampling matters — and what goes wrong when it fails

Original Text (English)

Effective sampling is of paramount importance to the success of any pharmaceutical critical utility system. Unfortunately, there is very little regulatory guidance with regards to sampling. Extracting a representative sample from a utility system is an involved and complicated process and error may be introduced due to:

  • Environmental conditions
  • Sample valve design
  • Sample point location
  • Technique
  • Container type
  • Sample handling
  • Sample storage and transport
  • Other factors

The impact of these factors is widespread and occurs throughout industry. Errors due to sampling are so common that the 1993 US FDA "Guide to the Inspection of High Purity Water Systems" specifically addresses sampling errors in recommendations to their field inspectors by stating that:

"It is expected that [Water For Injection systems] be essentially sterile. Since sampling frequently is performed in non-sterile areas and is not truly aseptic, occasional low level [bacteria] counts due to sampling errors may occur." — US FDA, Guide to the Inspection of High Purity Water Systems, 1993

Improper sampling may be responsible for generating Out Of Specification (OOS) data and prompting investigations when a system is actually producing and delivering acceptable quality. Conversely, improper sampling may also create a more dangerous scenario where the quality is actually OOS but the data incorrectly indicates that the quality is acceptable.

In either case, there could be negative impact on company image, cost, productivity, ethics, and regulatory liability, resulting from improper sampling. Identifying the elements of proper sampling and eliminating the underlying reasons for these negative consequences formed the primary driving force behind this ISPE Good Practice Guide.

Commentary and Practical Guide

Core Topic: Why Does Sampling Matter So Much?

The utility systems of a pharmaceutical plant (water for injection, purified water, steam, compressed air) are the foundation of product quality. The problem is that you can never test the entire system: you can only draw a small cup of water to represent the whole piping system.

This assumption of "representativeness" is the starting point of every problem.

Key Regulatory Background: The 1993 FDA Guidance

As early as 1993, FDA recognized how common sampling error is and told its field investigators that an occasional low microbial count may be a sampling error rather than true contamination of the system.

This is a rare example of a regulator acknowledging that "the act of measurement itself" introduces error. Thirty years later, this recognition remains a challenge for the industry.

Two-Way Risk: Both Errors Are Equally Dangerous

  • False positive OOS: The system is actually fine, but sampling error makes the data appear out of specification → unnecessary OOS investigation, production stoppage, wasted manpower
  • False negative OOS: The system truly has a problem, but the sample missed the point of contamination → data appear to pass → the problem is masked and the risk to drug safety rises

The latter carries far greater ethical and regulatory risk than the former.

CDMO Practical Application

In a CDMO environment, the consequences of sampling error are more complex: your clients (brand-name pharmaceutical companies) will review your OOS investigation reports, and any sampling-related OOS affects the contractual relationship and audit scores. Establishing an SOP for complete root cause analysis of "sampling error" is an important indicator of quality system maturity.

What is a Sample?

Defining the intent and inherent limitation of sampling

Original Text (English)

Simply stated, the intent of sampling is to take a small but representative portion of a much larger stream, where the sample collected accurately represents the content of the larger stream.

The sample collected should not be altered or changed in any way because the sampling process, but this is an almost impossibly difficult proposition as all sampled utilities come into contact with air, containers, etc. during the sampling process.

Commentary and Practical Guide

Core Definition: Representative Sample

The essence of a sample: a small portion taken from a large system, which must faithfully reflect the characteristics of the whole system.

This sounds simple, but the source text immediately points out that it is an almost unattainable ideal. Any sampling action exposes the sample to the outside world (air, containers, connectors) and thereby changes the state of the sample itself.

Analogy: A Chef Tasting Soup

Imagine a head chef tasting one spoonful from a big pot of soup to judge the saltiness of the whole pot. If:

  • The soup's ingredients are uniformly dissolved (salt, sugar) → any spoonful represents the whole pot
  • The soup has ingredients that sink to the bottom (chunks of meat) → skimming the surface will never tell you what is at the bottom

Pharmaceutical sampling is the same: chemical components are like salt, biological components are like chunks of meat. This analogy is developed fully in section 1.1.6.

Key Insight: The Act of Sampling Is Itself a Disturbance

The source text explicitly acknowledges: "all utility systems being sampled are exposed to air, containers, etc. during sampling". This means:

  • Microbial sampling → airborne bacteria may enter
  • TOC sampling → the container itself may release organic compounds
  • Conductivity sampling → container material and inadequate rinsing will affect the result

This is exactly why this guide exists: to tell you how to minimize these disturbances.

Practice Questions

  1. Does your plant have a standardized specification for WFI sampling containers (sample bottles)? Are the material, volume and pretreatment method covered by an SOP?
  2. When your microbiology analyst opens a container to sample in a non-sterile environment, what risk does this action introduce? How can that risk be quantified?
  3. What does "representative sample" require statistically? Can a single sampling point represent the entire distribution loop?

Why Do We Sample?

The five purposes of pharmaceutical utility sampling programs

Original Text (English)

It is most desirable to monitor quality parameters using in-line, on-line, or at-line monitors, which minimize and, in some cases, eliminate, sample handling issues completely. However, when the quality attribute of concern cannot be analyzed with an instrument or where an instrument is not present, a sample has to be removed from a stream and evaluated off-line. In most cases, these attributes are microbiological or endotoxin in nature.

Sampling generally occurs for one or more of the following reasons:

  • Regulatory requirements — compendial requirements for CQAs
  • Quality control purposes — ensure utility meets established quality parameters; samples collected exactly as the utility is used in manufacturing
  • Process control purposes — confirm a particular process is operating under a state of control; frequency differs from QC sampling
  • Technical, investigational or diagnostic purposes — triggered by Alert Level excursions; as-needed basis
  • Test method validation purposes — periodic duplicate sampling to document consistency
Note: There is no purpose classified as "sampling for informational purposes only." Every sample point should have a specific purpose. The authors recommend abandoning this designation as being too vague and non-specific.

When developing a sampling program, a risk-based approach should be utilized to establish or adjust sampling frequencies based on data collected over time. Alert (2σ) and Action (3σ) Levels are established based on actual operating conditions.

Commentary and Practical Guide

Monitoring Levels: Four Monitoring Modes

Because of technical limitations, microbial and endotoxin testing can currently be performed almost only off-line. This is why sampling technique is especially important for these attributes.

Five Sampling Purposes: Each with Its Own Frequency and Location Logic

  • Regulatory compliance sampling: Frequency is set by the pharmacopoeia or the competent authority; this is a basic compliance requirement and cannot be omitted
  • Quality control sampling: Samples are taken in a way that simulates manufacturing use; all specified quality attributes of that grade must be tested
  • Process control sampling: Confirms the performance of a purification step; only the contaminants targeted by that step need to be tested, not all attributes
  • Investigational (diagnostic) sampling: Initiated only when an alert level is triggered; frequency is not fixed and depends on need
  • Method validation sampling: Repeated sampling at regular intervals to verify the consistency of the method itself

Analogy: Health Check vs. Emergency Room

Regulatory compliance sampling = the routine annual health check (fixed frequency, fixed items)

Quality control sampling = the final health confirmation before hospital discharge (confirming standards are met before release)

Process control sampling = vital-sign monitoring during surgery (monitoring only the surgery-related indicators)

Investigational sampling = the emergency room (you go only when there are symptoms, and tests target the symptoms)

Method validation sampling = calibrating the blood pressure monitor (confirming the measuring tool itself is accurate)

Why "For Informational Purposes Only" Sampling Should Be Abolished

ISPE explicitly recommends eliminating the category "for informational purposes only". The reasons are:

  • Data without a purpose cannot have defined acceptance criteria
  • Vague categories are easily challenged by regulators
  • Every sampling point should have a clear decision logic

This matters especially for CDMOs: your client's auditor will ask, "What is the purpose of this sampling point? What happens if the result is out of limits?"

Alert Level and Action Level: 2σ and 3σ

Alert Level = 2σ: A warning signal that the system may be drifting from its normal range. It does not necessarily require immediate corrective action, but it must be monitored and recorded.

Action Level = 3σ: The system has deviated from its normal range, and corrective action must be taken to return it to a state of control.

These limits are established from actual operating data, not set arbitrarily. An effective sampling program lets you accumulate enough historical data to give the limits statistical meaning.

  2. Quality control sampling requires "fully replicating the manufacturing use". What challenges does this pose in practice? Do your current SOPs meet this requirement?
  3. How were the Alert and Action Levels for your WFI system established? Are they based on statistical data or taken directly from pharmacopoeial limits?

How Often Do We Need to Sample?

Risk-based frequency determination for different utility grades

Original Text (English)

Sampling frequency depends on the system and the grade of the critical utility.

For Water For Injection (WFI): it is an expectation that water samples will be taken from each Point Of Use (POU) and sample location in the distribution system at least once per week during validation. Industry has continued to utilize this same sampling frequency beyond the completion of the Performance Qualification, although it is not mandated.

By contrast, purified water has no such preconceived sampling requirements, so the end user is not mandated to follow any specific schedule during or following validation.

Pharmaceutical steam, compressed air, and process gases have different frequencies during validation and ongoing operation.

When no sampling frequency is specified, a risk-based approach may be utilized in conjunction with the purpose of sampling to establish initial sampling frequencies. As data is generated and an operating baseline is established, the collected data should be reviewed to determine if the operating limits, Alert and Action Levels, and/or the sampling frequency may be adjusted (increased or decreased) based on data generated on system operation.

Commentary and Practical Guide

Risk-Based Frequency Adjustment: Let the Data Speak

The source text raises an important concept: sampling frequency is not fixed but should be adjusted dynamically as data accumulate.

  • Initial phase: Higher frequency, to establish an operating baseline
  • Stable phase: If the data show the system is in control, the frequency can be reduced
  • When risk rises: For example, seasonal changes, after sanitization, or after system maintenance, the frequency should be increased

This kind of dynamic adjustment must be supported by a complete trend analysis system.

CDMO Practice: Frequency Decisions After PQ

Weekly WFI sampling is an "industry expectation", not a "regulatory mandate". Many CDMOs keep the weekly frequency even after PQ is complete, because:

  • Client contracts usually require compliance with industry best practice
  • Auditors assume this frequency by default, and reducing it requires strong, documented risk assessment support
  • WFI is the highest-risk utility system, so a conservative strategy is reasonable

Practice Questions

  1. Does your plant's WFI sampling program have clear "frequency adjustment triggers"? Under what circumstances would you increase or reduce the frequency?
  2. After PQ, have you ever justifiably reduced the WFI sampling frequency? What risk assessment documentation supported that decision?
  3. How is the sampling frequency for compressed air and process gases determined in your plant? Is there an independent risk assessment?

Where Do We Need to Sample?

Location selection driven by purpose and engineering judgment

Original Text (English)

Sampling locations are determined by the purpose of sampling and the information expected from the sampling location. Careful consideration and sound engineering judgment should be utilized to distinguish between sampling locations for:

  • Regulatory Compliance
  • Quality Control
  • Process Control
  • Investigational and Diagnostic purposes
Commentary and Practical Guide

The "Purpose Determines Location" Logic

Sampling locations are not chosen at random but derived backward from the sampling purpose. The reason for installing each sampling valve should correspond to a clear sampling purpose.

  • Regulatory compliance locations: Usually the Point of Use (POU), i.e., where the water is used in manufacturing
  • Process control locations: Usually intermediate points within the purification steps (e.g., RO membrane outlet, after UV treatment)
  • Investigational locations: Determined by the problem hypothesis; planned in advance but activated on demand

The Importance of Engineering Judgment

The source text emphasizes "sound engineering judgment". This is not an empty phrase: engineers must understand fluid dynamics, the formation of dead legs, and the effect of piping design on microbial accumulation in order to correctly judge whether a sampling location is representative.

For example, a sampling valve at the lowest point of the system, where water velocity is slowest, may be more prone to microbial excursions than a valve on a high-velocity main line. This does not mean the system has a problem; it is simply an issue of location selection.

Practice Questions

  1. Are there any dead legs in your plant's WFI distribution loop? Do those locations have sampling points?
  2. In your sampling program, is the purpose of each sampling point clearly documented? Can each be mapped to one of the four purposes above?
  3. If your WFI system had a microbial excursion tomorrow, which investigational (diagnostic) sampling locations in your SOP could be activated immediately?
1.1.6

Are All Quality Attributes Uniformly Distributed?

The most critical paradigm shift in pharmaceutical utility sampling

Paradigm Shift Alert

The answer is a resounding NO — and this is one of the most important paradigm shifts that the reader should understand.

The answer is a definitive "No", and this is one of the most important conceptual shifts a reader must grasp when evaluating a sampling program.

Original Text (English)

The answer is a resounding no, and this is one of the most important paradigm shifts that the reader should understand when defining and evaluating a sampling program.

In pharmaceutical water systems:

  • If a quality attribute is a chemical constituent (e.g., nitrates, conductivity, dissolved metals, TOC) then the constituent is considered uniformly or homogeneously distributed (Second Law of Thermodynamics).
  • However, if the quality attribute is biological in nature (e.g., bacteria, endotoxin), then the quality attribute will not be uniformly distributed. Non-uniform or heterogeneously distributed attributes are much more challenging to sample accurately as these attributes may vary from point to point in a system.

A significant portion of this Guide is devoted to providing guidance on the most effective methods for accurate sample collection involving these heterogeneously distributed quality attributes.

In pharmaceutical steam systems, compressed air, and process gas systems, attributes may or may not be uniformly distributed and sampling programs must take this into account.

Commentary and Practical Guide

The Second Law of Thermodynamics: Why Are Chemical Components Uniform?

Once chemical components dissolve in water, the molecules naturally tend toward uniform distribution (entropy increases). Conductivity reflects ion concentration and nitrate reflects molecular concentration; these molecules keep moving through Brownian motion in liquid water and eventually reach thermodynamic equilibrium.

In other words: if conductivity in your WFI piping is out of limits, you will measure an excursion at any sampling point, because conductivity is uniformly distributed.

Why Are Bacteria Non-Uniformly Distributed?

Bacteria are not chemical molecules. They are living organisms with the following behaviors:

  • Adhesion: Bacteria actively or passively attach to surfaces such as pipe walls, dead legs and valve sealing surfaces. Attached bacteria do not flow in the liquid phase.
  • Biofilm formation: Attached bacteria secrete a polysaccharide matrix and form a biofilm. The number of bacteria in a biofilm can be more than 1000 times the number of planktonic bacteria in the fluid.
  • Colony formation: Bacteria grow in colonies rather than spreading evenly: one dead leg may hold large numbers of bacteria while a pipe section a few centimeters away is completely free of them.
  • Detachment: Bacteria in a biofilm detach in large numbers when flow rate changes or under thermal shock (such as sanitization), entering the liquid phase and causing a sudden spike in counts at a given point in time.
Key Concept: Heterogeneous Distribution Explained

Analogy: Salt vs. Fish in the Ocean

Imagine the ocean:

  • Salt (NaCl): Uniformly dissolved throughout — a sample from any depth or location gives you the same salinity reading. Salt behaves like a chemical attribute.
  • Fish: Clustered in schools, near reefs, at specific depths — you could cast a net 10 times and catch nothing, then cast it in the same area a minute later and pull up hundreds. Fish behave like biological attributes.

If you're measuring the "fish quality" of the ocean, one sample from one spot at one time tells you almost nothing about the whole. The same applies to bacteria in a pharmaceutical water system.

Implications for Endotoxin

Endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) is the cell wall fragment of gram-negative bacteria. Its distribution in a water system follows the same heterogeneous pattern as bacteria — because endotoxin is released when bacteria die or shed their membranes, it tends to concentrate where bacteria concentrate.

However, endotoxin is more complex: once released into the water, it can aggregate and disaggregate depending on temperature, pH, and ionic strength. This further complicates representative sampling.

Analogy Analysis and CDMO Implications

The Deeper Meaning of the Ocean Analogy

Salt = chemical attribute: No matter where in the Atlantic you sample, the salinity is about the same. One sample is enough to understand the salinity level of the whole system.

Fish = biological attribute: Fish gather near reefs, at particular depths and in particular seasons. Sample the same patch of ocean ten times and you may come back empty-handed nine times and have a huge catch once. This does not mean there are "no fish" in the sea or that "the fish have a quality problem"; it only means your sampling time and place did not capture where the fish were.

Key conclusion: For biological attributes, where and when you sample are inseparable from what you measure. A negative result does not mean the whole system is free of problems.

Far-Reaching Implications for CDMO Aseptic Filling Plants

  • "Hot spots" in the WFI loop: Even if several of your POU samples pass, a low-flow branch or dead leg may still harbor a biofilm. This is the "fish" in a real system: that you did not catch it does not mean it does not exist.
  • Complexity of OOS investigations: A microbial excursion at one WFI POU may have a cause entirely unrelated to that point: a biofilm may have detached from an upstream dead leg and traveled with the flow to that sampling point. The sampling location is not the same as the source of the problem.
  • Post-sanitization sampling strategy: After sanitization, cell wall fragments of dead bacteria (i.e., endotoxin) are released into the water in large quantities. During this period, WFI may pass for microbial counts but exceed limits for endotoxin, because the two have different distribution patterns.
  • Audit preparation: FDA and EMA inspectors are increasingly well versed in the concept of sampling non-uniformity. They may question whether your sampling locations truly represent the microbial risk of the system.

Practical Action: How to Deal with Non-Uniform Distribution

  • Make sure sampling points cover the highest-risk locations of the system (dead legs, low-flow zones, lowest-temperature points)
  • Do not sample only at POUs: design a sampling program that also covers process control locations
  • Analyze trends regularly: a sampling point that is persistently high may be a sign of an upstream biofilm
  • Design the sampling strategies before and after sanitization separately

Practice Questions

  1. Which locations in your WFI distribution system are most likely to form biofilm? Are these locations included in your routine sampling program?
  2. If the microbial count at one of your WFI sampling points suddenly rises while all other points remain normal, what is your first reaction? Is it "sampling error" or "localized biofilm detachment"? How do you tell the difference?
  3. Does your plant have a purpose-designed post-sanitization sampling plan for the WFI system? Does that plan take into account the non-uniform distribution of endotoxin?
  4. Why do a conductivity excursion and a microbial excursion require completely different root cause analysis strategies? Explain using the concepts in this section.

  • HVAC systems for cleanrooms
  • Environmental monitoring
  • Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs)

Commentary and Practical Guide

The Importance of Scope Boundaries

This guide has a very clear boundary: its responsibility ends when the sample reaches the laboratory. Subsequent analytical methods, data interpretation and statistical analysis are covered by other guidance (such as USP method guidance).

This boundary is very practical: sampling and analysis are two independent, quality-critical steps, and discussing them together would only blur responsibilities.

Engineers as Core Readers: Why?

This guide explicitly names "design engineers" as the first group of readers. This is because the foundation of sampling quality lies in design, not in operation.

  • Sampling valve design determines whether stagnant water will accumulate
  • The location of the sampling point determines whether the sample is representative
  • Piping material and surface roughness affect biofilm formation

A poorly designed sampling system cannot yield truly meaningful samples, no matter how rigorously it is operated.

Out-of-Scope Items: Also Worth Knowing

This guide does not cover:

  • Cleanroom HVAC environmental monitoring → see ISO 14644 and PIC/S Annex 1
  • API and finished product sampling → see ICH Q7 and the pharmacopoeias

Understanding these boundaries helps you know clearly during an audit which SOP governs a given issue, avoiding gray areas between documents.

  2. When your design engineers take part in the design of new equipment or piping, do they consider design specifications for sampling points? Is there a standard for selecting sampling valves?
  3. Are HVAC environmental monitoring and utility system sampling covered by an integrated monitoring strategy, or are they managed independently?

Benefits

The business case for investing in proper sampling practices

Original Text (English)

This Guide aims to:

  • Provide industry with standards and best practices for sampling in a comprehensive single reference.
  • Help to minimize sampling errors and their contribution to Out of Specification (OOS) results, which can cause costly work and production stoppages, investigations, and disruptions in manufacturing, Quality Control (QC)/Quality Assurance (QA), and engineering organizations.

This Guide is expected to be of benefit to laboratory, QC/QA, engineering, facilities, and operations personnel.

Commentary and Practical Guide

The Business Logic of the Benefits

The benefits of improving sampling practices can be quantified along three dimensions:

  • Cost savings: Fewer false positive OOS → fewer unnecessary investigation costs and production-stoppage losses
  • Quality improvement: Fewer false negative OOS → less risk of real quality problems being masked
  • Regulatory compliance: Plants with a sound sampling system find it easier to pass FDA/EMA inspections

CDMO Perspective: The Cost of an OOS

In a mid-sized CDMO, the direct cost of a single WFI microbial OOS investigation typically includes:

  • QA/QC staff investigation time (typically 20-40 hours)
  • Additional sampling and testing costs
  • Production delays (if production is stopped as a result)
  • Client communication and document preparation

If the cause is ultimately confirmed as "sampling error", all of these costs were avoidable. The ROI of investing in sampling training and equipment is very clear.

  2. If sampling-related OOS could be reduced by 50%, how much would you estimate your plant would save each year?
  3. Does your plant regularly qualify its sampling personnel? Does this training program include a documented competency assessment?

Objectives

What this guide is designed to accomplish

Original Text (English)

This Guide aims to establish good practices for sampling to minimize sample contamination from:

  • Human contact
  • Error
  • Atmospheric or environmental conditions

Which could alter laboratory results and provide inaccurate data.

Commentary and Practical Guide

Three Categories of Contamination Sources

  • Human contact: Particles from gloves, microorganisms in exhaled breath, touching the bottle mouth, etc. People are among the largest sources of sampling contamination
  • Error: Insufficient flushing time, rinse liquid not fully drained, incorrect sampling sequence
  • Atmospheric/environmental: Opening containers to sample in a non-sterile environment, an air-conditioning vent blowing directly onto the sampling point, temperature and humidity affecting sample stability

People Are the Hardest Variable to Control

Machine behavior is predictable, but human behavior depends on training, attitude and the person's condition that day. This is why:

  • A sampling SOP must include detailed illustrated steps, not text alone
  • Sampling personnel must receive periodic retraining and competency assessment
  • Microbial sampling should be performed by personnel trained in aseptic technique

  2. Have your sampling personnel been assessed in aseptic sampling technique? How often is the assessment performed?
  3. Does your plant record the sampler ID in the record of every microbial sample? Is this data used in trend analysis?

Key Concepts / Terms

Foundational definitions used throughout the ISPE Sampling Guide

Original Definitions (English)

Alert Level

A level or range that, when exceeded, indicates that a process may have drifted from its normal operating range. May be established at two standard deviations (2σ) from the mean. Constitutes a warning and does not necessarily require a corrective action.

Action Level

A level established that, when exceeded, indicates that a process from its normal operating range. Typically established at three standard deviations (3σ) from the mean. Corrective actions should be taken to bring the process back into its normal operating range.

Contaminant / Impurity

Contaminant: Any foreign component present in another substance; used synonymously with impurity in this Guide.

Impurity: A foreign agent that is introduced as part of a process; used synonymously with contaminant in this Guide.

Note: Additional key terms (In-line, On-line, At-line, Off-line monitoring; Representative Sample; Measurement Uncertainty) are introduced and defined in later chapters of this Guide, where they are applied in context.

In-Depth Analysis of Terms

Alert Level vs. Action Level: The Statistical Basis

These two terms are used extremely often in pharmaceutical utility systems, but their statistical basis must be understood:

  • Normal distribution assumption: These limits assume the data follow a normal distribution. Microbial data, however, are usually not normally distributed; they tend to be right-skewed (counts are very low most of the time and occasionally high). For this reason, many plants take the logarithm of microbial data before establishing control limits.
  • 2σ (about 95.4% confidence level): An alert level means that in a normal system, roughly 1 in every 22 samples may exceed this limit purely by statistical chance, which does not mean the system really has a problem.
  • 3σ (about 99.7% confidence level): The probability of exceeding the action level in a normal system is about 0.3%. If it is exceeded frequently, the system has most likely shifted from its normal state.

Analogy: Body Temperature Monitoring

Think of your WFI system as a patient's body temperature:

  • Normal range: 36.1 – 37.2°C (corresponds to the normal operating range)
  • Alert Level: 37.5°C (slight fever, needs observation, no need for an injection right away)
  • Action Level: 38.5°C (fever, immediate action required)

There is no need to panic over an occasional 37.5°C reading, but if it keeps recurring, you should start investigating for a possible underlying infection. This is the point of trend analysis.

Summary of Monitoring Terminology (Preview of Later Chapters)

Because of technical limitations, microbial and endotoxin testing can currently be performed almost only off-line, which makes the rigor of the sampling procedure critical to the reliability of the results.

The Relationship Between "Representative Sample" and "Measurement Uncertainty"

Every measurement result has an uncertainty. For utility system sampling, the uncertainty comes from:

  • Random error in the sampling process (differences in personnel technique)
  • Temporal variation of the system (counts fluctuate over time)
  • Spatial heterogeneity (true differences between locations)
  • Error of the analytical method itself (culture medium, incubation conditions)

Only by understanding measurement uncertainty can you correctly interpret a result that is "close to but not over the action level": it represents a range, not an exact number.

Introduction

Water as the most widely used pharmaceutical material — roles and sampling fundamentals

2.1 Introduction

Water is one of the most widely used materials in the pharmaceutical industry and is commonly used as a:

  • Product or Ingredient
  • Cleaning agent
  • Sterilizing media
  • Analytical agent
  • Formulation aid

When water is used for pharmaceutical purposes it needs to be tested to ensure that it meets monograph requirements or other specifications for applicable CQAs (Critical Quality Attributes). When water is extracted for sampling purposes at any point in a system, proper sampling is the key to ensuring that the collected sample accurately represents the quality of the water at that particular point in the system.

When water is sampled in the same way as it is used during manufacturing, sampling is considered the best indicator available of the quality of water going into a product.

Key Principle: Sample as You Use

The closer a sampling method mirrors actual manufacturing use, the more representative the result. This principle drives every decision in this guide — from valve selection to flushing volume to sample container choice.

Water Types in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

All major pharmacopoeias define at minimum two compendial bulk grades:

  • Purified Water (PW) — for non-parenteral products, equipment cleaning, and general manufacturing
  • Water For Injection (WFI) — for parenteral products, ophthalmic preparations, and final equipment rinse in aseptic processing

The European Pharmacopoeia additionally defines Highly Purified Water (HPW) — meeting WFI chemical specifications but produced by a non-distillation process (e.g., double-pass RO + UF).

All pharmacopoeias (USP, EP, JP, Chinese Pharmacopoeia) require monitoring of the following CQAs:

  • Conductivity — ionic purity indicator
  • TOC (Total Organic Carbon) — organic contamination indicator
  • Endotoxin — WFI and HPW only; LAL or rFC method
  • Bioburden — not a monograph requirement, but universally tested
Commentary & CDMO Context

The five roles of water

Water plays five roles in pharmaceutical manufacturing at the same time: product ingredient, cleaning agent, sterilization medium, analytical reagent and formulation aid. In a CDMO environment, the same water loop may be used on the same day to prepare and clean for several clients' products, so a failure in water quality monitoring has cross-product impact.

Definition of a CQA

A Critical Quality Attribute (CQA) is a physical, chemical, biological or microbiological property that directly affects the safety, efficacy or quality of the product. For a water system, the CQAs usually include conductivity, TOC, endotoxin (WFI) and microbial content.

Analogy: water quality is like a city water supply

Think of a city's tap water system: the main pipeline (storage tank → distribution loop) represents overall water quality, and each faucet (POU) represents an actual point of use. Even when the main pipeline water is acceptable, the rubber washer in the end faucet or stagnant water in the pipe can still introduce microbial contamination. This is why ISPE stresses that "sampling should mimic the way the water is used".

CDMO practice: the challenge of a multi-client water system

At a multi-product CDMO site, the PW and WFI loops serve the product lines of different clients. The sampling strategy must take into account:

  • Individual client requirements for water specifications (which may be stricter than the pharmacopoeia)
  • Contamination risk of the shared loop (cleaning validation + water quality confirmation)
  • Several POUs in use at the same time during peak production, and the effect of changes in water pressure and flow velocity on microbial levels

Regulatory focus: "sample the way the water is used"

The 1993 FDA inspection guide clearly requires that water be sampled in a way that replicates, as far as possible, how it is used in manufacturing. This means that if a hose and a heat exchanger are connected during production, the same must be done when sampling — otherwise the sampling result is not representative and will be challenged in a deviation investigation.

2.1.1

Homogeneously Distributed Impurities

Chemical impurities, conductivity, TOC — same concentration throughout the loop

2.1.1.1 Homogeneously Distributed Impurities

Chemical impurities, small particulates, and other soluble impurities are typically expected to be homogeneously distributed throughout a distribution system because of the solubility and nature of these impurities, and because of chemical equilibrium processes.

Since the impurity levels at any one location in a distribution system should be identical to the impurity levels at every other location within the distribution system, sampling for homogeneously distributed impurities may not be required at every POU and may be performed at a limited number of locations, provided that there is no likelihood of downstream sources of contamination.

Homogeneously distributed impurities are candidates for continuous monitoring using on-line, in-line, or at-line instruments if the impurity levels at the instrumentation locations have been validated through sampling and laboratory analysis to be predictive of the levels at all POUs.

Sampling and testing of these impurities is typically done at the return of a distribution loop, but may be done in other locations. Impurities falling into the homogeneously distributed category include:

  • Ionic impurities (detected as electrical conductivity or resistivity)
  • Organic impurities (detected as TOC)
  • Nitrates (EP requirement for PW and WFI)
  • Heavy metals (EP requirement for PW — not required if conductivity meets WFI limits)
"Localized sources of chemical impurities may be introduced to a system downstream of a particular point... such as from hoses, gaskets, or a POU heat exchanger may contaminate the water as it exits the system at one particular location."

Sub-loops, parallel loops, or other isolated portions may not be accurately represented by on-line instrumentation in the main distribution system. In these cases, additional instruments or manual POU testing may be required.

2.1.1.2 Heterogeneously Distributed Impurities

Bacteria and endotoxin are attributes that are not uniformly distributed throughout a water system. The majority of bacteria present in the system (99.9 to 99.99%) reside in biofilm — attached to system surfaces, not free-floating.

It is the small fraction (0.01 to 0.1%) of free floating or planktonic bacteria that contribute to the bacteria and endotoxin levels detected by sampling.

Biofilm development occurs more rapidly in areas of low or no flow. Examples of low-flow areas that allow bacteria to attach include:

  • Within any non-flowing side connections of any length, including dead legs
  • On the upstream/wet side of valve sealing surfaces
  • On the water-stopping sealing surfaces within the valve
  • On the downstream side of valve surfaces (which may remain wet or moist)
  • On fittings and gaskets used to connect a hose or other connector
  • Within the hose or connector to the actual location of water use
  • Within any additional external device: flow meters, pressure regulators, heat exchangers, check valves, hoses, fittings, adapters

Because of variable local flow conditions, bacteria and endotoxin populations may vary widely at different locations. Therefore, sampling is required of each POU location to confirm the presence or absence of bacteria. Sampling only the supply and return is insufficient to characterize bacterial populations throughout the system.

Commentary: strategic implications of homogeneous vs. heterogeneous impurities

Homogeneous impurities

Key conclusion: conductivity and TOC have the same concentration throughout the loop, so an in-line instrument at the loop return can replace manual sampling at each individual POU. This is the main rationale for continuous monitoring in pharmaceutical water systems. However, sampling must first be used to verify that the reading at the instrument location really represents the concentration at all POUs.

Exception: sub-loop contamination

If chemical contamination could be introduced in a sub-loop or downstream of a POU (for example, plasticizer leaching from hoses or organics extracted from rubber gaskets), the in-line instrument on the main loop cannot detect such localized contamination. Manual sampling or a separate instrument must be added at the sub-loop or POU.

Heterogeneous impurities — the truth about biofilm

99.9–99.99% of the bacteria in a system live in biofilm, attached to surfaces such as pipe walls and valve seats, rather than suspended in the water. Sampling can detect only planktonic bacteria, which make up just 0.01–0.1% of the total bacterial population in the system.

Practical implication: a low microbial count does not mean the system is clean; if biofilm sloughs off after an upset (a temperature change or a sudden increase in flow velocity), the count can suddenly spike. This is why alert/action levels and trend analysis matter more than any single result.

High-risk CDMO scenario: deadlegs and low-flow zones

In a complex multi-product facility, the piping layout often creates "temporary deadlegs", for example branch lines to POUs that have not been used for a long time and connection lines to decommissioned equipment. ISPE clearly identifies these as biofilm hot spots. CDMO operators must:

  • Flush or close off unused POUs regularly (lock out / tag out)
  • Perform microbial sampling to confirm quality before returning them to use
  • Keep deadleg length within 3D (3 times the pipe diameter, an industry convention)

Analogy: biofilm is like limescale in a shower head

The inside of a shower head that is not used for a long time accumulates biofilm (what is commonly called "scale bacteria"). The moment you turn on the tap, large numbers of bacteria are flushed out — this is the danger that "overnight stagnation" poses to a WFI system. The solution for pharmaceutical water systems: maintain circulation flow velocity and carry out regular hot water sanitization or periodic steam sterilization.

Purpose of Sampling

Five categories: regulatory, consensus standards, process control, quality control, investigative

2.1.2 Purpose of Sampling

The purpose of sampling should be identified and documented for each potential sample location within a system as early as possible during the design process. This process should justify and document the requirement for sampling and identify the reason for sampling at a particular location.

The purpose of any potential sampling location should fall into one or more of the following categories:

  1. Regulatory requirements
  2. Consensus standards
  3. Process control purposes
  4. Quality control purposes
  5. Technical, investigational, or diagnostic purposes

The justification for a potential sampling location should also consider:

  • The impurities of interest at the potential location
  • The type of valve and other accessories best suited for the location
  • Whether sampling should be performed at an established frequency or on an event-based frequency (i.e., reaching an alert or action level)
  • Accessibility to staff and safety considerations
  • Any special considerations for sample handling after collection

2.1.2.1 Regulatory Requirements

All pharmacopoeias define two bulk grades of water for pharmaceutical purposes: Purified Water (PW) and Water For Injection (WFI). Highly Purified Water (HPW) is an additional grade defined by the European Pharmacopoeia.

The general consensus across pharmacopoeias is that the following impurities should be monitored:

  • Conductivity
  • TOC
  • Endotoxin (for WFI only)

Some pharmacopoeias also require testing for heavy metals and nitrates. While bacteria testing is not a monograph requirement, all pharmaceutical companies sample and test for bacteria.

The pharmaceutical industry references the 1993 US FDA "Guide to the Inspection of High Purity Water Systems" which stipulates the following maximum bacteria action limits:

  • PW: ≤ 100 cfu/mL
  • WFI: ≤ 10 cfu/100 mL

The pharmaceutical industry has universally adopted these limits. Thus, water systems are routinely sampled and tested for: Conductivity, TOC, Endotoxin (WFI only), and Bacteria.

2.1.2.2 Consensus Standards

Consensus standards such as ISO, ASTM, AAMI, and CLSI have application-specific standards for water purity. These may apply where the use of water within the facility is consistent with those standards. Water quality specifications from these standards may be found in Appendix 1 of the guide.

2.1.2.3 Process Control Purposes

Monitoring a water purification system for process control involves monitoring the performance and success of the various treatment processes for specific attributes of concern and includes following the quantitative trends of those attributes.

Process control indicating trigger values, such as Alert Levels and Action Levels, are typically used to assist in identifying when the process is deviating from its normal operating capabilities.

Alert Levels

Alert Levels should be set at levels near the top edge of normal data trends — typically at two standard deviations (2σ) from normal operating levels, based on analysis of historic performance data.

An occasional Alert Level excursion barely above the limit could be a normal expectation within the upper reaches of normal operating trends. However, frequent excursions should be considered out of trend. Responses include notification of quality, manufacturing, and maintenance personnel, along with closer attention to routine data.

Action Levels

Action Levels are typically set at three standard deviations (3σ) from normal operating levels. They should be:

  • Above normal data trends and indicative of routine process controls beginning to fail
  • Far enough from the specification so that remedial action can restore control before a specification excursion occurs

Resampling and retesting should be one of the first corrective actions. Where high values are confirmed, appropriate remediation activities should be instituted and the cause(s) determined to preclude recurrence.

Commentary: the five categories of sampling purpose

Why confirm the sampling purpose early in design?

ISPE stresses that the sampling purpose should be confirmed and documented as early as possible in the design process. This is because the location of sample points, the choice of valve type, the pipe size and the accessibility for personnel must be decided before the P&IDs and construction drawings are finished. Retrofitting later is expensive and difficult — this is the root cause of the unreasonable sampling plans at many facilities.

The FDA 1993 guide's action limits remain the industry standard

Although the USP, EP and JP pharmacopoeias do not specify bacterial count limits, the limits set in the 1993 FDA high purity water inspection guide (PW ≤100 cfu/mL, WFI ≤10 cfu/100 mL) have become the de facto standard that pharmaceutical plants worldwide actually apply. In deviation investigations and regulatory reviews, these numbers are the baseline.

Practice for statistically setting alert/action levels

The correct way to set Alert and Action levels:

  1. Collect at least 3–6 months of historical steady-state data
  2. Calculate the mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ)
  3. Alert Level = μ + 2σ; Action Level = μ + 3σ
  4. Make sure the Action Level is below the pharmacopoeial specification (leaving a buffer)

Common mistake: setting the Action Level directly at the pharmacopoeial specification, with no buffer. Such a setting leaves the system with no opportunity for early warning.

Analogy: alert/action levels are like a car dashboard

Alert Level = fuel warning light comes on (there is still fuel, but pay attention)
Action Level = urgent warning sound (refuel immediately)
Specification limit (OOS) = breakdown (the problem has already occurred)
A good system design gives you enough time to react before the "breakdown".

CDMO multi-client scenario: the challenge of alert levels

In a CDMO environment, the same water loop serves different clients, but each client may require different alert/action levels (the internal standards of some multinational pharmaceutical companies are stricter than the pharmacopoeia). Recommended approach: set the strictest common standard at the CDMO level and clearly document the compliance basis in the client contracts.

  1. A water system has historical WFI bioburden data with mean 0.5 cfu/100 mL and standard deviation 1.2 cfu/100 mL. Calculate the Alert Level (2σ) and Action Level (3σ). Are these below the FDA 10 cfu/100 mL action limit?
  2. Alert Level excursions occur at three POUs on the same day, all in the same sub-loop. What does the guide say about concurrent excursions at multiple locations? What is the appropriate next step?

2.1.2.4–2.1.2.5: Quality Control & Investigative Sampling

Duplicating manufacturing conditions; OOS investigations; diagnostic sampling

2.1.2.4 Quality Control Purposes

When monitoring for quality control of the water, the intent is to:

  1. Exactly duplicate the quality of water that is being used for manufacturing by collecting samples at routine intervals
  2. Collect water samples at the beginning of a period when the water is being drawn from the system for manufacturing use

Exact duplication of the preparatory procedures utilized by manufacturing should be used during sampling, and should include use of the same:

  • Outlet sanitization practice (if applicable)
  • Hose and gasket
  • Outlet flush procedure (if any)
  • Attached devices such as heat exchangers, reducers, adapters, check valves, and flow meters

The purpose of including all of these potential sources of contamination in the flow path for sampling is to attempt to duplicate the water quality delivered to the manufacturing process, including any heterogeneously distributed contaminants from associated biofilms on or within these components.

Hard-piped or otherwise permanent connections between the water system and manufacturing equipment, as well as automatic valves, create challenges for obtaining samples that duplicate the way water is used by manufacturing. One engineering solution may be to utilize a three-way divert valve that may be installed on the manufacturing equipment.

While POU valves with a built-in upstream sample port may be used when sampling for process control purposes, they are typically not used when sampling for quality control purposes. The only exceptions are:

  • The conduit is routinely sanitized with a validated procedure prior to water use for manufacturing (hot water, steam, or chemicals), so the conduit provides no added contamination
  • There is no feasible engineering solution that allows sampling exactly as used during manufacturing
  • The relevant attributes at that particular POU are not microbial (only homogeneously distributed chemical impurities, e.g., for a washer whose cycle ends with a hot drying step)

2.1.2.5 Sampling for Technical, Investigative, or Diagnostic Reasons

Sampling performed for technical, investigative, or diagnostic reasons may include:

  • Gathering data to better understand or pinpoint the root cause of a process deviation or specification excursion
  • To determine the extent of contamination within a system
  • To spot-check the long-term efficacy of a particular remedial action (e.g., from a CAPA response)

Additional sampling points installed for technical, investigative, or troubleshooting purposes may not be routinely sampled. Data from technical, investigative, or diagnostic sampling can also be used to establish a baseline for non-routinely measured water attributes which may be valuable in future investigations. Samplings of this nature are typically done only occasionally and only when needed for long-term profiling or as part of an investigation.

Alert, Action, and Out Of Specification (OOS) Levels

Sampling could be performed from the same sample location for both process control and quality control purposes. Different Alert and Action Levels could be established; however, process control levels may typically be established at tighter values than those for quality control.

For an excursion above a specification limit, an investigation should be performed to determine, correct, and prevent the cause. The particular investigation would be an OOS investigation and the impact of this water on the product should be assessed.

Commentary: strict requirements for quality control sampling

QC sampling: the core requirement of "simulating manufacturing"

The essence of quality control sampling is to replicate manufacturing practice. This means:

  • Use the same hoses and gaskets
  • Perform the same outlet sanitization procedure
  • Keep the same downstream equipment (heat exchangers, flow meters)
  • Sample before manufacturing starts (not install the equipment after sampling)

Direct impact: the sampling result represents "the quality of the water going into the product", not "the quality of the water in the loop".

Upstream POU sample ports: a trap for QC sampling

For convenience, many facilities install sample ports upstream of the POU valve. This is acceptable for process control (PC) sampling but is generally not acceptable for quality control (QC) sampling, because an upstream sample port bypasses the valve seat, hose, heat exchanger and other microbial hot spots and cannot represent the quality of the water actually going into the product.

CDMO practice: the challenge of sampling hard-piped manufacturing equipment

Modern aseptic filling equipment is usually hard-piped directly to the WFI system and cannot be disconnected for sampling. Solutions recommended by ISPE:

  • Three-way divert valve: installed at the equipment inlet, allowing the water flow to be diverted to a sample container
  • Sample port next to the equipment isolation valve: sample when water first flows into the manufacturing vessel
  • Direct disconnection of the piping (where feasible): direct the water flow to a sample container instead of into the equipment

These engineering solutions should be planned during facility design, rather than discovering the problem during validation.

Analogy: investigative sampling is like crime scene forensics

When routine monitoring finds an abnormality (for example, a sudden high microbial count at one POU), investigative sampling works like crime scene forensics: add sample points near the problem and narrow the scope step by step to find the source of contamination (perhaps a damaged valve seat or a section of piping that has formed a deadleg). Data from investigative sampling are normally not used for routine release but for root cause analysis.

2.2 Determining Sampling Locations

Risk-based approach; loop geometry; critical points; microbiological hot spots

2.2 Determining Sampling Locations

Locating sample points to ensure samples are taken from suitable process locations before and after unit operations, at points of use and elsewhere, should assure a successful water system monitoring program.

A Risk Analysis (RA) may be performed to help determine sample locations. The RA can provide a rationale for the sampling points in a water system and can help to define the intended purpose of each sampling location, based on established CQAs and Critical Process Parameters (CPPs).

Sample valve locations should be addressed not only on a P&ID but should also be addressed during equipment and piping installation. Components, hardware, or other items that interfere with correct sampling techniques should be avoided through careful planning and appropriate field execution. Considerations should include the disposition of waste from flushing, location of sample ports relative to other equipment, local environmental issues, and other concerns. Design in consultation with QA, QC, and Microbiology Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) is desirable.

"Sample valves located in return loop piping that are inaccessible to samplers, sample valves blocked by other equipment or with otherwise limited access... do not accomplish the goal of ensuring that the samples are representative. Improper location may put a sampler's safety at risk or may yield data with a high degree of variability, and may result in an increase in alert limit, action limit, and OOS findings."

Sampling locations should include all appropriate sections of the system including:

  • Source water
  • Pretreatment unit operations
  • Primary and final treatment
  • Storage
  • Distribution systems (main loops and sub loops)
  • POU and any special POU treatments

Commentary: a risk assessment approach to selecting sampling locations

The role of risk analysis (RA) in selecting sample points

ISPE recommends carrying out a risk analysis of sampling locations as early as the Conceptual Design phase of facility design. The RA should answer the following questions:

  • At which locations would a loss of water quality control directly affect product CQAs? (sample these first)
  • Which locations can use in-line instruments instead of manual sampling? (homogeneous impurities)
  • Which locations carry special microbial or chemical contamination risk? (hot spots)
  • Can personnel sample at the location safely and conveniently? (accessibility)

Key warning: sample point accessibility affects data quality

ISPE points out directly that inaccessible sample points lead to more alert/action level excursions, because personnel sampling under difficult conditions easily introduce external contamination, causing false positives. Common accessibility problems:

  • Return piping installed in the ceiling or in an interstitial pipe space
  • Sample points blocked by other equipment
  • Sample points higher than 1.8 meters, requiring a ladder
  • Sampling waste water cannot be discharged safely (dripping directly onto the floor)

Analogy: loop design is like city metro planning

A WFI loop is like a metro circle line: every station (POU) needs to be monitored, but the terminal station (the last POU and the loop return) is the most important, because the passengers (water molecules) have accumulated every possible contamination along the whole line. By the same logic, the water quality at the loop return represents the "worst case" for the whole loop and is the preferred location for process control monitoring.

2.2.1–2.2.3: Source Water, Pretreatment & Primary Treatment

Potable feed water requirements; unit operation monitoring; before/after sampling strategy

2.2.1 Source Water

Source water feeding any compendial pharmaceutical water system should meet local drinking water standards such as those specified by the WHO, the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), drinking water regulations of the EU or Japan, or other competent authorities.

Source water that does not meet potable or drinking water standards should be brought into compliance with recognized drinking water regulations prior to being used to feed a pharmaceutical water system.

Chlorination (or chloramination) of municipal water has become common in many areas of the world for microbiological control. If residual chlorine levels are inadequate for microbial control, rechlorination may be required and should be defined in a SOP and monitored on a regular basis through an appropriately located sample port.

Complete chemical analysis of source water that confirms it meets the appropriate potable or drinking water requirements may be performed on a periodic basis to trend incoming contaminant levels and to make system adjustments if required. If a source water storage tank is employed, additional testing after storage is advisable before water enters the water purification system.

2.2.2 Pretreatment Unit Operations

Sampling locations should be included before and after each unit operation for diagnostic purposes so that monitoring can be performed as required. Sampling before and after individual unit operations helps in establishing a unit operation's performance.

The following unit operations are examples of monitoring locations that may be considered:

  • Before and after media filtration (multi-media, cartridge, etc.)
  • Before and after softeners
  • Before and after Activated Carbon Filters (microbial monitoring is additionally critical here because activated carbon beds tend to promote microbial growth)
  • Before and after Ultraviolet units
  • Before and after chlorination or dechlorination units
  • Before and after Reverse Osmosis (RO), Continuous Electro Deionization (CEDI), or ion exchange units
  • Before and after any other unit operations within the pretreatment system boundary

2.2.3 Primary and Final Treatment

Primary and final treatment for compendial or non-compendial water may include several different treatment technologies. Examples include:

  • Reverse osmosis
  • Continuous electro deionization (CEDI)
  • Ion exchange deionization
  • Microfiltration / Ultrafiltration / Nanofiltration
  • Distillation (still for WFI)
  • Ultraviolet treatment
  • Heat exchangers

Sampling locations should be provided before and after each treatment unit operation as appropriate. Attributes to be evaluated are based on equipment performance expectations and may include: Conductivity, TOC, Chlorine, Ammonia, Hardness, Silica, Silt Density Index (SDI), Microbial, and Endotoxin.

Final treatment unit output water should be monitored using in-line, on-line or at-line analyzers where possible. Conductivity and TOC analyzers are usually used for these purposes. Where multiple final treatment units are present in a facility, there should be a sample point before and after each.

Commentary: sampling logic from source water to the purification system

The importance of source water quality

The performance of purified water and water for injection systems depends heavily on feed water quality. Seasonal changes in feed water quality (for example, more organics brought in by the rainy season, heavier chlorination in summer) affect the load and performance of downstream equipment. Recommendations:

  • Perform a full chemical analysis of the source water every quarter
  • Monitor residual chlorine (target: 0.2–0.5 mg/L)
  • Establish trend analysis of feed water quality as a trigger for preventive maintenance of the purification system

Activated carbon filters: a special point of attention for microbial monitoring

ISPE specifically points out that an activated carbon bed (Activated Carbon Filter) can promote microbial growth — this is counterintuitive! Although activated carbon removes chlorine (dechlorination), the dechlorinated water loses its bacteriostatic effect, and the huge surface area of the carbon bed makes it an ideal environment for biofilm growth.

Practical recommendation: the frequency of microbial sampling after the activated carbon bed should be higher than for other pretreatment equipment; some manufacturers choose ozone or UV instead of activated carbon to reduce microbial risk.

The diagnostic value of before/after sampling

The purpose of sampling "before and after" each unit operation is to establish a performance baseline for each piece of equipment.

Example: the difference in conductivity before and after RO reflects the rejection rate of the RO membrane. If conductivity after RO is higher than normal on a given occasion, the before/after sampling data can immediately show whether it is caused by membrane damage (the before/after difference narrows) rather than a feed water quality problem (the before/after ratio is unchanged).

CDMO challenge: the number of sample points with multiple purification trains

A large CDMO site may have 2–3 PW purification systems and 2 WFI stills, sharing several storage tanks and distribution loops. The number of sample points can exceed 50. Management strategy:

  • Cover homogeneous attributes with in-line instruments (TOC, conductivity)
  • Keep manual sampling only at the highest-risk points (after the carbon bed, after the still, loop return of the distribution loop)
  • Manage the sampling schedule and trend analysis with a LIMS

2.2.4–2.2.6: Storage Tanks, Distribution Systems & Points of Use

Tank sampling strategy; loop return worst-case; POU definition and sampling requirements

2.2.4 Storage Tanks

Sampling locations directly on a storage tank may provide valuable information if any microbial buildup (biofilm) is occurring in the tank and also allows testing of the stored water quality. A sample valve installed for direct sampling from the storage tank may be required for validation or for diagnostic purposes and may or may not be used for routine sampling.

Sampling valves located at storage tanks should be easily accessible and should minimize the risk of contamination of the sample and also the storage tank itself during sampling.

Tank water quality can also be assessed indirectly through monitoring of:

  • The primary generation supply (incoming water to storage)
  • The loop return (water returning to storage)
  • The tank outlet which feeds the pumps and subsequently the use points

These samples indicate the water quality prior to entry to the storage tank, after returning from the loop, and at the storage tank outlet, providing an indirect indication of the impact the storage tank is having on water quality.

2.2.5 Distribution Systems (main loops and sub loops)

Quality control sampling locations in the main distribution system should include all POUs, with sampling occurring in a manner identical to how the water is used by manufacturing. Process control sampling locations should be located before the first and after the last POU and at other specified locations.

Sampling the loop return after the last POU ensures process control as this location may be the worst case location for water quality in the entire distribution system. Water systems with multiple return loops should have sampling locations in each of the return loops. For one-way (non-recirculating) branch systems, all POUs should be sampled.

When temperature changes are required, the distribution system should be designed such that the pharmaceutical water is maintained at a higher pressure than heating/cooling media such as in a heat exchanger to prevent leakage from the non-sanitary utility to the sanitary side of the heat exchanger.

Each sub-loop returning to the main loop return should have a sampling point before the sub loop connects to the main loop return.

In the distribution loop of a pharmaceutical water system, all use point valves and sample valves should be of a sanitary design. Sample points in a laboratory system may not be sanitary and may contain dead legs such as may be present in a gooseneck faucet.

2.2.6 Points of Use

A Point Of Use (POU) is defined as the location where the water delivered from the water distribution system is actually used. POUs may be located on the main loop or in sub loops.

When sampling for quality control purposes, the POU is typically not the valve on the distribution system itself from which the water is taken. Instead, it is the end of the delivery system that transfers the water from the distribution system to the process (e.g., sinks, autoclaves, washers, process tanks). Sampling at POUs should include all components and surfaces along this water delivery pathway (e.g., heat exchangers, hoses, fittings) that the water needs to travel through to reach the process.

When the outlet of a POU cannot be sampled, the next most representative sampling location should be identified. Rationale for the selection should be provided in the risk assessment. Validation and/or routine monitoring data should conclude that the sample location is representative of the POU.

Every POU in a water distribution system that is not active should be locked out or tagged out of service and should be appropriately maintained to prevent contamination of the POU or the distribution system.

Commentary: sampling strategy for storage tanks, distribution loops and POUs

Tank sampling: direct vs. indirect assessment

Placing a sample point directly on the storage tank (such as a sample valve on the tank side wall) makes it possible to assess the biofilm condition inside the tank, but in practice the risk is higher (the tank may be contaminated during sampling). A more common and lower-risk alternative is to infer the effect of the tank on water quality indirectly, by monitoring the water quality at three locations: inlet, return and outlet.

Loop return: the meaning of worst case

The logic behind the conclusion that "the loop return is the location with the worst water quality in the whole distribution system":

  • Water leaves the tank, flows through the entire loop and finally returns from the loop return
  • Along the way, every POU, every valve and every pipe connection has a chance to introduce microorganisms
  • The water travels the longest distance in the loop and has accumulated all potential contamination

Therefore, process control sampling at the loop return gives the most conservative assessment of the overall condition of the loop. If the water quality at the loop return is acceptable, the points in the middle of the loop will almost certainly be acceptable as well (but POUs still need to be sampled independently because of their extended piping).

The correct definition of a POU: the importance of extended piping

ISPE's definition of a POU is easily misunderstood. A POU is not a valve on the distribution system but "the end location where the water is actually used".

Example: a WFI valve is followed by a hose, the hose connects to a heat exchanger, and finally the water enters the formulation tank. In this case:

  • POU = the formulation tank inlet (where the water is actually used)
  • QC sample point = as close as possible to the formulation tank inlet, including the hose and heat exchanger
  • The PC sample point can be the upstream sample port of the WFI valve

CDMO practice: managing decommissioned POUs

In a CDMO environment, as client products change, some POUs may be out of use for a long time (for example, when a production line is shut down). ISPE requires that unused POUs be locked out / tagged out and properly maintained. Best practice:

  • Define the management process for unused POUs in an SOP (close, drain, isolate)
  • Flush unused branch lines regularly (for example, weekly) or isolate and remove them completely
  • Before returning to use, microbial and chemical sampling must be performed first to confirm quality

Differential pressure management for heat exchangers

When the distribution system includes a heat exchanger (for example, for temperature control), ISPE requires that the pressure on the pharmaceutical water side be higher than that on the heating/cooling medium side, to prevent condensate or cooling water from the non-hygienic side from leaking into the pharmaceutical water. If the pressure differential cannot be assured, a double tube sheet heat exchanger should be used, and downstream conductivity/TOC should be monitored as a leak indicator.

Developing Sampling Plans

Introduction — life cycle approach, documentation requirements

Original Text (English)

The frequencies and durations of sampling plans have historically coincided with the phases of the validation program for the water system. Using this rationale, the final phase of validation forms the basis for ongoing sampling frequencies with the goal of ensuring that the system is maintained in a validated state.

However, the use of risk analysis tools coupled with periodic data review may be used to alter the frequency of sampling when the data provides overwhelming support for a change (increase or decrease) in sampling frequency. Regular data review should be documented as part of the planning process.

A sampling plan should define and document:

  • Potential sampling points and the purpose for each
  • Sample locations intended for continuous, regular, routine, periodic, or as-needed monitoring
  • Attributes to be tested at each sampling location
  • The frequency at which samples are required for any given attribute
  • The purpose and use of the resulting data for those attributes
  • When sample valves and use point valves may be used as the same valve

Sampling plans are required for both new and renovated systems and may involve sampling locations throughout the water purification and distribution systems, including source water.

In specific circumstances, adequate scientific and statistical rationale may be required to not sample at an installed sampling point.

Sample locations installed for technical, investigative, or diagnostic purposes may not be included in a routine sampling plan. The purpose and use of these sample locations should be defined, and they should be used on an as-needed basis, be suitably maintained, and not pose a contamination risk to the system.

Note: Designating sampling locations "For Information Only (FIO)" or "for future use" should be avoided.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Logic: The Evolution of the Sampling Plan

ISPE's sampling plan philosophy is dynamic, not fixed. The end of validation establishes the baseline for routine monitoring, but frequencies can later be adjusted using risk analysis and historical data. The key point is that any adjustment must have a scientific and statistical basis.

Inspection Focus: Essential Elements of the Sampling Plan Document

During a regulatory inspection, the inspector will expect to see all of the following six items clearly defined in the SOP or sampling plan document:

  • List of sampling points + the purpose of each point
  • Monitoring frequency categories (continuous / routine / periodic / on demand)
  • Attributes tested at each point
  • Sampling frequency for each attribute
  • Use of the data (release? trending? investigation?)
  • Conditions under which a sampling valve and a point-of-use valve are shared

Analogy: Why is the "FIO" label risky?

Labeling a sampling point "for information only" or "future use" tells the inspector that the point has no clear purpose. This raises questions such as "Why was it installed if it is not used?" or "Is this an excuse to avoid sampling?" In a CDMO environment, every installed sampling valve should have a clear, documented rationale.

Points to Consider

  1. Does your site's water system sampling plan clearly distinguish "routine monitoring points" from "diagnostic sampling points"? How do the management requirements for the two differ?
  2. If risk analysis supports reducing the frequency at a sampling point, what documents must be prepared for the inspector to accept it?
  3. When does a sampling plan need to be revised? List at least three situations that trigger a revision.

Background Philosophy

Life cycle approach to water system sampling — from design through PSE

Original Text (English)

Sample plan development should be based on the expected sampling required during the life cycle of the system.

A typical life cycle scenario for water sampling activities may include:

  • Conceptual and detailed design
  • Construction and installation
  • Commissioning
  • Performance Qualification (including worst case test and special scenario testing)
  • Ongoing testing during production
  • System changes and modifications (major and minor)
  • Periodic System Evaluation (PSE)

Sampling guidance is provided in Tables 2.2, 2.3, and 2.4 regarding specific sampling locations, testing frequencies, and the type of monitoring or testing for various activities including commissioning and qualification/verification.

Automated electronic monitoring of conductivity and TOC can provide a comprehensive database from which chemical water quality trends may be observed over time. When analyzed, this information may allow for minimization or elimination of manual chemical testing of the water during routine operations.

However, during the Performance Qualification, confirmation that the on-line or in-line readings are representative of water quality used should be verified if this data is going to be used for product release purposes.

Water may be delivered at several different temperatures for use at a single POU. The water sample collected from this point should be collected at the worst case temperature for microbiological control. For example, if water is delivered at both 65°C and 20°C from the same POU, the sample should be collected at 20°C.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Lifecycle Sampling Thinking

ISPE stresses that a sampling plan is not a one-time document but a dynamic framework that evolves over the system lifecycle. This is consistent with the product lifecycle management philosophy of ICH Q10.

Each of the seven lifecycle phases has a different sampling purpose: design establishes the baseline, commissioning builds engineering knowledge, PQ confirms compliance, routine monitoring maintains the validated state, and the PSE decides whether adjustments are needed.

The Relationship Between Online Instruments and Manual Sampling

This is an area many sites tend to misunderstand: online conductivity and TOC can replace manual chemical testing, but with a precondition: it must be confirmed during PQ that the online readings represent the quality of the water actually used. Where there is a risk of localized contamination (for example, a POU heat exchanger), downstream sampling or grab samples are still needed for release.

Worst-Case Temperature Sampling: Why Choose 20°C?

Microorganisms are more active and survive better at lower temperatures than at 65°C. If the same point of use can deliver both 65°C hot water and 20°C cold water, sampling at the hot water temperature is the "least favorable condition" for microorganisms, but the cold water is the real risk point where microorganisms have the greatest chance to proliferate. Worst case = the condition with the highest risk of microbial contamination.

Points to Consider

  1. During the PSE (periodic system evaluation), does your site assess whether the sampling frequencies are appropriate? What criteria are used to decide whether to adjust them?
  2. If online conductivity data are used for product release, what verification content is needed in the PQ report to support this decision?
  3. A system has just completed a major modification. Which lifecycle phase has sampling requirements closest to those of a brand-new system?

Regulatory Requirements Related to Monitoring Frequency

USP, EP, JP, ICH Q7, FDA — comparative regulatory landscape

Original Text (English)

No well-defined regulatory requirements exist regarding routine sampling frequency and duration. Various pharmacopoeias provide specifications for microbiological and chemical contaminants for the various grades of water, but do not define the frequency or duration of sampling.

General expectations from compendia and consensus standards:

USP <1231> — "Water systems should be monitored at a frequency that is sufficient to ensure that the system is in control, and continues to produce water of acceptable quality"; and "The sampling plan should take into consideration the desired attributes of the water being sampled."
EP GMP Vol. 4 Annex 1 — "Water sources, water treatment equipment and treated water should be monitored regularly for chemical and biological contamination and, as appropriate, for endotoxins."
JP16 General Information G8 (Section 4.2) — "Sampling frequency should be established based on the data from validation studies on the system."
ICH Q7 Section 4.20 — "All utilities that could impact on quality (e.g., steam, gases, compressed air) should be qualified and appropriately monitored and action should be taken when limits are exceeded."
1993 US FDA Guide to Inspection of High Purity Water Systems — "The results of the Commissioning & Qualification are designed to demonstrate that all of the process steps and components within a Purified Water or Water For Injection system are capable of consistently providing water meeting the necessary water quality requirements."

The FDA guide provides the most specific guidelines for sampling frequency during system validation, but does not stipulate frequency beyond validation and recognizes that more than one approach may be acceptable.

Summary: Routine sampling should always be adequate for the intended purpose. Frequency and duration decisions should be based on risk assessment with documented rationales.

Commentary and Regulatory Comparison

The Regulatory Reality: No One Tells You How Often to Sample

This is a point of confusion for many QA staff: no major regulatory document explicitly specifies the frequency of routine sampling. The regulations say only "sufficient", "periodic" and "based on validation data". This gives sites flexibility, but it also means you must be able to explain and defend the frequency you choose.

Differences in Inspection Focus: FDA vs. EMA

FDA inspectors tend to ask: "What is the scientific basis for your sampling frequency?" They accept a variety of approaches but require documented justification.

EMA/EP inspectors focus more on the requirements of Annex 1, especially whether endotoxin monitoring of water for sterile products is complete and whether there are two levels, alert and action.

CDMO note: when serving different clients, you may need to meet both FDA and EMA expectations at the same time. We recommend establishing a single, slightly stricter unified standard.

Points to Consider

  1. How is the frequency in your site's water system sampling SOP determined? Is it supported by a documented risk assessment?
  2. EP Annex 1 mentions monitoring endotoxin "as appropriate". In which situations is it "appropriate", and in which is it "not needed"?
  3. ICH Q7 applies to API manufacturing. If your clients are API manufacturers, how do their water system requirements differ from those of a drug product manufacturer?

Commissioning and Pre-validation

GEP-based testing to establish baseline before formal PQ

Original Text (English)

Water testing of the pretreatment, generation and distribution systems for PW and WFI systems during commissioning is an exercise in Good Engineering Practice (GEP). This activity may contribute to developing operational and maintenance practices and procedures that will be confirmed at a later time during qualification and may provide baseline information for the system that may be useful in the future.

The testing activities may include chemical testing, or monitoring as appropriate, for specific unit operations and microbial testing to identify the indigenous microbial bioburden of the system. The duration of commissioning activities may vary depending upon the complexity and size of the system (e.g., 1 to 5 days).

Once the entire system is operating, the effluent of each unit operation should be monitored or tested at least once during the commissioning phase in order to provide baseline values for the system.

It may be necessary to perform extended pre-validation microbiological sampling activities in order to achieve process knowledge and assure system robustness.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Commissioning Sampling: A GEP Activity, Not a GMP Requirement

Sampling during the commissioning phase is an engineering practice (GEP), not a formal GMP regulatory requirement. But the distinction matters: although commissioning data cannot be used directly as PQ data, they help you understand system behavior, establish SOPs and identify potential problems.

Three Purposes of Commissioning Sampling

  • Establish baseline values: test at least once at the outlet of each unit operation to understand what "normal" looks like for the system
  • Identify the resident flora: the native microbial population of the system, which provides a reference for subsequent monitoring
  • Develop operating procedures: the results of commissioning sampling directly influence the development of SOPs (cleaning, sanitization frequency, etc.)

Analogy: Commissioning = a New Car's Break-in Period

Just as a new car needs a break-in period, a new water system needs to run for a period before it formally goes "on the road" (PQ) so that engineers can learn its characteristics. The data from this period do not count as official mileage, but they are critical for judging whether the system is "ready" for formal evaluation.

  1. Commissioning sampling finds high microbial counts at the outlet of one RO unit. Does this need to be resolved before PQ starts? On what basis?
  2. Commissioning lasts 1-5 days. How do you decide how many days to commission a newly built large WFI system?

Source Water

Potable water requirements, responsibility boundaries, risk assessment

Original Text (English)

USP <1231> states: "To ensure adherence to certain minimal chemical and microbiological quality standards, water used in the production of drug substances or as source or feed water for the preparation of the various types of purified waters must meet the requirements of the National Primary Drinking Water Regulations (NPDWR) (40 CFR 141) issued by the U.S. EPA, or the drinking water regulations of the European Union or Japan, or the WHO drinking water guidelines."

Various nomenclature may be in use across the globe for drinking water including: potable water, tap water, city water, and feed water, however, the quality intent remains consistently the same.

WHO Technical Report Series No. 929, Annex 3 recommends daily sampling of incoming potable water during the early validation phase to verify its quality, but this may not be required in all areas of the world.

If there is little or no knowledge of the feed water quality at the system inlet, it is recommended to take daily samples for analysis. If the potable water quality is already well known and historical data shows good, stable water quality, a rationale can be made to perform less source water testing during the PQ period.

Typically, the potable/drinking water supplier is responsible for the potable water quality up to the property boundary; thereafter the responsibility is transferred to the property owner/site operator. Pharmaceutical product manufacturers are ultimately responsible for end product quality.

An impact assessment should be performed on potable water depending on the attributes, the treatment technology employed, and the end use of the water.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Responsibility Boundary: The Site Boundary Is the Key Dividing Line

The water utility is responsible only up to the site boundary; beyond the boundary, responsibility for water quality rests with the site. This means that even if the utility's water meets drinking water standards, the site still needs to confirm the incoming water quality itself. This is especially important in regions with seasonal water quality fluctuations (for example, the rainy season or the influence of agricultural water use).

Decision Tree for Source Water Sampling Frequency

  • New system / unfamiliar water source → daily sampling during the initial PQ (WHO recommendation)
  • Historical data available and water quality stable → frequency may be reduced, but documented justification is needed
  • High-risk region (large seasonal variation) → maintain a higher frequency, especially in the rainy season

CDMO Practice: The Challenge of Multiple Clients and Regions

A CDMO may manufacture products for clients in different regions across the US, Europe and Japan. The source water standard needs to meet the strictest of 40 CFR 141 (US), the EU Drinking Water Directive and the WHO standards at the same time. We recommend that the source water risk assessment explicitly list a comparison of all applicable standards.

Points to Consider

  1. Does your site obtain the water utility's water quality report regularly every year? Is that enough to relieve you of the responsibility to sample the water yourself?
  2. If there is agricultural activity near the site, pesticide residues may enter the water source during the rainy season. How should your source water sampling plan respond?
  3. The Chinese Pharmacopoeia requires the feed water to a WFI still to meet PW specifications, but USP/EP/JP do not. If your clients include products sold in China, how does this affect your pretreatment sampling plan?

Pretreatment Sampling

Unit-operation specific sampling — filters, softeners, carbon beds, RO, CEDI

Original Text (English)

Pretreatment represents designated treatment steps typically prior to primary/final treatment. When used as feed water for WFI or Pure Steam, pretreated water does not necessarily need to comply with PW requirements to ensure WFI/PS quality. Note: the Chinese Pharmacopoeia requires that supply water to a WFI still must comply with PW specification. USP, EP, and JP do not require PW quality as feed for production of WFI.

In order to ensure that the pretreatment fulfills its purpose, sampling is carried out specifically for each purification step:

  • Sand/mixed media filter: SDI measured before and after. Worst case: just before automatic back flush.
  • Water softener: Hardness measured before and after. Worst case: immediately before regeneration.
  • Carbon filter: Chlorine concentration before and after. Worst case: immediately before back flush.
  • RO system: Conductivity before and after the RO unit.
  • CEDI: Conductivity before and after the CEDI.

For all purification steps, microbiological testing may be required, including before and after the RO if applicable. Acceptable microbiological levels should be defined for each purification step. There should at least be an Action Level with consideration for adding an Alert Level if appropriate.

If, during risk assessment, the pretreatment is determined to be a no-impact system, samples should be taken for record purposes during PQ. If determined to be indirect impact, a rationale should be developed for the sampling frequency during PQ of the pretreatment.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Alert Level vs. Action Level: You Must Have One, Preferably Two

Alert Level

An early warning signal. When exceeded, an investigation and enhanced monitoring are required, but use of the water does not necessarily have to stop. It is usually set at a certain percentage of the regulatory limit (for example, 50-70%).

Action Level

Corrective action must be taken. When exceeded, it usually requires quarantining the water batch, investigating the root cause, and possibly raising a deviation report. It must be set and should be lower than the pharmacopoeial specification.

ISPE says "at least an Action Level is needed". In a CDMO environment we recommend setting both, because the Alert Level gives you an opportunity to intervene early and avoid exceeding the Action Level.

Purified Water (PW) Sampling

Rotational sampling strategies, alert/action levels for PW systems

Original Text (English)

Depending on the number of POUs and sample points in the distribution system, consideration could be given to rotational sampling for chemical tests since chemical contaminants are uniformly distributed in the water.

Commentary and CDMO Application

The Logic of Rotational Sampling

Chemical contaminants (TOC, conductivity) are uniformly distributed in the water: if the system outlet meets the limits, all points of use should also meet them. Chemical testing can therefore be done by rotational sampling, and not every point of use needs to be tested every time.

Microbial contamination, however, is localized: biofilm at one point of use will not affect other points. Microbiological sampling therefore cannot rely too heavily on a rotation strategy, especially for high-risk points of use.

2.3.4.3

Water for Injection (WFI) Sampling

POU rotation, daily sampling expectations, PSE considerations, frequency reduction risks

Original Text (English)

POU sampling plans should rotate through all use points on the system, with the expectation that samples are collected on a daily basis from various use points, and that all use points are sampled on a routine basis based on risk analysis. System representative locations (e.g., supply and/or return) may be sampled on a daily basis for process control purposes and to aid in the investigation of OOS results from individual use points.

During the PSE of a WFI (or PW) system, sampling frequency may be evaluated. Increases or decreases in sampling frequency should be based on historical data and should only occur when data indicates that more or less frequent sampling is justified based on performance of the use point or system.

Sample frequency should not be arbitrarily adjusted without adequate scientific and statistical justification. A decrease in sampling frequency may result in increased regulatory scrutiny.

Decreased sampling frequency also puts more product at risk during investigations because of longer periods between acceptable test results surrounding a deviant test result. In short, careful consideration should be given before decreasing sampling frequency relative to the increased number of product batches that will be put at risk.

Commentary and CDMO Application

High Sampling Expectations for WFI

ISPE's sampling expectations for WFI are clearly higher than for PW:

  • Rotational sampling of the points of use daily (not weekly)
  • Sampling of the supply and return daily as representative points of the system
  • All points of use must be covered in routine monitoring

This reflects the high risk of the WFI application (injectable products).

Three Risks of Reducing Sampling Frequency

1. Regulatory risk: it may draw special attention from inspectors and be seen as a signal of weakening quality control.

2. Investigation risk: if an OOS is found, the longer the interval between two passing results, the more product batches must be assessed for risk, potentially leading to a larger retrospective investigation and a possible recall.

3. Burden of proof: sufficient historical data and statistical analysis are needed to support a decision to reduce frequency.

Analogy: Reduced Sampling Frequency = a Smaller Safety Net

Imagine your WFI sampling as a safety net. The higher the sampling frequency, the finer the mesh, and the earlier and smaller the problems you catch. Reducing the frequency is like enlarging the mesh: an occasional small problem may slip through, and once a problem is found, all the batches between the mesh points may be at risk. In a CDMO environment, those batches may be products of different clients, which makes legal liability even more complex.

Points to Consider

  1. Your site's WFI system has 10 points of use, but there is no production on weekends. What worst case does the first sampling on Monday represent?
  2. A PSE assessment recommends reducing microbiological sampling at one WFI point of use from 3 times a week to once a week. What documented justification needs to be prepared?
  3. A microbial count at one WFI point of use exceeds the Action Level. How many batches of product need to be traced back? How does the sampling frequency affect this number?

Sampling for Performance Qualification/Verification

PQ framework introduction — new vs. modified systems

Original Text (English)

The PQ/Verification sampling plan covers three scenarios:

  1. Typical (nominal) use conditions
  2. Worst case use conditions (system limits)
  3. Special scenarios

The goal is to document that the stated water quality is delivered from the water system under all of the above conditions and that the water complies with the requirements for the grade of pharmaceutical water type specified.

The period for the PQ depends on whether it applies to a completely new system or to modifications made to an existing system.

PQ Sampling Phases — FDA Three-Phase Approach:

Phase 1 — Initial Sampling: Most intensive, typically 2–4 weeks (10–20 consecutive working days). All sample and use points in the distribution system tested daily. Worst case: first day after weekend if no water consumption occurred.

Phase 2 — Intermediary Sampling: Less intensive, 10–20 working days. Reduced sampling frequency. Demonstrates repeatability and consistent quality using SOPs. May provide additional worst case data since use points are not as frequently active.

Phase 3 — Extended Sampling: Not less than the remaining balance of one year (minus Phase 1 and Phase 2 time). Captures seasonal variations in potable water feed, development of the system's natural flora, and any other annual variations. Typically reduced frequency. Forms the basis for the routine monitoring and testing program once validation is complete.

Commentary: The Complete Three-Phase PQ Framework

The Three-Phase PQ: Core Concept of the 1993 FDA Guide

The three-phase PQ approach established by the 1993 FDA guide remains the industry gold standard today. Each phase has a different purpose, intensity and duration:

The Critical Importance of Phase 3: Seasonal Validation

Why a full year? Because the incoming water quality (microbial species, temperature, chemical composition) varies with the seasons. Summer water is not the same as winter water. Only a full year of data can demonstrate that the system operates reliably under all seasonal conditions.

This is also why the Phase 3 frequency forms the basis of the routine monitoring program: it represents the true performance of the system under real-world conditions.

2.3.5.1

Purpose of PQ Sampling

Documenting water quality delivery under all operating conditions

Original Text (English)

The purpose of PQ/verification is to document that the stated water quality is delivered from the water system during:

  1. Typical (nominal) use conditions
  2. Worst case use conditions (system limits)
  3. Special scenarios

...and that the water complies with the requirements for the grade of pharmaceutical water type specified.

The period for the PQ depends on whether it applies to a completely new system or to modifications made to an existing system. When minor changes are made to a qualified system, reduced sampling requirements may be considered.

The PQ sampling protocol should describe the purpose, scope, and strategy (including justification). The sampling plan and the expected data from in-line/on-line measuring equipment should be described in the protocol. The prerequisites and the acceptance criteria for the performance testing should also be stated in the protocol.

All stakeholders involved in the PQ (production, engineering, quality control, and quality assurance) should have an opportunity to review and comment on the protocol. Communication and information sharing between the different participants before, during, and after the PQ are essential for efficient and successful qualification.

Commentary and CDMO Application

PQ ≠ "confirming the system has no problems"

The purpose of PQ is to document that the water quality meets specifications under all conditions, not just to "test". The distinction matters: PQ is a formal, documented confirmation process that must have a pre-approved protocol, clear acceptance criteria and a complete report.

Checklist of Essential PQ Protocol Elements

  • Purpose and Scope
  • Strategy with Justification
  • Description of expected data from online/in-line instruments
  • Prerequisites: IQ/OQ completed, SOPs approved
  • Acceptance Criteria
  • Description of worst cases and special scenarios
  • Production activity considerations (for example, no production on weekends)

The Importance of Cross-Functional Collaboration

PQ is not a solo performance by QC or Engineering. Production knows what real "normal use" and "maximum usage" are; Engineering understands the system design limits; QC performs the testing; QA ensures compliance. Each of the four departments has an irreplaceable role.

In a CDMO, you also need to consider whether client-specific requirements need to be incorporated into the PQ design.

Points to Consider

  1. Do the "acceptance criteria" in your PQ protocol clearly distinguish chemical attribute criteria, microbiological criteria and endotoxin criteria? What is the source of each standard?
  2. ISPE says that when missing samples are found during PQ execution, they should be communicated immediately and documented in a deviation. Does your site have this process?
  3. "Minor system changes may justify reduced PQ sampling requirements": how do you define a "minor change" in your documentation?
2.3.5.2

Typical Use Conditions (Nominal)

Normal operational range definition with documented rationales

Original Text (English)

All PQs include a period of normal operation or simulated normal operation to demonstrate that the water quality during normal operation complies with the requirements for the actual specified pharmaceutical water quality.

Normal operational usage ranges are defined with rationales.

This forms the baseline against which worst case conditions and special scenarios are compared. The normal operating range encompasses the typical day-to-day variations in production demand, usage patterns, temperature fluctuations within design parameters, and normal maintenance cycles.

Commentary and CDMO Application

What Is the "Normal Operating Range"? It Must Be Defined in Writing

"Normal" is not an assumption; it must be clearly defined and justified. A typical definition of the normal operating range includes:

  • Range of daily water consumption (minimum to maximum)
  • System circulation temperature range
  • Number of points of use and simultaneous use
  • Operating days per week (for example, a 5-day vs. a 7-day schedule)
  • Sanitization frequency and time windows

Applications of "Simulated Normal Operation"

When a new system has been installed but commercial production has not yet started, simulated normal operation can be performed (for example, using water to simulate the water demand). However, the simulation must realistically reflect the expected operating range and have a clear justification in the PQ protocol.

2.3.5.3

Worst Case Use Conditions

System limit testing — stagnation, maximum flow, sanitization boundaries, maintenance events

Original Text (English)

A worst case scenario tests the limits of the system and is defined as the most extreme situations during the operation of the water system.

Examples of worst case conditions:

  • No water consumption (e.g., during a weekend or shutdown): Can result in a rise in the level of bioburden and conductivity. The first day of the week after no weekend consumption may represent a worst case scenario for sampling.
  • Maximum use of water: Confirms the system or unit operation is capable of delivering suitable water quality during a period of maximum delivery.
  • Just before sanitization: If routine sanitization occurs, sampling just before the start of sanitization captures the maximum time of accumulation of bioburden.
  • Just after sanitization: Confirms that sanitization has been effective and that chemical sanitant residuals are within acceptable limits.
  • After a maintenance procedure: After a periodic event such as derouging and/or passivation, confirming water quality restoration.
Commentary and CDMO Application

The Logic of Defining Worst Case

Worst case is not "picking a random bad situation" but systematically identifying all operating conditions that may put the greatest stress on water quality, then sampling under those conditions and confirming that the water quality still meets specifications.

CDMO-Specific Worst-Case Considerations

A CDMO typically has multiple products and multiple clients, with production schedules that change greatly. The following are worst-case scenarios specific to the CDMO environment:

  • After long holidays (Lunar New Year, Christmas): the system may stand idle for 1-2 weeks, the most severe stagnation test
  • Client audit weeks: the system stands idle while production is paused
  • Client changeover on shared equipment: confirmation of water quality after extensive flushing

Points to Consider

  1. Does your site's PQ report include sampling data for all five categories of worst case? If a category was missed, how can it be added afterwards?
  2. Is the acceptance criterion for "post-sanitization sampling" the same as for "normal operation sampling"? How are acceptance criteria set for chemical sanitant residues?
  3. After a derouging operation, how long a rinse is needed before water quality is restored? Does your SOP specify the requirements for resuming sampling?
2.3.5.4

Special Scenarios

Power failure, emergency maintenance, seasonal qualification — pre-validation to minimize future disruptions

Original Text (English)

Special scenarios are situations which may occur during normal use either as unplanned or as planned scenarios. Often these special scenarios occur at inconvenient times, and it would be advantageous to validate these scenarios to avoid delays when they occur.

Validation of special scenarios can be part of the ordinary PQ of the pharmaceutical water system, or it can be performed as a specific PQ for only this purpose.

The validation of a special scenario may need to be performed in a worst case scenario and be documented by water quality sampling data. The outcome and conclusion of the validation, based on the data, can decrease time spent on the procedure during daily operation, when going from the special scenario to normal condition, due to less sampling, sanitization, etc.

Examples of special scenarios:

  • Power failure: To document that the pharmaceutical water quality is unchanged after restarting after a defined period of time without any sanitization. A prerequisite for this test is no breach of integrity of the water system. If water retains quality for three hours after power failure, then up to a three-hour power failure can be followed by normal operation without additional sanitization (provided system integrity is not otherwise compromised).
  • Emergency or unplanned maintenance procedures: Including unusual or unexpected component failure, system damage from internal or external action. Validation of all emergency or unplanned maintenance procedures is not practical.

Acceptance criteria should be set up properly for the special scenario. A special scenario test may be performed for a longer period than the period used as the limit, to incorporate a safety factor (e.g., testing four hours to validate a three-hour limit).

Commentary and CDMO Application

The Core Value of Special Scenario Validation

The logic of special scenario validation is that testing in advance under controlled conditions is far more effective than dealing with it afterwards in an emergency. Pre-validation can:

  • Establish a clear "acceptable time window" (for example: no sanitization needed if the power failure lasts less than 3 hours)
  • Reduce the need for emergency sampling when an event occurs
  • Shorten the wait before the system is returned to use
  • Avoid unnecessary production delays

Seasonal Validation: Different Challenges in Summer and Winter

Summer challenges:

  • Higher feed water temperature → faster microbial growth rate
  • Cooling water temperature limits → reduced efficiency of some freeze dryers or heat exchangers
  • Sanitization frequency may need to be increased

Winter challenges:

  • Risk of freezing in outdoor piping
  • Feed water conductivity may rise because of de-icing salts (northern regions)
  • Some microorganisms grow more slowly at low temperatures, but certain psychrophiles are in fact more active

Analogy: The Logic of a Fire Drill

Special scenario validation is like a fire drill: you do not wait for a real fire before testing the evacuation procedure for the first time. The purpose of rehearsing in advance is to confirm that the standard operating procedure (SOP) really works in an emergency and that everyone knows their role. A special scenario validation for a 3-hour power failure tells your operators: "In this situation, follow this SOP; no sanitization is needed, and the water quality is safe."

Points to Consider

  1. Does your site have a power failure special scenario validation? What is the validated time window in hours? What are the acceptance criteria?
  2. If system integrity is compromised during a power failure (for example, a valve leak), can the conclusions of the power failure special scenario validation still be used? Why?
  3. ISPE says "it is impractical to validate all emergency or unplanned maintenance procedures". How, then, does the site handle water quality recovery after emergency maintenance without prior validation? What documents are needed?

Risk-Based Approach to C&Q/Verification

CQA definition, impact classification, non-critical equipment testing strategy

Original Text (English)

A risk-based approach to C&Q/verification may be utilized to develop the monitoring and testing strategy. Risk analyses or impact assessments may be utilized to determine what equipment or functions of the water system are considered critical.

There may be unit operations or process steps within the generation or distribution system that require testing during initial commissioning as well as during ongoing routine operation (e.g., UV treatment, pumps, heat exchangers) that may not be considered critical.

Testing relating to the continued operation of a unit process may become part of a routine monitoring program based upon the impact to the system and the finished quality of the water.

Frequency of routine testing could be adjusted based on the consistency of equipment performance. These tests assure proper maintenance and operation of the water system (e.g., regeneration of resin beds, sanitization of equipment).

A Critical Quality Attribute (CQA) is a test defined as critical, based on the need to meet a specific compendia specification or other defined non-compendial requirements. For example, conductivity may be a CQA of water when measured at the outlet of a PW generation system.

A risk-based approach may also be utilized for determining the sampling approach to qualify changes made to an existing validated water system.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Risk Classification Determines the Sampling Strategy

ISPE's risk classification framework:

  • Direct Impact: equipment that directly affects water quality → PQ required
  • Indirect Impact: supporting equipment → commissioning tests required; sampling frequency during PQ can be based on risk assessment
  • No Impact: no effect on water quality → commissioning records are sufficient; only the purpose needs to be documented during PQ

Example of Applying the CQA Definition

Measuring conductivity at the outlet of the PW system is a CQA (critical quality attribute) because:

  • There is a pharmacopoeial specification (USP temperature table)
  • It represents the quality of the final product (PW)
  • It can be monitored online
  • An excursion directly affects product release

By contrast, the SDI at the inlet and outlet of a sand filter is not a CQA (it is a pretreatment performance indicator), but it is an important indicator of system performance.

PQ Risk Assessment After a CDMO System Change

A CDMO's water system may be modified because of different client requirements or new product lines. A risk assessment approach is used to decide the scope of PQ after the modification:

  • Major change (for example, adding a distribution loop) → close to a full PQ
  • Minor change (for example, replacing a valve) → limited-scope confirmation sampling
  • Like-for-like replacement (same model and specification) → documented assessment + limited verification sampling

Risk-Based Approach to PQ Sampling

A Risk-Based Approach to Validation Sampling

Original Guidance Text

The PQ sampling plan should be developed using a risk-based approach. The risk assessment should consider the following factors when determining the scope and frequency of sampling:

  • The type and grade of water required (e.g., Purified Water, Water for Injection)
  • The intended use of the water and the associated product risk
  • The design and complexity of the water system (purification and distribution)
  • The historical performance of similar systems
  • The number and criticality of Points of Use (POUs)
  • Existing commissioning data and knowledge
  • Regulatory expectations applicable to the specific market

The outcome of the risk assessment should be documented and should provide justification for:

  • The selection of sampling locations
  • The attributes to be tested at each location
  • The testing frequency at each location during each phase of the PQ
  • The duration of each PQ phase
"The total period required for the actual PQ should be based on a documented risk evaluation with rationales for the decisions, but for not less than one year."
— ISPE GPG Sampling (2016), referencing FDA 1993 High Purity Water Guide

Risk analysis tools (such as FMEA, risk ranking and filtering, or fault tree analysis) may be used to support the determination of sampling scope and frequency. The output of such tools provides documented justification that can be referenced during regulatory inspections.

Where commissioning data is available and demonstrates acceptable performance, it may be possible to reduce the duration of the initial PQ phase, provided that a documented risk rationale supports this decision. Conversely, systems with limited commissioning data or complex designs should plan for longer initial phases and higher sampling frequencies.

It may be possible to spend more time and sampling effort during commissioning and pre-validation in order to gain improved process knowledge and thereby execute initial and intermediary sampling each at the lower end of the recommended time frame.

Commentary and Application

Core Concept: Risk-Based Sampling

ISPE's starting point is very clear: more sampling is not better; precise sampling is better. Risk assessment decides three things:

  • Where to sample (which points)
  • What to test (which attributes)
  • How often to test (frequency)

All three must have a documented risk rationale, not rely on habit or intuition.

Analogy: Emergency Room Triage

An emergency room nurse does not run a full set of tests on every patient. The nurse first assesses the risk level (triage), then decides which tests are needed and how fast results are required. The logic of PQ sampling for pharmaceutical water is exactly the same: assess the risk first, then set the frequency.

Critical point of use (direct product contact) = critically ill patient → monitor daily
Non-critical point of use (pre-wash water) = mild case → frequency can be reduced

Regulatory Focus: PQ Lasts at Least One Year

The FDA 1993 "Guide to Inspections of High Purity Water Systems" explicitly requires a total PQ duration of no less than one year. This requirement spans all four seasons because:

  • Hot summer → higher microbial risk
  • Cold winter → changes in source water quality
  • Rainy season → higher organic and endotoxin load in source water

A PQ shorter than one year will be challenged directly during an inspection.

CDMO Application: Structure of the Risk Assessment Document

In a CDMO setting, a good PQ risk assessment document should include:

  • System description: PW or WFI, system size, number of points of use
  • Risk scoring matrix: severity × occurrence × detectability for each point of use
  • Sampling plan summary table: point × attribute × frequency in each of the four PQ phases
  • Deviation decision logic: when to trigger an OOS investigation and when to pause PQ

Principle of Decreasing Frequency

Sampling frequency across the four phases follows a "funnel-shaped" decline:

  • Commissioning phase: confirmation, usually a one-time comprehensive test
  • Initial phase: highest frequency, daily sampling, to build baseline data
  • Intermediate phase: frequency reduced to between daily and weekly
  • Extended phase: reduced to weekly or lower, becoming the routine monitoring frequency

Key principle: frequency may be reduced only when the data remain consistently stable; you cannot skip the high-frequency phases from the start.

Analogy: A New Employee's Probation Period

For a new employee:

  • Month 1 (initial phase) → the manager checks work quality every day
  • Months 2 to 3 (intermediate phase) → weekly checks
  • After confirmation (extended phase) → monthly or quarterly reviews

Water system PQ follows exactly the same logic. A system that performs well can naturally be checked less often, but the data must support it.

Extended Phase = Starting Point for Routine Monitoring

Note B (see Tables 2.3 and 2.4): the sampling frequency and scope of the extended phase usually become the initial frequency for future routine validation maintenance testing, but may be adjusted based on accumulated data and risk analysis.

This means that one goal of PQ is to use 12 months of data to scientifically establish the "minimum but sufficient" routine monitoring frequency.

What the Five Sampling Locations Mean

  • Distribution pump outlet: represents water "leaving the purification area and before entering the distribution loop" — the quality starting point of the whole distribution system

Conditions for Using On-line Monitoring

Table 2.3 mentions an "on-line" option in several places, but using on-line data in place of sampling has preconditions (Note D):

  • The equivalence of on-line readings and sample results must be verified
  • The equivalence verification must be completed during the PQ phase and documented
  • Only after equivalence is established can on-line data be used for product release purposes
  • If a point of use has a risk of local contamination (such as a POU heat exchanger), sampling is still required; on-line monitoring alone is not sufficient

Table 2.3 adds requirements for the end of the loop: in addition to the standard tests, the commissioning phase must also confirm the physical attributes (flow, temperature, pressure), separately at the minimum and maximum demand scenarios defined in the URS.

Confirming that every point still has sufficient flow velocity (usually ≥1 m/s) at maximum water demand is key to preventing biofilm formation.

Principles for Setting Endotoxin Alert/Action Levels in WFI Systems

  • USP/EP/JP WFI specification: ≤0.25 EU/mL

  • Action Level: usually set at the specification limit, i.e. 0.25 EU/mL
  • Alert/Action Levels during PQ may be stricter to confirm normal system trends

These values should be clearly defined in the sampling plan and stated in the PQ report.

  • Weekly: cover all 8 POUs (spread over 7 days)
  • On-line monitoring: conductivity and TOC around the clock, but equivalence verification is required
  • Trend analysis: compile the number of Alert Level excursions monthly as input to the PSE

Regulatory Boundaries of the "Critical Point of Use" Definition

The definition of CUP is clear: ingredient water, direct product contact, final rinse. Commonly disputed cases in practice include:

  • Buffer preparation → CUP (ingredient water)
  • Final rinse of equipment → CUP (final rinse)
  • Pre-wash of equipment → NCUP (not final contact)
  • Detergent preparation → NCUP (no direct product contact)

The "Minimization" Principle for Microbial Identification

Notes E/F state that microbial identification is only needed for colonies that "look different". This principle has practical significance:

  • If POU A and POU B both show colonies of the same appearance, and A has been identified as Ralstonia pickettii, B does not need to be identified again
  • This reduces unnecessary identification cost, but it must be supported by documented logic
  • During an audit, the auditor may ask "why were the colonies in this batch not identified to species level?"

Writing Validation Reports and Annual Periodic Review

Reporting Requirements

Intermediary Phase Summary Report: At the conclusion of the PQ intermediary sampling, a summary report should be prepared that includes recommendations for the PQ extended sampling plan (sampling sites, parameters and frequencies).

Final PQ Report: At the conclusion of the PQ sampling (all portions) the final report, including any recommendations, should be made available to the participants of the PQ as well as to current and future users of the pharmaceutical water system, as this information may provide useful input regarding their daily use of the water and the associated SOPs, instructions, etc.

Annual Periodic System Evaluation (PSE): At least annually, as part of the PSE, data that has been generated from the sampling of a water system should be reviewed. This review affords the opportunity to:

  • See seasonal trends
  • Make changes to the sampling frequency (i.e., daily to weekly or weekly to monthly) based on the accumulated data
  • Adjust Alert Levels and/or Action Levels previously established
"The 1993 US FDA Guide to the Inspection of High Purity Water Systems describes the suggested sampling frequencies and durations; however, the total period required for the actual PQ should be based on a documented risk evaluation with rationales for the decisions, but for not less than one year."

POU Sampling Expectations: POU sampling is expected; however, some POUs cannot be sampled for practical reasons or physical limitations. In cases where POU sampling is not possible, a representative sample point may be substituted, but rationales for any substitute sampling sites should be established before the start of the PQ.

Initial POU Valve Sampling: When POU valves that may be inaccessible for sampling once permanently connected are initially installed, these POU valves may be included in the initial sampling and compared with data from the substitute sampling points for confirmation of suitability.

Commentary: Structure of PQ Reports and the Annual Review

Two Required PQ Reports

  • Intermediate phase summary report (after the intermediate phase ends): the core output is the "recommendation for the extended-phase sampling plan" — which points, which attributes, what frequency. This report is the ticket for "graduating" from the intermediate phase.
  • Final PQ report (after all sampling is complete): addressed to a wider audience (QA, production, future users). It is not just a data compilation; it should also include recommendations for routine operating SOPs.

Annual PSE: A Mechanism for Dynamic Adjustment of Sampling Frequency

After PQ ends, sampling frequency is not fixed. The PSE (Periodic System Evaluation), performed at least once a year, provides a data-driven opportunity to adjust frequency:

  • If all data over the past year are well below the Alert Level → consider reducing the frequency
  • If a seasonal rise in microbial counts appears → increase sampling in summer
  • If the number of Alert Level triggers increases → raise the frequency or reassess the limits

The PSE is the core tool for "continual improvement" in the ISPE framework.

Analogy: The PSE is Like an Annual Health Check

You do not take an ECG every day, but you have a full health check once a year and adjust your lifestyle (i.e. sampling frequency) according to the results. If your blood sugar was high last year, you need to monitor it more often this year. The PSE is the "annual health check" of the water system.

  • Identify regulatory risks associated with exceeded hold times and improper sample chain of custody

Sample Valve Design

Valve types, dead volume, sanitary requirements, outlet configuration

2.4 Original Text (ISPE GPG 2016)

General Design Principles

Good sample valve design should not allow contamination of the sample itself and should take into account both the internal function and external operation of the valve. Design considerations should include the method of connection, the length of the flow path, water retention within the valve body upstream of the seat/seal, and the amount of water held within the exit path after the valve has been closed.

This water downstream of the valve is either trapped or held by capillary properties of water. Valve outlet configuration should allow for drainage while not obstructing the sampling process. Good sample valve outlet fitting design should not be so constructed as to facilitate water holdup in the valve resulting in microbial growth.

Any hardware added after the sample valve often poses a significant risk of contaminating samples by introducing extrinsic contaminants that are not representative of the actual water produced and delivered by the system.

Sample valves should also be suitable for use in the selected system such that they should be appropriate for the pressure, temperature, and flow of the system. Sample valves should also be selected based on the location within the system.

"Sample valves designed to sanitary requirements should be employed when feasible and appropriate. Sanitary diaphragm valves are a well-accepted standard within the pharmaceutical industry although other designs have been successfully employed."

Ball valves and other non-sanitary valves should be avoided and only implemented when design constraints (high pressure, the requirement to meter flow rate, or other constraints) necessitate their use. In these cases, additional microbial control consideration may be required.

Commentary & CDMO Application Analysis

Core Design Principles

The core logic of sample valve design: the valve itself must not become a source of contamination. ISPE specifically highlights three design traps:

  • Dead volume: Water trapped upstream of the valve seat is a breeding ground for microorganisms. The system must be flushed thoroughly before every sampling to displace this stagnant water.
  • Outlet water retention: After the valve is closed, water remaining in the outlet path (held by surface tension) is exposed to ambient air, so the risk of microbial contamination is very high.
  • Add-on hardware: Any fitting, bend or adapter downstream of the valve is a potential entry point for extraneous contamination and should be kept to a minimum.

Key Concept: Hygienic Design Grades

In pharmaceutical water systems, the hygienic design grade increases with position in the system:

  • Feed water / pretreatment → industrial-grade valves acceptable (chemical sampling)
  • Downstream of primary purification → sanitary connections recommended
  • After final purification (PW/WFI distribution system) → hygienic design is mandatory

CDMO Practice: Valve Purchasing Decisions

When selecting sample valves, consider all of the following: (1) effective drainage (outlet pointing downward); (2) handle material (avoid heat-conducting stainless steel handles in hot water systems); (3) whether the valve supports alcohol injection (sanitizable valve). Before installation, physically test whether the commonly used sample containers can reach the outlet.

Key Points on Outlet Configuration and Material Selection

The sanitary clamp is the standard connection method in pharmaceutical distribution systems. It offers easy disassembly for cleaning, no threaded dead legs and fast assembly. Microbial sample valves should use this as the standard connection type.

Analogy: An Upward-Facing Outlet Is a Water-Collecting Funnel

Imagine a funnel turned upside down: the water does not run out, and instead pools in the neck. An upward-facing sample valve outlet works on the same principle: after the valve closes, residual water cannot drain by gravity and becomes a breeding ground for microorganisms. The correct design points the outlet downward to ensure natural drainage.

Special Attention for High-Temperature Systems

WFI systems are typically circulated at 80°C or above, at which temperature stainless steel valve handles are dangerous to touch. CDMO Cooling Valve Station Design Recommendations

WFI / pure steam sampling points should be equipped with a cooling device (such as a coil cooler or plate heat exchanger). The equipment must be adequately sized, and the cooling equipment itself should be sanitized regularly so that it does not become an additional source of contamination.

2.5

Sampling Techniques

Representative sampling, container selection, aseptic technique, step-by-step procedure

2.5.1–2.5.2 Representative Samples & Containers

2.5.1 Obtaining Representative Samples

The goal of any sampling program is to obtain samples that accurately reflect the contents of the larger system and/or to ensure water of suitable quality is used for manufacturing or other designated purposes. The vast majority of chemical constituents are uniformly distributed and relatively easy to sample with a minimal amount of rinsing and flushing.

Sampling technique, including flushing, that is representative of use should be performed, as bacterial and endotoxin contaminants will not be uniformly distributed because bacteria primarily adhere to surfaces in a system.

"The 1993 US FDA Guide to Inspection of High Purity Water Systems states that WFI should essentially be sterile and that the allowance of bacteria counts of 10 cfu per 100 ml are to account for sampling error."

2.5.2 Use of Appropriate Sample Containers

When taking samples, efforts should be taken to ensure that sampling containers do not introduce measurable amounts of the contaminant being monitored to the sample. This implies that several different sample containers may be required to sample any given location.

  • TOC: certified low-TOC containers — many containers will introduce extractables that increase TOC values
  • Bacteria: pre-sterilized containers
  • Endotoxin: depyrogenated or pyrogen-free containers (glass preferred, heat-treated ≥250°C for ≥30 min)
  • Conductivity: clean plastic containers (glass leaches sodium and silicate ions)

Sample containers should allow samples to be collected in an aseptic manner and minimize splashing and aeration upon collection. They should provide an adequate sealing mechanism and provide a means to record pertinent sample information.

Commentary & Key Points on Container Selection

The Microbial Challenge of Representative Sampling

Chemical attributes (TOC, conductivity) are uniformly distributed in the water, so in theory any sampling point gives the same result. Microorganisms and endotoxin, however, are not uniformly distributed: they adhere to surfaces and concentrate especially in the biofilm that forms on pipe walls, valve outlets and joints.

This is why FDA expects sampling at the actual POU: microbial data from a sample taken mid-loop in the distribution system cannot represent the water quality actually used by the process equipment.

Container Material Selection Principles (Three "Nots" and One "Must")

  • Non-additive: does not leach the analyte
  • Non-reactive: does not react chemically with the analyte
  • Non-absorptive: does not absorb the analyte
  • Must provide a means of aseptic sealing

Common Error #1: Mixing Up Containers

  • Glass bottle used for conductivity sampling → silicate and sodium ions leach out → result biased high
  • Flexible plastic bag used for endotoxin sampling → hydrophobic surface adsorbs endotoxin → result biased low (false negative)
  • Ordinary plastic bottle used for TOC → organics leach out → TOC biased high, triggering an OOS investigation
  • Tissue culture flask used for microbial sampling → the cell-adhesion-promoting surface coating may encourage bacterial attachment and growth

CO₂ Contamination: The Hidden Enemy of Conductivity Sampling

Atmospheric CO₂ dissolves into the sample water to form carbonic acid (HCO₃⁻), which raises conductivity. Even filling the container quickly cannot fully prevent this. This is why offline conductivity is almost always higher than the online reading: it does not mean the system has deteriorated, but that the sampling process introduces error. Online instruments should be the first choice for conductivity trend control.

2.5.3–2.5.9 Hoses, Training & Full Sampling Technique

2.5.3 When Hoses Are Involved

When the sample port is located in a tough to reach spot, hoses may be employed. However, hoses provide an opportunity to introduce contamination. If a hose is not disconnected and allowed to fully drain and dry between uses, bacteria may grow inside the hose that would introduce contamination to the sample. Hoses used for sampling should be installed immediately before samples are taken and then removed immediately afterward or within a qualified period.

2.5.4 Training

Personnel taking samples should be trained on proper sampling technique. The vast majority of variations in results can be traced back to variations in how samples are taken. Ideally, one person would be designated to take all samples and their technique would be regularly reviewed. Without rigorous training and continual review, variations in water quality data may reflect variations in sampling technique rather than actual variations in water quality.

2.5.7 Sample Point Preparation Before Taking Samples

Samplers should understand that preparation of the sample point for one type of analysis may introduce extrinsic contamination for a subsequent sample. Sample point preparation may involve flushing, spraying, disinfecting, or other treatments. Spraying and disinfecting a sample point with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) prior to taking a sample for bacterial testing could increase the value of a sample taken subsequently for TOC measurement.

Sampling order: Samples for bacteria and endotoxins should be taken last in the sequence; samples for chemical constituents, metals, and TOC should be taken first.

2.5.8 Sampling Technique — 10-Step Procedure

When sampling for process control or diagnostic purposes:

  1. Locate a proper size receptacle or drain to receive the water being flushed from the valve. The receptacle must be large enough.
  2. Slowly open the valve until it is fully open (or meets 8 FPS).
  3. Allow water to flow at full force for at least 30 seconds, then slowly close the valve to reduce flow to a comfortable rate.
  4. Collect samples for any required non-microbial constituents (conductivity, TOC) in appropriate containers.
  5. If spraying with IPA for bacteria/endotoxin, slowly close the sample valve.
  6. Spray the outlet of the sample valve with disinfectant (IPA).
  7. Wait 30 seconds.
  8. Repeat steps 2 to 3 and collect bacteria and endotoxin samples in appropriate containers.
  9. Close sample valve and post-treat if appropriate to remove or displace retained water in the outlet flow path.
  10. Ensure samples are labeled and handled/processed properly.
Commentary & Common Error Analysis

2.5.3 Managing the Contamination Risk of Hoses

Common Error #2: Leaving the Hose in Place

If a hose is not removed immediately after installation, the residual water inside it can form noticeable biofilm within a few hours at room temperature. At the next sampling, the preceding flush may not be enough to displace the bacteria growing inside the hose, giving a high result and triggering an OOS investigation.

Correct practice: Install the hose before sampling and remove it immediately afterwards, and establish through validation the maximum time it may remain in place (qualified period).

2.5.4 Personnel Training Is the Foundation of Data Quality

Analogy: The Sampler Is the System's Last Analytical Instrument

However good the WFI system is, if the sampler does not follow the procedure, the data are flawed. Differences in sampler technique (flush time, container choice, how the cap is opened) are often the largest source of variability in water quality data, and this is easily misjudged as a system problem.

Memory Aid for Sampling Order

Correct order: chemical first, microbial last

  1. Conductivity
  2. TOC
  3. Metal ions / other chemical attributes
  4. Microbial (bioburden): sample after IPA disinfection
  5. Bacterial endotoxin: sample last

Reason: if IPA is sprayed and TOC is then sampled after the IPA, IPA residue will raise the TOC result.

Common Error #3: QC Sampling That Replicates Process Operations

The purpose of QC sampling is to replicate how the process actually uses the water, not to "obtain the cleanest water". If the process uses a 30-second flush at 8 fps, QC sampling must flush the same way. If the process does not spray IPA, neither should the sampler. Any deviation makes the QC data unrepresentative.

Post-Sampling Valve Treatment

Water remaining in the outlet after sampling is a breeding ground for microorganisms (2.5.9). Correct practices:

  • Spray alcohol and let it evaporate to drive out the residual water
  • Use a special valve with a sanitant injection port to seal in the sanitant
  • Blow the outlet dry with sterile compressed air (closed-system POU)
  • Do not put a wet cap on the valve: standing water + a seal = bacterial growth

2.6

Handling of Samples

Hold times, transport temperature, storage, chain of custody

2.6 Original Text — Sample Handling

Offline Analysis Contamination Risks

If sample analysis is being performed by an in-line or on-line instrument or analyzer, then the sample fluid never leaves the closed confines of the water system en route to analysis. Where justifiable, having in-line or on-line instrumentation that can measure a homogeneously distributed attribute is generally preferable to collecting a sample and transporting it to an off-line analyzer in a remote laboratory.

Sampling for off-line analysis in the laboratory can affect the measured quality of a given attribute, exposing it to external contamination by any of several routes:

  • Through manual sampling procedures that could be poorly performed due to negligence, lack of training or inadequate procedural detail
  • From transfer between the closed confines of the water system and the sample container
  • Through contact of the inside of the sample container with the outside surface of a sample port
  • During its transport between the sample location and the laboratory
  • During storage prior to analysis
  • Through sample container opening and re-closure in the laboratory
  • During transport and/or restorage for repeat or investigative retest

Transport & Temperature Control

The transport of the samples to the laboratory for testing should be executed so that sampling container contents spillage or coolant seepage into samples does not occur. This may involve protective bagging of sample containers and/or the application of additional barriers (such as protective tape) to protect the sealing surfaces from leakage or seepage during transport.

Depending on the attributes of concern and its intended testing destination, temperature control may also be a consideration. This may involve the use of insulated containers, as well as the use of coolant materials such as waterless cold packs. The use of water and ice should be avoided since sloshing and seepage of this melting coolant into inadequately sealed sampling containers could occur. Similarly, the use of ultra-cold coolants should be avoided since the freezing of the sampled water in the container is usually contraindicated for most types of samples.

The duration and temperature of sample storage is a hotly debated issue, especially for attributes that could quantitatively change during that storage period.

2.6.1 Conductivity

Container cleanliness and atmospheric CO₂ are the two greatest risks. Sample containers should be filled as much as practical to minimize air entrapment in the headspace. As a best practice, samples for conductivity testing should not be refrigerated during storage while waiting testing, because it increases the time required to run the Stage 2 conductivity test. Sample testing should occur as soon as possible.

2.6.2 Total Organic Carbon (TOC)

A significant impact on TOC during sample storage can be from organic residues leaching from the container. For TOC samples only, if certified low TOC containers are used, the samples should be suitable for up to 2 to 3 days at room temperature or when refrigerated without suffering any substantial increase or decrease in TOC level. If reused sample containers are employed, testing within a few hours after collection is advised.

2.6.3 Microbial Count

The concern is that either microbial proliferation or microbial loss due to adsorption or starvation could occur if the elapsed time after sample collection is more than a few hours. Generally, refrigeration is recommended if testing cannot commence within about 2 hours of sample collection. Refer to USP <1231> or other relevant compendia for guidance regarding refrigerated hold times.

2.6.4 Bacterial Endotoxin

Because at least a portion of the endotoxin in a sample could be of a particulate nature, the same guidelines as for microbial testing should be followed for endotoxin test samples.

Commentary & Impact of Laboratory Scheduling

Seven Contamination Windows in Offline Analysis

ISPE lists seven contamination risk points in offline sampling. Each is a potential weakness in laboratory QC data quality:

Contamination Window Analysis (CDMO Perspective)

  • Windows 1-3: At the sampling site: personnel technique, container handling, outlet contact
  • Windows 4-5: Transport and storage: temperature control, hold time management
  • Windows 6-7: Opening the sample in the laboratory, handling of retests

This is why inline/online instruments are the best choice: they eliminate all seven windows.

Principles of Transport Temperature Control

The Prohibition on Ice-Water Transport

ISPE states explicitly that ice and water mixtures must not be used as coolant, because melted ice water can seep into containers that are not fully sealed. Use waterless cold packs in an insulated box instead. Also avoid ultra-low-temperature coolants (such as dry ice) so that the sample does not freeze.

CDMO Laboratory Scheduling Recommendations

  • Conductivity: Test as soon as possible (refrigeration not recommended); sample in the morning and analyze the same morning
  • TOC: Use certified low-TOC containers, with a buffer of 2-3 days at most; but be sure to schedule within the time limit
  • Microbial: Start incubation within 2 hours; otherwise refrigerate immediately (2-8°C) and record the time refrigeration started
  • Endotoxin: Same storage conditions as microbial; refer to USP <1231>

In practice, on a day with multi-attribute sampling, the "sampling to laboratory" timeline must be planned so that the most stringent item (microbial) can be processed within 2 hours.

Common Error #4: Delivering Samples to the Laboratory Too Late

One of the most common causes of deviations is samples sitting at the site too long after sampling (waiting for other sampling points or for the transport person). The temperature and time during this period are not recorded, leaving a gap in the chain of custody, so the sample status cannot be reconstructed in a later OOS investigation.

Solution: Specify the maximum sample holding time at the site in the sampling SOP, label each container with the sampling time, and make the laboratory verify it on receipt.

Why Conductivity Samples Are Not Refrigerated

Why is it actually better not to refrigerate conductivity samples?

The USP <645> Stage 2 conductivity test must be performed at a specified temperature (usually 25°C). A sample taken out of a 2-8°C refrigerator has to wait to warm up to room temperature before testing can start, which adds waiting time and more opportunity for CO₂ absorption. For conductivity, testing at room temperature as soon as possible is therefore more reasonable than testing after refrigeration and warm-up.

2.6.6 Avoiding Extrinsic Contamination Through Closure Design

When a sample is collected, the ease and cleanliness of opening and closing the sample container should be considered. This activity occurs multiple times between sampling and eventual analysis in the laboratory. The process of opening and closing the container during sampling has specific precautions:

  • Placement of fingers near, or on, the sealing surfaces of the closure should be avoided to prevent a source of extrinsic sample contamination, even when the hands are gloved and alcohol sanitized
  • Fingers should be able to grasp and open the closure at a reasonable distance from any sealing surface
  • Sealing surfaces of the container should not contact the potentially contaminated external surfaces of the container or closure during opening and closing

2.6.7 Chain of Custody

Maintaining a chain of custody provides documented proof of where the samples are and have been and under what conditions, at what times, and handled by whom. This may be a significant consideration when there is an excursion in the value of one or more attributes. The sample's history could have a bearing on how to interpret the test results.

Chain of custody information needs to be proactively collected on every sample in order to have a retrospective interpretation. Chain of custody should also be considered for samples being sent to contract laboratories or sister sites within the same company for testing.

If the test results for samples become included in any legal disputes, chain of custody should be able to verify that the samples are legitimate and have maintained their integrity. The user may not know this in advance.

Cap Design and Aseptic Handling

The "Do Not Touch the Sealing Surface" Principle

Whether or not the gloves have been sprayed with alcohol, fingers should never touch the sealing surface of the container cap. In microbial sampling, gloves are only "clean", not "sterile". Choose containers designed so that the cap can be opened without touching the sealing surface, such as flip-top caps or screw caps designed for side gripping.

Chain of Custody

Required CoC Records

  • Sampling location / sample ID
  • Sampling date and time (to the minute)
  • Name of the sampler
  • Packing time and temperature record
  • Cold chain verification (thermometer reading or electronic temperature record)
  • Laboratory receipt time and name of the person receiving
  • If retesting is needed: time of each opening and the person handling it

  • Microbial hold time exceeds that specified in USP <1231> → OOS result is unreliable and the investigation conclusion may be rejected
  • Chain of custody missing → sample integrity cannot be confirmed during an inspection, which may trigger a 483 observation
  • Conductivity sample tested directly after refrigeration (without warming up) → Stage 2 temperature condition not met, data invalid
  • Endotoxin sample stored in a plastic container for a long time → endotoxin lost by adsorption → risk of false negative

2.7

Parametric (Real Time) Release

Online monitoring, PAT, real-time release, microbial limitations, rapid methods

2.7 Original Text — Parametric Release

Concept and Regulatory Basis

Releasing products in real time based on monitoring and control of manufacturing in process parameters critical to finished product quality has been a long-term goal for pharmaceutical water systems. This approach employs Process Analytical Technology (PAT) and Statistical Process Control (SPC) and should be properly validated.

Generally, these parameters should be continuously monitored, so that deviations from acceptable ranges can be detected and process adjustments made to maintain the acceptable finished product quality. Speed and timeliness of these analyses is crucial, which is why real time measurements are needed for real time product release.

The traditional and untimely alternative for release of pharmaceutical waters is to collect water samples and send them to a lab for testing prior to release, all by human mediated procedures.

2.7.1 Chemical Attributes — Real-Time Capability

Since 1996, with the changes made with USP 23rd Edition, 5th Supplement, USP has facilitated the use of:

  • On-line monitoring of TOC (USP <643>)
  • In-line monitoring of Conductivity (USP <645>)

The only provision is that the data from these on-line, in-line, and at-line instruments should be representative of those attributes as measured at the POU. Chemical attributes are homogeneously distributed hence the values measured at remote locations in a simple water distribution system usually are identical to the values measured at the POUs, except for the unavoidable issues associated with sample contamination during collection and off-line testing.

However, if opportunities for non-equivalence exist, due to complex system designs (with sub or parallel loops, heat exchangers, etc.), and potentially TOC contributing hardware, then additional sampling points may be required.

2.7.2 Microbiological Attributes — Limitations

Microbiological attributes of pharmaceutical water are not homogeneously distributed. Although microbiological quality is not a USP monograph requirement, suggested action levels are furnished in USP <1231> which reflect the original guidance established by the FDA for their inspectors in 1993.

Microbial counts in the water being used from POUs could vary significantly in the distribution system, as planktonic or free floating microorganisms originate from biofilms and biofilms are localized in the piping or hardware between the water distribution system and the POU. This localized phenomenon can cause rapid on-line microbial testing to be an inaccurate representation of the microbial quality of the water that is being used for manufacturing.

Similar concerns exist with bacterial endotoxin, as it is produced by the Gram-negative microorganisms that can inhabit the water distribution system, the water purification system, and may be present in the feed water.

Commentary & Real-Time Release Strategy Analysis

Core Logic of Parametric Release

The Essence of Real-Time Release (RTR)

Traditional approach: sample → send to laboratory → wait for results (hours to days) → release the water based on the results. RTR aims to replace offline sampling and testing with continuous online measurement: measure and know immediately, and release in real time.

Key prerequisite: the online measurement data must be equivalent to the offline results at the POU, and this must be validated. This equivalence is the core condition under which USP allows RTR.

The Fundamental Difference Between Chemical and Microbial Attributes

Analogy: Uniform Soup vs. Clay Lumps

TOC and conductivity in water are like a well-stirred soup: every spoonful has the same composition. Monitoring at any point in the loop therefore gives results representative of the POU water quality.

Microorganisms and endotoxin are like clay lumps (biofilm fragments) floating in the water: they are unevenly distributed and cluster locally. An online instrument at a mid-loop point simply cannot represent the true microbial status near the POU outlet valve.

Summary of USP Regulatory Basis

  • USP <643>: TOC test method (supports online monitoring)
  • USP <645>: Conductivity test method (supports online monitoring)
  • USP <1231>: Guidance on water for pharmaceutical purposes (microbial action levels, hold times, etc.)
  • FDA 1993 Guide: Guide to Inspections of High Purity Water Systems (WFI microbial action levels)

Real-Time Release Does Not Apply to Microbial Attributes: Regulatory Risk

If a firm tries to use a mid-loop online microbial instrument in place of POU grab sampling for product release, this practice is contrary to current USP <1231> and FDA positions, because:

  • Biofilm is localized in the piping near the POU, which a mid-loop online instrument cannot represent
  • POU testing is the basis of regulatory "release testing"
  • Rapid microbiological methods (RMM) can replace traditional culture, but still require a grab sample from the POU

2.7.3 Grab Sampling vs. On-Line/At-Line Monitoring

Because of the potential for localized, outlet specific contamination, grab samples for microbial and endotoxin testing have been unequivocally accepted from POUs as reflective of the quality of the water being used at the POU. However, manual sampling and conventional testing approaches can lead to days of delay in data availability and potential for artificially influenced good or bad data.

If rapid on-line or at-line technologies were installed for microbial and endotoxin testing at a single point in the distribution system, the resulting data may be valuable for process control purposes, but could not be used for product release purposes. POU testing is required for release.

2.7.4 Rapid Microbial Detection Techniques

Rapid techniques can be discriminated as being either destructive (microbial cell is killed during analysis — cannot be speciated) or non-destructive (cells not killed — identification possible by culturing).

Rapid microbial detection techniques can also be discriminated by whether or not there is a need to amplify the number of original CFUs. The quickest techniques which can generate data in perhaps 1 to 2 hours usually involve destructive techniques. Many of the slower techniques are non-destructive but take longer (1 to 3 days) for data availability.

2.7.5 Rapid Endotoxin Detection Techniques

The detection of the miniscule levels of endotoxin in WFI can currently only be accomplished by the molecular amplification process involved with the bioassay employing Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) reagents. Analogs and chemical modifications of these reagents have allowed endotoxin to be quantitated photometrically in as little as 15 minutes. The LAL technology has been commercialized as a rapid portable version that can be easily employed at-line, making it a near real time at-line test, rivaling the test result turnaround time of some on-line TOC instruments.

2.7.6 Future of Real Time Release for Pharmaceutical Water

The localized formation of biofilm between the distribution loop outlets and the POUs has consigned the definitive microbial testing of the water used by manufacturing to be from that POU where the water is delivered to a process or piece of equipment.

From an on-line instrument perspective, and with the rapid advances occurring with instrumentation, real time release of the water for microbial attributes may become a more accepted practice in the relatively near future. For WFI systems where endotoxin levels are also an important attribute, the use of the portable at-line endotoxin test kit essentially adds endotoxin to the list of attributes that can be assured as passing in the finished water with an essentially real time test.

A 1 to 3 day delay in result availability is not suitable for a parametric or real time release approach, but currently emerging technologies may make real time release a reality.

Commentary & Rapid Method Strategy Analysis

Classification Logic of Rapid Microbiological Methods (RMM)

Destructive vs. Non-Destructive Methods

Destructive: The test kills the bacterial cells. Advantage: fast (1-2 hours). Disadvantage: speciation is not possible. Suitable for: process control (knowing whether organisms are present, without needing to know which species).

Non-destructive: Does not kill the bacteria. Advantage: allows subsequent culture and identification. Disadvantage: still needs some incubation time (1-3 days). Suitable for: OOS investigations (where the species must be identified).

Analogy: Rapid LAL Reagent Is Like a "Rapid Test Kit"

Traditional LAL must be performed in the laboratory, with results taking several hours to the next day. A portable LAL kit gives a result on site in 15 minutes, much like a COVID rapid test kit: you still need a grab sample (taken from the POU), but the waiting time is greatly shortened, approaching "real-time release".

CDMO WFI Release Strategy Recommendations

The currently best-optimized combined WFI release strategy:

  • Chemical attributes (TOC + conductivity) → continuous monitoring with online instruments + periodic POU equivalence verification → real-time release
  • Endotoxin → portable LAL on a POU grab sample → result within 15 minutes → near real-time release
  • Microbial → rapid RMM (1-2 hr, destructive) or traditional culture (2-5 days) → still the slowest bottleneck

Manufacturing scheduling must account for the longest wait for microbial results in order to decide the risk management strategy for "use the water first, complete the results afterwards".

The Important Distinction: "Online Bio-Instruments for Process Control vs. Product Release"

If an online microbial counter is installed in the middle of the system:

  • Process control: Can be used, and is valuable: monitoring trends and detecting system anomalies
  • Product release: Cannot be used, because the data cannot represent POU water quality

This distinction is critical in regulatory review and must not be confused.

Chapter 2.7 Summary: Key Takeaways for Real-Time Release

  • Chemical RTR is established (since 1996): TOC (USP <643>) and Conductivity (USP <645>) support on-line/in-line release provided POU equivalence is validated.
  • Microbiological RTR is not yet achievable for release: Biofilm localization means POU grab sampling remains the regulatory gold standard. On-line microbial data is for process control only.
  • Endotoxin is approaching near-real-time: Portable at-line LAL (~15 min) from POU grab sample is a practical near-RTR solution for WFI systems.
  • Rapid microbial methods (RMM) reduce wait time but don't eliminate POU requirement: 1–2 hr destructive methods improve scheduling; 1–3 day non-destructive methods allow speciation.
  • The future is approaching: Emerging on-line technologies may eventually make microbial RTR from POU a reality, but current pharmacopoeial and regulatory frameworks do not yet support this.

Steam Type Selection — Table 3.1 Summary

Pure Steam — Key Characteristics

Pure steam may also be referred to as clean steam, pyrogen-free steam, WFI steam, or USP purified water steam. Regardless of the term used, when condensed, the steam condensate must meet requirements for USP/EP WFI.

Pure steam is predominantly used for sterilization. Steam used for sterilization in autoclaves for international manufacturing should also meet the requirements of BS EN 285:2015 for non-condensable gases, degrees of superheat, and dryness.

  • No additives; limited generated superheat (except when generated pressure is significantly higher than use pressure).
  • Condensate must meet USP/EP WFI requirements, have no buffer, and have a relatively low pH compared to plant steam.

Feed Water Pretreatment Requirements

Feed water requires proper pretreatment, as all generators are susceptible to scaling and corrosion. Pretreatment includes:

  • Minimizing or preventing scale formation to minimize or prevent corrosion.
  • Removal of objectionable volatiles, such as ammonia, that are not removed by distillation/steam generating processes and would carry over into the clean steam.
  • Utilization of the facility's high-purity water system (e.g., PW, WFI). Note: steam generators may not require feed water to meet PW or WFI requirements. This practice, however, ignores the ability of the pure steam generator to remove impurities.
Commentary and Application — Section 3.1

Regulatory implications: when must a PSG be used?

The rule of thumb is simple: whenever the steam or its condensate comes into direct contact with product, containers, API or the load being sterilized, a Pure Steam Generator (PSG) must be used. This is the regulatory floor, and "the chemicals have been removed" is not an acceptable substitute argument.

CDMO practice note: when taking on a new client's product, a contract manufacturer should confirm the source of pure steam supplied to the autoclaves at the very start. If the client requires EP compliance for export to Europe, BS EN 285 compliance becomes mandatory.

Analogy: pure steam is the "gaseous version of water for injection"

Picture steam condensing into water when it reaches the surface of the item being sterilized. If that water drips into a vial or touches the product, its quality is equivalent to water for injection (WFI). So the pure steam standard is, in essence, "extending the WFI quality requirement to the gas phase."

What this means for CDMO autoclave qualification

In an aseptic fill-finish CDMO, all autoclaves used to sterilize containers, equipment and filters use pure steam. During qualification you must demonstrate that the steam entering the chamber meets:

  • Condensate chemical quality (WFI standard)
  • BS EN 285 physical parameters (NCG, dryness, superheat)

This is also why pure steam sampling cannot simply be handled in the water system chapter and needs its own sampling plan.

Commentary — Regulatory Requirements

Two complementary regulatory frameworks

Pure steam must satisfy requirements at two levels at the same time:

  • USP <1231> / EP pure steam monograph: controls condensate chemical quality — the "water quality" side, with WFI as the benchmark
  • BS EN 285:2015: controls physical steam quality — the "steam state" side, covering NCG, dryness and superheat

The two complement each other and neither can be dropped. Passing on chemistry but failing on physical quality still cannot assure sterilization efficacy.

Why can a nitrogen blanket push NCG out of specification?

A nitrogen blanket is used to prevent microbial growth in WFI/PW storage tanks, but nitrogen dissolves in the water. When that water is heated in the pure steam generator, the dissolved nitrogen is released and becomes non-condensable gas (NCG) in the steam.

Practical takeaway: if nitrogen-blanketed WFI is used as PSG feed water, a degassifier / deaerator should be added; otherwise NCG is likely to exceed the 3.5% limit in BS EN 285.

Analogy: degassing is like boiling mineral water

When you open a bottle of sparkling mineral water, bubbles appear (dissolved CO₂). Heat it to boiling and the gas escapes. The degassing unit on a pure steam generator works on the same principle: the water is heated before it enters the boiler so that dissolved gases escape "early" instead of travelling with the steam into the sterilizer chamber and creating cold spots.

Points for CDMOs serving the European market

If your CDMO manufactures sterile drug product for the European market, the qualification report must state explicitly that the pure steam complies with BS EN 285. This is not only a technical requirement but also a common inspection focus. We recommend adding a dedicated section to the Qualification Plan that maps to each applicable clause of BS EN 285.

Hierarchy of standards

In the pure steam field there is a network of standards that refer to one another:

3.2.3 Distribution Systems
Materials and Design Practices

Pure steam distribution systems have additional requirements for contact materials due to the aggressive nature of pure steam. Corrosion-resistant 316L stainless steel tubing or solid drawn pipe is typically utilized in new systems. Legacy systems may be constructed of 304/304L stainless steel.

Surface Finish Guidelines

Surface finishes for pure steam distribution systems are not as strict as for pure water distribution systems because of the high operating temperatures and self-sanitizing nature of pure steam. Considerations include:

  • Mill finish or mechanically polished pipe may be used; typical mechanical polishes of 35 to 20 Ra are utilized for clean steam generators and distribution systems.
  • Internal surfaces, including orbital and mechanical welds, may be passivated to improve corrosion resistance.
  • Electropolishing may be utilized but may not be necessary because of the high operating temperatures and an increase in the formation of rouge.

General Design Practices

  • Allowance for thermal expansion and the ability for condensate to drain effectively.
  • Sloping of piping between 1/8 inch/foot (10 mm/m) to 1/4 inch/foot (20 mm/m) as outlined in ASME BPE-2014, but good engineering practices and risk analysis tools may be used to determine the appropriate slope.
  • Sloping of piping in the direction of steam flow and to a low point condensate trap for adequate condensate removal.

Note: Sloping of distribution piping in the direction opposite of flow is not recommended because condensate may be easily reintroduced into the pure steam.

Reference: ISPE Baseline Guide: Water and Steam Systems (Second Edition), Chapter 10; and ASME BPE-2014 (Figures SD-4.2.2-1 and SD-4.2.2-2).
Commentary — Distribution System Design

316L vs 304L: why use 316L for pure steam?

In a high-temperature steam environment, the molybdenum (Mo) content of 316L gives better resistance to pitting corrosion, especially where trace chlorides are present. 304L is widely used in ambient-temperature water systems, but under the high-temperature cycling conditions of pure steam, long-term use may lead to crevice corrosion.

System design risks of condensate pooling

Improper slope design of steam lines is one of the most common design defects in pure steam systems. Pooled condensate can:

  • Reduce the dryness of the steam entering the chamber (dryness falls)
  • Cause "water hammer" that damages piping
  • Create a risk of microbial growth at low points in the system

The 10-20 mm/m slope specified by ASME BPE is a minimum requirement; at the design stage the larger slope value should be preferred.

Analogy: steam piping is like a drainage ditch

Imagine a drainage ditch with no slope: the water cannot flow away and just stands there. Pure steam piping works the same way — it must slope in the direction of steam flow so that condensate keeps draining to the steam trap rather than pooling in the pipe. A "reverse slope" (a slope against the direction of steam flow) lets condensate run back into the steam stream and is a serious design error.

Piping design checks at the Factory Acceptance Test (FAT)

When a CDMO accepts a pure steam system, the Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) should include physical confirmation of piping slope (measured with a level). Pay particular attention to:

  • The actual slope after thermal expansion (deformation of the piping at operating temperature)
  • Whether support/hanger locations match the design slope
  • Whether the longest horizontal runs have enough drain points

Commentary — Principles for Selecting Sampling Locations

The logic of CQA-driven sampling locations

Different quality attributes need different sampling equipment and locations. This is the core principle of sampling design for pure steam systems: define the CQAs first, then decide where and how to sample based on the characteristics of each CQA.

3.3.1 Critical Quality Attributes Define Sampling Locations

Summary: Steam Quality Parameters — Specifications, Test Methods & Significance

Overview of steam quality parameters — specifications, test methods and significance

3.3.1.1 Non-condensable Gases (NCG)

If non-condensable gas removal is required, these gases may be removed in the pretreatment to a pure steam generation system either by applying heat or using a degassifier because these gases pass directly through a pure steam generator and into the pure steam.

Including a degassifier at the beginning of a pure steam distribution system may be considered. The degassifier can be as simple as a tee with the vent leg pointing upward and terminating with a balanced pressure air vent that opens to expel NCGs from the pure steam.

Non-condensable gases should be sampled with a sample valve suitable for its intended purposes. Sampling for chemical and endotoxin purposes would use a different sample valve.

Proper sampling for non-condensable gases is described in BS EN 285:2015, Chapter 22.1, and uses the equation:

NCG Concentration Formula

Cn = (Vb / Vc) × 100%

  • Cn = concentration of non-condensable gases (%)
  • Vb = volume of water displaced from the burette (ml)
  • Vc = volume of water collected in the graduated cylinder (ml)

Limit: Cn ≤ 3.5% per BS EN 285:2015

3.3.1.2 Dryness

Dryness refers to the level of steam saturation and is the ratio of vapor mass to the mass of the steam mixture. Dryness is dimensionless; a value of 1.0 represents ideal dry saturated steam.

Dry saturated steam may have excess heat introduced, which creates superheated steam. The amount of superheating should be minimized for efficient steam sterilization, since superheated steam is not saturated and will create uneven temperature distribution at the surface being sterilized.

Dryness may be adversely impacted by system design issues:

  • Incorrectly or inadequately sloped lines
  • Inadequate condensate removal (insufficient or incorrectly located traps)
  • Insufficient pipe insulation causing excess condensation
  • Incorrect point-of-use (POU) design

Specification: Dryness should be greater than 0.95 for metal loads and 0.90 for other loads per BS EN 285:2015, Chapter 13.3.3.

For dryness sampling, the sample should be taken from the center of the steam distribution pipe.

Note: this sample does not take into account the moisture present as a film on the pipe wall or the condensate on the bottom of the pipe. It is, therefore, not an accurate value, but represents only an approximation. However, this sampling technique has become the most commonly used method.

3.3.1.3 Superheating

Superheating must be below 25°C when the pure steam is expanded to atmospheric pressure per BS EN 285:2015, Chapter 13.3.4. Samples should be taken from the center of the steam pipe.

Practical simplification: If the pressure is below 2 barG (~30 psi) and the dryness value is below 1.0, the steam cannot get superheated and will always pass the superheat requirement for pure steam.

For higher pressures (>2 barG), the formula is:

Superheat Check Formula (P > 2 barG)

D × H + (1 – D) × h < 2724.53 kJ/kg

  • D = measured dryness
  • H = enthalpy of steam at measured steam pressure at POU (kJ/kg)
  • h = enthalpy of water at measured steam pressure at POU (kJ/kg)
  • Limit: 2724.53 kJ/kg (= atmospheric enthalpy 2673.8 + 2.029 × 25°K)
3.3.1.4 Conductivity, TOC, and Endotoxins

Condensate collected from pure steam sample points should comply with the specifications for WFI grade water. Such sample points should be placed at:

  • The outlet of the pure steam generator
  • POUs or as close to POUs as possible

There may be one sampling cooler at each sampling point, or coolers can be transported from sample point to sample point, depending on the design of the system.

A simple method for sample collection is to have a valve with a sanitary connection at each drainage point, allowing connection of a steam trap and condensate cooler without closing the system down. Design considerations for sample valves are identical to those for water systems (Chapter 2 of this Guide).

In-Depth Commentary — NCG, Dryness and Superheat

NCG: the most overlooked cause of failure

When a steam penetration test fails during autoclave validation, operators' first reaction is often to check the temperature probe calibration or the biological indicators. In field practice, however, excessive NCG is the most common root cause, especially right after system maintenance or a change of feed water system.

How NCG works: non-condensable gases (mainly air, nitrogen and CO₂) form an insulating air pocket in the chamber that prevents steam from condensing on the load surface, so local temperatures never reach the sterilization temperature (121°C or 134°C).

Analogy: NCG is like the air in a thermos

Imagine heating a sealed container with steam. If the container is pre-filled with air, the steam mixes with the air to form a "steam-air mixture." The saturation temperature of this mixture is lower than that of pure steam, meaning that at the same pressure the temperature is lower and sterilization cannot be achieved. This is how a "cold spot" forms.

What a dryness value of 0.95 means physically

A dryness value of D = 0.95 means that 95% of the steam is vapor (gas phase) and 5% is suspended water droplets (liquid phase). For metal loads this ratio is sufficient — a small amount of moisture does not significantly affect the heat transfer capability of the steam.

But when dryness is < 0.90, too many liquid droplets will:

  • Form a water film on the load surface that hinders steam penetration
  • Dilute the saturated steam concentration in the chamber
  • Leave the load still wet after sterilization, affecting packaging integrity

Limitations of the sampling method: error from centerline sampling

ISPE states explicitly that dryness values sampled from the center of the pipe do not include the wall film and the condensate at the bottom of the pipe, so they are approximations rather than exact values. Even so, this method has become standard industry practice.

Key point: when interpreting test results, understand the limits of the test method, and state clearly in the qualification report that the sampling method follows BS EN 285:2015 Ch. 22.2, so as to maintain the linkage to the "established acceptance values."

Steam quality testing strategy for CDMO autoclave qualification

In an aseptic fill-finish CDMO, autoclaves are typically used to sterilize:

  • Glass vials and stoppers for filling
  • Process equipment (compounding vessels, filter housings)
  • Autoclave bags and consumables

Recommended qualification strategy:

  1. Perform all three steam quality tests (NCG, dryness, superheat) at the OQ stage
  2. Repeat the three tests during the initial sampling phase of PQ
  3. Periodic annual retesting (at least once a year)
  4. Retest immediately after every PSG maintenance activity

Overview — Sampling Plan Framework
"Pure Steam is prepared from suitably pretreated source water analogously to either the pretreatment used for Purified Water or Water for Injection." — USP

Pure steam that is used in aseptic applications, as well as applications where the steam or condensate directly contacts products, excipients, container closures, or equipment/components, should be appropriately sampled.

  • Restoration from scheduled/unscheduled shutdown
  • Maintenance
  • Validation
  • Normal routine operation

Risk analysis tools may be used to develop a sampling rationale that takes into account:

  • The criticality of the POUs on the system
  • The frequency of use of each POU
  • The accessibility for sampling
  • Historical performance
Commentary — Design Thinking Behind the Sampling Plan

Four system-state dimensions of the sampling plan

ISPE classifies the sampling needs of a pure steam system by "system state" rather than simply giving one fixed frequency. This reflects the risk-based approach of modern GMP:

  • Recovery after shutdown: highest risk, requires the most intensive sampling
  • After maintenance: system integrity may have been disturbed
  • During validation: follows the three-phase PQ plan
  • Routine operation: periodic monitoring at a frequency set on the basis of the qualification results

Applying risk analysis in sampling plan design

Different POUs may carry different risk levels. A POU close to a direct product contact point (for example, an autoclave used for filter sterilization above a filling machine) carries a higher risk than a POU for non-contact use. Sampling frequency should reflect this difference.

3.4.1 Commissioning and Qualification (C&Q)
Definitions
"Commissioning: a well-planned, documented, and managed engineering approach to the start-up and turnover of facilities, systems, and equipment to the end-user that results in a safe functional environment that meets established design requirements and stakeholder expectations." — ISPE GPG: Approaches to C&Q of Pharmaceutical Water and Steam Systems

Qualification is intended to provide documented evidence with oversight by quality assurance personnel that a pure steam system will consistently produce the appropriate quality of steam to end users in accordance with regulatory expectations.

Commentary — The C&Q Concept

The essential difference between commissioning and qualification

Commissioning is an engineering activity: confirming that equipment operates to its design specifications. It is Good Engineering Practice (GEP), is not formally overseen by QA, but its data can be used to support later qualification.

Qualification is a regulatory activity: under QA oversight, it provides documented evidence that the system consistently delivers pure steam that meets specification.

ISPE recommends making good use of commissioning data: if the commissioning plan is sufficiently rigorous and the acceptance criteria have been approved by QA, commissioning data can be used to support the design of the PQ sampling plan and may even shorten the formal PQ.

3.4.2 Commissioning Sampling & Qualification (PQ)
Commissioning Sampling

Sampling and monitoring during commissioning is considered GEP. Commissioning is where the intended operating parameters of the equipment are identified and tested.

  • Chemical testing with condensate meeting applicable WFI monograph requirements (EP, USP <1231>, JP16).
  • Duration varies depending on complexity and size of the system.
  • Once the entire system is operating, each unit operation should be tested at least once during commissioning.
  • If any changes are made to defined CPPs during commissioning, the commissioning phase should be repeated.
3.4.2.1 Qualification — Three-Phase PQ

Qualification typically consists of IQ, OQ, and PQ. PQ is organized into three phases:

  1. Initial sampling
  2. Intermediary sampling
  3. Extended sampling

Phase 1: Initial Sampling

  • Demonstrates the system consistently operates within predetermined ranges and delivers required steam quality.
  • Sampling points: immediately after the PSG, at POUs, and at worst-case locations (most distant from PSG or lowest points in the system).
  • Physical attribute testing (NCG, superheat, dryness) should be performed while equipment (e.g., autoclaves) is operating.
  • Duration: minimum 3 consecutive working days, up to 10 working days.
  • Pure steam should NOT be used for production purposes during initial sampling.
  • Formal provisional release requires QM review and summary report.
  • For larger systems with multiple branches: sample ends of each individual pipe branch.

Phase 2: Intermediary Sampling

  • Same duration as initial phase; occurs immediately after initial phase completion.
  • Sampling frequency nearly identical to initial phase, except PSG outlet need not be sampled daily (reduced frequency — e.g., start and end of intermediary phase).
  • QM assessment required before uninterrupted progression to extended phase.
  • Pure steam produced during intermediary phase may be provisionally released for manufacturing, provided initial phase was assessed as successful by QM.

Summary Interim Reports

Required after both initial and intermediary phases. Reports:

  • Post-initial: justifies provisional system release for manufacturing.
  • Post-intermediary: justifies full release for manufacturing purposes.

Phase 3: Extended Sampling

  • Demonstrates the system over a longer period (typically balance of the year).
  • PQ is considered complete when performance data for a full year (inclusive of all phases) are obtained.
  • PSG outlet: sampled weekly.
  • Other locations: rotating basis so that each location is sampled every two weeks.

A final report documenting the overall qualification process (all phases) with a clear conclusion statement must be reviewed and authorized by all relevant functions including QM.

In-Depth Commentary — The Three-Phase PQ Strategy

Criteria for selecting "worst-case locations"

Initial sampling must be performed at "worst-case locations," which are usually:

  • The POU farthest from the PSG: steam quality may degrade over a long run of piping
  • The lowest point of the system: most likely to accumulate condensate, with the worst dryness
  • The end of the longest branch: for systems with multiple branches

This design thinking is the same as the "worst-case challenge" concept in biological safety testing: if the worst location passes, there is greater confidence in all the others.

Analogy: the three-phase PQ is like a new employee's probation period

Initial: the first month on the job, closely observing whether the person can meet the requirements of the role.
Intermediary: the second month, confirming consistency of performance; the person can begin to take on part of the formal workload.
Extended: a full year of probation, going through all seasonal changes (summer and winter temperature swings, equipment maintenance), before the person is formally recognized as reliable.

PQ timeline planning when starting a new CDMO client project

For a CDMO taking on the first commercial batch for a new client, the PQ timeline of the pure steam system should be included on the critical path of the project plan:

  • Initial + Intermediary: at least 6-20 working days (depending on system size)
  • Extended: the remainder until a full 1 year is reached
  • The first commercial batch can be produced at the earliest after QM approval at the Intermediary phase

This means that from system start-up to the first formal commercial batch, a PQ buffer of at least 2-4 weeks is needed. Overlooking this time requirement in the project plan is a common cause of CDMO project delays.

Routine Monitoring Considerations

Following successful qualification, routine ongoing monitoring should involve sampling and testing at frequencies supported by a documented risk analysis process. Considerations include:

  • The use of the system, including processes where steam is used (e.g., autoclaves)
  • Design and implementation of POUs (frequency of port use, cool points, dead legs, line slopes, types of valves, location of port)

Commentary — The Routine Monitoring Program

Different monitoring frequencies for chemical/biological and physical attributes

ISPE states explicitly that the physical attributes of steam quality (NCG, dryness, superheat) and the chemical/biological attributes (conductivity, TOC, endotoxin) may be monitored at different frequencies.

This is because:

  • Chemical/biological attributes: mainly affected by feed water quality; weekly or monthly sampling can detect trends in good time
  • Physical attributes: mainly determined by system design and change slowly once the system is stable; however, they may be affected after maintenance or by seasonal temperature changes

In the example Table 3.5, steam quality (NCG/superheat/dryness) only needs to be tested once a year at the autoclave point of use, whereas chemical quality is tested weekly at the PSG outlet.

Trend management: Alert Level vs. Action Level

Routine monitoring data must feed a trending mechanism:

  • Alert Level: an excursion indicates that the system is starting to drift and needs investigation, but production does not have to stop immediately
  • Action Level: an excursion indicates that the system may be out of control and CAPA must be taken immediately

If trends show that the system is in a "state of control," a reduction of sampling frequency can be considered (implemented through change control). Conversely, if an adverse trend appears, sampling frequency must be increased.

Why is testing for total aerobic count (TAC) not required?

Steam itself has lethal properties — saturated steam above 121°C kills all microorganisms. Testing pure steam condensate for total aerobic count is therefore scientifically unnecessary: if the steam reaches the specified temperature, no viable organisms can survive.

Note, however, that if condensate is not handled properly after collection, it may be re-contaminated from the environment in the container. Aseptic technique in the sampling procedure itself is important.

The challenge of a pure steam system shared by multiple CDMO clients

In a CDMO, the same pure steam system may serve the manufacture of several clients' products at the same time. The design of the routine monitoring program must take into account:

  • The most stringent client requirement determines the overall monitoring frequency (the strictest governs)
  • System monitoring records must be available during client audits
  • If one client requires EP compliance, all products using this system must meet the EP requirements (including the nitrate/appearance tests)

Original Text (English)

3.5 Sample Valve Design

This is covered in Section 2.4 of this Guide.

For pure steam sampling applications, sample valves must be compatible with the high temperature and pressure conditions of pure steam systems. Key considerations include:

  • Valve body and seat materials must withstand saturated steam at 3–4 bar (approximately 135–145°C) without leaching contaminants into the condensate sample.
  • Diaphragm valves are the preferred design for pure steam sample points due to their sanitary construction and ability to be cleaned and sterilized in place.

  • The sample valve should be located downstream of the condensate trap where applicable, to avoid sampling mixed condensate and vapor.
  • Valve materials (including seals and gaskets) must not contribute TOC, endotoxin, metals, or particulate to the condensate sample.
  • Where sample valves are located at manufacturing-grade POU connections, the design standard should match that of the distribution system.
"Sample valves should be of a sanitary design, although laboratory points may not need to be as hygienically designed as those supplying pure steam to manufacturing."
— ISPE Sampling Guide, Section 3.6.4.1
Commentary

Key Points of Sample Valve Design

Why use a diaphragm valve?

The diaphragm valve is the most common sampling valve design in pure steam systems. Its advantages:

  • No dead leg: steam cannot be trapped inside the valve body
  • SIP-capable (steam-in-place): it is sterilized together with the system, with no need to dismantle it
  • The sealing surface (diaphragm) is replaceable, preventing leaks
  • Simple flow path that is easy to clean

Analogy: the valve is the doorway for sampling

Think of the sampling valve as your doorway into the steam system. If the door frame is made of poor material (leaching metal ions or TOC) or the door gap accumulates dirt (a dead leg), the sample you collect will be contaminated no matter how clean the steam itself is. So "a well-chosen valve gives a good sample".

Practical Points for CDMOs

In a CDMO's pure steam qualification, the material certification of the sampling valve is a must-check item at IQ. Confirm the following:

  • 316L stainless steel with electropolish
  • PTFE or EPDM diaphragm with USP Class VI certification
  • Silicone-free: silicone readily releases TOC

3.6

Pure Steam Sampling Techniques

Condensate collection, equipment requirements, sample containers, temperature

3.6.1 Obtaining Representative Samples

The goal of the sampling program is to obtain samples that accurately reflect the contents of the steam system. When sampling a liquid, contaminant distribution should be accounted for as part of the sample evaluation.

Pure steam cannot be evaluated in its vapor state; therefore, pure steam needs to be condensed in order to be evaluated. Based on compendial requirements (USP, EP, JP, etc.), condensed pure steam should be evaluated using the same criteria used to evaluate WFI. The techniques used are similar to those for water; however, there are two issues that can affect pure steam condensate sampling:

  1. The equipment used to condense the pure steam and collect samples
  2. The final temperature of the condensate

3.6.1.1 Equipment Considerations

When condensing pure steam for sampling, the equipment should not contribute contaminants to the sample. The equipment should be designed to allow for complete cleaning and drainage. It should also be possible to sterilize the equipment and for it to be depyrogenated to ensure that no endotoxins are added to the sample.

In addition, the equipment typically requires a cooling source (such as chilled water) to facilitate condensation. The amount of chilled water required may be significant, necessitating larger than expected equipment sizes and/or cooling media flow rates, because of the significant amount of latent heat that needs to be removed to condense the pure steam.

Sampling heat exchangers can be sterilized in place, using the pure steam from the system. This can help to simplify the sampling process, but may result in a larger cooling load.

Heat exchangers used for pure steam condensation may need to meet the requirements of heat exchangers used for WFI sampling in order to mitigate the risk of undetectable cooling water contamination of the condensate sample. Requirements may include double tube sheet design and routine maintenance.

The size of this equipment will depend on the:

  1. Pure steam pressure (and resulting temperature)
  2. Sample temperature desired ("delta T")
  3. Temperature and flow of cooling media available

3.6.1.2 Final Temperature of the Condensate

The condensate temperature for steam sampling should be < 50°C (122°F) and preferably near ambient temperature. It is recommended that sample conditioning condensers, coolers, and associated fittings and valves be constructed of 316 stainless steel and passivated.

Any special sampling equipment required that can make steam contact during collection (e.g., POU gaskets, sanitary fittings, hose barbs, steam condensing equipment) should be pyrogen free or must have been depyrogenated.

"Note: bioburden is not required of steam sampling per the USP/NF. However, establishing that the pure steam line has no thermophilic bioburden should be an evaluation done prior to or during the PQ."
Commentary

The Challenge of Representative Samples

Core concept: steam must be condensed before it can be tested

This is the most fundamental peculiarity of pure steam sampling: you cannot send "steam" itself to the laboratory. The steam must be condensed into liquid water at the sampling point, and this condensing process is itself the main contamination risk.

Therefore, the quality of the sampling equipment (condenser/heat exchanger) directly determines the validity of the sample.

Analogy: the condenser is your "sample tube"

When testing water quality, your sample bottle must be a TOC-grade glass bottle. In the same way, the heat exchanger that condenses the steam is your "sample tube": if it carries endotoxin or TOC, or if cooling water leaks into it, your sample is contaminated and you will not know it.

Double Tube Sheet Design: A Critical Line of Defense

The purpose of a double tube sheet design: a leak-detection space is left between the two tube sheets. If the cooling water (usually purified water or ordinary cooling water) leaks, the liquid first flows into the space between the tube sheets and is detected, rather than directly contaminating the condensate sample.

This design is the same as the heat exchanger requirement for WFI systems, because the consequences are equally serious.

Why must the condensate temperature be < 50°C?

Reason 1: the higher the temperature, the less soluble CO₂ is in water, so the conductivity test becomes inaccurate.
Reason 2: a hot sample continues to evaporate in the container, which changes pH and conductivity.
Reason 3: microbiological samples need a suitable temperature to keep the viable count accurate.
Best practice: control the temperature close to room temperature (20–25°C).

What is thermophilic bioburden?

Ordinary bacteria cannot survive in a steam environment (>100°C), but some spore-forming bacteria can survive at high temperature as spores. If viable organisms are found in steam condensate, it usually indicates:

  • Steam trap failure, with stagnant condensate supporting bacterial growth
  • Inadequate cleaning of the sampling equipment
  • Contamination during the sampling operation

USP/NF does not require bioburden testing of steam, but an assessment should be carried out once before PQ.

  1. If your pure steam sampling condenser has only a single tube sheet design, what additional controls are needed during validation?
  2. When the condensate temperature exceeds 50°C, which tests are most likely to be inaccurate? Why?
  3. If the conductivity of the steam condensate is OOS while the conductivity of the WFI system is fully compliant over the same period, what is the most likely cause?

3.6.1 (cont.) Sample Containers & Types

Sample Container Requirements

For most high purity and pure steam samples, three to four groups of samples are taken:

1. TOC

TOC samples should be taken in TOC collection vials (glass). Plastic containers can introduce TOC to the sample results. The TOC vials typically hold a 40 ml sample and should be filled completely before the closure is applied.

2. Conductivity and pH

Typically, glass (borosilicate), sterile polypropylene plastic, or inert fluoropolymer containers are the most common container material. Glass containers may introduce trace residues that will change both the conductivity and pH. Plastics tend to avoid the ionic leachables, but most are somewhat permeable to atmospheric CO₂, which will alter the conductivity of the sample. Containers should be able to hold at least 300 ml of sample to allow for Conductivity Stage 1, Stage 2, and Stage 3 testing.

3. Bioburden

Bioburden samples should be taken in sterile containers, typically an inert plastic such as polypropylene or a suitable fluoropolymer.

4. Endotoxin

Endotoxin sampling should be done using pyrogen free containers of either glass or inert plastic. If considering plastic, the type of plastic container is important to minimize the potential for endotoxin adsorption issues that could result in false negative results. Collecting approximately 10 to 20 ml in sterile pyrogen free containers.

"Efforts should be made to ensure that sampling containers do not introduce measurable contaminants. This may necessitate using several types of sample containers to sample a given POU."
Commentary

The Logic of Sample Container Selection

Why use a different container for each test?

Every container material has its "weak points" that interfere with particular tests. If the wrong container is chosen, the result is invalid.

Practical reminder: the importance of blanks

Each sampling event should include a blank container (not exposed to the sample) sent to the laboratory. An abnormal blank result indicates a problem with the container batch, so the cause can be identified without rejecting the whole batch of samples. This is especially important during validation.

Original Text (English)

3.6.2 Training Sample Collectors

Operators should be properly trained and should have demonstrated their ability to collect samples following the approved sample collection SOP. There should be a particular emphasis on safety training when sampling pure steam, its condensate, or other hot liquid. Pressurized pure steam, often at pressures of 30 to 45 psig (135°C to 145°C) and its condensed water (at temperatures near 82°C) can cause severe burns and potentially death.

During their training samplers should have observed a trained sampler who demonstrates the collection procedure for each type of collection, as well as the proper handling of each type of collected sample prior to hand off to the testing function. When collecting samples during training, there should be a duplicate set of samples taken by the trainer.

Based on industry accepted best practice, trainees may be required to demonstrate that they can collect a minimum of three distinct sets of samples, one set per day for each type of collection and each unique location.

3.6.3 Preparation for Sample Collection

Materials should be prepared before beginning the actual sampling process. Examples of typical items that may be staged for use include:

  • Rolling railed cart, stainless or cleanable plastic
  • Plastic/wire baskets and TOC racks
  • Sample containers (all types, labeled)
  • Labels, waterproof markers and indelible ink pens
  • Laminated sampling plan
  • Heat resistant gloves

3.6.3.1 Sample Point Preparation

Sample point preparation for sampling pure steam may or may not include flushing, spraying, disinfecting, or other treatments, based on the temperature, and assuming proper design. If particulate contamination from the local environment may be a problem, physical cleaning of the sample outlet may be indicated.

3.6.3.4 Duplicate Samples

An important but often times overlooked part of any quality control program is to perform a predetermined percentage of all analytical tests in duplicate. Duplicate samples should be taken from the same sample location, but in two separate sample containers. Both containers should be labeled with the same sample identification, but can be distinguished by adding "-1" and "-2" after the sample number.

3.6.3.5 Blanks

A blank containing no sample should be submitted to the laboratory for each type of container used during sampling. The blank collection container should be from the same lot as the collection container used for that date.

Commentary

Personnel Training: Safety First

Safety warning: pure steam can kill

A pure steam system operates at 30–45 psig and 135–145°C. Escaping steam is silent and invisible, leaving too little time to react. Condensate can still be as hot as 82°C and can also cause severe scalds. Operators must be trained and qualified before they sample independently.

Training Qualification Criteria (Industry Best Practice)

  1. Observe a senior sampler demonstrate (observation stage)
  2. Sample under the supervision of a senior sampler (OJT stage)
  3. For 3 consecutive days, collect one set of samples each day and compare the results with the trainer's; differences must be within predefined limits (independent verification stage)

This "3 days, 3 sets" standard ensures that the trainee has not just been lucky but has truly mastered the technique.

Analogy: like surgical training

A surgeon cannot operate just by watching surgeries; he or she must perform several operations under an attending surgeon's supervision and may operate independently only after meeting the standard. Steam sampler training follows the same logic, because the consequences (scalds / invalid samples) are both serious.

The Quality Significance of Duplicate Samples

A duplicate sample is not a "backup" but a quality control tool:

  • If the results of -1 and -2 differ greatly: possibly a sampling operation problem (inconsistent technique of the personnel)
  • If both are OOS: possibly a system problem (a true water quality excursion)
  • This distinction is very important for the direction of CAPA

CDMO Validation Workflow: Training Planning Before PQ

Before PQ (Performance Qualification), the sampling personnel training records must be complete. Recommended flow:

  1. After IQ/OQ is complete and the system is running normally, the trainer first finalizes the SOP
  2. Designate at least 2 qualified samplers (backing up each other)
  3. During PQ, all samples are collected by qualified personnel, with reference to their training records

3.6.4 Sampling Procedures: General & Labeling

GMP gowning, flush requirements, sample labeling, TOC/Conductivity/Bioburden/Endotoxin collection steps

3.6.4.1 General Considerations

For routine operation of critical systems that have completed PQs, there should be detailed sampling procedures approved for use. It is a good practice to keep pure steam sampling procedures separated from those used to collect pure water samples.

When systems are new and undergoing PQ or have been modified and as part of a requalification PQ with a new sampling plan, it is acceptable to use the draft procedures.

Safety: Care should be taken to protect the sampler from scalding. Proper heat resistant gloves should be present in the collection kit and worn to protect the sampler from accidental burning.

If the POU is located in a dirty area, clean the sample port with 3% hydrogen peroxide or 70% sterile IPA prior to flushing the sample point. The cleaning procedure must be qualified prior to routine use and must account for the fact that the use of sterile IPA will impact TOC results.

Samplers should wear powder free gloves before taking samples or handling sample containers.

Samplers should open the sample valve and flush the POU by running steam/condensed water to waste for the qualified flush time interval.

3.6.4.2 Sample Labeling

Samplers should label all sample containers with:

  1. Sample identification — POU (include system ID and building)
  2. Type of sample (e.g., condensed steam) and indicate if a duplicate sample
  3. Date and time of sampling
  4. Analysis required
  5. Sampler's name

3.6.4.3 Sampling Procedure for TOC, Conductivity/pH, Bacteria, and Endotoxin

  1. Flush the POU for the qualified flush time interval and at the qualified flush flow rate.
  2. Wear clean powder free gloves.
  3. Remove and set aside the sample container closure, then manually open the valve on the condenser.
  4. To prevent contamination, do not let anything except the sample touch the inside of the container or closure.
  5. Fill each container completely, except for conductivity/pH samples where approximately 1 inch of air space should be left at the top to allow for adequate agitation prior to analysis.
  6. Securely close the sample containers to prevent leakage or contamination.

If there is any question whether a sample has become contaminated, it should be discarded and a new sample taken.

Commentary

GMP Requirements for Sampling Procedures

Why should the pure steam SOP be separate from the purified water SOP?

The sampling equipment, steps and safety requirements of the two systems are completely different:

  • Pure steam requires condenser equipment, a cooling water source and heat-resistant gloves
  • Pure steam sampling carries a scald risk; purified water does not
  • A combined SOP easily leads to missed steps or operating errors

In a CDMO, it is recommended to establish a separate PS-SOP (pure steam sampling procedure) and to track it separately in the training records.

The Effect of IPA on TOC: Cross-Contamination That Cannot Be Ignored

70% IPA is an organic solvent, and the TOC (Total Organic Carbon) test is extremely sensitive to organic matter. If IPA residue enters the sampling flow path, the TOC result may be falsely elevated.

Solution: after cleaning with IPA, a qualified flush must be used to ensure the IPA is completely removed, and the method validation must confirm that IPA does not interfere with the result.

  • Type: condensed steam, and whether it is a duplicate sample
  • Time: sampling date + time (to the minute)
  • Tests: which analyses are to be performed
  • Name: the sampler's name (traceability)

Analogy: leave 1 inch of headspace in the conductivity bottle

The sample must be agitated before the conductivity test so that it makes full contact with the electrode. If the bottle is too full, the liquid cannot move when shaken and the measurement is inaccurate. It is like shaking a completely full bottle of cola: nothing moves.

3.6.4.4–6 Steam Quality Tests: NCG, Dryness & Superheat

Steam Quality Test Method Comparison

3.6.4.4 Non-Condensable Gases (NCG) — Henriksen Method

It is recommended that a sequence of tests should be performed to determine the variability in the level of non-condensable gases. The procedure is as follows:

  1. Fill the 2 L container and burette with cold, deaerated water. Invert the burette with great care to ensure no air is trapped in the burette.
  2. Remove the steam sampling pipe from the 2 L container and open the needle valve to purge all air from the pipe. Return sampling pipe to container, allowing it to bubble into the funnel. Add deaerated water to the 2 L container until water flows out through the overflow pipe.
  3. Place the graduated cylinder to collect overflow. Adjust the needle valve to allow a continuous flow of steam into the funnel, ensuring that the steam enters the funnel in a way that any non-condensable gases will be collected in the burette.
  4. Close the sampling valve.
  5. Start the sterilization cycle and when steam is flowing to the sterilizer, open the sampling valve, allowing a continuous sample of steam into the funnel at a rate sufficient to cause a small amount of steam hammer.
  6. Allow the steam sample to condense in the funnel. Any non-condensable gases will collect in the top of the burette. Collect the overflow in the graduated cylinder and observe the temperature of the water in the 2 L container. When the temperature in the container is between 70°C to 75°C, close the needle valve.
  7. Record the volume of water displaced in the burette and the volume of water collected in the graduated cylinder.
  8. Calculate using the equation below.

NCG Calculation Formula

Cn (%) = (Vb / Vc) × 100

Where:
  Cn = concentration of NCG (%)
  Vb = volume of non-water displaced from burette (mL)
  Vc = volume of water collected in graduated cylinder (mL)

Acceptance criterion: Cn ≤ 3.5% (V/V)
Commentary: NCG Test

Non-Condensable Gases: The Most Overlooked Sterilization Killer

What are non-condensable gases (NCG)?

Non-condensable gases are gases such as air, CO₂ and N₂ mixed into the steam. In a pure steam system, they come mainly from:

  • Gases dissolved in the feedwater (the main source)
  • Poor pretreatment equipment (such as CO₂ / NH₃ carry-over into the steam)
  • Steam generator design deficiencies (no NCG venting provision)

Analogy: NCG is the "cold bag" in the autoclave

Imagine steam at 134°C in an autoclave killing all microorganisms. But if the steam contains NCG (air), these gases cannot condense and form an insulating layer around the load, like wrapping it in a cold bag, so steam cannot reach the surface of the items and sterilization fails. This is why EN 285 requires NCG ≤ 3.5%, and why the NCG test must pass before the dryness test.

Principle of the Henriksen Method

The principle is very intuitive: steam condenses when it meets cold water (its volume drops sharply), but air (NCG) does not condense, so it travels to the top of the inverted burette and is collected. The volume of water displaced from the burette = the NCG volume (Vb), and the volume of water that overflows = the volume of steam condensate (Vc).

Key operation: deaerated water must be used, otherwise the air dissolved in the water will be released and interfere with the NCG measurement (falsely elevated results).

Why stop at 70–75°C?

During the test, the water in the 2 L container must rise to 70–75°C before stopping. This ensures that enough steam sample has passed through the apparatus (so the data are representative); also, 70–75°C is the temperature at which most of the dissolved gas in the water has already been released, which makes the test more accurate.

Link to Sterilization Validation

In the validation of an autoclave, the NCG test is usually a required OQ test (per EN 285). If NCG > 3.5%, the dryness test is invalid, and the problem is usually accompanied by uneven heat distribution. For a CDMO, excessive NCG in pure steam is one of the common causes of PQ failure. Directions for troubleshooting:

  1. Dissolved gas content of the feedwater (is a deaeration device used?)
  2. Venting design of the steam generator (is there an NCG vent valve?)
  3. Geographic location / source water (for example, municipal water containing chloramine produces NH₃)

  1. If the burette shows gas bubbles rather than a continuous gas column, what is the effect on the test result? What does this phenomenon indicate?

3.6.4.5 Dryness Fraction

Dryness — Dewar Flask Method

Steam should be tested for, and should pass the non-condensable gases test, prior to being tested for dryness. This test assumes the use of a large steam sterilizer operating in the textile cycle.

  1. Assemble the apparatus as shown in BS EN 285:2015 Figure 3.7.
  2. Weigh the assembled Dewar flask and record the mass as Me.
  3. After several sterilization cycles, select the textile cycle with a temperature of 134°C.
  4. Remove the stopper assembly and place 650 ml of water into the Dewar flask, with the water at a temperature at or below 27°C. Weigh the new assembly and record the mass as Ms.
  5. Place a standard test pack (details in BS EN 285:2015) in the sterilizer.
  6. Introduce a temperature sensor into the Dewar flask and note the temperature of the fluid in the flask, record this value as T1.
  7. When the sterilizer steam valve first opens, attach the rubber tube to the pitot tube ensuring free drainage of condensate into the Dewar flask. Record the steam temperature as T3.
  8. When the water in the Dewar flask reaches approximately 80°C, disconnect the rubber tube, agitate the flask to thoroughly mix the contents and record the temperature as T2.
  9. Weigh the Dewar flask again, record this mass as Mf.

Dryness Fraction Formula

D = [(T2 – T1) × (Cpw × (Ms – Me) + A)] / [L × (Mf – Ms)]
    – [(T3 – T2) × Cpw] / L

Where:
  D    = dryness fraction
  T1   = initial water temperature in flask (°C)
  T2   = final mixed temperature in flask (°C)
  T3   = steam temperature (°C)
  Ms   = mass of flask + 650 mL water (g)
  Me   = mass of empty flask assembly (g)
  Mf   = mass of flask after test (g)
  L    = latent heat of steam at T3 (kJ/kg)
  Cpw  = specific heat of water = 4.18 kJ/kg/°K
  A    = effective heat capacity of apparatus = 0.24 kJ/°K

Acceptance:
  D ≥ 0.95 for metal loads
  D ≥ 0.90 for other load types
Commentary: Dryness Test

Dryness: How Much Water Droplet Is Actually in the Steam?

The Physical Meaning of Dryness Fraction

A dryness D = 0.95 means that in every 1 kg of steam, 0.95 kg is dry steam (water vapor) and 0.05 kg is suspended water droplets (liquid water). D = 1.0 is ideal, fully dry steam, which is practically never achieved.

The EN 285 acceptance criterion: D ≥ 0.95 (metal loads) or D ≥ 0.90 (other loads such as textiles).

Analogy: dryness is the steam's "moisture content"

Think of steam as a cup of frothy coffee: D = 0.95 means 95% is froth (dry steam) and 5% is liquid coffee (water droplets). If there are too many droplets (D too low), the droplets carry minerals and conductive substances onto the load, which may:

  • Dilute chemical disinfectants (such as H₂O₂)
  • Leave water stains and mineral deposits on metal instruments
  • Impair heat transfer, causing localized cold spots

Principle of the Dewar Flask Method (Conservation of Energy)

The formula is based on conservation of energy: the heat brought in by the steam entering the Dewar flask = the heat required to raise the temperature of the water in the flask.

  • The water vapor fraction of the steam releases latent heat (L) as it condenses into water
  • The water droplets already present in the steam (the wet fraction) contribute only sensible heat (Cpw)
  • The dryness is calculated back from the measured temperatures and masses before and after

This is why it is called the "calorimetric method".

Test Detail: The Role of the Dewar Flask

The Dewar flask (vacuum flask) provides thermal insulation, ensuring that no heat is lost to the environment during the test so that the energy-conservation calculation is valid. If an ordinary glass bottle were used, heat would be lost too quickly and the calculated result would be low (dryness appearing high, a false pass).

CDMO Practice: When to Perform the Dryness Test

The dryness test is usually performed at the following times:

  • At the sterilizer OQ (Operational Qualification) stage
  • At the pure steam system PQ stage
  • Requalification after PSG maintenance or replacement
  • Annual periodic requalification

Note: the dryness test is performed under the textile load cycle (134°C) rather than the general instrument cycle (121°C), because EN 285 requires it.

3.6.4.6 Superheat

Superheat Measurement

Superheat refers to the condition where steam temperature exceeds the saturation temperature corresponding to the steam pressure. Superheated steam behaves more like a gas than a condensing vapor.

The procedure per BS EN 285:2015 involves:

  1. Assemble the apparatus as indicated in Figures 3.8 and 3.9 of BS EN 285.
  2. Run a sterilization cycle with an empty chamber.
  3. At the completion of this cycle, immediately place a full textile load and run another sterilization cycle. At the completion of the sterilization cycle, check the temperature recordings and confirm that the temperature did not differ by more than 3°C from that measured in the steam pipe during the dryness test.
"Superheated steam does not condense as readily as saturated steam, reducing the rate of heat transfer to the load. This impairs the sterilization process."

Common causes of superheat:

  • Pressure drops through steam traps or pressure reducing valves (PRVs)
  • Excessive pipeline heat loss in long, uninsulated steam mains
  • Throttling of steam through partially open valves

Acceptance criterion: Steam temperature should not exceed saturation temperature by more than 25°C, and temperature difference between steam pipe and chamber should not exceed 3°C compared to the dryness test measurement.

Commentary: Superheat

Superheated Steam: The Hidden Killer of Sterilization Efficacy

Saturated Steam vs Superheated Steam

Saturated steam: at a given pressure, the temperature is exactly the boiling point (for example, 134°C at 3 bar abs). It condenses immediately on contact with a cold surface, releasing a large amount of latent heat (2.26 MJ/kg) and achieving efficient sterilization.

Superheated steam: the temperature is above the boiling point at the same pressure. It behaves like a dry gas and does not condense immediately on contact with the load, so heat transfer efficiency is greatly reduced.

Analogy: saturated steam is like "ice water", superheated steam is like "hot air"

Compare putting your hand in 0°C ice water with putting it in 0°C cold air: the ice water feels much colder because liquid water conducts heat far better than air. The heat transfer efficiency of condensing saturated steam is more than 10 times that of superheated steam. This is why sterilization needs saturated steam and not superheated steam.

Common Causes of Superheated Steam

In a CDMO, the most common causes of superheated steam are:

  • Excessive throttling at the pressure reducing valve (PRV): after steam is reduced in pressure through a PRV its temperature does not fall, producing superheat
  • Poor pipework insulation: over long steam lines heat is lost, so the pressure falls while the temperature stays relatively high
  • Steam trap failure (closed): condensate cannot be discharged and superheated steam accumulates

Questions to Consider

  1. The temperature in your sterilizer chamber is 5°C lower than the steam line temperature (exceeding the allowed 3°C deviation). What are the possible causes and the corrective actions for each?
  2. Is superheat exceeding the limit a system problem or an operating problem? In an OOS investigation, how do you distinguish between the two?
  3. In pure steam validation, the superheat, dryness and NCG tests must all pass for the system to be acceptable. If only the superheat fails, what is the effect on the validity of the dryness and NCG results?

Sample Handling

Condensate handling, time to analysis, temperature effects, endotoxin considerations

3.7 Sample Handling

Clear instructions should be provided to personnel collecting and handling pure steam condensate samples. A sample holding time limit should be established prior to the PQ to confirm the maximum time between collection and testing.

Samples should be transferred to the laboratory and placed on test within an hour of collection, to help increase the accuracy of the test results. If multiple samples are being collected and the collection process takes longer than an hour, the collector should divide up the sampling process, deliver samples to the testing laboratory, and then return to sampling.

Refrigerating samples will help to inhibit microbial growth, but gas absorption may create inaccurate test results.

"A sample holding time limit should be established prior to the PQ to confirm the maximum time between collection and testing."

Key handling requirements for pure steam condensate samples:

  • Maintain sample integrity from point of collection to laboratory
  • Minimize exposure to atmosphere (CO₂ absorption affects conductivity)
  • Do not allow condensate to cool below ambient where phase separation may occur
  • Endotoxin samples: keep at room temperature, test within validated hold time
  • Bioburden samples: refrigerate at 2–8°C if not tested within 1 hour, but consider CO₂ absorption impact on other tests
  • Chemical (conductivity, TOC, pH) samples: test as soon as possible, within qualified hold time
Commentary

Sample Handling: Time Is Everything

The 1-Hour Rule: Why So Strict?

Pure steam condensate samples are particularly prone to change over time:

  • Conductivity: CO₂ from the air dissolves into the water to form carbonic acid, and conductivity rises (falsely elevated, possibly causing a mistaken OOS)
  • pH: dissolved CO₂ lowers the pH and affects interpretation
  • TOC: relatively stable, but plastic containers may slowly release organics
  • Endotoxin: adsorbs onto the container wall (false negative); more stable when stored at room temperature
  • Bioburden: microorganisms multiply at room temperature and the sample needs refrigeration, but refrigeration affects CO₂ solubility

Analogy: a sample is like sashimi

Fresh sashimi you have just bought at the market is of completely different quality 2 hours later than 6 hours later. Pure steam condensate samples are the same: the sooner they are tested, the better the results represent the system condition at that time.

The Refrigeration Paradox: Bioburden vs Conductivity

This is the most difficult trade-off in sample handling:

  • Bioburden samples need refrigeration (2–8°C) to suppress microbial growth
  • But refrigeration increases the solubility of CO₂ in water, and when the sample warms up the CO₂ is released, changing the conductivity

Solution: collect separately in different containers; refrigerate the bioburden sample, and test the chemical samples (conductivity, TOC) at room temperature as soon as possible (within 1 hour). This is why a single POU usually needs 3–4 sets of different containers.

CDMO Pre-Validation Activity: Hold Time Study

Before PQ starts, a Sample Hold Time Study must be completed:

  1. Use water samples from the same system (or WFI meeting the specification)
  2. Test each parameter at different time points (0 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h)
  3. Determine the maximum valid hold time for each test
  4. Record the storage conditions (temperature, container type)

The results of this study are written directly into the sampling SOP as a prerequisite for PQ.

Other Factors Influencing Sampling Strategies

Geographic variation, source water types (WFI / PW / potable), PSG design, mist elimination

3.8 Other Factors

Sampling strategies may be influenced by other factors such as:

  • Geographic location of the plant
  • Incoming water quality
  • Pretreatment equipment
  • PSG (Pure Steam Generator) design

The geographic location of the plant may lead to variation in incoming water quality as well as variations in the type of chemical treatment in use (e.g., chlorine, chloramine, ozone, etc.). In some instances, ammonia may be present as a byproduct of the chloramine process. Ammonia and carbon dioxide are particularly troublesome since they will pass through a pure steam generator and will influence the non-condensable gas content as well as the conductivity.

3.8.1 Source Water

3.8.1.1 Feed Water from WFI Systems

When the feed water to a PSG already has very low or undetectable levels of endotoxin, and is otherwise chemically pure, no additional PSG feed water pretreatment system monitoring is required. When properly operated, the chemical attributes of the PSG feed water are essentially the same as those of the steam it generates.

3.8.1.2 Feed from Purified Water Systems

With PW, there is no requirement that it is free from bacterial endotoxins; therefore, without specific microbial and endotoxin control precautions, excessive levels of endotoxin may be present.

Since PSGs have a finite impurity removal capability (including endotoxin), the endotoxin levels in the PW feeding the PSG should be monitored to ensure that the removal capability is not overwhelmed. Assuming a minimum 3-log endotoxin removal capability and proper equipment operation, the endotoxin level in the feed water would have to be 100 EU/mL or greater to be problematic.

3.8.1.3 Potable Water

Although there is no compendial requirement that this water first meets PW or WFI specifications, it should be suitable feed water for the steam generator in terms of its chemical content. The source of the potable water supply could have a dramatic impact on its starting purity, and therefore, on the required pretreatment.

3.8.2 Steam Generator Mist Elimination Capability

The USP monograph for pure steam specifically states that the pure steam generation process uses a process that "prevents source water entrainment." Well designed and well operated systems using WFI as steam generator feed water essentially have no need for mist elimination since the feed water quality is already suitable for steam.

A standalone PSG feed water system that is either not well designed or not properly maintained carries a high risk of contaminant carry over into the PS. These systems can be significantly more susceptible to the variable quality of the incoming potable water and could also experience endotoxin and TOC generated in situ by the pretreatment system.

The purification system generating the water feeding a PSG typically needs the same monitoring and maintenance attention as pretreatment systems feeding distillation units that generate WFI. It is commonly accepted that the phase change and mist elimination processes of distillation will purify that feed water by at least three orders of magnitude (≥ 1000-fold or ≥ 99.9% impurity reduction).

Commentary

The Fundamental Determinant of the Sampling Strategy: Feedwater

How the Three Feedwater Types Affect the PSG

The core logic of this section: the cleaner the feedwater, the less sampling/monitoring is needed; the poorer the feedwater, the stricter the control measures must be.

Ammonia: The Hidden Problem of Chloraminated Water Sources

Many cities disinfect tap water with chloramine (as opposed to chlorine alone) because chloramine is more stable and produces fewer disinfection by-products. But chloramine decomposes at high temperature, releasing ammonia (NH₃).

NH₃ is a volatile alkaline gas that passes through the steam generator into the pure steam and affects:

  • Conductivity (raised)
  • pH (raised, alkaline)
  • NCG content

Solution: add an ammonia removal step to the pretreatment (such as strong-acid-type RO or special ion exchange) and add ammonia monitoring to the sampling strategy.

The Significance of 3-Log Endotoxin Reduction

A PSG can remove at least 3 logs of endotoxin through distillation (water to vapor phase).

Calculation: if the feedwater endotoxin = 100 EU/mL, after a 3-log reduction the residual = 0.1 EU/mL (still below the WFI specification of 0.25 EU/mL).

Therefore the feedwater endotoxin would need to be ≥ 100 EU/mL to cause the pure steam endotoxin to exceed the limit. When a PW system is well controlled, it is unlikely to reach this level, but regular monitoring is still needed to confirm it.

The Importance of Mist Elimination

The mist eliminator is an important design element of a PSG. The USP pure steam monograph requires that the generation process "prevents source water entrainment".

Without a mist eliminator: water mist from the feedwater (with all its impurities: endotoxin, TOC, metal ions) is carried directly away by the steam, greatly reducing the purification effect and invalidating the 3-log reduction assumption.

Facility risk ranking: WFI feed + mist elimination = lowest risk; potable water feed + no mist elimination = highest risk, requiring the strictest sampling strategy.

CDMO Sampling Strategy Design: Risk Matrix Thinking

When designing a pure steam sampling strategy, start from this framework:

  1. Identify the feedwater type (WFI / PW / potable water)
  2. Assess the PSG design (presence of mist elimination, NCG venting design)
  3. Identify geographic/seasonal risks (seasonal changes in source water quality, use of chloramine)
  4. Design the monitoring frequency: high risk = high frequency (such as weekly); low risk = low frequency (such as monthly or quarterly)
  5. Design the trigger conditions: under what circumstances is increased sampling needed? (for example, after maintenance, after a water quality excursion, after a seasonal change)

Phase 4: Ongoing Monitoring

  • Routine: conductivity and TOC at each POU (frequency per risk assessment)
  • Periodic: endotoxin, pH, NCG (quarterly or annually)
  • Trigger-based: full requalification after major maintenance, PSG replacement, or feed water source change
Commentary: Key Points of the Validation Process

The Particularities of Pure Steam Validation

Pure steam qualification has additional complexity compared with WFI systems:

  1. Dedicated steam quality test equipment is required (Dewar flask, burette, etc.)
  2. Tests must be performed concurrently with sterilizer operation (NCG, dryness tests)
  3. NCG, then dryness, then superheat: a mandatory execution order
  4. Sampling personnel need additional safety training

ISPE Guidance on PQ Sampling Days

The ISPE guide recommends collecting at least 3 samples from each sampling point during PQ (3 consecutive days), covering representative operating conditions, including different shifts, different loads and different seasons (if applicable). A new facility usually adopts 3 weeks with sampling every week to ensure statistical representativeness.

Strategy for Seasonal Variation

Section 3.8 specifically mentions geographic location and seasonal variation. Taking Taiwan as an example:

  • Summer: high temperature increases biological activity in the water source, raising the endotoxin risk
  • After typhoons: source water quality changes abruptly, requiring increased sampling
  • Waterworks disinfectant switching (winter/summer): the chlorine/chloramine ratio changes, affecting NH₃ and conductivity

It is recommended to include a seasonal assessment in the annual sampling plan and to increase sampling frequency at seasonal transitions (for at least 1 month).

This chapter contains information and best practices gathered from several major pharmaceutical organizations listing their current practices for assuring the quality of gases used in their operations. Testing may be performed by the pharmaceutical manufacturer or by a certified independent laboratory.

The quality of product contact compressed gases should be assured through adequate design, appropriate controls (e.g., change control or change management procedures), and routine maintenance of the system. If it is determined that analytical testing of a distribution piping system is necessary, the specification limits for the testing should be suitable for the intended use of the gas.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Core Framework: Three Pillars of Quality Assurance

ISPE's logic for assuring process gas quality is consistent with the systems thinking of ICH Q10:

  • Adequate Design: eliminate risk at the engineering stage, for example by using oil-free compressors
  • Appropriate Controls: change control, preventive maintenance, alert and action limits
  • Routine Testing: with adequate controls in place, periodically confirm that the system remains in control

Key Points for CDMOs

Many CDMOs treat compressed air as a "plant utility" rather than a "raw material", so the level of testing is far below what is actually needed. When compressed air comes into direct contact with sterile product (for example, N₂ overlay of the injectable liquid surface, or compressed air driving the filling machine), it should be managed at the same level as WFI.

4.1.1 Types of Process Gases Covered

This Guide considers the most commonly used gases. The process gases covered include:

  • Compressed air
  • Argon (Ar)
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
  • Nitrogen (N₂)
  • Oxygen (O₂)

Process gases may be generated on site or may be provided by a qualified supplier as portable tanks, compressed gas cylinders, cylinders containing liquefied gases, or other types of containers. There may be variations in the number of sample points as well as the frequency of sampling. Decisions should be made after a risk analysis is performed.

Process Gas Types and Aseptic Filling Applications

Analogy: Gas Is the "Invisible Raw Material"

In aseptic filling, N₂ is used to overlay the injectable liquid surface, CO₂ is used to carbonate products, and compressed air drives robotic arms. These gases are invisible, but they contact the product directly. If they contain microorganisms or oil mist, it is equivalent to contaminated raw material.

4.1.2 Regulatory Requirements and Consensus Standards

No well-defined regulatory requirements exist regarding the frequency, extent, or method of routine sampling for compressed air or other process gases. None of the pharmacopeia monographs specifies limits for particles or total viable counts (bacteria), but some consensus standards and US FDA guidance documents provide additional information and should be consulted as part of the risk analysis process.

Some monographs stipulate that the moisture content of compressed air and other process gases should be below -46°C (-50°F) (equivalent to 67 ppm for compressed air).

US FDA (2004) — Aseptic Processing Guidance:
"A compressed gas should be of appropriate purity (e.g., free from oil) and its microbiological and particle quality after filtration should be equal to or better than that of the air in the environment into which the gas is introduced. Compressed gases such as air, nitrogen, and carbon dioxide are often used in cleanrooms and are frequently employed in purging or overlaying."

Regulatory Framework and ISO 8573

ISO 8573 Compressed Air Quality Classes

ISO 8573 is the general industrial standard for compressed air quality and is divided into several parts:

  • ISO 8573-1: quality class specifications (solid particles, moisture, oil content)
  • ISO 8573-3: test method for moisture (dew point measurement)
  • ISO 8573-4: test method for particle counting

In pharmaceutical aseptic environments, compressed air is typically required to meet ISO 8573-1 Class 1 (particles) and Class 1 (moisture, dew point ≤ -70°C).

Link to EU GMP Annex 1 (2022)

EU GMP Annex 1, clause 4.36 explicitly requires that process gases in direct contact with product be sterile filtered, and that their microbial and particulate quality be equal to or better than the cleanroom environment they enter. This is the European regulatory counterpart of the FDA text cited in the ISPE Guide.

4.1.3 Critical Quality Attributes of Compressed Air and Process Gases

A sensible and logical method to determine the requirements for compressed air or process gases is to review the role played by the gas in the actual process in which it is used. Examples of applications include:

  • Displacement or conveyance of product from one location to another
  • As a feed source to fermentation processes
  • Actuating pneumatic valves in classified and controlled areas, with resulting exhaust gases venting into processing spaces
  • Occupying head space in lyophilization vials, solution vials, ampoules, etc.
  • Occupying head space or acting as a blanket in a bioreactor
  • Drying of product in fluidized beds
  • Drying of product contact equipment surfaces
  • As a carrier in spray tablet coatings
  • As a carrier to suspend an API during micronizing or milling steps
  • As a blanket to provide an inert atmosphere within API vessels
  • As a blanket or to pressurize WFI storage tanks

The CQAs most generally defined are:

  • Moisture content (dew point)
  • Total hydrocarbons (oil aerosol + oil vapor)
  • Particles (nonviable)
  • Viable particles (bacteria)

CQAs and the Aseptic Filling Risk Matrix

Gas Contact Scenarios in Aseptic Filling

On a CDMO aseptic filling line, there are far more contact points for compressed air and N₂ than people imagine:

  • N₂ overlay: N₂ blanketing of containers before/after filling to prevent oxidation — direct product contact
  • Compressed air pneumatic valves: after a pneumatic valve in a grade B/C area actuates, the exhaust may enter the cleanroom space

4.2

Sampling Locations

Where to sample in compressed air and process gas systems

Original Text (ISPE GPG 2016)

Sample points may be installed for varied reasons in a compressed air or process gas system, including:

  • Quality control purposes
  • Process control or diagnostic purposes

Sample points intended for quality control purposes should be determined based on the CQA being monitored and on a suitably developed sampling plan.

Sample points intended for process control or diagnostic purposes should:

  • Be used as needed to ensure that processes are under control
  • Be regularly maintained
  • Not introduce a risk of contaminating the system
  • Not be included in the sampling plan

The determination of sample point location should be initiated during system design. Risk analysis tools may be utilized to define the sampling strategies. Expectations for sampling points include:

  • Sampling points should be easily accessible
  • Avoid locations common to other clean utilities (e.g., high purity water or pure steam) to prevent cross contamination
  • Clearly and uniquely identified on P&ID drawings and tagged in the field
  • Test results should be trended and reviewed annually, with sampling frequencies adjusted accordingly

Key principle: Typically, a sampling location should reflect the quality of a gas at the POU. However, for a gas line fitted with a POU sterile filter, the sampling point should be located before the sterile filter to avoid product contamination risk from a sample point downstream of the filter.

Commentary and CDMO Application

QC Sampling Points vs. Process Control Sampling Points

ISPE clearly distinguishes two types of sampling points, which matters greatly for system design and SOP writing:

  • Quality control (QC) sampling points: included in the sampling plan, with defined frequency and specifications; results are recorded in batch records, and alert/action limit excursions trigger a deviation investigation
  • Process control/diagnostic sampling points: used for system commissioning and troubleshooting; they are not included in the sampling plan and should not appear in quality documents

The Logic of Sampling Upstream of the POU

If the system has a 0.22 μm sterilizing filter before the point of use (POU), the sampling point should be located upstream of the filter, because:

  • it evaluates the quality of the gas delivered to the sterilizing filter (confirming the filter workload is reasonable)
  • it avoids placing a sample valve downstream of the filter, where the act of sampling could itself contaminate the sterile line

This differs somewhat from the logic of WFI sampling at the point of use, and is a design principle specific to gas systems.

4.2.1 Sampling Locations for Pharmaceutical Compressed Air Systems

Pharmaceutical compressed air is used for many purposes. Air that comes in direct contact with product and/or product contact surfaces can affect product quality.

Process control sample point locations:

  • After the compressor, dryer, and coalescing filter on the central system
  • Before and after any in-line filters on the central system and prior to the distribution system

Quality control sample locations:

  • Following any central treatment process intended to control the levels of an identified CQA
  • The central supply header of the distribution system
  • The most distant POU from the central system on each floor
  • Prior to a POU final filter if the POU cannot be sampled

CQAs for the compressed air system typically involve sampling for:

  • Moisture content — following drying process and at farthest distribution line end
  • Total hydrocarbons — after final coalescing filter, at end of distribution lines, at critical POUs
  • Non-Viable & Viable Particles — after central final filter, at most distant POU, at critical POUs

  • Incoming Inspection: for each N₂ delivery, review whether the CoA covers all specification requirements
  • Distribution system sampling: although the quality of cylinder N₂ is guaranteed by the vendor, the distribution piping (especially the farthest point of use) still needs periodic confirmation to prevent contamination from the piping
  • POU filters: N₂ points of use on the aseptic filling line should be fitted with 0.22 μm sterilizing filters, with a defined replacement frequency

4.3

Sampling Plan: Tests, Frequency, and Duration

Risk-based framework for routine monitoring and qualification

Original Text (ISPE GPG 2016)

4.3.1 General Considerations

Compressed air or other process gases should be monitored at a frequency that would assure that gas does not adversely affect the environment or the product. Routine monitoring frequency should be similar to the frequencies established for the cleanroom, but these frequencies can be adjusted using the risk analysis tools once a base of historical data has been generated.

4.3.2 Risk Analysis for Sampling

Risk analysis of process gas systems should be performed upon initial system design and construction, or following significant system modifications. This risk analysis should identify sampling points that may present a risk to product, environment, or the integrity of the gas system.

Risk analysis principles should include the inherent risk of both viable and nonviable particulate level requirements that may already be controlled through adequate system design and utilization of critical POU filtration and maintenance.

Compressed air and process gas systems typically are designed, controlled, and maintained in a dry state (i.e., very low dew point), which inherently reduces the risk of microbial growth.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Monitoring Frequency Should Match the Cleanroom Grade

An important ISPE principle: the monitoring frequency for process gases entering a cleanroom should be consistent with the environmental monitoring frequency of that cleanroom. For example:

  • Grade A area (filling zone): N₂/compressed air entering grade A should have the highest monitoring frequency
  • Grade B area: corresponds to the EU GMP Annex 1 grade B monitoring frequency
  • Grade C/D: a lower frequency may be used, but a plan is still required

Risk Analysis Determines the Sampling Strategy

Low dew point (low moisture) is a natural inhibitor of microbial growth: compressed air systems are designed to be extremely dry (typical dew point ≤ -40°C), and microorganisms struggle to survive in such an environment. This is the scientific basis on which a risk analysis may support reducing the frequency of microbial testing, but it cannot be used to eliminate microbial sampling entirely.

4.3.3 Background Philosophy — Lifecycle Phases

Sampling and testing of compressed air and process gases can be broadly classified based on phases of system life cycle:

  • Commissioning (prequalification)
  • Initial performance qualification (PQ)
  • Requalification during major or minor changes
  • Routine monitoring

4.3.3.2 Performance Qualification

Qualification typically involves a minimum of three days of sampling and testing at specified sampling locations. CQAs monitored include:

  1. Total particulates (viable and nonviable)
  2. Viable particulates
  3. Total hydrocarbons (aerosol + vapors)
  4. Dew point (moisture content)

4.4

Sample Valve Design

Design requirements for gas sampling valves in pharmaceutical systems

Original Text (ISPE GPG 2016)

Sample valves located within a central compressed air or process gas treatment system may be ball valves when installed prior to the central system final filter and prior to distribution.

Sampling valves and POU valves located in the distribution piping system have stricter requirements and are usually diaphragm valves.

For example, when the installation of a hygienic, pharmaceutical grade 0.22 μm filter is required in close proximity to the POU, it should be installed in a suitably clean housing attached to the POU valve to minimize contamination of the gas stream.

The filter housing with the filter placed in it requires the ability to be sterilized and kept sterile until it is placed in the piping system for use.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Hygienic Design Advantages of Diaphragm Valves

In clean utilities, diaphragm valves are the standard choice, because:

  • No dead legs (zero dead leg): the valve body design ensures no fluid is retained
  • The diaphragm material (usually PTFE/EPDM) does not contaminate the gas
  • They can be steam sterilized or CIP cleaned in situ
  • Compared with ball valves, the risk of particle shedding is significantly lower

Analogy: The Same Logic as WFI Sample Valves

The design logic of POU sample valves for process gases is the same as for WFI sampling stations: both use hygienic diaphragm valves, both require minimal dead legs, and the POU sample valve (or sampling connection) needs contamination protection (such as protective caps or periodic sterilization).

4.5

Sampling Techniques for Compressed Air and Process Gases

Methods for microbiological, particulate, moisture, and hydrocarbon testing

Original Text — 4.5.1 Total Particles, Non-viable and Viable

The selection of a sampling method is dependent on the concentration and size range of the impurity/contaminant being monitored. Other evaluation criteria include sampling times, air volumes, and allowable outlet pressure restrictions.

Any gauges, adaptors, fittings, or tubing used to connect the sampling equipment to the sampling outlet should be clean and particle free, and should not introduce contamination. The preferred connection should be:

  • As short as practical
  • Constructed of stainless steel or other suitable material
  • Without sharp bends (approximately 6 inch radius if bends are present)
  • Non-restrictive

Softer metals such as brass or copper can produce particles when joined and should not be utilized.

Methods include: laser particle counting with a high-pressure diffuser, condensation nucleus counting coupled with scanning mobility particle sizing, differential mobility analysis, and membrane capture as covered in ISO 8573-4:2001.

Important notes:

  • Flow meters, control valves, pressure gauges, quick connect fittings and polymeric tubing can shed particles — specify non-shedding materials
  • When closed, valves capture particles; when opened they release them — use high purity diaphragm or bellows valve for flow regulation
  • Seal material must not be rubber or other polymeric materials, with exception of Teflon
  • Valves and regulators should be fully opened to minimize particle shedding

Tubing materials for minimizing particle contamination (in order of preference): stainless steel, conductive polymer, polyester, vinyl (if plasticizer does not interfere), polyethylene, copper, glass, Teflon, and aluminum.

Commentary — Particle Sampling Techniques

The "Sampling Connection" Itself Is a Contamination Risk

This is the most commonly overlooked issue in gas sampling: the tubing, fittings and valves used to connect the sampling instrument can themselves be a source of particles. ISPE explicitly states that brass or copper fittings must not be used. In a CDMO audit, the use of non-hygienic materials for sampling connections may be cited as a deficiency.

Practical Considerations for Compressed Air Particle Sampling

  • Purging before sampling: purge a sufficient volume of gas before sampling (typically 1-5 minutes) to ensure the stagnant gas in the line is replaced
  • Flow control: high-pressure gas must be reduced through a pressure regulator to the pressure the instrument requires (usually atmospheric pressure); the regulator itself may shed particles in the process
  • Calibration records: the particle counter must be within its valid calibration period, with traceable calibration records

4.5.2 Solid Particles — Viable Bacteria

Viable particles or microbial particles may be monitored using several different techniques:

  • Slit-to-agar (STA) technique: Fixed rate air flow, fixed motor carriage rotates an agar plate at constant speed (typically 30 minutes per revolution). Plate media surface positioned at defined distance from air outlet slit.
  • Impaction technique: Air forced perpendicular to the agar surface through a diffuser of fixed holes to monitor bacterial levels.
  • Hydrophilic membrane technique: Fixed volume of air/gas passed through membrane where microorganisms are captured. Conventional laboratory enumeration techniques used to determine bacterial counts.
  • Real time monitoring: Rapidly evolving technique; gas stream passed through analyzer with direct microorganism detection. Progress is being made rapidly due to huge demand for on-line, real time microbiological monitoring.

Comparison of Microbial Sampling Methods

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) Position on RTM

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022), clause 9.31, recognizes real-time microbial monitoring technologies and encourages their use in grade A environments. For process gases entering grade A directly, if RTM technology is used for continuous monitoring, it should be supported by adequate method validation, with alert and action limits established.

4.5.3 Water Vapor (Moisture / Dew Point)

Moisture content is typically monitored using on-line instrumentation providing real time information. For systems where the level of moisture is considered critical, the instrumentation and alarms associated with monitoring should be commissioned and qualified.

"Moisture in process gases is usually measured as either dewpoint or pressure dewpoint. Methods include condensation devices (Cooled Mirror Dewpoint, Electrolytic, and Impedance Hygrometers, and Polymer Film Relative Humidity Sensors), instrumentation devices (Chilled Mirror, Electrolytic, and Impedance Hygrometers, Polymer Film RH Sensors)." [ISPE Process Gases Guide]
"When using a hygrometer, the temperature and line pressure should be accounted for during dewpoint measurements. The instruments may be used in line for continuous monitoring or may be used to periodically monitor critical POUs. The type used should be determined by the degree of accuracy required for downstream processes, maintenance and calibration schedules, costs, and equipment reliability."

ISO 8573-3:1999 Annex C guidance on dew point sampling:

  • Flush sample lines and hygrometers with dry gas, or by evacuating to low pressure before measurement
  • Bake assemblies if possible to drive off stray residual water
  • Select impermeable materials (steel and metals are practically impermeable; PTFE/Teflon is slightly permeable but satisfactory above -20°C; PVC, nylon, and rubber are not satisfactory)
  • Polished or electropolished stainless steel is recommended for best results
  • Sample tubing should be as short as possible; minimize connections and dead legs

When using chemical reaction detector tubes manually: break tube tips and insert immediately into sampling device; read color change immediately upon removal; readings in mg/m³, then convert to ppm for comparison to specifications.

Moisture/Dew Point Test Methods and CDMO Practice

Concept of Converting Dew Point to ppm

Dew point is an indirect expression of moisture content:

  • Dew point -46°C = 67 ppm (lower limit required by the EP/USP pharmacopeias)
  • Dew point -40°C ≈ 128 ppm
  • Dew point -70°C ≈ 2.6 ppm (ISO 8573-1 Class 1, the highest quality class)

On a CDMO aseptic filling line, N₂/compressed air entering grade A typically requires a dew point ≤ -40°C to -70°C, depending on product requirements.

Effect of Sampling Tubing Material on Dew Point Measurement

ISO 8573-3 explicitly states that PVC, nylon and rubber tubing adsorb moisture, which distorts the measured value (it reads drier than reality, or continues to release moisture after drying). Sterile pharmaceutical plants should use electropolished stainless steel tubing or Teflon tubing (when the dew point is >-20°C) to connect the dew point meter, and keep the tubing as short as possible with no dead legs.

4.5.4 Total Hydrocarbon: Oil Aerosol and Oil Vapor

Total hydrocarbon includes both oil aerosols (oils that can form an aerosol remaining suspended in air until impinging a surface) and oil vapors (also called total volatile hydrocarbons). Both are typically associated with oils introduced as part of a compressed air system; oils are typically not present in cylinder-based systems.

The total hydrocarbon content within compressed air systems is typically addressed through design or engineering controls by installing oil-free compressors, which eliminate the vast majority of hydrocarbon and related issues. Removal of oil aerosols is performed through coalescing filters in the distribution system.

Where design or engineering controls are in place, oil aerosols are not typically expected to be present, and annual or less frequent testing or monitoring is required.

Methods for sampling oil aerosols:

  • Pre-weighed filter membrane(s) — analysis by gravimetric or infrared techniques
  • Detector tubes

Warning: If oil liquid is observed, the system must be immediately shut down and the cause of failure determined — oil liquid is intolerable in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

For oil vapors:

  • ISO 8573-1:2010 considers oil vapor measurement optional if purity limit is >1.0 mg/m³
  • Glass or stainless steel cylinders: suitable for lower molecular weight hydrocarbons (C1–C10)
  • Charcoal tubes or cylinders packed with charcoal: suitable for higher molecular weight vapors (C6+)
  • ISO 8573-1:2010 defines oil as mixture of hydrocarbons with ≥6 carbon atoms (C6+)

Oil Content Testing: Aerosol vs. Oil Vapor

An Oil-Free Compressor Does Not Mean Zero Oil Contamination Risk

Even with an "oil-free compressor", sources of hydrocarbon contamination may still exist in the system:

  • Lubricating oil for the compressor bearings (not in direct contact with the gas, but it may leak through seals)
  • Residual cutting oil in the piping (introduced during system construction)
  • Plasticizers released by aging rubber seals
  • Residual lubricant after valve maintenance

Therefore, even if the system is designed to be oil-free, an annual oil content verification program is still needed.

Sample Handling

Special considerations for gas samples and chain of custody

Original Text (ISPE GPG 2016)

Samples should be analyzed as quickly as possible after collection.

Samples should be clearly and uniquely labeled. Labels for internal analysis should include:

  • Sample or location identification
  • Analysis to be performed
  • Type of preservation required, if any
  • Sampling media identification numbers
  • Sampling parameters (sampling time, flow rate, temperature, pressure, etc.)
  • Date and time sample collected
  • Sampling technician name and signature

Labels for samples submitted to third parties for analysis should also include the company name and contact information.

4.6.1 Chain of Custody

Chain of custody documentation establishes that the sample has been properly transmitted from sample collection through sample analysis and is a recommended practice. If a collected sample is submitted to an outside firm for analysis, sample transport will be required and the bill of lading or airbill will become part of the chain of custody. Chain of custody seals or evidence tape may be used but are not mandatory.

Commentary and CDMO Application

Special Characteristics of Gas Samples

Sample handling for process gas sampling is fundamentally different from that for liquid samples:

  • Microbial samples (culture media plates/membranes): after sampling, transfer immediately to temperature-controlled incubation; the time of exposure to the environment must be recorded; the time from sampling to the incubator should not exceed 4 hours (following general environmental monitoring practice)
  • Dew point measurement: usually a real-time reading, so no sample needs to be "preserved", but the instrument records must be archived
  • Oil content (gravimetric filter membrane): after sampling, seal and store to avoid contamination by ambient oil vapor, and analyze as soon as possible
  • Detector tubes: read immediately after sampling; they cannot be stored and read later

GMP Requirements for CDMO Sample Labels

The label information listed by ISPE gives direct guidance for a CDMO's sampling SOP:

  • Sampling parameter records: flow rate, pressure, temperature, sampling time — these are critical parameters for the validity of the result and must be recorded in the raw data sheet
  • Sample identification code: corresponds to the P&ID sampling point identification, ensuring results are traceable to a specific location
  • Sampler's signature: the electronic record requirements of 21 CFR Part 11 or EU GMP Annex 11 apply

Chain of Custody for Samples Sent to External Laboratories

Although ISPE states that chain of custody is "recommended but not mandatory", for a CDMO, if the test results of gas samples (especially microbial samples) will be used for batch release decisions, it is advisable to establish complete chain of custody documentation:

  • Sample handover records (handwritten or electronic)
  • Transport condition records (temperature, time)
  • External laboratory receipt confirmation
  • Result report return records

Key Takeaways

  1. Gas quality = environmental quality: Any compressed gas or process gas in direct contact with sterile products or their environment must meet the same microbiological and particulate standards as the cleanroom. This is a regulatory expectation from both FDA (2004) and EU GMP Annex 1 (2022).
  2. Risk analysis drives everything: Sampling frequency, test scope, and sampling locations must all be justified by risk analysis tied to the specific use of the gas in the process.

  5. Monitoring frequency by CQA: Dew point = continuous; NVP/VP = quarterly; oil content = annual. These frequencies can be increased or decreased based on risk analysis and historical data.
  6. Sample connection matters: The tubing, fittings, and valves used to connect sampling instruments can be contamination sources. No brass or copper; prefer electropolished stainless steel.

Key Points (CDMO Perspective)

  1. Gas quality = environmental quality: the microbial and particulate quality of all process gases in direct contact with sterile product must be equal to or better than the cleanroom environment they enter. This is an explicit regulatory requirement of the FDA (2004) and EU GMP Annex 1 (2022), not a recommendation.
  2. Risk analysis drives everything: sampling frequency, test scope and sampling locations must all be based on a risk analysis of the specific gas use scenario; generic procedures must not be copied wholesale.
  3. QC vs. process control sampling points: the two types of sampling points must be clearly distinguished on the P&ID and in SOPs. Out-of-limit results at QC sampling points require a deviation investigation; results from process control sampling points are used only for system diagnostics.

Common deficiencies in process gas quality management:

  • No microbial sampling plan for compressed air, only oil content and particle testing

Commentary and Practical Application

When to Use Appendix 1

Use it when a QC laboratory or process engineer needs to select a "non-pharmacopeial" reagent water, for example for HPLC mobile phase, solution preparation or water supporting bioanalysis. These waters are not PW or WFI, but they still have strict specifications.

Overview of Where Each Standard Fits

  • ISO 3696: a European/international laboratory standard that controls organics by UV absorbance, suited to optical analysis
  • ASTM D1193 Type I: the highest-purity reagent water, resistivity 18 MΩ·cm, equivalent to CLRW, used for precision analysis
  • ASTM Type II: general laboratory water, resistivity 1 MΩ·cm, suitable for routine QC analysis
  • CLRW: clinical laboratory reagent water as defined by CLSI, 18.2±1 MΩ·cm, endotoxin <0.03 EU/mL (Grade A)

Practical Points for CDMO QA/QC

  • TOC comparison: ASTM Type I requires ≤50 ppb TOC, which is stricter than the 0.5 mg/L (500 ppb) of pharmacopeial PW/WFI and suits high-sensitivity analysis
  • Conductivity: ASTM Type I conductivity is 0.0555 µS/cm, far stricter than pharmacopeial PW (about 1.3 µS/cm); if a laboratory substitutes pharmacopeial PW for Type I, analytical bias may result
  • Microbiology: CLSI defines three grades (A/B/C), while ASTM itself sets no bacterial limit; clinical laboratory water requires specific reference to CLSI
  • Endotoxin: the CLSI Grade A limit of 0.03 EU/mL is close to the WFI limit of 0.25 EU/mL, and is suitable for water supporting cell culture or bioassays

Laboratory Water Selection Decision Tree (Simplified)

  • HPLC/UHPLC mobile phase → ASTM Type I or ISO Grade 1 (ultrapure water)
  • General buffer and solvent preparation → ASTM Type II or ISO Grade 2
  • Final rinse for glassware washing → ASTM Type III or ISO Grade 3
  • Cell culture, bioassays → CLRW (Grade A), with endotoxin control added
  • WFI process water → pharmacopeial specification (see also Chapter 2 of this guide)

Relationship to Pharmacopeial Water

Pharmacopeial PW (USP <1231>) has a TOC of ≤0.5 mg/L (500 ppb) and conductivity per the Stage 1/2/3 tables, which are less strict than ASTM Type I. This means PW cannot directly replace the Type I reagent water needed for high-precision analysis. In a CDMO laboratory, source each separately according to the intended use, or set up a separate ultrapure water system.

Appendix 2 — Examples of Water System Sampling Point Locations

Note: These figures are found in Appendix 3 of the ISPE Good Practice Guide: Approaches to Commissioning and Qualification of Pharmaceutical Water and Steam Systems (Second Edition). They have been modified and reproduced in this Sampling Guide. System Boundary designations apply.

Figure Descriptions — Five POU Configuration Types

Figure 6.1🔍: POU Piping Direct Connect to Process Equipment

Configuration: Direct Hard-Pipe to Process Equipment

System boundary: From the distribution loop POU valve through a short direct-connection pipe directly into the process equipment inlet (e.g., bioreactor, formulation vessel).

Sampling point placement: Sample valve positioned immediately downstream of the POU valve, before the process equipment connection. This captures loop water quality at point of delivery, representative of what enters the equipment.

Key characteristic: Simplest geometry — minimal dead-leg risk if properly drained. Sample valve should be accessible and installed as close as possible to the POU valve on the distribution side.

Figure 6.2🔍: POU Hard-Piped to Process Equipment (Extended Run)

Configuration: Hard-Piped Extended Connection

System boundary: Distribution loop POU valve → extended hard piping run → process equipment. The piping between POU valve and equipment represents a potential accumulation zone.

Sampling point placement: Sample valve at the POU valve on the loop side AND consideration of sampling at the equipment inlet where the extended run joins the equipment. The extended piping section falls within the system boundary and must be qualified.

Key characteristic: Risk of stagnant water in the connecting pipe between uses. Flush volume should be established during qualification. The sampling plan must account for the flush period before collecting a representative sample.

Figure 6.3🔍: POU Piping with Integral Heat Exchanger

Configuration: POU with Inline Heat Exchanger (Cooler)

System boundary: Distribution loop POU valve → heat exchanger (cooling to ambient or process temperature) → process equipment connection.

Sampling point placement: Sample valve on both the inlet side (hot loop side) and the outlet side (post-heat exchanger, cooled water side) to detect any heat exchanger fouling or ingress. The post-exchanger sample is especially important for detecting microbial activity at lower temperatures.

Key characteristic: Heat exchangers introduce a microbial risk zone. Hot loop water is microbiologically controlled by temperature; once cooled, stagnant areas can support biofilm. Sampling frequency downstream of the HX should be justified in the risk assessment.

Figure 6.4🔍: POU Piping with Hose Connection

Configuration: Hose-Connected POU (Mobile/Flexible)

System boundary: Distribution loop POU valve → flexible hose → process equipment. The hose is a detachable element and represents a significant hygienic risk zone.

Sampling point placement: Sample valve at the POU valve (loop side). For critical applications, an additional sample port at the hose outlet nozzle may be required during requalification or investigation.

Key characteristic: Hoses must be controlled under a separate SOP: defined draining, drying, storage, and re-connection procedures. Hoses are typically covered by a periodic re-qualification program (not continuous monitoring) and are identified as a separate system sub-boundary.

Figure 6.5🔍: Water Distribution System Sub-Loop

Configuration: Main Loop with a Sub-Loop Branch

System boundary: Main distribution loop → sub-loop branch (may serve a separate production area or building) → return to main loop or dedicated tank.

Sampling point placement: Sample points required at: (a) the supply take-off from main loop, (b) within the sub-loop at representative POUs, and (c) the return connection back to main loop or tank. This ensures the sub-loop is independently characterized and cannot re-contaminate the main loop.

Key characteristic: Sub-loops with lower flow rates or intermittent use carry higher biofilm risk. The sub-loop must have its own documented flushing and sanitization protocol. The return valve from sub-loop to main loop is a critical control point.

General Sampling Point Principles from Appendix 2

  • All POU sample valves should be of hygienic design (zero dead-leg or minimal dead-leg per ASME BPE standards)
  • Sample valves should allow complete flushing of the valve itself before the sample is collected
  • System boundary demarcations determine which components fall under the qualified water system versus process equipment scope
  • Sampling locations should be documented on the P&ID with unique identification numbers corresponding to the sampling plan
  • The sampling frequency at each location is determined by the risk assessment (Chapter 2 of this Guide) and the qualification phase (Initial/Intermediary/Extended)
Commentary and Practical Application

When to Use Appendix 2

Use it when designing or reviewing the sampling plan for a PW/WFI distribution system. When a QA engineer or validation specialist needs to decide "where should the sample valves go?" and "which configuration applies to my system?", these diagrams provide an intuitive reference framework.

Common CDMO Issues

  • Hose management: hoses are the deficiency most often cited by validation reviewers; a "hose control SOP" is needed, specifying flushing time before connection, storage method (hung to dry) and checks before use
  • Sampling after heat exchanger cooling: many plants sample only in the hot loop and ignore the cold water at the HX outlet. If the HX cools the water to <65°C, a sampling point must be added and the frequency increased
  • Sub-loops: sub-loop designs are common in multi-site or multi-purpose CDMO plants; note the need for independent IQ/OQ/PQ records and an independent sampling plan for each sub-loop

Meaning of the System Boundary

The system boundary determines the "validation scope": the piping, valves and sampling points within the boundary must all be defined in the URS/FS and included in the IQ/OQ/PQ test plans. Equipment outside the boundary (such as the process equipment itself) is handled separately. Sampling points are usually located on the water system side of the boundary line, to ensure the sample represents the quality of the controlled water system.

P&ID Documentation Requirements

Every sampling point (including the sample valve number) must be shown on the current P&ID and correspond to the sampling point ID in the sampling plan SOP. Reviewers often cross-check the P&ID against the sampling SOP; ensuring the two are consistent is a basic operating requirement for CDMO QA.

Appendix 3 — Factors Influencing Pure Steam Generator Performance

Pages 102–105 · Source water · Mist elimination · Non-condensable gases · Blow down · Chloramine · Additives

Original Content — Appendix 3
The purification system generating the water feeding a PSG typically needs the same monitoring and maintenance attention as pretreatment systems feeding distillation units that generate WFI grade water. This monitoring can help to optimize control of the pretreatment unit operations and steam generation processes, and alert the user to take remedial action to avoid Action Limits and specification failures in the steam.

The process of generating steam from water is reasonably efficient at separating pure water vapor from chemical contaminants. However, the separation is not absolute due to potential entrainment of mist from the incoming water (and its chemical contaminants) and carryover of contaminants into the pure steam.

Key Principle: 3-Log Reduction

It is commonly accepted that the phase change and mist elimination processes of distillation will purify feed water by at least three orders of magnitude (≥ 1000-fold or ≥ 99.9% impurity reduction), allowing no more than 0.1% of the feed water impurities to remain in the distillate or steam (see ISPE Baseline® Guide: Water and Steam Systems, Second Edition [7]).

7.1 Source Water

7.1.1 WFI as PSG Feed Water

Organizations may choose to feed their PSGs with WFI from a POU valve on the WFI loop. When feed water has very low or undetectable endotoxin levels and is chemically pure, no additional PSG feed water pretreatment monitoring is required. Chemical attributes of the WFI feed water will be very similar to those of the steam it generates.

7.1.2 Purified Water (PW) as PSG Feed Water

Many organizations feed PSGs from a PW distribution loop. Because PW has no endotoxin requirement, without specific microbial and endotoxin control precautions, excessive endotoxin levels may be present in the feed water.

With an assumed 3-log endotoxin removal capability and proper operation, the endotoxin level in the feed water would have to exceed 100 EU/mL to challenge PSG removal capability. Endotoxin levels this high are typically not seen in a well-controlled PW system. However, the actual log reduction capability of a PSG depends on its design (especially mist elimination presence).

7.1.3 Potable Water as PSG Feed Water

The USP monograph for PS requires preparation from a starting material meeting drinking/potable water requirements. While there is no compendial requirement that PSG feed water meets PW or WFI quality, water quality should meet the PSG manufacturer's specifications. Water quality attributes such as conductivity, TOC, endotoxin, ammonia, and others should be monitored to ensure they do not exceed PSG purification capabilities.

Based on initial cost and maintenance economics, it may be more practical to include additional capacity in PW/WFI systems to support the PSG rather than to build a dedicated potable water pretreatment system.

7.2 Steam Generator Mist Elimination Capability

The USP monograph for pure steam specifically states that the generation process "prevents source water entrainment." PSGs without mist elimination will have poorer log reductions of feed water contaminants and this should be considered when devising a sampling plan.

  • Well-designed systems using WFI feed water: low risk — no need for mist elimination since feed is already pure. No need to monitor feed water for conductivity, TOC, or endotoxin if WFI complies with USP.
  • PW feed water: mist elimination becomes more important since endotoxin levels may be higher.
  • Standalone PSG pretreatment (not well-designed or maintained): highest risk — most rigorous monitoring required regardless of mist elimination presence.

7.3 Non-condensable Gas (NCG) Removal

Without a dissolved gas removal process, non-condensable gases pass through the PSG and into the distributed steam. In low-usage systems, NCGs can accumulate. NCG presence reduces the efficacy of steam sterilization processes. BS EN 285:2015 and HTM 2010 specify NCG requirements for steam used in sterilizers.

Hot WFI feed water contains very little dissolved gas — few NCG problems. Ambient temperature feed water contains dissolved CO₂, O₂, and N₂. If highly deionized ambient feed water is used, CO₂ will be absent but N₂ and O₂ may be present (especially with ozone-based TOC/microbial control).

NCG removal methods: vacuum degasification or high-efficiency deionizers (CO₂ removal only).

7.4 Blow Down Adjustment

Monitoring of the blow down discharge coupled with periodic testing of conductivity, TOC, and endotoxin in the feed water may be appropriate to establish a blow down rate aligned with the manufacturer's recommendations and guidelines.

7.5 Potable Water Chloramine Use

Chloramine use in potable water disinfection is gaining popularity over chlorine (avoids THMs and HAAs). However, when chloramines are removed by pretreatment, residual ammonia may remain. Ammonia passes through most pretreatment processes and through a PSG, causing PS to fail its conductivity specifications.

Processes designed to remove chlorine are usually not as effective at removing chloramine. Changes from chlorine to chloramine disinfection by a municipality without notification may cause equipment damage and non-compliant WFI or PS — potentially impacting product quality and patient safety. Both ammonia and chlorine should be monitored after a dechlorination step when chloramine is in use.

7.6 Anti-scaling Steam Additives

The use of steam additives to minimize corrosion in PSG and distribution piping is strictly forbidden. Plant steam additives are intended to carry over into the steam. If used in PSG feed water, they would cause PS to fail its quality attributes. Plant steam condensate is never returned to PSG feed.

7.7 Monitoring Locations and Frequency

There are no pretreatment system monitoring requirements stipulated by regulatory authorities. Quality attributes should be periodically monitored at:

  • Incoming source water
  • Between pretreatment unit operations
  • Feed water to the steam generator

The frequency should be appropriate for each system and informed by risk assessment and historical data.

Commentary and Practical Application

When to Use Appendix 3

Use it when designing a pure steam sampling plan, investigating pure steam quality anomalies (high conductivity, endotoxin excursions), or reviewing the monitoring strategy for the PSG pretreatment system. Appendix 3 is also a reference document that a CDMO plant must review when changing the disinfection method of the municipal water supply.

Practical Meaning of 3-Log Removal

3-log = 99.9% removal of impurities. If the endotoxin in the PW feed is 10 EU/mL, the pure steam condensate is expected to be ≤0.01 EU/mL (meeting the USP requirement of <0.25 EU/mL). But if the endotoxin in the PW feed is as high as 500 EU/mL, even with 3-log removal the condensate may still reach 0.5 EU/mL, which is out of specification. This shows that microbial control of the PW system is critical to PSG performance.

The Chloramine Issue: A Practical Warning for CDMOs

Taiwan and many other regions have begun using chloramine instead of chlorine to disinfect tap water. If the activated carbon bed pretreatment is designed for dechlorination (not chloramine removal), ammonia nitrogen will break through and cause pure steam conductivity to exceed limits.

  • Confirm whether the local municipal water utility uses chloramine, and request the water quality report from the utility
  • Confirm that the activated carbon bed design can effectively remove chloramine (an empty bed contact time (EBCT) of ≥10 minutes is usually effective)
  • Monitor ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N) at the activated carbon bed outlet
  • Any change in the disinfection method of the municipal water supply should trigger a CAPA assessment

Non-Condensable Gases (NCG) and Steam Sterilization

NCG is one of the most common causes of steam sterilization failure. NCG accumulates at cold spots in the sterilization chamber and prevents steam contact; even if the thermometer shows the target is met, the local cold spot temperature may still be insufficient.

  • Hot WFI feed: little dissolved gas, so NCG is a minor problem
  • Ambient-temperature deionized water feed: a vacuum degassing unit must be installed
  • Ozone sanitization systems: ozone decomposes to produce oxygen, which may increase NCG
  • Periodically verify steam quality with the Bowie-Dick test or an NCG test

Blow Down Management

Blow down is the key operation for controlling the accumulation of total dissolved solids (TDS) in the PSG. If the blow down rate is too low, TDS concentrates and conductivity exceeds limits; if it is too high, energy is wasted and feedwater consumption increases. Recommendation: periodically test the conductivity and TOC of the blow down water, set the blow down rate according to the manufacturer's recommendation, and document it.

Practical Use of the Acronym Table

The first place to look when you meet an unfamiliar acronym in an ISPE or FDA document. New CDMO engineers or QA staff undergoing water system training are advised to read through the acronym table first to build a basic vocabulary.

Easily Confused Acronym Pairs

  • PSG vs PSE: PSG = pure steam generator (equipment); PSE = periodic system evaluation (activity)
  • CEDI vs RO: CEDI = continuous electrodeionization (the continuous version of EDI); RO = reverse osmosis (pretreatment)
  • CQA vs CPP: CQA = critical quality attribute (water/steam quality indicator); CPP = critical process parameter (operating condition affecting quality)
  • OOS vs Alert/Action Limit: OOS = out of specification (requires investigation); exceeding the Alert Limit = warning (requires monitoring); exceeding the Action Limit = action required (not necessarily OOS)

The Guide's Three Core Philosophies

  1. Representativeness: every sample must truly represent the actual state of the system being monitored; the flushing procedure, sampling point location and sampling timing all affect representativeness
  2. Risk-Based: sampling frequency and the number of sampling points should be determined by risk assessment, not by "the more the better"; over-sampling wastes resources, but under-sampling creates regulatory risk
  3. Continuous Improvement: through trend analysis (SPC), periodic system evaluation (PSE) and the CAPA mechanism, the sampling plan is optimized as the system matures

Guide Updates and Subsequent Developments

This guide was published in 2016. Important changes since publication:

  • In 2017 the EP allowed WFI to be produced by non-distillation methods (affecting WFI system design and monitoring strategy)
  • EudraLex Annex 1 was substantially revised in 2022 (introducing the CCS concept, affecting water system requirements in sterile plants)
  • USP <1231> continues to be updated (new grades such as HPWFI, and updated sampling and test methods)
  • Advances in RMM (rapid microbiological methods) technology (ATP fluorescence, flow cytometry) are changing microbial monitoring models
  • PAT and Industry 4.0 are driving more continuous monitoring and real-time release (RTR) applications

When using this guide, it is advisable to perform a gap analysis against the latest versions of the relevant regulations and pharmacopeial chapters.
